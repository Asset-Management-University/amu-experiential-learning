§1 Purpose of This Work Experience Module

WE-02 assesses your ability to coordinate lifecycle decisions in a real workplace — specifically, contributing to a maintenance strategy selection, a TCO analysis, or a replace/repair decision using structured lifecycle thinking. This module mirrors PM-02 (Lifecycle Coordination) but requires real workplace evidence.

Lifecycle coordination at NQF Level 6 means understanding that every asset decision — whether to maintain, repair, modify, or dispose — has a cost implication across time. The practitioner's role is to assemble the cost and condition evidence that allows the asset owner to make a defensible lifecycle decision, rather than reacting to failures ad hoc.

The lifecycle practitioner's contribution: You may not be the person who makes the final decision on whether to repair or replace an asset — but you are the person who assembles the evidence that makes the decision possible. A TCO comparison, a condition assessment, a maintenance history extract — these are the practitioner's contribution to the lifecycle decision. Without them, decisions are made by intuition or by whoever shouts loudest.

§2 What Workplace Evidence Is Required

Minimum evidence:

  • One logbook entry describing a lifecycle decision you contributed to — repair/replace analysis, maintenance strategy review, or TCO comparison
  • A document you produced or contributed to: TCO calculation, maintenance history extract, condition assessment, or decision recommendation — with your specific contribution identified
  • Supervisor verification of your role

Your entry must describe: the asset and the decision context, the lifecycle methodology you applied, the data you gathered and analysed, the recommendation you made, and the outcome.

§3 Worked Example Logbook Entry

Model Logbook Entry — WE-02 | PAP NQF Level 6

Date of workplace event: February 2026 (repair/replace decision for HY-002)
Organisation: KD Auto Workshop, Tembisa
My role: Physical Asset Practitioner (learner)
Module: WE-02 — Lifecycle Coordination

After the asset register audit (WE-01), Kagiso asked me to support the repair-or-replace decision for HY-002 (the 2-post hoist that failed in August 2025). The hoist had been out of service for six months with failed seals, and the question was: spend R4,500 repairing the seals, or replace the unit entirely at R22,000 (reconditioned).

Total Cost of Ownership — HY-002: Repair vs Replace

Data gathered: (1) Repair quote — hydraulic workshop: R4,500 for seal replacement + pressure test; (2) replacement quote — Titan Hoists: R22,000 reconditioned, 12-month warranty; (3) HY-002 maintenance history from workshop job cards 2020–2025: R2,200 seal repair (2022), R1,800 cylinder top-up (2023), R4,500 current repair = total repairs to date R8,500; (4) Kagiso confirmed HY-002 is a secondhand unit acquired in 2020 for R14,000; (5) current workshop bay income contribution: approximately R6,000/month per bay.

Cost factorOption A: Repair (R4,500)Option B: Replace (R22,000)
Immediate costR4,500R22,000
Cumulative repair costs to dateR8,500 (historical) + R4,500 = R13,000R0 (new unit)
Expected remaining useful life2–3 years (aging secondhand unit, 6 years old)8–10 years (reconditioned, warranty)
Annual maintenance cost estimateR2,000–R3,000 (recurring seal issues likely)R500–R800 (new unit warranty period)
Downtime riskHigh — recurring failure pattern, 6 months lost alreadyLow — warranty covers failure for 12 months
Revenue at risk per month downR6,000/monthR6,000/month
5-year TCO estimateR4,500 + (R2,500 avg annual maint × 5) = R17,000 + risk of further failureR22,000 + (R650 avg × 5) = R25,250 but high reliability

My analysis: The 5-year TCO of repairing is R17,000 vs replacing at R25,250 — a R8,250 difference. However, the repair option carries a high downtime risk: HY-002 has already been down for 6 months (lost revenue: 6 × R6,000 = R36,000 in lost bay capacity). A further failure event in the next 2 years would make the repair option more expensive than replacement. The replacement option eliminates downtime risk for the warranty period and extends useful life by 5–7 years beyond the repair option.

Recommendation: Replace HY-002. The downtime cost risk under the repair option (R6,000/month) outweighs the R8,250 cost saving over 5 years. A single further failure event lasting 1.5 months eliminates the entire cost advantage of repair. I presented this analysis to Kagiso in a one-page written summary with the recommendation and the supporting data.

Outcome: Kagiso accepted the replacement recommendation and placed the order. The R22,000 replacement cost will be funded from the fleet contract revenue over 2 months.

What I would do differently: I estimated the expected remaining useful life (2–3 years for repair option) based on general knowledge of secondhand hoist lifespans, not on a formal condition assessment. A condition assessment (checking cylinder wear, structural welds, load test result) would have made the estimate more defensible. For future lifecycle decisions involving equipment over 5 years old, I will build in a formal condition assessment step before finalising the TCO analysis.

Lifecycle insight: The 5-year TCO comparison shows repair is cheaper — but the analysis is only meaningful if you include the downtime risk. An asset that is cheap to repair but unreliable is not a low-cost asset — it is a high-cost asset with unpredictable timing. Lifecycle analysis must include risk-adjusted costs, not just direct costs, to give the asset owner a complete picture.

§5 Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Only comparing acquisition cost: "New machine costs R22,000, repair costs R4,500, repair is cheaper" is not a lifecycle analysis. The initial cost is one factor. Maintenance cost, reliability, downtime risk, and useful life are equally important.
  • Not identifying data sources: Cost figures without a source cannot be verified. State where each figure came from — quote, maintenance records, estimate, or prior experience.
  • Analysis without a recommendation: The purpose of a lifecycle analysis is to support a decision. An entry that ends with a comparison table but no recommendation has not fulfilled its purpose.

§6 Connect to Your Qualification

WE-02 connects PM-02 (Lifecycle Coordination) to real workplace decisions. The repair-or-replace framework, TCO methodology, and maintenance strategy selection skills from PM-02 are the tools. WE-02 requires you to show that these tools can be applied to a real asset with real cost data and real consequences for the decision-maker.
