Lifecycle costing models, and what each one leaves out

Where this begins
Two quotes for the same pump. One is R180,000 and one is R240,000, and everybody in the room can see which is cheaper. What nobody in the room can see is that the cheaper one has a seal that must be replaced annually at R14,000 fitted, and the other has one rated for five years - and the purchase order goes out on Friday.

What you will be able to do
By the end of this module you can put a whole life on one page - knowing which costing model you are using, what it captures, what it leaves out, and how the planning around it works.

Capital and operating budgets, and the difference that matters

Where this begins
The overhaul was refused because there was no capital left, and three months later the same work was done as an emergency repair out of the operating budget at nearly twice the cost. Both decisions were correct within their own budget. Nobody was looking at both.

What you will be able to do
By the end of this module you can tell a capital budget from an operating one and say why the difference matters, apply real budgeting methods, and coordinate the two so a portfolio is planned rather than defended.

The ethics of a lifecycle decision

Where this begins
You are asked to sign that the vessel is fit for service. You believe it is not. The person asking is your manager, the plant is needed on Monday, and you have a family that depends on this job.

What you will be able to do
By the end of this module you can name the ethical dimension of a lifecycle decision - transparency, conflict of interest, and what a signature actually asserts - and know what the record is for.

My manager told me to sign off an unsafe pressure vessel. If I refuse, I could lose the job my family depends on. What do I actually do?

Opens on the hardest form of it: an unsafe pressure vessel, a manager, and a job that feeds a family. This module has one criterion and it deserves to be met at full weight.
