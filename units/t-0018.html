T1: The Ache

It is 14:00 on a hot Thursday and you are standing on the steel walkway above the clear-water channel at Fairwater Water Works, the small municipal plant that cleans river water for about 18,000 homes. One key step is chlorine dosing - a measured amount of chlorine added to kill bacteria before the water reaches taps, with a target of 0.8 mg/L of free chlorine at the outlet and a legal range of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L.

In your hand is a paper logbook. The last entry, in the operator Keabetswe's neat writing, reads: "12:00 - chlorine 0.9 mg/L - pump 55%." You dip the test bottle, wait for the colour to settle, and read the comparator: 0.4 mg/L. Below the legal floor. For two whole hours, water leaving this plant has been under-dosed, and nobody knew. The afternoon flow has climbed to 115 litres per second - nearly triple the morning's quiet 40 - but the pump is still where Keabetswe left it at noon, pushing the same dose into far more water.

She folds her arms. "Every shift it's the same fight," she says. "I set it, I walk away, the flow moves, and the number drifts. I cannot stand here with a test bottle every five minutes. I have a plant to run." There is real strain in her voice - she has been written up once already for an out-of-range reading, and the plant manager wants answers by Friday.

You feel the pull to just turn the pump up now and move on. But you know the trap: in three hours the flow will fall back to 60 litres per second, the dose will be far too strong, and someone will be tasting bleach at the kitchen tap. Manual guessing, every two hours, against a flow that never holds still - it cannot hold the line. The dose has to follow the flow and the measured chlorine on its own, second by second, the way Keabetswe never can by hand.

The chlorine drifts every time the flow swings, and I was written up for a reading I could not prevent. How do I build a system that senses the process, decides, and acts on its own - holding the dose on target without a person on the walkway with a test bottle?

Before you read on, decide. The chlorine has to sit at 0.8 mg/L at the outlet. The channel flow swings anywhere between 0 and 150 L/s and never holds still. Keabetswe walks out with a test bottle every two hours and adjusts the dosing pump by hand. She has just been written up for a reading she could not have prevented. Write down what you would build so the dose holds — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what they all leave in the loop

• “Test more often — every thirty minutes instead of every two hours.” More of the thing that is already failing. The flow changes faster than any human schedule, so a tighter vigil narrows the gap without ever closing it, and costs Keabetswe her whole shift.

• “Dose a bit heavier so it never drops below target.” Trades one failure for the opposite one: at low flow the same dose puts the chlorine over the limit, and now the reading is wrong in the direction that is harder to defend.

• “Set the pump to a fixed rate matched to the average flow.” Correct on average and wrong almost all the time, because the flow is essentially never at its average — that is the whole problem.

• “Put an alarm on it so somebody knows when it drifts out.” That tells you afterwards. Keabetswe already is the alarm, and being the alarm is exactly what she was written up for.

• “Train the operator better.” This answer has already been given, in the form of a written warning, for a drift no vigilance could have prevented.

• Every one of them leaves a person inside a loop that is faster than a person. The move is to close the loop: something measures the chlorine continuously, something compares it against the target and works out the error, something acts on the dosing pump, and then it measures again — so the dose follows the flow second by second, which is the one thing Keabetswe can never do by hand. Notice what that does to her job rather than to her: she stops being the mechanism and becomes the person who watches it and can step in, which is where a human belongs in a loop like this. And it is ordinary engineering, not magic — an instrument chosen to survive the actual water, a valve sized for the actual dosing flow, a controller that knows what on-target looks like.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to build a control system that senses, decides, and acts - on its own. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

What you need is an instrumented process control system: the standard engineering answer to a process that must hold a measured value on target while conditions keep changing. It has four jobs - sense (instruments turn a physical quantity into a signal), decide (a controller compares the measurement to the target, the setpoint, and works out the error), act (a valve or pump changes the process), and show and connect (a screen lets a human watch and step in; a data link carries readings onward) - run continuously as a closed control loop: measure, compare, act, measure again. Watch Keabetswe and the technician Oratile build it on the chlorine dosing line.

Step 1 - Sense (the instruments and their signal)

The loop must know two things: the channel flow (the disturbance) - a magnetic flow meter, no moving parts to clog in conductive water, range 0-150 L/s, 4-20 mA; and the free chlorine at the outlet (the controlled variable) - an amperometric analyser, 0-2 mg/L, 4-20 mA. The 0.8 mg/L target maps to 4 + (0.8/2.0) x 16 = 10.4 mA, so the controller knows on-target at a glance. (Pressure and tank level are sensed too, but for protection alarms, not the main loop.)

Step 2 - Act (size the final control element)

The worst-case dosing flow (a small metering flow of dilute hypochlorite) is Q = 0.4 gpm, pressure drop dP = 16 psi across the valve, hypochlorite specific gravity about 1.2: Cv = 0.4 x sqrt(1.2 / 16) = 0.4 x sqrt(0.075) = 0.4 x 0.274 = 0.11. They do NOT pick a valve rated at 0.11 (fully open, nothing left to control); they choose one with a rated Cv of 0.25, so at worst case it runs at 0.11 / 0.25 = 44% open - comfortably inside the band, with room to act.

Step 3 - Decide (tune the PID controller)

By the Ziegler-Nichols closed-loop method: raise pure-proportional gain until the output just oscillates steadily - the ultimate gain Ku = 4.5, ultimate period Pu = 90 s. Then Kp = 0.6 x Ku = 2.7, Ti = Pu/2 = 45 s, Td = Pu/8 = 11.25 s (rounded to 11, trimmed small because the chlorine signal is noisy). Through the same 40-to-115 L/s morning swing that drifted the manual system to 0.4 mg/L, the tuned loop now holds chlorine between 0.74 and 0.86 mg/L - inside target, never near the legal floor.

Step 4 - Draw it and place it (ISA-5.1 diagrams, ISA-95 levels)

ISA-5.1 balloon tags: FT-101 (flow transmitter), AT-102 (analyser transmitter), AIC-102 (the PID controller), FV-102 (the dosing valve) - the first letter is the measured variable, the rest its function. On the ISA-95 Purdue model: Level 0 the process; Level 1 the field devices (FT/AT/FV); Level 2 control (the PLC and SCADA); Level 3 operations reporting; Level 4 business systems. The non-negotiable rule: the billing software at Level 4 must NEVER be able to move the chlorine valve - data flows up, control stays low.

Step 5 - Build it and connect it (PLC, HMI, data pipeline)

Oratile's PLC reads FT-101 and AT-102, runs the PID (setpoint 0.8, Kp 2.7 / Ti 45 / Td 11) with a flow feed-forward, drives FV-102, and on a low chlorine tank or a line-pressure leak raises an alarm and holds the valve safe - scanning every 100 ms, 600 times a minute, against the single reading Keabetswe used to take every two hours. Her SCADA HMI shows a clean mimic with live flow, chlorine and valve-%, the setpoint, an Auto/Manual switch, colour-banded alarms (red below 0.5 or above 1.0), and a 24-hour trend so a drift is a visible slope long before it alarms. The readings publish over OPC UA to the reporting system, and via MQTT to a cloud dashboard.

See what the loop did. The flow tripled, and the dose followed it - the chlorine held between 0.74 and 0.86 mg/L through a swing that, by hand, had drifted the plant below the legal floor for two hours. And see the mission in it, because this is not automation for its own sake - it is two acts of care at once. First, the people downstream: 18,000 homes drink this water, and an under-dose lets bacteria through while an over-dose is bleach at a child's kitchen tap, so holding the band, second by second, is a public-health duty no two-hourly test bottle could ever discharge. Second, the operator: Keabetswe was set an impossible vigil and then written up for failing it - blamed for not being in two places at once - and the loop does not replace her, it frees her. The machine takes the relentless, repetitive watching that no human can sustain, and gives her back the judgement only a human can bring: she now oversees a plant instead of chasing one number with a bottle. One rule guards it all: control is kept apart from the business systems, so the billing software can never move the chlorine valve - data flows up, commands stay low. The cost of the system is the measure of what safe water is worth; the aim is the line held, the people safe, and the operator freed for real work.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the loop in five moves - sense, act, decide, then draw it, then build it.

  • 1. Sense. Choose the instruments and their signals - the variable you hold, the disturbance that moves it.
  • 2. Act. Size the final control element so it has room to control.
  • 3. Decide. Tune the PID controller - fast enough to hold, stable enough not to oscillate.
  • 4. Draw it and place it. The ISA-5.1 diagrams, and the ISA-95 levels that keep control low.
  • 5. Build it and connect it. The PLC logic, the operator's screen, the data link upward.

The ideas underneath. The whole thing is a closed control loop: it measures, compares to the setpoint, acts on the error (setpoint minus measurement), and measures again, forever. The 4-20 mA signal is the field standard - a live zero, so a broken wire reads 0 mA and is instantly a fault, not a false reading. The Cv (flow coefficient, the heart of ISA 75.01) sizes a valve, kept between 20% and 80% of travel so it has room to act. The PID controller sets its output from three terms - P reacts to the error now, I removes the stubborn offset P leaves behind, D dampens overshoot (kept small on a noisy signal) - tuned by a recognised method such as Ziegler-Nichols. The P&ID and loop diagrams (ISA-5.1) let anyone read the plant; the ISA-95 Purdue model sorts everything into levels so control (low) is kept apart from business systems (high). A PLC runs the logic; a SCADA HMI is the operator's screen; feed-forward nudges the dose the moment the flow moves, before the error even appears; an interlock holds the plant safe on a fault. It fits any process with a measured variable that must be held against disturbances - dosing, heating, pressure, level, flow; it does not fit a one-off manual job or a simple on/off task (a tank that needs only a float switch does not need a tuned PID) - match the tool to the job.

Two disciplines keep the loop trustworthy. First, leave the valve room to control, and leave the operator a manual override - a valve sized to run fully open has nowhere to go when demand rises, and a loop with no Auto/Manual switch traps the operator the moment something unexpected happens; the automation must extend the person's reach, never remove their hand. Second, keep control apart from everything that is not control - the single most important line in the whole architecture is that the business and reporting systems can read the process but can never command it; data flows up to the dashboards, commands stay down at the plant, and a tuned, well-drawn loop is worth nothing if a billing query can swing a chlorine valve.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your control loop true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Leave the valve room to control. Size it to run mid-travel (20-80%), never fully open. A valve with no travel left cannot act when the process moves.

• A live zero, so a fault looks like a fault. The 4-20 mA standard means a broken wire reads 0 mA and alarms - not a false "zero" the controller trusts.

• Tune for stable, not just fast. A gain pushed too high oscillates and overshoots. The aim is to hold the line through a disturbance, calmly - test it against a real swing.

• Control stays low; data flows up. Keep the loop apart from business systems. The reporting layer may read the process; it must never command it.

• Automate to free the operator, not to trap them. Always leave an Auto/Manual override and a clear view of the state. The loop extends the person's reach; it does not take their hand off the plant.

You came onto the walkway with a drifting reading, a flow that never holds still, and an operator written up for a number she could not chase fast enough. You can leave with a loop that chases it for her: the sensors chosen, the valve sized to sit mid-travel, the PID tuned to hold 0.8 mg/L through a tripled flow, the diagrams drawn and the levels placed so no billing query can touch the valve, and a clean screen that lets her see the whole plant at a glance. A closed loop that senses, decides, and acts on its own, second by second - holding the line so the people downstream are safe and the operator is freed to do real work. That is what control engineering is for: not to take the human out, but to take the impossible vigil off her - so the machine does the watching it can do without rest, and the person does the judging only a person can.
