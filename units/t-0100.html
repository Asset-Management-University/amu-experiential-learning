Where the truth actually lives

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. Your manager catches you in the corridor with what sounds like a five-minute question: "Which of our high-lift pumps failed most often this year - and what did each failure actually cost us, in parts and labour?"

"Give me an hour," you say. It takes a week.

Because the moment you sit down, you hit the wall. The failures are logged in the maintenance system. The parts costs are in the finance system. The labour hours are in a third system. And the running data - how hard each pump was working before it broke - lives in a fourth. None of them talk to each other. To answer one question, you have to beg four different people for four separate exports, then stitch them together by hand in a spreadsheet, praying the pump names line up (they do not - one system calls it "Pump 4", another "HLP-004").

The maddening part: the answer exists. Every fact you need is sitting in a database right now. It is just shattered into pieces that do not fit together - and you have no map of where each piece lives.

Every fact I need exists somewhere in our systems - but it is scattered across four that do not talk, and I waste a week stitching it together by hand. How do I know where the truth actually lives, and make the pieces connect?

Before you read on, decide. The question is simple: what did that pump failure actually cost us? The work is recorded in the maintenance system. The parts and labour are in the finance system. When the pump was bought and what it has cost over its life is in the asset register. The vibration history that would say whether it was predictable is in the condition system. Every fact exists and is accurate. Assembling it took you a week by hand — and the systems name the pump three different ways. Somebody wants the answer again next month. Write down what you would do — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the boring detail that is actually blocking you

• “Replace them all with one system that does everything.” Expensive, slow, and it misreads why they are separate: each is genuinely better at its own job than a single system would be at four. It also does not fix the problem — a new system inherits the same question about what anything is called.

• “Build a report that pulls from all four.” The right instinct, and it will fail on the first join, because there is nothing reliable to join on. A report cannot match records that identify the same pump three ways.

• “Keep a spreadsheet that bridges them.” This is the week of manual work, made permanent and given a filename. It will drift the moment somebody stops maintaining it, and now people will trust it.

• “Clean all four systems so they agree.” Agree on what? Without deciding which one is authoritative for each kind of fact, cleaning is four teams making four sets of plausible changes, and the disagreement returns within a year.

• “Do the week of work again when it is needed.” Honest, and it means the answer effectively costs a week, so the questions worth asking are limited to those worth a week — which is almost none of them.

• This is not missing information; it is unconnected information, and those need opposite remedies. The systems are separate for good reasons and should stay separate. What is missing is two decisions. For each kind of fact, which single system is the master of it — the work, the money, the asset's life story, its condition — with everything else pointing at that rather than keeping a copy, which is what ends the arbitration between plausible versions. And one shared name for each real thing, used by all of them. That second one sounds like housekeeping and is the actual blocker: your week of work was not spent finding facts. It was spent deciding which records referred to the same pump.

There is a tool for exactly this - a map of where every truth lives. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the map Onalenna wished she had on day one - the whole information landscape of Corathyl on a single page. Five systems, and the one truth each is the master of. Read across them: notice that her week-long question is really just three of these boxes, joined.

1. The Doing - what work or activity happened?

Owns the maintenance truth: every work order - what broke, what was fixed, the spares used, the planned jobs. This is the CMMS (Computerised Maintenance Management System). Single source of truth for the WORK. Must link to the money system by the work-order number.

2. The Money - what did it cost, where are the funds?

Owns the financial truth: parts bought, purchase orders, labour rates, budgets. This is the ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) - the big company finance system. Single source of truth for the MONEY. A failure in the work system links here to find its true cost.

3. The Record - the master list of what exists, and its life story

Owns the asset's life truth: when each asset was bought, its capital cost, warranty, condition over its whole life to disposal. This is the EAM (Enterprise Asset Management). Single source of truth for the ASSET RECORD - every other system must point to its asset ID, never keep its own list.

4. The Health - how are things doing right now?

Owns the condition truth: the analytics that predict failure - vibration trends, remaining useful life, alarms. This is the APM (Asset Performance Management). Single source of truth for asset HEALTH. It reads its raw data live from the historian.

5. The Live - what is happening this second, and its history?

Owns the real-time truth: the pressures, flows and temperatures the plant is producing this second, and the full history of them. This is the DCS/Historian (the plant control system and its data store). Single source of truth for OPERATING DATA.

That is the whole idea: each system is the single source of truth for one kind of fact - the work, the money, the record, the health, the live data - and the skill is knowing which owns which, and how they must link. Onalenna's question was hard only because no one had ever drawn this map or connected the boxes. Now let us walk through how to build it.

T3: The Blueprint

This is an Asset Systems Map. Its job is to make an invisible thing visible: the landscape of systems that hold your asset truth, so you always know where to look. The whole map rests on one principle:

Single Source of Truth. For any one fact - a pump's cost, its last failure, its current pressure - there is exactly one master system that owns it. Other systems may read that fact, but only one may own it. Break this, and you get Onalenna's nightmare: the same "fact" in three systems, all slightly different, none trusted.

Now meet the systems - each one named by the question it answers (the names are just labels for tools that solve a real need):

  • "What work did we do?" -> the CMMS (Computerised Maintenance Management System): work orders, repairs, planned maintenance, spares.
  • "Where is the money?" -> the ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning): the company finance and purchasing system - costs, orders, budgets.
  • "What is each asset's whole-life story?" -> the EAM (Enterprise Asset Management): the master asset register, from purchase to disposal.
  • "How healthy is it right now?" -> the APM (Asset Performance Management): condition monitoring and analytics that predict failure.
  • "What is the plant doing this second?" -> the DCS / Historian: the control system and its store of real-time pressures, flows and temperatures.

No single system holds everything - and that is fine. The goal is not one giant system; it is a team of systems that each do one job well and connect cleanly (the work order in the CMMS carries the same asset ID as the record in the EAM, so a failure can find its cost in the ERP). Know the map, enforce one source of truth per fact, and connect the boxes.

Does it scale? Just as Corathyl has separate systems for the work, the money, and the live plant data, an airline runs distinct connected systems for flight operations, maintenance, ticketing and finance - and answering "what did that delayed flight truly cost?" depends entirely on knowing the map between them.

T4: Your Turn

Now map your own world. Pick something you help run - a student society, a team, a side hustle - and ask, for each kind of truth, which "system" owns it? (A system can be an app, a spreadsheet, a chat group, a drive, a notebook.)

  • The Money - the bank app? the treasurer's spreadsheet?
  • The Record - who are the members? where is the master list?
  • The Doing / Decisions - the minutes on a shared drive? the group-chat history?
  • The Live activity - the group chat? a live sign-up sheet?

Draw your map, then find the cracks: where is the same fact kept in two places that disagree? What one question is impossible to answer because two systems do not connect? That is your Onalenna moment - and now you can see it.

T5: The Guardrails

A systems map is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then strengthen your map.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The "one system to rule them all" myth. Believing a single piece of software can do everything perfectly. It cannot. The goal is a team of well-connected systems, each doing its own job well - not one bloated system that does all of them badly.

• Duplicating the truth. The most common failure: the asset list lives in the maintenance system and a spreadsheet and the finance system - and none of them match. Enforce one source of truth: one system owns the list; all others point to it.

• Boxes with no lines. Five perfect systems that cannot talk are still useless - Onalenna's week proves it. The connections (a shared asset ID, a shared work-order number) matter as much as the boxes.

• Confusing the system with the data inside it. A maintenance system full of "fixed the pump" (instead of the real failure mode) is a tidy box full of useless truth. The map shows you where the data lives; keeping it good is the job of your information strategy.

Onalenna lost a week not because the answer was missing, but because she had no map of where its pieces lived. With the map - and one source of truth per fact, and clean lines between the boxes - that same question becomes minutes, not days. Know where the truth lives, and you can always find it.
