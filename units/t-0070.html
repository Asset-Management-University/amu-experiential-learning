Step 1 of 5

Conduct a regulatory compliance audit for capital-intensive, regulated operations

T1: The Ache

Delta Crest Petroleum Ltd runs a small onshore oil field - the Otakikpo marginal field - in Rivers State, Nigeria. "Marginal field" just means a small field the big companies handed back, now run by a smaller Nigerian operator. Delta Crest pumps about 6,500 barrels of oil a day, employs 140 people, and ships its oil through a shared export line. You are the newly hired Compliance Lead. Mrs Ngozi Eze is the Managing Director - calm, but just embarrassed in front of a regulator. Tunde Bello is the Operations Manager - knows every pump and valve, has no patience for paperwork. Inspector Abang is a field officer from the national regulator - polite, thorough, writes everything down.

It is 7:40 on a Tuesday morning and you are standing in the Delta Crest site office at Otakikpo. The air conditioner is losing its fight with the heat, and you can smell the faint sourness of crude oil drifting in from the tank farm outside the louvre windows.

On the desk in front of you is a single sheet of paper: a Notice of Non-Compliance, stamped yesterday by Inspector Abang. It lists three findings from his surprise visit - the produced-water discharge log was missing for two weeks, one flare meter had no current calibration certificate, and the local-content training spend for the year could not be shown on request. At the bottom is a line that makes your stomach tighten: "Operator to demonstrate full compliance within 30 days or face suspension of operating consent."

Mrs Eze puts her coffee down without drinking it. "Last month I told the board we were fully compliant," she says quietly. "I believed it. Then Abang walks in and finds three problems in two hours. Three." She looks at you. "I do not even know how many rules apply to us. Tunde says hundreds. The lawyers say it depends. Nobody can give me one straight list of what we must do, who checks it, and whether we are doing it."

Tunde leans against the doorframe, arms folded. "We run a safe field," he says. "But every agency wants a different form. Some I have never seen filled in. I cannot tell you which ones are done and which are not."

You feel the pull to do the obvious thing - open the filing cabinet, grab the most recent inspection report, and start fixing the three findings on the notice. Close them out, send Abang the evidence, move on. But you already sense the trap. Three findings is what one inspector found in two hours. Fixing only those three is like swatting three mosquitoes and calling the swamp drained. In 30 days another inspector could walk in and find five more.

Mrs Eze does not need three problems patched. She needs to be able to look at a single page and know - for every rule that applies to Delta Crest - what it requires, who enforces it, and whether the company can prove it is meeting it today. How do I lay out every obligation, check the evidence for each one, and show the true state of compliance - not a guess, not "Tunde thinks we're fine," but a defensible record?

Before you read on, decide. Three findings on a notice, from one inspector in two hours. Tunde says the field is run safely and also that he cannot tell you which forms are done and which are not. Mrs Eze wants to look at one page and know, for every rule that applies, what it requires, who enforces it, and whether the company can prove it today. Write down what you would do first. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one can prove

• “Close out the three findings and send the evidence.” Necessary, urgent, and it is not the job you were given. Three findings is what one inspector found in two hours — fixing exactly those is swatting three mosquitoes and calling the swamp drained, and in thirty days another inspector finds five more.

• “Pull the last few inspection reports and work from those.” It tells you what has been looked at, which is a different set from what applies. The obligations nobody has ever inspected are the ones with no report to find, and they are exactly the ones Tunde says he has never seen filled in.

• “Ask each department whether they’re compliant.” You will get sincere yeses. “Tunde thinks we’re fine” is the current state of knowledge, and it is precisely what Mrs Eze is asking you to replace — not because he is wrong, but because nobody can check it.

• “Hire consultants to do a full compliance review.” They will produce a report that is true on the day it is written and cannot be re-run next quarter. What is wanted is a page the company can maintain, not an event it can buy.

• “Start a filing system so everything is in one place.” Useful and it is the second half. Filing organises the evidence you have; it cannot tell you what evidence should exist, and the dangerous gaps are the rows nobody has ever created a folder for.

Notice the shape of what she asked for, because every answer above is narrower than it: not the problems somebody found, but every rule that applies, what it requires, who enforces it, and whether you can prove it today.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to do this. Here is the tool.

T3: The Blueprint

You needed two things working together: a compliance matrix, and a compliance audit run against it. Take each as the answer to a plain question.

"How do I get one straight list of everything we must do?" The answer is a compliance matrix - simply a table. Each row is one obligation - one thing the law requires you to do. The columns tell you everything you need to know about it: where it comes from, who enforces it, what evidence proves it, how often it is due, who in your company owns it, and whether you are meeting it right now. It turns a vague fog of "hundreds of rules" into a countable list you can work through one row at a time. That is what the eight-row table in T2 is.

"How do I find out whether we are actually doing each one?" The answer is a compliance audit - a calm, evidence-based check. For each row, you ask one question: show me the proof. Not "do we do this?" - anyone will say yes. Instead: show me the document, the log, the certificate, the receipt that proves it happened. If the proof exists and is current, the row is compliant. If it is missing, expired, or cannot be found, the row is a gap. That is what the second table in T2 did to every row of the first.

A matrix without an audit is a nice-looking list nobody has tested. An audit without a matrix is three mosquitoes swatted at random. You need both.

The columns, and why each earns its place. Ref (a short ID so you can talk about a row); Obligation (the thing you must do, in one plain sentence); Source (which law or instrument requires it); Regulator (who enforces it); Evidence required (the exact proof that satisfies it); Frequency (how often it is due); Owner (the named person responsible); Status (Compliant / Gap / At risk). The two columns people forget are Evidence required and Owner. Without "evidence required," an audit becomes opinion. Without a named owner, every gap is everybody's job, which means nobody's.

The three sources of rules, in plain words. PIA - the Petroleum Industry Act, the master law for the whole oil and gas industry in Nigeria, passed in 2021. It sets up everything else: who may operate, how royalties and taxes work, and the duties operators owe to host communities and the environment. NUPRC - the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission. "Upstream" means the finding-and-producing end of the business - drilling and pumping oil out of the ground, as opposed to refining or selling it. NUPRC polices upstream operators: licences, production measurement, flaring (burning off unwanted gas), and field operations - this is who Inspector Abang works for. NCDMB - the Nigerian Content Development and Monitoring Board. "Nigerian content" (often just "local content") means using Nigerian people, Nigerian companies and Nigerian-made goods instead of importing everything. NCDMB checks that operators hire and train Nigerians, spend with Nigerian suppliers, and report it honestly - this is who the missing training-spend figure belongs to. There are others - environmental rules, tax, safety - but these three are the spine for an upstream operator, and they map cleanly onto the three findings in T1.

When this fits, and when it is overkill. A compliance matrix and audit fits whenever many separate rules apply to the same operation, different bodies enforce different rules, and you must be able to prove compliance on demand, not just claim it. That describes almost any regulated asset - an oil field, a mine, a power plant, a hospital, a bank. It is overkill when only a handful of simple rules apply and the proof is obvious to everyone - you do not build a 60-row matrix to track whether your office fire extinguisher was serviced. The matrix earns its keep when the number of obligations, the number of enforcers, or the cost of getting it wrong is high. For Delta Crest, with a suspension notice on the desk, it earns its keep many times over. And it is not a one-off rescue - the point of a matrix is that it lives. You build it once, then it becomes the thing you re-audit every quarter, so the next surprise inspection finds nothing.

Sharpen the tool on five more cases before you rely on it. Try each fully before opening the reveal - write your answer down first.

Task 1 - Read a row (warm-up)

Here is a single matrix row: DC-09 - Obligation: Submit an annual environmental management report. Source: PIA. Regulator: NUPRC. Evidence required: ? Frequency: ? Owner: ? Status: ? Fill in the four missing fields with sensible entries for Delta Crest.

What good looks like: Evidence required should be a named, checkable document - e.g. "Signed annual environmental management report, NUPRC-acknowledged." Frequency: "Yearly." Owner: a real role, e.g. "HSE Officer." Status: you cannot set this without checking evidence - write "Unknown - audit pending," not "Compliant." Guessing the status is the single most common beginner error.

Task 2 - Convert a vague claim into a testable obligation

Tunde says: "We handle our chemicals properly." Rewrite this as a proper matrix obligation row (Obligation + Evidence required + Frequency), so that an auditor could pass or fail it.

What good looks like: The obligation is specific and the evidence is something you could physically be handed. For example - Obligation: "Store and dispose of drilling chemicals per the approved chemical-handling procedure." Evidence required: "Chemical inventory log + disposal manifests, current month." Frequency: "Monthly." If your evidence column could not be photographed or filed, it is still a claim, not a test.

Task 3 - Run a mini-audit and assign status

You request proof for three rows and get this back: DC-10 (spill-response drill held twice a year) - last drill recorded 8 months ago. DC-11 (community grievance log kept) - log present, up to date, signed. DC-12 (gas-flare permit in date) - permit expired last week; renewal "in progress." Assign Compliant / Gap / At risk to each, with a one-line reason.

What good looks like: DC-11 = Compliant (current, signed proof in hand). DC-12 = Gap (the permit is expired - "in progress" is not proof; an expired licence is a hard fail, not an at-risk). DC-10 = At risk or Gap depending on the rule: drills are due twice a year, so at 8 months you have already missed the window - that is a Gap. The lesson: "in progress" and "almost" do not earn a compliant status. Only current, held evidence does.

Task 4 - Prioritise the fixes (judgement)

From Tasks 1-3 you now have several gaps. You have limited time before an inspection in 14 days. Put the gaps in the order you would fix them, and say why.

What good looks like: You order by enforcement risk and reversibility, not by what is easiest. An expired permit (DC-12) usually comes first - operating on an expired permit can halt production and is fixable by renewal. A missed drill (DC-10) is serious but can be scheduled fast. There is no single perfect order, but a good answer explains the reasoning - consequence of being caught, and how quickly the proof can be made real - rather than just listing tasks.

Task 5 - Build a six-row matrix from scratch (the real skill)

Choose any regulated operation you know - it need not be oil and gas. Build a six-row compliance matrix with all eight columns. At least two rows must be genuine gaps, and every gap must have a dated, owned corrective action.

What good looks like: Eight columns present and filled. Every obligation is one plain sentence. Every evidence cell is something a person could be handed. Every row has a named owner (a role is fine, "the team" is not). Status is set from evidence, not from optimism. Gaps carry a what/who/by-when action. If a stranger could pick up your matrix and run the audit without asking you a single question, you have got it.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your compliance matrix honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Every row says "Compliant" and the matrix was built in an hour. Cause: you audited opinion, not evidence - you asked "do we do this?" and wrote down the yes. Fix: re-run each row asking only "show me the proof." A row is compliant only when current evidence is physically in front of you. Expect your first honest audit to show more gaps than you are comfortable with. That discomfort is the matrix working.

• A gap sits open for weeks and nobody fixes it. Cause: the row has no named owner, or the owner is "the team." Fix: put one real person against every row. A gap owned by everyone is owned by no one.

• You fixed the three findings on the inspector's notice, then got caught on a fourth. Cause: you treated the notice as the full list. It was only what one inspector found in the time they had. Fix: always audit the whole matrix, not just the cited findings. The findings are a symptom; the matrix is the diagnosis.

• "Renewal in progress" / "the payment is coming" keeps getting marked compliant. Cause: confusing intention with evidence. Fix: only held, current proof is compliant. Anything in progress is At risk at best; anything expired is a Gap. Be strict - the regulator will be.

• The matrix is perfect but six months later it is wrong again. Cause: you built it as a one-off rescue, not a living tool. Fix: diarise a quarterly re-audit and a weekly check of due-dated rows. A compliance matrix is a habit, not a document.

• Two people argue over whether a row passes. Cause: the "evidence required" cell is vague ("good records"). Fix: tighten the evidence cell to a single named, dated artefact ("signed monthly production report, filed by the 10th"). If two reasonable people can still disagree after looking at the proof, the evidence definition is not specific enough yet.

A note on people and pressure. When an audit turns up gaps, it can feel like an accusation - Tunde may hear "you failed." It is not about blame. Frame every finding as a gap in the system, not a fault in the person: the log was missing, not "you forgot the log." People help you find the truth when the audit is safe to be honest in. An audit run as a hunt for someone to blame is an audit that gets lied to.

You came in with a stamped notice and 30 days on the clock. You leave with a matrix that surrounds every obligation, an honest 50% picture instead of a false "fully compliant," four dated actions already assigned, and a quarterly habit that means the next inspector finds nothing.
