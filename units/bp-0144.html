I've found a flaw and genuinely can't tell if it's a death-trap or nothing - so how do I decide, between panic and hope, whether this is safe to keep using, and for how long?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tariro, and you run a busy community hall - weddings on weekends, a children's creche through the week, the heart of the neighbourhood. During a repaint, up a ladder, you spot it: a crack in the main wooden roof beam, the one that holds everything up. Your stomach drops. Is it about to come down - or has it been there, harmless, for years?

There's a wedding booked Saturday and the creche needs the room Monday. But if that beam failed with people underneath, it is unthinkable. You freeze between two terrible options: cancel everything and close the hall indefinitely - safe, but you let down dozens of people and lose the income that keeps the hall's doors open - or tell yourself "it's probably fine" and carry on - cheap, but you would never forgive yourself if you're wrong. You stare at the crack, and staring tells you nothing.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to shut it all down, indefinitely, to be safe - it feels responsible, but it may be a needless shutdown: the crack might be entirely harmless, and you'd deny everyone the hall and lose what keeps it running, perhaps for nothing. The opposite pull is to carry on and hope - "it looks about the same as last year" - but that's reckless, gambling with people's lives on a guess. So you try to just look at it harder, agonising, turning it over and over - but staring gives you no actual basis to decide; dread is not data.

Panic-stop and hope-on are both guesses dressed up as decisions. So before you read on: what would let you make a real, defensible judgement between them - and who should make the final safety call?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

There is always a flaw somewhere - so the question is never "is it perfect?" but "is THIS flaw, THIS size, fit to keep going, and for how long?" That is a disciplined middle judgement, not a coin-flip between panic and hope. Five moves:

  • Judge it against what it needs to do, not against perfect: a thing in use is never as-new, so 'is it flawless?' is the wrong test - the question is whether it is still strong enough for the job it must actually do, with margin to spare. A crack can look frightening and still sit on a part with far more strength than its real load demands; you cannot judge the flaw without first knowing what the thing truly needs to carry.
  • Look at its shape and where it is, not just its size: the same-size flaw is a different thing in a different place: a crack at a low-stress spot is not the crack at the highest-load point, and a long thin flaw is not a deep sharp one. Where it sits and what shape it is matter as much as how big it is - judging by size alone can frighten you about a harmless one and reassure you about a dangerous one.
  • Measure how fast it's getting worse, not just today's size: mark the flaw (a pencil line at each end, a dated photo) and look again later - is it growing, and how fast? A stable flaw and a fast-growing flaw of the very same size are completely different calls: one may be an old, settled mark that has not moved in years; the other is a countdown. Today's size is half the story; the rate is the other half.
  • Match how deep you look to how close the call is: don't analyse everything to death, and don't wave through the borderline: where the answer is obvious - plainly fine, or plainly dangerous - a quick look is enough; spend real, expert effort only where the call is genuinely close. Putting your deepest scrutiny exactly where the decision is hardest is how you stay both safe and sane.
  • Give a verdict with a condition - and escalate the real safety call: decide, and attach a condition: keep running / keep running under a watch (with a limit and a recheck date) / repair at the next opportunity / stop now - never 'fine forever' and never 'closed forever'. And for a genuinely safety-critical thing - a beam people stand under, a chain that lifts loads - the final fitness ruling belongs to a QUALIFIED professional; this disciplined thinking is what lets you act sensibly, ask the right questions, and know exactly when the call is close enough that you must bring one in.

See it free Tariro from the freeze. She calls in an engineer, who explains the beam was built to hold an old, heavy slate roof - a load of perhaps 10 tonnes - but the hall now has a light metal roof weighing only about 2 tonnes, so the beam has roughly five times the strength it actually needs. Tariro marks the 15cm crack at each end in pencil and dates a photo; a month later it hasn't moved a millimetre - an old crack from when the timber settled long ago, not a growing one. The engineer's verdict: safe to keep running, under a watch - re-check every six months, call immediately if it ever starts to grow. The wedding and the creche go ahead, safely. (Those numbers only show the thinking; for any real building the final safety ruling must be a professional engineer's - in South Africa, one registered with the Engineering Council of South Africa (ECSA).) There is always a flaw - the real question is whether THIS flaw, THIS size, is fit to keep going and for how long: judge it against what it needs to do, not perfect; weigh its shape and place and the rate it worsens, not just today's size; study hardest where the call is closest; and reach a verdict with a condition - never a blank cheque, never a needless shutdown - escalating the real safety call to a professional.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a roof beam - the same judgement frees you wherever a flaw is found in something you depend on. Watch a lifting hoist.

See these moves with a worn lifting chain

A worker finds wear on a link of the chain on a workshop hoist. Panic says scrap the whole hoist; hope says keep lifting. Instead, run the moves:

• Against what it needs: the chain is rated for heavy lifts, but this hoist only ever lifts light loads - margin to spare.
• Shape and place: is the wear on a critical link at a bend, or a minor spot?
• Rate: measure the link's thickness now, and again next month - is it thinning fast or stable?
• Verdict with a condition: "use only for the lighter loads, and replace the chain within 30 days" - with the rigging specialist confirming the call for anything safety-critical.

Same logic, different flaw: judge against need, look at shape and rate, and decide with a condition - not a blank cheque, not a needless scrapping.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of something you depend on where you've found, or might one day find, a flaw and must decide stop-vs-run - a beam, a lifting chain, a wheel, a wooden deck, a critical part:

  1. Refuse both guesses. Don't panic-stop and don't hope-on - and don't just agonise; both extremes are guesses, and dread is not data.
  2. Run the four lenses, then make the fifth move: the verdict. Judge the flaw against what the thing actually needs to do (not perfect); note its shape and location, not just its size; mark it and measure the rate it's worsening; and study it hardest exactly where the call is closest.
  3. Decide with a condition - and escalate the real safety call. Reach a verdict (run / watch / repair-next / stop-now) with a clear limit and a recheck date - and for anything genuinely safety-critical, let a qualified professional make the final ruling, using your thinking to know precisely when to call them in.

That you froze was never weakness - it was the response of someone who genuinely cares about the people who stand beneath that beam, who would rather agonise than gamble with their safety. The missing piece was never your conscience; it was a method to turn that care into a clear, defensible decision. So refuse the false choice between closing forever and hoping for the best: ask what the thing truly needs, read the flaw honestly, watch whether it moves, and decide with a condition - leaning on a qualified expert for the calls that carry lives. That way you protect the people who trust your hall, and you keep its doors open for them too. Both, not one - because serving people and keeping them safe were never opposites.
