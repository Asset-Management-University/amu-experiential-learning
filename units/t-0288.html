Step 1 of 5

The report cites eight studies and recommends R240 million of pipe - and I cannot tell whether those were the best eight or just the eight somebody found. How do I check a review that left no trail?

T1: The Ache

Olivier District Water is about to standardise on a single pipe material for a twenty-year replacement programme - about R240 million of buried pipe across a district whose soils are mostly expansive clay.

The technical report recommending it is on your desk. It is competent, well written, forty pages, and it cites eight published studies, all of which say the material performs well.

You are the asset manager and your job this week is to check it before the committee commits.

You read all eight. They are real papers in real journals, correctly summarised. Nothing has been misquoted and nothing has been invented. And by the second one you have a feeling you cannot act on, which sharpens with each of the remaining six: every single one of them is from a temperate region with stable soils. Not one is from ground that moves the way this district's does.

So you look for the thing that would settle it - how the eight were chosen. What was searched, what came back, what was left out and why.

There is nothing. Not a suppressed appendix; there simply is no record. The report cites eight studies and says nothing at all about how it arrived at eight, which means you cannot tell the difference between the best eight studies in the world and the first eight somebody found. And neither, you realise, could the person who wrote it.

The committee meets on Thursday. "I have a feeling about the soils" will lose to forty pages and eight citations, and it should.

Eight real studies and no record of how they were chosen. How do I find out what this review left out - and produce something nobody has to take on trust?

Before you read on, decide how you would check it. R240 million, forty competent pages, eight sound citations that all happen to come from the wrong soil, no record of the search, and Thursday. Write down what you would actually do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five ways this gets challenged, and what each one achieves

• "Point out that all eight are from temperate soils." True, sharp, and the strongest thing available to you today - and it is an observation about their eight. The obvious reply is that those are the studies that exist, and you have nothing to say to that, because you also do not know what exists.

• "Find a study that contradicts them." Better, and if you find one you will be told it is an outlier - correctly, since one paper against eight is one paper against eight. You will have swapped their unrecorded selection for yours.

• "Ask the author how they chose the eight." Fair, worth doing, and the honest answer will be that they searched, read what came back, and cited what was relevant. That is what most reviews are, it is not misconduct, and it does not tell you what was missed.

• "Get an independent expert opinion." Buys authority rather than evidence, takes weeks, and produces another document whose selection is equally invisible.

• "Recommend delay until we have local data." Might be right and sounds like obstruction, because it offers the committee nothing to weigh - and the programme has a reason to proceed that does not go away because you are uneasy.

• What every one of these fights over is which studies to believe. That argument cannot be won, because the thing missing from the report is not a study - it is the trail. Nobody can say what was left out, so nobody can say whether the eight are representative or convenient. The move is not to find a better eight. It is to do the search in a form where the count at every stage, and the reason for every exclusion, is written down - so that what you produce can be checked, repeated and argued with, and so can the gap between it and theirs.

T2: The Tool in Action

Write the question narrowly enough to be searchable. Write the search down before running it, and run exactly that. Screen in stages, recording the count and the reason at each one. Then put your set beside theirs and look at what is in yours and not theirs. Watch it run on the pipe report.

Step 1 - Write the question narrowly enough that a search can answer it

The original report's question, reconstructed from its text, was effectively 'is this pipe material good?'. Thandi Sebeko writes the answerable version: 'in buried potable water reticulation, in aggressive or expansive soils, how does the failure rate of this material over twenty years compare with ductile iron?' Population, comparison and outcome, all named. She notes what the narrowing costs and gains: it excludes pressure mains and industrial use, which is a real limit and one anybody can now see and argue with.

Step 2 - Write the search down before you run it, then run exactly that

Thandi writes it out: two databases plus the national water research repository, terms combining the material, buried water reticulation and failure or leakage, English and Afrikaans, 1995 to date. She runs it and gets 214 records. She saves the exact query strings in the appendix - not a description of them, the strings - because a description is something a reader has to trust and a string is something they can paste.

Step 3 - Screen in two passes, and record the count and the reason at every step

Two hundred and fourteen identified; 37 duplicates removed, leaving 177 screened; 121 excluded on title and abstract as wrong application or wrong material; 56 full texts sought; 9 not retrievable, which she lists by citation so the gap is visible rather than silent; 47 assessed in full; 31 excluded with the reason recorded against each - mostly no comparison group or under five years of data; 16 included. Every one of those numbers is in the report, and the nine she could not get are named.

Step 4 - Put your set beside the original one and look at what is in yours and not theirs

All eight of the original report's studies are inside Thandi's sixteen, so nothing it cited was invented or misread. But her sixteen contain eight studies the report does not mention, and five of those report worse performance than the eight - and four of the five are from soils and climates closer to this district than any of the eight, which are largely from one temperate region. She writes the conclusion carefully, because it matters that it is not an accusation: the original eight were not chosen dishonestly, they were the ones a search found and found agreeable, and nothing in that report records enough for anybody - including its author - to have noticed.

Step 5 - Say what the evidence supports, what it does not, and where it is thin

Thandi's finding is not that the material is bad. It is that the evidence supporting it comes overwhelmingly from soils unlike this district's; that the five studies from comparable ground report failure rates two to three times higher; that no study anywhere follows the material past fourteen years in expansive clay, so the twenty-year assumption is unsupported by anybody's data rather than contradicted; and that the cheapest thing the department could do before committing R240 million is a trial section in the worst clay it has, monitored for three years, at about R2 million. The recommendation is not to abandon the material. It is to stop treating an absence of evidence as evidence.

See where the finding actually came from. All eight of the original studies are inside the sixteen, so nothing in that report was invented or misread - and the sixteen contain eight studies it never mentions, five of them reporting worse performance, four of those five from ground closer to this district than any of the eight. The difference between the two reviews is not diligence and it is certainly not honesty. It is that one of them recorded what it searched and what it discarded, so the five became visible; the other could not have noticed them, and neither could its author. And notice the shape of the conclusion Thandi actually writes: not that the material is bad, but that no study anywhere follows it past fourteen years in expansive clay - so the twenty-year assumption is unsupported rather than contradicted, and the cheapest next step is a R2 million trial section rather than a R240 million commitment or a delay.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the second is the one that makes the rest worth anything.

  • 1. Write the question with the population, the comparison and the outcome all named - narrow enough that a search can answer it.
  • 2. Write the search down before running it - databases, terms, languages, dates - and save the exact query strings, not a description of them.
  • 3. Screen in stages, recording the count and the reason at every one, and naming what you could not retrieve.
  • 4. Compare your set with theirs, and look hardest at what is in yours and not in theirs.
  • 5. State what the evidence supports, where it disagrees and where it is thin - and what would change the answer.

What you have produced is a systematic review, and what makes it systematic is not thoroughness - a diligent reader can be thorough - but that the method is recorded before the results are known and can be run again by somebody else. The record of counts through each stage is a flow diagram, and the convention for reporting it is PRISMA, which is worth knowing by name only because a committee that has seen one before will look for it. The pre-written question and search are the protocol, and writing it first is what stops the search quietly narrowing toward whatever it is finding. The stated reasons for exclusion are the part reviewers actually read, because the excluded studies are as much the finding as the included ones. And the fault this whole apparatus exists to defeat has a name: selection bias - not lying, but the ordinary human tendency for a search to stop when it has found enough that agrees.

It fits any decision large enough that somebody will ask "why those studies?" - a technology choice, a standard, a treatment, a policy, a design assumption. Two limits belong with it. It is far slower than reading around a subject, so it is for decisions where being challenged is likely and expensive, not for every question. And it finds only what was published and indexed: a material that performs badly and whose failures were never written up will look fine, so an absence of contrary evidence is never evidence of absence - which is exactly the trap the twenty-year assumption in this case had fallen into.

Two disciplines separate a review from a reading list. First, write the search down before you run it, and then run that search rather than the better one you think of on the way. This feels pedantic and it is the entire mechanism: a search that evolves as it goes will drift, invisibly and in good faith, toward the terms that are producing the papers you find interesting - and nobody, including you, will ever be able to see that it did. If you genuinely need to change the search, record that you changed it and why, and run the whole thing again. Second, report the exclusions and the things you could not get, by number and by reason. The instinct is to present what you found; a reader's actual question is what you discarded and on what grounds. Naming the nine papers you could not retrieve is not an admission of weakness - it is the difference between a gap somebody can assess and a gap nobody can see.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the review honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A search written after the results are in. Then it is a description of what you happened to find, and it will read exactly like a protocol. Write it first, save the strings, and record any change to it.

• Counts without reasons. "121 excluded" tells a reader nothing. "121 excluded as wrong application or wrong material" can be disagreed with, which is the point.

• Unretrievable papers left unmentioned. A silent gap looks like no gap. Name them; nine papers you could not get is a known limitation, and nine papers nobody knows about is a flaw.

• Thoroughness mistaken for method. A careful reader who found forty papers has still selected them invisibly. What makes this different is only that somebody else can run it and get your set.

• No contrary evidence read as support. Failures often go unpublished. If nothing anywhere follows the thing past fourteen years, that is an absence and not a reassurance.

• The finding written as a verdict. A review that concludes "the material is suitable" has done what the unrecorded one did. Say what the evidence supports, where it disagrees, and what would change it.

• Treating the other author as dishonest. Almost always they searched, read and cited in good faith - the fault is in the method, not the person, and saying so is both true and the only way the finding gets accepted rather than fought.

You had forty competent pages, eight sound citations, a feeling about the soil that was worth nothing, and Thursday. You can put something else in front of the committee: a question written to be answerable, a search anybody can paste and re-run, 214 records narrowed to 16 with the count and the reason at every step and the nine you could not get named - and inside it the five studies nobody had seen, from ground like this district's, reporting failure rates two to three times higher. The thing missing from that report was never a study. It was the trail - and without one, nobody can tell the best eight from the first eight. The recommendation is R2 million of trial section in the worst clay in the district, and it is a recommendation the author of the original report would agree with, because there was nothing wrong with his eight except everything he could not see.
