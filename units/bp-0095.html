Step 1 of 5

We should have seen it coming - and we did

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, halfway down a steep hill, the bakkie loaded with gas for a new customer. You press the brake - and it's soft. You press harder; the pedal sinks toward the floor and the bakkie barely slows. For a few endless seconds the whole world shrinks to the smell of hot brake pads and a desperate hope the handbrake will bite. It does, just. You sit shaking on the verge, heart hammering - the delivery late, the customer let down, a repair bill of thousands you hadn't planned for. Take a breath. Being shaken by a close call is human, not weakness - and brakes that sink to the floor are an emergency: a vehicle like that stays parked until they are fixed.

But that isn't the worst of it. The worst is the cold drop in your stomach as you remember: two weeks ago Sipho climbed out after a run and said, "Boss, the brakes on this one are getting a bit soft, hey." You nodded, mid-phone-call, thought must sort that - and forgot.

It wasn't a surprise. It was a warning you let expire. It had a voice, a face, a clear detail - and nowhere to live. No list, no owner, no date. So a thing you genuinely KNEW arrived dressed as an emergency. Afterwards people will say "we should have seen it coming." The truth is harder: you did.

The brakes didn't ambush me - I was warned and I knew. So how do I stop the things I ALREADY know from arriving as disasters?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to blame yourself - "be more careful, remember better" - or make a rule: fix any brake problem at once. You get this bakkie's brakes sorted, perfectly. But it won't hold, because the problem was never your memory or this one brake. While you were focused here, you forgot the tyre someone said was going smooth, and the licence disc on the other bakkie that's about to expire. You fixed one symptom; the real gap remains - you have no reliable way to turn a quiet warning into committed action. The next "surprise" is already waiting its turn.

Before you read on: if the failure was foreseen, then noticing wasn't the problem. So what exactly is the gap - and what would close it?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here's the truth most people learn too late: the failures that hurt most are almost always foreseen. The gap is never in noticing - it's in turning noticing into doing. That's a system, not a memory trick. Five moves build it:

  • The hard truth: the worst failures were FORESEEN: the disasters that hurt most are usually the ones someone already saw coming - a note, a worry, a passing 'hey, watch this'. The gap is NOT in noticing. It is in turning noticing into DOING. A warning that stays in someone's head, or dies in a passing comment, will arrive later dressed as a 'surprise' that never was.
  • One living list - a home for every warning: get one place - a notebook, a board, a shared note - call it the Worry List. The rule is absolute: any 'this could go wrong', from anyone, big or small, goes on it. A driver's comment, a funny noise, a looming licence date. Now warnings stop dying in conversations and start being seen.
  • Size each: how likely x how badly it hurts: you can't fix everything at once, so score each item: LIKELY (1 = almost never, 5 = it's only a matter of time) and HOW BAD (1 = minor hassle, 5 = catastrophic for money, safety, or trust). Multiply. Soft brakes (4 x 5 = 20) come before a cracked mirror (2 x 1 = 2). Act on the worst, not the loudest.
  • Give every item an OWNER and a DATE: this is the bridge from noticing to doing. A risk with no owner is an orphan no one tends; a task with no date is just a wish. Not 'soft brakes' but 'Muhammad gets two brake quotes by Friday 2pm'. Now it is a concrete, accountable action - not a feeling.
  • 'Managed' means ACTED ON - or it counterfeits safety: review the list often and CROSS THINGS OFF because they're DONE. The goal isn't a tidy list - it's an empty one. A long, neat list nobody acts on is WORSE than none, because it feels like safety while it's really just a record of your future failures. (And make it safe for people to raise warnings - never punish the messenger.)

The make-or-break is that last move. It is dangerously easy to make a neat list, feel responsible, and stop - while nothing actually changes. A tidy list nobody acts on is worse than no list at all, because it counterfeits safety: it feels like you're managing risk when you're only documenting your future failures. The goal is never a beautiful list - it's an empty one, because the dangerous items keep getting crossed off, DONE.

And notice the human part: this only works if people feel safe to speak up. Sipho's warning was a gift; if raising it had earned him a snap, the next warning dies unspoken. A home for worries needs a welcome for the people who carry them.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't about bakkies - it's about any warning you already hold. Watch it somewhere completely ordinary.

See the same move with the smoke alarm everyone ignores

For three weeks the smoke alarm has chirped every minute or so - that flat-battery chirp. You KNOW what it means. You know you should change it. But every time you hear it you're busy, and you think "later". The knowledge ("this is a safety risk") never becomes the action ("I changed the battery"). That's the whole gap, in your own home.

• List it: on a page on the fridge - your home's Worry List - write "smoke alarm battery flat".

• Size it: a fire may be unlikely (say 2), but the harm is catastrophic (5) - score 10. That beats the wobbly chair leg (score 4).

• Own it + date it: "Me - buy a 9-volt battery Saturday morning."

• Act on it: Saturday you fit it, press test, hear the loud BEEP - and cross it off. A nagging "it'll probably be fine" becomes real, tested safety.

Same move, no engine in sight: a warning you already had, turned into one owned, dated, finished action.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This is a master skill for a whole life, not just a fleet. Think of the vague worries floating in your head right now - stealing your peace precisely because they're shapeless and un-owned: a weird noise in your car, a pension statement unchecked for a year, a hard conversation you keep postponing, a health thing you're avoiding. Take them out of your head and give them a home:

  1. One list. Write every "this could go wrong" in a single place - your worries, your bakkie's, your business's. Get them out of memory and into the light.
  2. Size them. Score each by how likely it is and how badly it would hurt, and act on the highest first - not the loudest or the easiest.
  3. Owner and date - then DO it. Give your top worry a named owner (you) and a concrete, dated first step - not "fix my finances" but "find my pension login tonight". Then act, and cross it off. Keep emptying the list.
R

Write your worry list this week, size the top three, and give number one an owner and a date. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

Almost every disaster that "came out of nowhere" was, in truth, seen by someone first. The people who stay safe and steady aren't the ones who notice more - they're the ones who turned what they noticed into something owned, dated, and done. Close that small gap between knowing and doing, and you trade the fuzzy static of worry for the calm hum of a plan in motion - and far fewer foreseeable things will ever ambush you again.
