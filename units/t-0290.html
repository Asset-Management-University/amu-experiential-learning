Step 1 of 5

The arithmetic says this machine loses money, and it would make money if I knew one fact I will know in eleven months - so how do I put a price on waiting?

T1: The Ache

Baloyi Steel Fabricators employs thirty-one people in a shed outside Vereeniging, cutting and welding structural steel for mines and municipalities.

You have a quotation on the desk for a plasma cutting machine, R6.8 million - about a fifth of annual turnover, and the largest single thing this business has ever considered buying. It would take work that currently goes out to a subcontractor at a margin you keep watching leave.

You have also done the arithmetic properly, which is why you are stuck.

Your biggest client's framework tender is awarded in eleven months. Win it and their fabrication demand roughly doubles for six years, and the machine pays for itself comfortably. Lose it and the machine earns a little on existing work and does not come close. The client's own people put their chance at about 45 per cent, and you have no reason to think better of it than they do.

So you weight the two outcomes, add them, subtract the cost, and get minus R80,000. A marginal loss. On that number the answer is no, and the number is not wrong.

And you cannot make the feeling go away that the answer is still incomplete - because in eleven months you will know. Not estimate. Know. There will be a letter. And nothing in the calculation has anywhere to put that fact: the arithmetic assumes you are buying today, because that is the only timing it has a row for.

Your bank manager, who is reasonable, has already used the phrase "wait and see", and it landed the way it always does - as the absence of a decision. You are fairly sure it is not, and you cannot say why in a way that would survive a page.

The arithmetic says no, and in eleven months I will simply know the answer. How do I put a number on waiting, so it stops reading as indecision?

Before you read on, decide. R6.8 million, thirty-one jobs, a 45 per cent chance, an award date eleven months out, and a properly done calculation that says minus R80,000. Write down what you would do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does with those eleven months

• "Follow the arithmetic. Do not buy." Disciplined and it is the right answer to the question that was asked. It quietly throws away something valuable, because it treats the choice as buy-or-not rather than buy-now, buy-later or never, and only one of those was priced.

• "Buy it - back yourself, the margin is walking out of the door." The instinct of somebody who has watched that subcontractor's invoices, and it commits a fifth of turnover on a coin toss slightly worse than even. Thirty-one jobs sit behind that number.

• "Raise the win probability - our relationship is strong." Very tempting, and it is adjusting an input until the answer changes. The client's own estimate is 45 per cent, and preferring your own to theirs is a decision about which number you like.

• "Lease it instead of buying." Genuinely worth pricing and a different question - it changes the shape of the cost, not the fact that the demand is unknown for eleven more months.

• "Wait and see." Hold on to this one, because it contains the right answer and is stated in the way that guarantees it will be dismissed. As a phrase it means no decision has been made. As a plan - buy this machine if and only if the tender is won, decided now, with the option deliberately kept alive - it is a different thing entirely, and it can be priced.

• What the first four share is that they all decide today. But the position you are actually in is unusual and valuable: a specific unknown, with an external date on it, that you will not have to guess about. Waiting is not the absence of a choice here - it is a choice, it has a value, and the reason the arithmetic gave it none is that nobody calculated it.

T2: The Tool in Action

Name the one unknown and the date it resolves. Value the investment in each branch separately. Do the conventional calculation and watch it fail. Then price the other plan - wait, and act only on good news - including what waiting costs. Watch it run on the plasma cutter.

Step 1 - Name the one thing you do not know, and the date you will know it

Hlengiwe Baloyi writes it in one line: the client's framework tender is awarded in eleven months, and if they win it their fabrication demand roughly doubles for six years. If they lose it, demand stays where it is. That is the only thing about this decision she does not know - the machine's price, its capacity and its running cost are all firm quotes. And the date is real and external: it is the client's award date, not an estimate of when the market will feel clearer, which is the kind of date that never arrives.

Step 2 - Work out what the investment is worth in each branch, separately

The plasma cutter costs R6.8 million. If the tender is won, the extra margin it makes possible is about R2.9 million a year for six years, which at 12 per cent is R11.92 million in today's money. If the tender is lost, the machine still earns something - about R600,000 a year on existing work, or R2.47 million over the six years. Two branches: R11.92 million against R2.47 million. Hlengiwe writes them side by side and does nothing else with them yet.

Step 3 - Work out the answer for committing now, and see it fail

The client puts their chance of winning at about 45 per cent, and Hlengiwe uses their figure rather than a friendlier one. So 0.45 times R11.92 million plus 0.55 times R2.47 million is R6.72 million of expected value, against a cost of R6.8 million. The answer is minus R80,000 - a marginal loss, which on any normal committee is a rejection, and correctly so on its own terms. And look at what those terms are: every part of that sum assumed the machine is bought today. Nowhere in it is there a place to write down that the tender is awarded in eleven months.

Step 4 - Now value the other thing you could do: wait, and act only if the news is good

Wait eleven months, then buy only if the tender is won. In that branch the margin starts later, so its present value falls from R11.92 to about R10.73 million, and the machine's price escalates about 4 per cent to R7.07 million - so the good branch is worth R3.66 million and it only happens 45 per cent of the time: R1.65 million. In the bad branch she buys nothing and loses nothing. So waiting is worth about R1.65 million, against minus R80,000 for committing now. The difference - roughly R1.7 million - is what the freedom to wait is worth on this decision, and it was worth exactly nothing in the calculation the committee was given.

Step 5 - Say what you are actually recommending, and what would destroy the value

Hlengiwe recommends not buying, and the paper says precisely what that means. Keep the option alive: hold the supplier's quotation validity by paying the R40,000 extension fee twice, keep the floor space unallocated, and do the electrical provisioning now at R180,000 because it takes fourteen weeks and would otherwise be the thing that makes the machine late. Spend nothing else. The decision returns to the table the week the tender is awarded, diarised. And she writes the sentence that stops this being read as timidity: this is not a decision to wait and see. It is a decision to buy the machine if and only if the tender is won, made now, and worth about R1.7 million more than deciding either way today.

See where the R1.7 million was hiding. Committing today is worth minus R80,000 and the calculation is correct. Waiting and buying only if the tender is won is worth about R1.65 million - after paying for eleven months of forgone margin and 4 per cent of price escalation, both of which are in the figure. Nothing about the machine changed. What changed is that a second plan got priced, and the first calculation had no row for it because it assumed the purchase happens now. And notice how Hlengiwe writes the recommendation, because this is where the idea usually dies: not "wait and see", which reads as no decision, but buy this machine if and only if the tender is won - decided today, with R220,000 spent deliberately on keeping the option alive and the electrical work done now because it has a fourteen-week lead time. For thirty-one people in a shed outside Vereeniging, the difference between those two sentences is whether the business risks a fifth of its turnover on a coin toss.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the fourth is the one nobody does.

  • 1. Name the one unknown and the date it resolves. No date, no value - and it must be an external date, not a feeling that things will be clearer.
  • 2. Value each branch separately, in today's money, over the life. Do not average yet.
  • 3. Do the conventional calculation - weight, add, subtract the cost - and note what it assumed about timing.
  • 4. Price the wait-then-act plan, including forgone earnings and price escalation.
  • 5. Say what preserves the option and what would destroy it, and diarise the date.

What you have valued is a real option - the word "real" only meaning that it attaches to a physical decision rather than a traded contract. The specific one here is an option to defer, and its value comes from a fact worth stating plainly: uncertainty plus the freedom to act later is worth money, where uncertainty alone is only risk. The conventional calculation you did in move 3 is a net present value, and it is not wrong - it is answering the question "is this worth buying now", faithfully and completely. What it cannot do is compare timings, because a single NPV has one implied decision date and no row for another, which is why the freedom to wait comes out at exactly zero every time. And the cost side of move 4 has a name too: the earnings you forgo by starting later, plus escalation, are the cost of deferral, and an option is only worth holding while its value exceeds that.

It fits a decision with a genuinely uncertain outcome, a specific date by which you will know more, and the practical ability to act later - all three, and the third is the one people miss. Three limits, and the first two are how this technique is abused. It is not a justification for delay: where nothing will be learned by waiting, or where the position will be gone if you wait, the option is worth nothing and the honest answer is to decide now. It requires somebody else's probability where possible, because the value rises with uncertainty, which means anybody who wants to justify waiting can get the answer they want by widening the range. And it says nothing about affordability: an option worth R1.65 million is still worthless to a business that will not exist in eleven months.

Two disciplines keep this honest, and the first is the one that separates the technique from an excuse. Write the resolution date first, and require it to be somebody else's date rather than yours. A tender award, a licence decision, a harvest, a court date - these resolve whether or not you are ready. "When the market is clearer" and "once we understand it better" are not dates; they are the shape this method takes when it is being used to avoid a decision, and they will produce a large option value that never gets exercised because the moment never arrives. Second, take the probability from whoever is closest to the unknown, and write down whose it is. Option value grows with uncertainty, so a person who wants to wait can always widen the range and be rewarded for it - which means using your own optimistic or pessimistic number is not a small liberty, it is the one input that can manufacture whatever answer you already wanted.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the valuation honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• No resolution date, or a date of your own invention. "When things are clearer" is not a date. Without an external one, nothing is learned by waiting and the option is worth nothing - and the calculation will still hand you a comfortable number.

• The cost of waiting left out. Forgone earnings and price escalation are real and belong in the figure. An option priced without them is always worth holding, which is how this becomes a licence to defer everything.

• Your own probability instead of theirs. Option value rises with uncertainty, so whoever picks the range picks the answer. Use the estimate of whoever is closest to the unknown and say whose it is.

• The option not actually available. If the machine has a nine-month lead time, or the site will be sold, or the price is fixed only until Friday, there is nothing to hold. Check that acting later is genuinely possible before valuing it.

• The recommendation written as "wait and see". That phrase means no decision was made and will be treated accordingly. Write the conditional decision instead: do this if and only if that, decided now.

• Nobody spends the small money that keeps the option alive. Quotation extensions, long-lead items, reserved space. An option that expires because nobody diarised it was never held, only described.

• Value confused with affordability. An option worth R1.65 million does nothing for a business that will not survive eleven months. Value and cash are different questions and both get answered.

You sat with a quotation for a fifth of your turnover, a correct calculation that said minus R80,000, thirty-one people, and a bank manager saying "wait and see" as though that settled it. You can put a page up instead: the two branches valued separately, the conventional answer shown and shown to be answering only the buy-now question, and the wait-then-act plan priced at about R1.65 million after paying for eleven months of forgone margin and four per cent of escalation. Uncertainty on its own is only risk. Uncertainty plus the freedom to act later is worth money - and the reason it came out at zero was that nobody had calculated it. The recommendation is not to wait. It is to buy this machine if and only if the tender is won, decided today, with R220,000 spent on making sure that choice is still there in eleven months.
