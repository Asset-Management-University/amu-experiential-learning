The work is the teacher

T1: The Ache

You take development seriously. You spend the training budget. You send people on the courses. The certificates go up on the wall, and the register says everyone is developed.

And yet the capability is not in their hands when you need it. The new people, fully qualified on paper, still lack the judgement the work demands. The new standard everyone was "trained" on is not really understood on the floor. And your most experienced person is about to retire and take three decades of hard-won plant knowledge out of the door with them - knowledge no course can replace. You developed the paperwork. You did not develop the people.

I spend the training budget and send people on courses, and still the real capability is not there - and an expert's knowledge is about to walk out for good. How do I actually choose a strategy for developing my people?

Before you read on, decide. The training record is immaculate: every technician has attended their courses, the budget was spent, the certificates are filed. And when the new dosing plant tripped last week, nobody on shift could work out why, and you rang Piet at home. Piet has been here thirty-one years, understands the old plant in a way nobody has ever written down, and retires in seven months. Last month there was a strip-down that a younger technician could have worked alongside him on — and you gave it to Piet alone, because he would be quickest. Write down how you would develop your people — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the strongest teacher

• “Send more people on more courses.” The training record is already immaculate and the capability is still not there. There is no course containing what Piet knows about these machines, because that knowledge was made here.

• “Have Piet write it all down before he goes.” Worth doing and it will capture a fraction. Most of what he holds is judgement — what to listen for, when a reading matters — which does not survive being written down and cannot be read back into somebody.

• “Offer him a consultancy arrangement after he retires.” A reasonable safety net and a permanent dependency, and it is asking a man who has given thirty-one years to keep being the answer instead of building others.

• “Recruit someone experienced to replace him.” They will bring real expertise and none of this plant, and they will spend three years learning what somebody could have been learning alongside Piet.

• “It is a shame, but people retire — that is life.” They do, and seven months is genuinely time enough to do something. The loss is not the retirement; it is having no route for what he knows.

• Hold two things first, because they frame everything else. A person's experience is a treasure to be shared, not a resource to be depleted; and a developing person is a promise to be fulfilled, not a problem to be solved. Read that way, Piet is not a risk to be mitigated — he is somebody whose knowledge deserves a route out into other people while there is still time to give it. Then un-learn that development means courses, and that a training budget is a strategy. People grow in three quite different ways, and the neat purchasable one is, on its own, the smallest lever. The two that build the most carry no invoice and are harder to measure, which is exactly why they are neglected — they need deliberate design rather than a procurement process. The strongest teacher is the work itself: real assignments just beyond somebody's current ability, given with support, because durable capability is built in the daily act of deciding, solving and being trusted. That is a design choice about how you hand out the work — and last month you handed out a lesson to nobody, for the sake of a faster strip-down.

A person's experience is a treasure to be shared, not a resource to be depleted; and a developing person is a promise to be fulfilled, not a problem to be solved. Let us learn to develop them properly.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you choose a strategy for continuing professional development - seeing the three modes by which people actually grow, diagnosing what capability you truly need, and designing a deliberate blend that leans hardest on the modes that build the most and cost the least to buy.

1. Development is not courses - it is a system, not a spend

The first thing to un-learn is that 'PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT' MEANS 'SENDING PEOPLE ON COURSES', and that a 'TRAINING BUDGET' is the same as a CPD STRATEGY. It is not. People grow in three quite different ways, and the neat, purchasable one - the FORMAL COURSE - is, on its own, the SMALLEST LEVER. The two modes that build the most capability are usually the NEGLECTED ones, precisely because they do not come with an invoice and are harder to measure: they require DELIBERATE DESIGN, not a procurement process. A CPD strategy is a SYSTEM of development, not a spend.

2. The three modes - doing, from others, formal

LEARNING BY DOING - challenging, stretching work: real assignments just beyond a person's current ability, given with support. THIS IS WHERE MOST DURABLE CAPABILITY IS ACTUALLY BUILT - in the daily act of making decisions, solving problems, and being trusted with real responsibility. It is not a cost line; it is a DESIGN CHOICE about how you hand out the work. LEARNING FROM OTHERS - mentoring, coaching, shadowing, and genuine communities of practice where practitioners work through real problems together: this is how TACIT KNOWLEDGE, the kind no course carries, passes from person to person. FORMAL LEARNING - courses, qualifications, structured study: essential for the FOUNDATIONS, the theory, and the tickets a role requires - but the SMALLEST LEVER WHEN IT STANDS ALONE. A real strategy uses all three, and leans hardest on doing and on others.

3. Diagnose the need - capability, person, knowledge-risk

You choose the blend by reading THREE things. THE KIND OF CAPABILITY: codified theory and foundations lean formal (you cannot discover the chemistry of chlorination by trial and error); but JUDGEMENT, CRAFT AND TACIT KNOW-HOW lean on doing and mentoring (no course teaches the feel of a plant running wrong). THE PERSON AND WHERE THEY ARE: a novice needs foundations and close mentoring; an expert grows through stretch assignments and, powerfully, by MENTORING OTHERS. And THE RISK OF LOSING KNOWLEDGE: when an experienced person is close to leaving, a deliberate mentoring-and-community strategy is HOW A PLANT KEEPS ITS HARD-WON KNOWLEDGE ALIVE across a generation, instead of losing it with a single retirement.

4. Design the blend - the strategic moves

Then you make the strategic moves. DESIGN THE WORK TO DEVELOP PEOPLE: rotate them through the hard problems, stretch them just past comfort, delegate real responsibility - DELIBERATELY, as a development act, not by accident. BUILD THE PERSON-TO-PERSON CHANNELS: pair the experienced with the developing, and MAKE COMMUNITIES OF PRACTICE REAL TIME ON THE CALENDAR, NOT POSTERS ON THE WALL. And USE FORMAL COURSES PRECISELY: for the foundations nobody has and the tickets the role requires - NOT AS A SUBSTITUTE for the other two. The blend is different for every need, and naming that is the craft.

5. Review and justify - and whose growth this is

Finally, you REVIEW the strategy against the real need: DID THE CAPABILITY ACTUALLY IMPROVE (not: did people attend), is the critical knowledge being kept alive, and is development shared FAIRLY rather than hoarded by a favoured few? And hold the human truth at the centre of all of it. Development is not a maintenance schedule for skill-units; it is an INVESTMENT IN WHOLE, GROWING PEOPLE. A PERSON'S EXPERIENCE IS A TREASURE TO BE SHARED, NOT A RESOURCE TO BE DEPLETED, AND A DEVELOPING PERSON IS A PROMISE TO BE FULFILLED, NOT A PROBLEM TO BE SOLVED. A CPD strategy that honours that will build capability a course-list never could.

T3: The Blueprint

A course is for the foundations. The capability is built in the work. The most powerful ways to develop people - through the work itself and through each other - are design choices you make, not invoices you pay. Five questions:

  • What is development, really? Not courses. People grow in three ways, and the formal course - the one that comes with an invoice - is, on its own, the one that changes the least.
  • What are the modes? Doing, from others, formal. Learning by doing challenging work; learning from other people; and learning through structured courses. Each does a different job, and they are not the same size.
  • How do I choose the blend? Diagnose the need. The kind of capability, the person and where they are, and the risk of knowledge walking out the door.
  • What are the strategic moves? Design the blend. Design the work to develop people; build the person-to-person channels; and use courses precisely, for what only they can give.
  • How do I know it worked? Review and justify. Did the capability actually grow, is the knowledge being kept alive, and is development fair across your people?

T4: Your Turn

Onalenna faces a workforce capability situation at Corathyl: her most experienced process controller retires in eighteen months, holding decades of tacit plant knowledge; two new technicians are fully qualified on paper but have no real judgement yet; and a new plant-control software system goes live in six months that no one has used. Your job is to design her CPD strategy.

  1. Name the three needs. State the three distinct capability needs in this situation.
  2. Blend the modes for each. For each need, propose a blend of the three modes (doing / from others / formal) and justify why that blend fits that need.
  3. Keep the knowledge alive. Say specifically how you would ensure the retiring controller's tacit knowledge stays in the organisation.
  4. Show why courses alone fail. Explain why "send everyone on a course for the new software" would not meet all three needs.
  5. Justify it. Write the one-paragraph justification for your blended strategy, as you would for a management review.

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the three needs: (1) the whole team must learn the new software before go-live; (2) the two new technicians must build judgement, which they cannot get from a certificate; (3) the retiring controller's tacit knowledge must be captured and passed on before it leaves.

Second, the blends: the software need leans formal (a structured course on the system) plus doing (supervised practice on it before go-live) - a foundation everyone lacks. The judgement need leans hard on doing (stretching, supervised responsibility on real shifts, rotated through the hard problems) and from others (close mentoring) - formal training cannot build judgement. The tacit-knowledge need is almost entirely from others - it lives in a person and can only pass person to person.

Third, keeping the knowledge alive: pair the retiring controller with the two technicians for a deliberate, protected eighteen-month hand-over - shadowing, working the hard calls together, and capturing the "how I know when something is wrong" that no manual holds - and start a practitioners' session where the plant's real problems are worked through together, so the knowledge lives in the group, not just one head.

Fourth, why courses-only fails: a software course gives the whole team the software foundation, but it builds no judgement in the new technicians and rescues none of the retiring controller's tacit knowledge - two of the three needs are untouched. Ticking the course box would leave the real capability gaps exactly where they were.

Fifth, a sound justification names each need, matches a deliberate blend to it, protects the knowledge hand-over as the highest priority (because it is the only one with a hard deadline set by a person leaving), uses the course precisely for the software foundation, and commits to reviewing whether judgement and knowledge actually grew - not just whether attendance happened.

T5: The Guardrails

A course is for the foundations; the capability is built in the work and through each other - so a CPD strategy is a deliberate design of doing, relationships and formal learning, not a training budget.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Equating development with courses. The formal course is the smallest lever on its own. Ask what real work and what mentoring would build the capability first, then use the course for the foundation.

• A training budget instead of a strategy. A spend is not a system. Design the blend deliberately against real capability needs, do not just procure seats.

• Neglecting the two invoice-free modes. Doing and mentoring build the most and are easiest to skip because nothing bills you for them. They need deliberate design and protected time, or they do not happen.

• Losing tacit knowledge to retirement. When an expert leaves, decades can go with them. Plan a deliberate hand-over long before, and make the knowledge live in a group, not one head.

• Communities of practice as posters. A named group with no protected time is a decoration. Put real practitioner time on the calendar to work real problems together.

• Development as a perk for a favoured few. Growth hoarded is trust lost. Share development fairly; a developing person is a promise to be fulfilled, not a reward to be rationed.

• Measuring attendance, not capability. A full register is not a capable team. Review whether the capability actually grew, not whether people showed up.

You spent the budget and hung the certificates, and the capability still was not there - because you bought development instead of designing it. Now you can choose. See the three modes and lean on the two that build the most; diagnose what capability you truly need and who holds the knowledge you cannot lose; design the stretching work and the mentoring first and use the course for the foundation only; and review whether people actually grew. A course is for the foundations - the capability is built in the work and through each other - so develop your people as the whole, growing, knowledge-holding humans they are.
