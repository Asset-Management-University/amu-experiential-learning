Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Fikile, and for three years you have done the hard work: saved with discipline, putting money away every single month. In your savings account sits R60,000 - the proof of every sacrifice, the deposit for your first small home. You feel secure; you have money put away.

Then your mother has a sudden health emergency. In the rush and worry, the only thing that matters is getting her the best care you can find; she's admitted to a private hospital, and the bill climbs fast - within a week it reaches R45,000. You pay it the only way you can: from your savings account, from your house deposit. The relief of her recovery is mixed with a heavy, sinking feeling - three-quarters of three years' work, gone in seven days. You did everything right. You saved. And still, one event you couldn't control emptied almost everything.

I saved for years and still one emergency wiped it out - so is the answer just to save even more, or was my money never actually protected?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to vow to just save more - build the pile back, bigger this time. But you can't out-save a shock that can drain R45,000 in a week; a bigger pile in the same open place is just a bigger target. Or you think about locking the savings somewhere you can't easily touch - but then you can't reach the money in a real crisis, and a true emergency forces you to break it anyway, at a loss. The last thought is to just hope it never happens again - but a severe health shock is like a fire: rare, yet one is enough to burn the whole house down. Hope is not a plan.

So before you read on: the pile was doing one job, in one open place, with nothing guarding it - what was missing that would have stopped a single shock from reaching all of your savings?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The problem was never the size of your pile - it's that it was one pile, doing one job, with no wall around it, so a single shock could reach all of it at once. The answer is to build that wall, on purpose, before the storm comes. Five moves:

  • See the trap - one pile, one job, no wall: your R60,000 was already doing an important job - being a house deposit - but because it was your ONLY pile, it was also your emergency pile. With nothing separating the two jobs, a single shock could reach all of it at once, and the emergency wiped out the dream. The flaw wasn't your saving; it was that one open pile carried everything, with no wall around it.
  • You can't out-save a shock - it's rare but severe: the instinct is to rebuild the pile bigger this time. But you can't out-save a shock that can drain R45,000 in a week - a bigger pile in the same open place is just a bigger target. A severe health shock is like a fire: rare, but one is enough to burn the whole house down. The problem isn't the size of the pile, it's the structure - so hope, and 'save more', are not the plan.
  • Build a wall - separate an emergency buffer: the most important step: keep a SEPARATE emergency buffer, apart from your long-term savings - a smaller pile with only one job, to take the first hit from a sudden shock. The shock strikes the buffer first, while the house deposit you worked so hard for stays standing behind it. Two piles, two jobs - not one pile forced to do both.
  • Put the right cover behind it - and know the public path: some shocks are too big for any buffer; for those, the right KIND of cover is a second, much stronger wall - a medical scheme, a hospital cash plan, gap cover, or insurance for a specific risk. Get qualified, independent help to choose what fits YOU (this names kinds, not products). And find out the cheaper or free PUBLIC health path before you ever need it - a real and dignified option, so in a crisis you are never left with only the biggest bill.
  • Face the honest tension - build the wall in the calm: a wall and cover cost something now against a shock that may never come, so in good times they feel like wasted money - and that is exactly why most people only discover they need a wall when the crisis hits, too late to arrange. You build the wall in the calm, on purpose, to protect everything you have in the storm. And you needn't build it all at once - a small wall is far better than no wall.

See how the wall would have saved Fikile's deposit. With a separate emergency buffer of, say, R15,000 taking the first hit, and a hospital cash plan paying a set amount toward the rest, the R45,000 shock is absorbed by the buffer and the cover - and the R60,000 house deposit stays standing behind the wall, untouched. The same three years of saving; one structural change between them and disaster. One pile, one job, no wall can be emptied by a single shock - so you can't out-save it, you wall it: separate an emergency buffer from your long-term pile, put the right cover behind what's too big to buffer, know the cheaper public path before you need it, and build the wall in the calm, because in the storm it is too late to arrange.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a house deposit - any one-pile-no-wall is at risk. Watch a freelancer.

See the same wall with a freelancer's single account

A freelancer keeps all her earnings and savings in one account. If she falls ill and can't work for a month, she's attacked from both sides at once: a big medical bill arrives at the exact moment her income drops to zero. The single open pile takes both blows together.

The solution is the same wall: a separate emergency buffer to cover living costs while income is gone; the right kind of cover to carry the medical bill (qualified help to choose); and knowing the public path in advance. One pile with no wall is always exposed - two piles with two jobs, plus cover and a known public path, is a wall that one shock can't breach.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at the valuable pile you have built - savings for a home, your small business's cash, the money set aside for a child's future. It is the result of your love and your labour. Now ask honestly: is it one pile, doing one job, with no wall? If it is, you can change that - and you don't have to do it all today:

  1. Start building your wall. Open a separate account and begin an emergency buffer, even with a small amount - a small wall is far better than no wall.
  2. Look into the right cover. For the shocks too big for any buffer, get qualified, independent help to understand what kind of cover fits your life - not which brand, but what shape of protection.
  3. Know your public path. In the calm, find out how the free or cheaper public systems work for a health emergency - it's a dignified and powerful part of your plan.
