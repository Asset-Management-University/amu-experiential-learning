He did report it

T1: The Ache

The pump fails on a Tuesday, and in the investigation an operator says quietly that it had been making that noise for about three weeks.

You are Onalenna, and your first reaction is frustration. Why did nobody say anything?

Then you check. And he did.

He mentioned it to a supervisor, who meant to raise it. There is no record. There was nowhere for it to go, no form that fitted, nobody whose job it was to receive it. The observation was correct, it was early, it was free - and it evaporated somewhere between a walkway and a good intention.

So you ask around, and you find something worse than a lost report. The operators stopped telling anyone things years ago. Not because they stopped noticing. Because the last several times they spoke up, nothing happened and nobody ever came back to them.

You have forty people walking past every critical asset in this works several times a day - seeing, hearing and smelling things no instrument you own is fitted to detect - and you have an organisation that has quietly, politely taught them not to bother.

My operators see the failures coming and my organisation has no way to hear them - and has trained them out of trying. How do I design a rounds programme where an observation actually lands somewhere and something happens?

Before you read on, decide. The bearing failed and took the pump with it. Going back through it, you find that an operator mentioned the noise three weeks earlier, and again the following week. Nothing happened. Nobody went back to him. When you ask why he did not push it, he says — without bitterness, which is somehow worse — that the last several times he spoke up nothing happened and nobody ever came back to him either. You have forty people walking past every critical asset in this works several times a day, hearing and smelling things no instrument you own is fitted to detect. Write down how you would design a rounds programme where an observation actually goes somewhere — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and which half is actually hard

• “Write a proper rounds checklist.” The obvious answer, and it is the easy half. A better sheet collects better observations and delivers them into the same void that has already trained forty people to stop bothering.

• “Tell the operators to report anything unusual.” He did report it. Twice. Asking harder is asking people to do again the thing that has never once produced a result.

• “Fit sensors on the critical bearings.” Worth doing and it is not a substitute. You would be instrumenting the assets you thought to instrument, for the failure modes you thought of, while forty pairs of ears walk past everything several times a day.

• “Discipline is the issue — the round should have been done properly.” The round was done and the observation was made. The failure is downstream of the operator, and locating it in him is how you lose the last few people still speaking up.

• “Put the rounds on a tablet so the data is captured.” Captures it faster into the same absence of a response. Now the record will show precisely how long you knew.

• The waste here is specific: the richest early-warning source you own has been switched off from the inside, politely, over years. So — the checklist is the easy half. What matters is that an observation lands somewhere that acts, and that the person who made it hears what happened: feedback is not courtesy, it is the mechanism that keeps the next observation coming. Two things then make the round worth walking at all. Choose its scope by which assets and which failure modes matter, using the criticality work you have already done — a round covering whatever is convenient to reach will be busy, cheap and useless. And derive the frequency rather than preferring it: there is a window between the moment a failure becomes detectable and the moment it becomes a failure, and if your round is slower than that window it is structurally incapable of catching anything in time, however diligently it is walked. Design to a fraction of that window — commonly half — so that at least one look, and usually two, falls inside it.

The checklist is the easy half. Here is the whole of it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the operator rounds programme - what is checked, how often, and where it goes.

1. What is the round FOR? (the frequent sensory layer, not a substitute)

Decide what the round is FOR before writing a single line of it. It is NOT a cheaper substitute for the maintenance inspection route, and NOT a replacement for condition monitoring. It is the FREQUENT, SENSORY, EARLY layer - the one that exists because your people walk past these assets several times a day and can see, hear and smell things no sensor is fitted to catch. So choose its scope deliberately: which ASSETS, and which FAILURE MODES on those assets, drawing on the criticality and failure-mode work already done rather than covering whatever is easy to reach. At Corathyl that is 40 assets. Everything else follows from this choice, and a round that covers what is convenient rather than what is critical will be busy, cheap and useless.

2. Set the frequency from the P-F interval (this is the whole justification)

The frequency is not a preference, it is derived. The P-F INTERVAL is the time from the point a failure becomes DETECTABLE to the point of FUNCTIONAL FAILURE - for this bearing mode, 30 DAYS. THE RULE IS DECISIVE: if the inspection frequency is LONGER than the P-F interval, the approach will always be REACTIVE. And the standard design target is HALF the P-F interval, so that at least one and on average two inspections fall between detection and failure - here, 15 DAYS. Now compare. THE MONTHLY MAINTENANCE ROUTE runs at 30 days, which equals the P-F interval and FAILS the test: at most one look lands inside the window and easily none, since nothing makes it fall after the point of detectability. THE DAILY OPERATOR ROUND runs at 1 day and passes it THIRTY TIMES OVER. That is the mathematical justification for the entire programme - and the cost is 40 assets at 90 seconds = 60 MINUTES PER SHIFT, walked by someone already passing every one of them.

3. Write checks a person can actually answer

Three kinds of check: VISUAL; SENSORY - unusual SOUND, VIBRATION, SMELL and HEAT, which is the operator's real advantage over instrumentation; and INSTRUMENT READINGS such as temperature, pressure and speed. Then the discipline that separates a useful checklist from one that gets ticked in the control room: EVERY CHECK MUST BE SPECIFIC, OBSERVABLE AND ANSWERABLE IN SECONDS. Not 'check pump condition' - nobody can answer that, so everybody ticks it. Instead: 'Is there oil on the floor under the pump?' 'Does the bearing sound different from yesterday?' 'Is the coupling guard warm to the touch?' 'What does the discharge gauge read?' Keep them mostly CLOSED-FORM so answers can be counted and trended, with ONE free-text field for anything else - free text everywhere cannot be trended and quietly becomes unreadable. And keep the route walkable in the time available, because a checklist too long for the shift is completed at a desk.

4. The route into the CMMS - this part IS the programme

The checklist is the easy half. THIS is the programme. An ABNORMAL ANSWER MUST CREATE A RECORD AUTOMATICALLY - not a conversation, not a note, not an intention - with a PHOTO and a MEASUREMENT attached, so the next person sees what the operator saw. That record must reach a NAMED PERSON with a stated RESPONSE TIME, so it is somebody's job rather than everybody's. RECURRING ABNORMALITIES must be FLAGGED, because three 'slightly warm' readings in a fortnight is a trend that no single reading is. And the findings across many inspections should FEED PM OPTIMISATION, since the round is quietly generating the failure evidence a maintenance plan should have been built on all along. Understand what is at stake in this box: a system that captures observations and does nothing with them is WORSE than no system, because it produces a permanent record that somebody noticed and nobody acted.

5. Close the loop - to the operator, and back to the checklist

Two loops, and the first decides whether the programme is alive in a year. TELL THE PERSON WHO REPORTED WHAT HAPPENED. Not a broadcast, not a statistic - the operator who wrote it down learns what was found and what was done. This single control determines whether people keep reporting, because an organisation that does not answer its operators has taught them not to speak. AN OPERATOR ROUNDS PROGRAMME IS AN ACT OF ENGINEERING AND AN ACT OF RESPECT. AN ORGANISATION THAT DOES NOT CREATE A RELIABLE, ACKNOWLEDGED CHANNEL FOR ITS OPERATORS' OBSERVATIONS HAS NOT CREATED A MAINTENANCE PROGRAMME; IT HAS CREATED A CULTURE OF SILENCE. THE FEEDBACK LOOP IS NOT AN ADMINISTRATIVE NICETY; IT IS THE CONTROL THAT PROVES THE SYSTEM WORKS AND THAT THE PEOPLE IN IT MATTER. The second loop: REVIEW THE CHECKLIST AFTER EVERY FAILURE and ask whether an additional inspection point would have given earlier warning. That is how a round gets sharper each year instead of going stale.

Notice that only box 3 is the checklist. Boxes 4 and 5 are what make the checklist worth writing - and they are the two that get left out, which is precisely why so many rounds programmes are quietly abandoned in their second year. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Your operators are the cheapest inspection capacity you own - and an observation is worthless unless it lands somewhere that acts on it. Five questions:

  • What is the round for? The frequent, sensory, early layer - not a substitute for the maintenance inspection route or for condition monitoring. Scope it by asset and failure mode from work you have already done, not by what is easy to reach.
  • How often? Derived, never preferred. The P-F interval is the time from detectability to functional failure, and the rule is decisive: if the inspection frequency is longer than the P-F interval, the approach will always be reactive. The standard design target is half the P-F interval, so at least one and on average two inspections fall in the window.
  • What do you ask? Three kinds of check - visual, sensory (unusual sound, vibration, smell, heat) and instrument readings - every one specific, observable and answerable in seconds, mostly closed-form so answers can be trended, with one free-text field for anything else.
  • Where does an abnormal answer go? Automatically into a record with photo and measurement, to a named responder with a response time, with recurring abnormalities flagged and findings feeding PM optimisation. This part is the programme.
  • Who hears back? The operator who reported, every time - and the checklist itself, reviewed after every failure to ask whether an additional inspection point would have given earlier warning.

The principle this unit will not soften. An operator rounds programme is an act of engineering and an act of respect. An organisation that does not create a reliable, acknowledged channel for its operators' observations has not created a maintenance programme; it has created a culture of silence. The feedback loop is not an administrative nicety; it is the control that proves the system works and that the people in it matter.

And note where the fault lay in T1. The operator was not insufficiently diligent - he noticed a developing failure three weeks early and he said so. The system lost it. This technique is not a way of making operators try harder; it is the design of a system worthy of what they already do.

One honest boundary. You design the programme and its route into the maintenance system. You are not specifying inspection techniques for any particular asset, not performing the condition monitoring, and not writing the maintenance plan itself. What you are building is the engineered process that closes the gaps a series of good intentions leaves between them.

Does it scale? A hospital ward does not diagnose patients by scanning them monthly. It takes observations several times a day - temperature, colour, breathing, whether they seem themselves - by the people already at the bedside, and every one of those observations has a place to go and someone who must act on it. The scan is still needed; it is simply far too infrequent to notice a patient deteriorating. Your monthly inspection route is the scan. Your operators are the ward.

T4: Your Turn

You are designing a rounds programme for a pumping station with four asset groups. The failure modes and their P-F intervals have already been established:

AssetFailure modeP-F interval
Transfer pump bearingsBearing degradation - audible change, then heat, then seizure21 days
Mechanical sealsSeal weep developing to gland leak10 days
Drive motorWinding insulation degradation - rising temperature60 days
Suction strainerBlinding - rising differential pressure4 days
  1. Frequencies. Using the half-the-P-F-interval design target, state the maximum inspection interval for each failure mode, and then say which of them a daily operator round covers adequately and which needs something else.
  2. Checklist. Write six checklist lines for this station - at least one visual, one sensory and one instrument reading - each specific, observable and answerable in seconds.
  3. The route. Design what happens when an operator answers one of your lines abnormally. Be specific about the record, the evidence, the named responder and the response time.
  4. Recurrence. Say how three mildly abnormal readings in a fortnight would come to anyone's attention.
  5. The loop. State how the operator finds out what happened, and when the checklist itself gets reviewed.

The test of a real answer: three failures, and two of them feel like good work at the time.

First, in step 1, look hard at the strainer. A 4-day P-F interval needs an interval of about 2 days - which a daily round covers comfortably - while the motor's 60-day mode would be covered by an interval of 30 days, meaning the daily round is far more frequent than that failure mode requires. This does not mean the motor should be removed from the daily round; the operator is already walking past, and the check costs almost nothing. The route's frequency is set by the most demanding failure mode (the strainer's 2-day target), and other assets are included for near-zero marginal cost. The point is not that more is always better: it is that the frequency must be derived, and asking whether each mode is genuinely covered is the calculation the programme rests on.

Second, if any of your six lines reads like "check seal condition - OK / not OK", cross it out. Nobody can answer it, so everybody will tick it, and you will have built a system that generates confident evidence that everything was fine right up until it was not.

Third, and the one this unit exists for: if your answer contains a checklist and a CMMS record but no step in which a human being tells the operator what happened, you have designed exactly the system that trained Onalenna's operators into silence. It will work for about a year.

T5: The Guardrails

Rounds programmes fail in ways that all look like implementation.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• A frequency longer than the P-F interval. Then the round is decorative. If the interval does not fit inside the window, you are doing reactive maintenance with a clipboard.

• Unanswerable checks. "Check condition" cannot be answered, so it is always ticked - and the record becomes evidence that nothing was wrong.

• A route too long to walk. If the checklist does not fit the shift, it gets completed at a desk, and you have built a fiction with timestamps.

• Free text everywhere. Unreadable at volume and impossible to trend. Mostly closed-form, with one place to say anything else.

• Observations that create no record. The T1 failure exactly. A conversation is not a system.

• Records with no named owner or response time. Everybody's job is nobody's, and the record sits there proving somebody noticed.

• No recurring-abnormality flag. Three mild readings are a trend that no single reading is, and nobody spots it by memory.

• No feedback to the reporter. The programme dies within a year, quietly, and everyone concludes the operators were not interested.

• Never reviewing the checklist after a failure. Every failure is a free question: would another inspection point have caught this earlier?

• Treating the round as a substitute. It is the frequent early layer, not a cheaper replacement for inspection or condition monitoring.

• Designing a system that feels like surveillance instead of empowerment. This is a listening tool, not a performance management tool - and the moment completion rates are used against people, the observations become compliant and worthless.

An operator told somebody a bearing sounded wrong, three weeks before it failed, and the organisation had nowhere to put it. Decide what the round is for and scope it by criticality; derive the frequency from the P-F interval, because an interval longer than that window is reactive maintenance with a clipboard; write checks that are specific, observable and answerable in seconds; build the automatic route into the maintenance system with a named responder, a response time and a recurrence flag; and close both loops - back to the person who reported, and back into the checklist after every failure. The checklist is the easy half. The programme is what happens after somebody notices something. And an organisation that does not answer its operators has taught them not to speak. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, ask three operators what they last reported and what happened next - the answer to the second question is your programme's real design.
