Step 1 of 5

The benchmark that comforts you

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and this month you are not running Corathyl Water Treatment Works. You are sitting in an industry benchmarking consortium, where operators of very different plants pool their turnaround data anonymously and review one another's events, because none of them can learn enough from their own.

On the table is the closeout report for a recently completed turnaround at one of the member sites - a large process plant, twenty-eight days planned. The report's headline sentence reads: the turnaround was delivered at 3.9 per cent of total replacement value, in line with industry norms.

Everyone in the room relaxes at that sentence. It is a real number, honestly calculated, against a genuine industry band.

And you cannot make it sit right, because you have read the rest of the pack. The turnaround ran thirty-four days, not twenty-eight. It cost thirty per cent more than its own budget. More than a fifth of the work actually executed had never been planned at all. And three people were hurt badly enough to need a doctor.

So you have one number telling you the event was normal, and four facts telling you it was not. They cannot both be the whole truth, and you have no way to say which is - because in twenty years nobody has taught you how to compare a turnaround against anything except itself.

One benchmark says this turnaround was ordinary and everything I know about it says otherwise. How do I benchmark a TAR properly - so the assessment reveals performance instead of reassuring the room?

Before you read on, decide. The closeout report leads with one number: the turnaround cost R109.2m against a plant replacement value of R2,800m, which is 3.9 per cent — inside the industry band of two to four. On that measure it was an entirely ordinary event, and the number is honest; nobody has manipulated anything. Against it: a fifth of the planned scope was deferred to next time, a large piece of work appeared that had never been planned at all, the restart slipped, and three people were hurt badly enough to need a doctor. One number says ordinary. Four facts say otherwise. Write down how you would benchmark this properly — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the comfortable measure wins

• “The number is from a credible industry study — trust it.” It is credible, and it is one measure. A cost ratio is silent about scope, about safety and about whether the deferred work was pushed into somebody else's future — those things are not absent, they are invisible to it.

• “Compare against our own last turnaround.” Comparing an event against yourself tells you whether you are consistent, not whether you are any good. If the last one was also poor, you will be reassured twice.

• “Report both — the number and the concerns.” Better, and in practice the number wins: it is quantified and external, and the concerns read as anecdotes beside it. That is exactly what happened in the room.

• “Drop the benchmark and just describe what happened.” Throws away the one thing that could locate you against anybody else, and leaves you back where you started — with an event that can only be compared to itself.

• “Investigate the injuries separately.” Necessary and it is not benchmarking. Handled apart, the harm never enters the assessment of whether the event went well, which is how an event with three injuries gets recorded as normal.

• Start before any comparison at all, with definitions — because the same event yields materially different figures depending on where the measurement starts and stops and what it includes. Duration from feed-out or from mobilisation? Cost with or without the capital work done because the plant was open, and the lost production? Harm counted at which threshold? If the reference and your record define a measure differently, every conclusion drawn from the comparison is decoration — the definitions are the comparison; the rest is arithmetic. Then normalise, since a bigger plant always has a bigger turnaround, so raw figures compare sizes rather than performance — which your 3.9 per cent does correctly. And then the discipline that settles this: compare on several normalised measures rather than one. Any single measure can sit comfortably while the event failed on dimensions it cannot see. A benchmark that reassures you is doing the opposite of its job.

You do not benchmark it once. Here is the assessment.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the benchmark assessment - the same turnaround, compared properly.

1. What exactly does each measure mean? (definitions before comparison)

Before comparing anything, pin down what each measure MEANS - on both sides. DURATION from what to what? Feed-out to feed-in, or first isolation to full rate, or the contractor's mobilisation to demobilisation? Those are different numbers for the same event and can differ by days. COST including what? Execution only, or also contractor mobilisation, capital work done in the window because the plant was open, the owner's team, and lost production? SAFETY counting what - recordables only, or first aid and near misses too? If the study defines duration as shutdown-to-startup and your closeout report measures mobilisation-to-demobilisation, then every conclusion drawn from the comparison is decoration. THE DEFINITIONS ARE THE COMPARISON; everything after them is arithmetic.

2. Normalise, or you are just comparing sizes

A bigger plant always has a bigger turnaround, so raw figures compare SIZES rather than PERFORMANCE. Normalise. The standard normaliser for cost is TAR COST AS A PERCENTAGE OF TOTAL REPLACEMENT VALUE, and industry data puts typical turnarounds in a 2 TO 4 PER CENT OF TRV band. The TAR under review cost R109.2m against a plant TRV of R2,800m, which is 3.9 PER CENT - at the high end of the band. On this measure the event looks entirely NORMAL, and this is the number the closeout report leads with. Normalise duration and effort too (days or man-hours per unit of work or per unit of capacity) so that scale is removed there as well. Normalisation is what makes comparison legitimate - and, as the next boxes show, it is also what can make a bad turnaround look ordinary.

3. Four reference points, not one (and expect them to disagree)

Never benchmark against a single reference, because a flattering one always exists. Use FOUR: the INDUSTRY AVERAGE, YOUR OWN HISTORY, SPECIFIC PEERS of similar type, and TOP PERFORMERS - because comparing yourself to the average tells you if you are ordinary, while comparing yourself to the best tells you what is possible. Then expect them to DISAGREE, because THE DISAGREEMENT IS THE FINDING. Here: against the industry cost band, 3.9 per cent of TRV says normal. Against its OWN PLAN, the same TAR ran 34 days instead of 28 (+21.4 per cent) and cost R109.2m against R84m (+30 PER CENT). Both statements are true. One says the event was unremarkable and the other says it was out of control, and an assessment that reports only the first is not wrong - it is incomplete in a way that is indistinguishable from dishonest.

4. The full KPI set - including the ones that embarrass

Report the whole set, especially the uncomfortable members. SCHEDULE and COST performance against plan. SCOPE COMPLETION - planned work done versus deferred. DISCOVERY WORK, the percentage of executed man-hours that was never planned: 22 PER CENT here, which is the honest measure of how well the scope was ever known. CRITICAL PATH ADHERENCE. SAFETY as TRIR - Total Recordable Incident Rate - which is recordables x 200,000 divided by hours worked, the 200,000 representing 100 full-time workers at 2,000 hours a year: 3 recordables in 186,000 hours gives TRIR = 3.23, against a refining sector average around 1.2, so ROUGHLY 2.7 TIMES WORSE. And LOST PRODUCTION, almost always omitted: 6 extra days at R4.5m a day is R27.0m, which never appeared in the TAR budget and exceeds the R25.2m cost overrun. The real cost of the overrun was R52.2m.

5. A benchmark is a pointer, never a diagnosis

A benchmark tells you WHERE you differ. It never tells you WHY. It is a pointer to an investigation, not the conclusion of one - and a report that leaps from a number to a cause has invented the cause. So state each difference and what it POINTS AT: 22 per cent discovery work points at inspection and scoping before the event, not at the crews who found the work. A TRIR of 3.23 against 1.2 points at how the work was done - and here the two findings are probably one finding, because compressed timelines correlate consistently with cost overruns, schedule delays AND safety incidents, all through inadequate preparation. That is a HYPOTHESIS worth investigating, not a conclusion. AND ON THE SAFETY NUMBER: a TRIR is an industry standard, but it is NOT A SCORE. Behind this TAR's 3.23 are three specific people who were injured badly enough to need medical treatment beyond first aid. Benchmarking safety is about discovering whether your system protects people as well as the industry's best does. A benchmark used to reassure management that a high injury rate is an acceptable deviation is an act of profound organisational negligence.

Box 3 holds the whole lesson. Both statements about this turnaround are true: it was industry-normal on cost per unit of asset, and it was thirty per cent over its own plan. The closeout report contained one of them. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A benchmark assessment is a forensic tool that uses multiple normalised comparisons to reveal a turnaround's true performance - because any single measure can be used to hide failure. Five questions:

  • What does each measure mean? Definitional consistency on both sides - duration from what to what, cost including what, safety counting what. A measure defined differently at each end is not a comparison.
  • How do you remove size? Normalisation. Cost as a percentage of total replacement value (typically 2 to 4 per cent), effort and duration per unit of work or capacity. Without it you are comparing plants, not performance.
  • Against what? Four reference points - industry average, your own history, peers, and top performers. The average tells you whether you are ordinary; the best tells you what is possible. Expect them to disagree, because the disagreement is the finding.
  • Which measures? The full set: schedule, cost, scope completion, discovery work percentage, critical path adherence, TRIR, and lost production - which is routinely larger than the cost overrun and routinely absent.
  • What does it prove? Nothing. A benchmark shows where you differ, never why. It is a pointer to an investigation, not a diagnosis.

On the safety number, which is not a number. A Total Recordable Incident Rate is an industry standard, but it is not a score. Behind this turnaround's TRIR of 3.23 are three specific people who were injured badly enough to require medical treatment beyond first aid. Benchmarking safety is not about grading performance; it is about discovering whether your system is protecting people as well as the industry's best. A benchmark used to reassure management that a high injury rate is an acceptable deviation is an act of profound organisational negligence.

One honest boundary. You are evaluating a completed turnaround against external reference points. You are not running one, freezing its worklist, building its schedule, or writing its safety plan - those are separate techniques. This is the difference between being the chef and being the critic: one executes, the other judges, and an organisation that never does the second keeps repeating the first.

Does it scale? It is the difference between a single blood test result and a full panel. One marker inside its reference range is genuinely reassuring and genuinely meaningless on its own - and a doctor who reported only the normal result, from a panel containing three abnormal ones, would not be practising medicine. The panel matters because the markers disagree, and the disagreement is where the diagnosis begins.

  1. Plan versus actual. Calculate schedule and cost performance against plan. What do these two measures say?
  2. Normalise. Calculate the cost as a percentage of total replacement value and compare it with the typical band. What does this measure say?
  3. Safety. Calculate the TRIR and compare it against a sector average of 1.2.
  4. Reconcile. The measures disagree. Say what each one is actually measuring, and what the disagreement points at.
  5. Report it. Write the two-sentence headline you would put on this assessment - the one the first report should have had.

The test of a real answer: this turnaround is the mirror image of the one in the demonstration, and if you have taken a rule of thumb from that example it will fail you here.

This TAR hit its schedule exactly and came in two per cent over budget - outstanding predictability, and any organisation would be pleased. But normalise it and the cost is 5.1 per cent of replacement value, well above the typical band. Both are true again, and the reconciliation is the point: plan-versus-actual measures predictability, while normalised comparison measures efficiency. Hitting your plan tells you nothing whatever about whether the plan was right. This site may be over-maintaining, over-scoping, or estimating generously and then delivering to the generous estimate - which looks like excellent control and is expensive.

And do not walk past the single recordable because it is only one. Run the number.

T5: The Guardrails

Benchmarking fails in ways that all look like rigour.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Comparing raw numbers across differently sized plants. A bigger plant has a bigger turnaround. Without normalisation you have measured size.

• Comparing measures defined differently at each end. Duration, cost and even "recordable" mean different things in different reports. Establish the definitions first or the arithmetic is decoration.

• Using one benchmark. A flattering measure always exists. One comparison is a quotation, not an assessment.

• Choosing the benchmark that flatters. The most dangerous version, because it is done with real numbers and can be defended line by line.

• Omitting lost production. Usually larger than the cost overrun, and usually absent from the TAR budget entirely, so the event looks cheaper than it was.

• Ignoring discovery work. It is the honest measure of how well the scope was ever known, and the one most likely to explain everything else.

• Treating TRIR as the whole safety picture. It counts recordables. It does not see near misses, first aid cases, or process safety events - and a low TRIR has coexisted with catastrophic process safety failures.

• Concluding causes from a benchmark. It shows difference, never reason. A report that names a cause has left the evidence behind.

• Benchmarking to defend rather than to learn. This is how organisations produce genuinely accurate, entirely honest reports that reassure everyone about events in which people were hurt.

Onalenna had one number saying ordinary and four facts saying otherwise. Establish what each measure means on both sides before comparing anything; normalise so you are comparing performance rather than plant size; use four reference points and treat their disagreement as the finding rather than as noise; report the full set including discovery work and lost production, which are the two that embarrass; and remember that a benchmark points at where to look and never at why. A single measure can always be found that comforts the room. The purpose of an assessment is not comfort - and behind a safety rate there are always specific people. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take your organisation's last major shutdown and calculate just two things: its cost as a percentage of replacement value, and its discovery work percentage.
