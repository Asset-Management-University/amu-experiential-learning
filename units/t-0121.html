The oil was always there

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and the new pump-house gearbox keeps running hot. So you do the diligent thing - the thing everyone would praise. You check it daily. You never once let it run low. It is always full of good oil from the main store, the expensive drum, topped up faithfully.

It fails in six months.

When the fitters open it, you lean in expecting something obvious - a broken tooth, a piece of dirt, an empty sump. Instead the surfaces are polished. Scored and mirror-bright, in the exact pattern of two pieces of metal that have been rubbing directly against one another. And you cannot square it with what you know, because the oil was there. It was there the whole time, in the right quantity, every single day.

It takes a while for the real answer to land, and when it does it is worse than a simple mistake: the oil was present but it never formed a film. It was too thin for this load, this speed and this temperature, so the surfaces were touching all along - grinding quietly under a sump full of oil. You were faithfully topping up the wrong oil, and "it has oil in it" turns out to mean almost nothing at all.

My gearbox was full of oil every single day and it still wore itself out, metal on metal. If having oil in it is not the point, what IS the oil supposed to do - and how do I choose one that actually does it?

Before you read on, decide. You never let it run dry. The level was checked, the oil was topped up, the records are there — and the gearbox is worn through with unmistakable metal-to-metal damage. The oil in the sump is clean and in good condition. This gearbox carries about 20 kN, turns at 1450 rpm and sits at around 65 degrees C all day. Write down what the oil was actually supposed to be doing, and how you would choose one that does it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the oil is actually for

• “Change the oil more often.” Fresh oil of the wrong sort fails in exactly the same way, on a cleaner schedule. Frequency addresses degradation, and this oil was not degraded.

• “Use a better brand.” Brand is not the variable, and buying a premium version of an unsuitable grade is one of the more expensive ways to make no difference at all.

• “It must be a bearing or alignment fault, not the oil.” Reasonable, and worth ruling out — but the damage is metal against metal across the working surfaces, which is what happens when nothing is holding them apart, not what a single component fault looks like.

• “Add more oil — keep it fuller.” More of a fluid that is not forming a film gives you a deeper sump and the same contact. Quantity was never the thing that was short.

• “Use a thicker oil to be safe.” Closer, and as a rule of thumb it is a guess in the right direction, which can cost you drag, heat and churning if you overshoot. The point is to choose it against something rather than to err.

• Here is the reframe the whole subject rests on. The oil's job is not to be there — it is to hold the surfaces apart on a film, and whether it manages that depends entirely on matching its thickness to what this machine actually does: how hard it is loaded, how fast it turns, and how hot it runs. Too thin at that duty and the film never forms, and the surfaces grind continuously beneath a full sump. So the oil is a chosen component specified against a duty, not a consumable that gets topped up. Two things follow. Temperature is not a footnote — oil thins sharply as it warms, so a thickness that is right cold can be wrong at 65 degrees, and a specification without the temperature it applies at means nothing. And here is where most of your metal actually went: even a perfectly specified machine has no film at all at every start-up and shutdown, because the film is made by motion and does not exist until things are moving. Those seconds cannot be designed away, which is why a finished oil is base fluid plus deliberate chemistry to protect the metal through them.

There is a selection sheet that answers this, and behind it one curve that explains every lubricated machine you will ever meet. Let us show you the sheet - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the page that should have been written before a drop of oil went in - the specification that treats the lubricant as a chosen component, not a consumable.

1. The duty - what must this oil actually survive?

The duty of gearbox GB-02: a radial load of about 20 kN, an input speed of 1450 rpm, and an operating temperature of around 65 degrees C. Load, speed and temperature - these three are the whole brief the oil has to answer.

2. The regime aimed for - where on the curve should it run?

Aim: full fluid film in normal running - the surfaces carried entirely by the oil, never touching. But be honest: at every start-up and every shutdown this gearbox passes through boundary contact, when the film has not yet formed. Those seconds are where the metal is lost.

3. The grade - the viscosity that forms the film at THIS duty

ISO VG 46: by ISO 3448 that means 46 centistokes at 40 degrees C, with a tolerance of plus or minus 10% - an allowed band of 41.4 to 50.6 cSt. Chosen because it is thick enough to build a film at 1450 rpm under 20 kN, and still thick enough at 65 degrees C, where oil thins sharply.

4. The additives - what the base oil cannot do alone

A finished oil is base oil plus a designed additive package. Here: anti-wear chemistry to protect the metal through every start-up and shutdown, an anti-oxidant because it runs hot, a rust and corrosion inhibitor for a damp pump-house, and an anti-foam so the film is not full of air.

5. The check - is the film actually holding?

Do not assume - verify. Trend the running temperature (a film that is failing shows up as heat), and trend the wear metals in the oil analysis. Rising iron means the surfaces are touching, whatever the level gauge says. A full sight-glass is not proof of a working film.

Read the first box again: load, speed, temperature. Nothing about the oil at all - because until you know what the contact faces, you cannot possibly know what will hold it apart. Onalenna's oil was chosen by what stood in the store; this one is chosen by the duty. Now let us look at the curve that makes sense of all of it.

T3: The Blueprint

Here is the idea that reframes everything: the oil's job is not to be present - it is to hold two surfaces apart. Whether it manages that depends on a simple contest between how thick the oil is, how fast the surfaces move, and how hard they are pressed together. Engineers map that contest on the Stribeck curve, and it has three territories:

  • Boundary. The film is too thin to separate the surfaces, so the load is carried by the high spots of the metal itself, touching. Friction and wear are at their highest. Nothing but the chemistry - the anti-wear additives - stands between the two surfaces. This is where metal is lost.
  • Mixed. The film begins to lift the surfaces apart, though some high spots still touch. This is the steepest part of the curve, where friction falls away fast for only a small gain in speed.
  • Hydrodynamic, or full film. The load is carried entirely by the fluid - the high spots never touch at all. This begins at the very lowest point of friction on the curve. Push beyond it and friction creeps up again gently, as the oil's own viscous drag starts to cost you.

Now trace your machine's day along that curve, because this is the part people miss. At start-up the speed is zero: you are at the far left, in boundary, metal on metal. As it runs up you slide down the steep mixed slope while the film builds. At operating speed you settle into the trough - full film, surfaces safe, the design intent. At shutdown you travel all the way back through mixed into boundary again. Every start and every stop crosses the dangerous ground. That is precisely why anti-wear chemistry is never optional, even in a perfectly designed machine.

The selection then follows, each part answering a question:

  • Which grade? The ISO 3448 classification makes this sayable: the ISO VG number is the oil's kinematic viscosity in centistokes at 40 degrees C, give or take 10%. ISO VG 46 means 46 cSt at 40 degrees, so anything from 41.4 to 50.6 is in grade. Each step up the ladder is roughly half as thick again. Choose too thin and the film never forms; too thick and you pay in drag, heat, and an oil that will not reach the contact when cold.
  • Which chemistry? A finished lubricant is base oil plus an additive package - anti-wear and extreme-pressure additives for the boundary moments, anti-oxidants for heat, rust and corrosion inhibitors for damp, anti-foam so the film is not full of air - each chosen for the duty.
  • And is it working? Trend the running temperature and the wear metals in the oil. A full sight-glass proves nothing; iron that stays low proves the film is holding.

Does it scale? Onalenna's one wrong oil is the exact reason your car takes a multi-grade engine oil: thin enough to flow and protect the instant you turn a cold key, thick enough to hold a film once the engine is hot and working - which is also why most engine wear happens in the first seconds after a cold start, down in the boundary regime.

T4: Your Turn

Now read a lubricated contact you already know - a bicycle chain, a hinge that squeaks, or a car engine on a cold morning. Pick one and work it through:

  1. Name the duty. Roughly what load, what speed and what temperature does that contact face?
  2. Place it on the curve. In normal use, is it running in boundary, mixed, or full film? (A slow, heavily loaded hinge and a fast, lightly loaded shaft live in very different places.)
  3. Reason the grade. What would go wrong with an oil far too thin? And far too thick? Say both.
  4. Name the chemistry. What must the additives cover here - wear at start-up, water and rust, heat, dirt?

A worked feel for it: a bicycle chain is slow, heavily loaded at each roller, and exposed to grit and rain - it lives largely in boundary, so its lubricant is chosen mostly for what the chemistry and film strength do at metal-to-metal contact, not for building a hydrodynamic wedge. That is why chain oil and gearbox oil are not the same thing.

T5: The Guardrails

Lubricant selection is genuine engineering, and a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you sign a specification.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Treating oil as a consumable, not a component. The trap that cost Onalenna a gearbox. "Keep it topped up" is housekeeping; "choose the grade for the duty" is engineering. A machine can be full of the wrong oil and wearing out the entire time.

• "Thicker must be safer." It is not. Too thick means viscous drag, wasted energy and a hotter machine - and worst of all, an oil that will not flow to the contact quickly when cold, stretching out the very boundary period you were trying to protect.

• Forgetting temperature. Oil thins sharply as it warms. A grade that feels right in a cool workshop can be far too thin at 65 degrees in service. Choose for the operating temperature, not the ambient one.

• Casual mixing and topping up. Adding a different grade or a different additive package to what is already in there gives you an oil nobody specified and nobody can predict. Match the specification, or drain and change.

• Assuming a good design needs no chemistry. Even a machine that runs beautifully in full film passes through boundary at every start and every stop. The anti-wear additives are earning their keep in those seconds.

Onalenna's gearbox was never starved of oil - it was starved of the right oil, which is a different failure entirely and a far easier one to prevent. Now you hold the logic: name the duty, decide the regime you are designing for, choose the grade that builds a film at that speed, load and temperature, specify the chemistry for the moments the film cannot help you, then verify with heat and wear metals rather than a glance at a sight-glass. Do that and the machines in your care will float instead of grind - which is, in the end, the whole of tribology.
