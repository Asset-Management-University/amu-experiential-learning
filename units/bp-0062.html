Every impressive thing I buy dazzles me and then changes nothing - so how do I stop wasting money on tools that gather dust?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Eliza, and you run a small workshop making beautiful custom furniture. Six months ago you saw a demonstration of new design software and it was amazing: in minutes it turned a customer's rough idea into a perfect 3D model, with a price and a cutting list. It would change everything, you thought - so you bought it. It cost a lot, but it would be worth it; the salesperson installed it, ran a quick training session, and your team was impressed.

That was six months ago. Today the software sits on a computer in the corner, almost never opened. When a new order comes in, you and the team go back to sketching on paper and working the price out with a calculator and a spreadsheet - slower, sometimes wrong, but it's what everyone knows. And you feel that familiar, sinking feeling: the software has joined your "graveyard of good ideas" - right next to last year's expensive digital measuring tool and the online planner you still pay for that no one ever updates.

Every impressive new thing I buy ends up gathering dust - so why does it never become part of how we actually work, and how do I stop wasting money on tools that change nothing?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

This isn't your first time here, so you've tried to fix it. You blame the tool and hunt for a better one - "it was just too complicated" - and start eyeing a newer, even more impressive system; but the next one will die the same way, because the tool was never the problem. You force the team to use it - "from now on, all new jobs must use the software!" - and it holds for a day or two, until a tricky job with a tight deadline comes in and everyone quietly slips back to the old way (no one owns it, no one to ask when stuck). Or you give up on new things entirely - "we'll stick with what we know" - which stops the waste but also stops you improving while competitors get faster.

Each time, you judged the new thing by its dazzling demonstration. But a demo is a performance. So before you read on: what does it actually take to make a bought thing a real part of your team's daily work?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The hidden failure was never the technology - it's the gap between buying a tool and making it part of how you actually work. A demo shows what a thing can do; it never shows whether your team will do it. So from now on, you buy the change, not the thing. Five moves:

  • See your graveyard: be honest: write down the tools, software and courses you've paid for that now gather dust, and see the pattern. This list isn't shame - it's your best defence. It reminds you how easily a dazzling demo leads to a dead end, so the next impressive thing gets real questions before it gets your money.
  • Judge the change, not the demo: a demo shows what a thing CAN do; it never shows whether your team WILL do it. So ignore the features for a moment and ask: to make this work, WHO has to do something differently? What do they stop doing and start doing? Who is the one person who OWNS it (the expert we go to)? How does it join the way we already work? No clear answers = the next headstone in your graveyard.
  • Anchor it to a real problem worth real money: don't buy to 'be more modern' or 'improve efficiency'. Name the specific problem it solves and what that problem costs you now - e.g. 'we lose about 10 hours a week to quoting errors, roughly R2,000 a month in wasted time and materials.' Now you know what a fix is worth. No real problem, no purchase.
  • Run a small, real-use test: before you buy the big system for everyone, test it in the real world - not another demo. Give it to ONE person, for ONE part of their job, for a limited time. Instead of R4,000 software for the whole workshop, buy a single R80-a-month licence for one person and let them use it on real jobs.
  • Write down what success looks like: agree, BEFORE the test starts, what a good result is - e.g. 'success is when Dineo can make an accurate quote for a standard chair in under 20 minutes.' The tool must EARN its place with real evidence from your own workshop, not promises from a salesperson - you only grow it if the test proves it truly helps you serve customers better.

See it work for the design software. Instead of being dazzled again, you ask the real questions: whose work changes, who owns it, how it joins what you already do - and you anchor it to a real problem (you lose about 10 hours a week to quoting errors, roughly R2,000 a month). So rather than R4,000 of software for the whole workshop, you buy one R80-a-month licence for one person, on real jobs, with a written goal agreed up front: "Dineo can make an accurate quote for a standard chair in under 20 minutes." If the evidence from your own workshop earns it, you grow it; if not, you've lost R80, not R4,000, and dodged another headstone. A dazzling demo proves nothing; real proof is a small, successful change in how your people actually work - so buy the change, not the thing.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You don't need a workshop to fall into this - watch it with a gym membership.

See the buy-the-thing trap with a gym membership

The demo is the tour: shiny machines, a pool, and a vision of a fitter you - you're sold on the idea. The purchase is signing up for the monthly payment. But the change to your life is getting up at 6am, packing a bag, driving there in the rain, and actually exercising when you're tired - and the tour never sold you on that. So the membership card sits in a drawer, unused: you bought the thing, but never made the change.

A small, real-use test would have been five pay-as-you-go passes and a written goal - "success is going three times in the next two weeks" - testing whether the change was even possible before committing to the big monthly expense.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of one thing you're tempted to buy right now - software, a machine, a course - or one already in your own graveyard. Don't look at the demo or the brochure; look at the change:

  1. Whose daily work has to change for this to succeed? Be specific - name the person. And who will own it, the go-to expert?
  2. What real problem does it solve, and what's that costing you now? Put a number on it - no real problem, no purchase.
  3. Run the smallest, cheapest real-use test, with a written goal. What would that one person do, for how long - and what single sentence would mean it succeeded? Grow it only if the evidence earns it.
R

Name the thing, the change it really needs, and the small test you'd run first. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Walk a real purchase through these questions together.)

Being moved by a brilliant demonstration isn't foolishness - it's the good, hopeful instinct of someone who wants to serve people better, and that instinct is worth keeping. It was simply aimed at the wrong thing: at the tool, not at the change. A demo is a promise of what could be; a small, real test in your own hands is proof of what will be. Buy the proof, weave the change patiently into how you truly work, and your money will start buying something real at last - not another beautiful thing gathering dust, but a genuinely better way to serve the people who count on you.
