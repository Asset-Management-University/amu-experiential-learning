Step 1 of 5

What does it REALLY cost - over its whole life?

T1: The Ache

You're at the counter. The salesperson smiles, taps the calculator, and turns it to you: "It's a great machine - and it's easy. Only R550 a month." That number feels small. It feels possible. You can almost say yes on the spot.

But a knot tightens in your stomach. That friendly little number is hiding something, isn't it? It adds up to more over time - how much more? And what about the cost to run it? You can feel the real number is hidden behind the small one, and you have no way to drag it into the light. So you cannot tell a genuine deal from a beautifully disguised trap.

That "small monthly number" is hiding the true cost - so how do I see the real number, over the whole time I own it, before I sign?

Before you read on, decide. A R10,000 display fridge, or "an easy R550 a month for 24 months". Write down what you would need to know before saying yes, and how you would work it out - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one leaves out

• "Ask what the interest rate is." A good question, and a rate is a number most people cannot turn into rands standing at a counter. You can be quoted a rate that sounds reasonable and still hand over thousands more than you meant to.

• "Check if I can afford R550 a month." The question the payment plan is designed to make you ask, because the answer is usually yes. Affording the instalment and the thing being worth the money are two different questions, and only one of them is being put to you.

• "Compare the R10,000 to other shops' prices." Sensible, and you are comparing the wrong number - the sticker is not what this deal costs. You could find a cheaper sticker on a worse plan and come out behind.

• "Sleep on it." Better than signing now, and it does not turn the small number into the real one. In a week you will be standing here with the same two numbers and the same feeling.

• "Just pay cash and avoid all this." Often the right answer, and it is a rule rather than a reason - it will also stop you taking a genuinely good deal, and it still leaves out the cost of running the thing for two years.

• What the first five have in common is that they all stay inside the numbers the shop chose to show you: the monthly and the sticker. Neither of those is what the fridge costs. The real number is every rand that leaves your hand because you own this thing, over its whole life, minus what you get back at the end - and it includes at least two large amounts nobody at that counter has mentioned. It is four lines on a calculator, and it changes the answer.

T2: The Tool in Action

Muhammad wants a glass-front display fridge to show off Tank You's accessories - R10,000 on the sticker, or "an easy R550 a month for 24 months." Watch the true number appear:

1. The friendly number they showed me

'Only R550 a month, easy!' - on a R10,000 display fridge. Both numbers feel small and manageable. That's exactly what they're designed to feel like.

2. Multiply it out - what the payments actually add up to

R550 x 24 = R13,200 handed over. The sticker said R10,000, so R3,200 of that is the cost of borrowing - it was never mentioned at the counter. The fridge is not a R10,000 fridge on this plan; it is a R13,200 fridge. (And paying it off early stops that cost on every month I skip.)

3. The whole-life total (every cost, then what's left)

Cash out over 2 years: R13,200 (on credit) + R600 card fees + R2,760 electricity = R16,560. Minus what it's worth at the end (~R3,000 resale) = R13,560 true cost to own. The electricity alone is R2,760 - nearly as much as the borrowing cost, and nobody at the counter mentioned it either.

4. The verdict + my move

The 'R10,000 fridge' really costs about R13,560 to own for two years on this plan. My move: save ~R2,500 a month for 4 months and pay R10,000 CASH. Same fridge, same electricity, no borrowing cost and no card fees - a whole-life total of about R9,760 instead of R13,560. The move is worth R3,800, and I can say so out loud.

Look what surfaced. The "R10,000 fridge" on that easy plan really costs about R13,560 to own for two years - the R10,000 sticker, plus R3,200 of borrowing cost and R600 in card fees that the friendly R550 was covering up, plus about R2,760 of electricity, less roughly R3,000 back if he sells it at the end. Two of those lines are bigger than most people's guess and neither was mentioned at the counter: the borrowing cost, which only appears when you multiply the payment out, and the electricity, which appears only if you remember that a fridge runs day and night for two years. And once the whole number is on the page, the decision makes itself - paying cash for the same fridge comes to about R9,760 all in, so Muhammad's move is worth R3,800 and he can say so in one sentence. The number was never small. It was only ever made to look that way.

T3: The Blueprint

Here is the repeatable method to see the true number behind any big purchase.

  • 1. Challenge the friendly number. A monthly payment or a sticker price is never the real number. Stop and ask: "What is the WHOLE number, over the WHOLE life of this choice?"
  • 2. Multiply the payment out. Monthly payment x number of months = what you will really hand over. Subtract the sticker, and what is left is the cost of borrowing. One line, and it is the single move that defends you most often.
  • 3. Run the whole-life checklist. Add every rand that leaves your hand because you own this thing: price (cash, or the total of all payments) + finance charges + recurring fees + running costs (electricity, fuel, data, insurance, licence). Then subtract what it is worth at the end. The running costs are the ones people forget, and on anything that plugs in or burns fuel they are often the biggest line on the page.
  • 4. Compare honestly, then decide. Put the friendly number and the true total side by side, price your alternative the same way, and let the difference make the call - in rands, so it is a decision and not a mood.

One sentence holds it all: the real number is the whole number, over the whole life - never the friendly one they show you. It works on anything you buy and keep: a bakkie, a phone, a printer, a generator, a second-hand freezer.

Two limits worth knowing. The whole-life total tells you what something costs, never what it is worth - a fridge that brings in R1,500 a month of extra sales is worth paying R13,560 for, and this method will not tell you that; you have to put the earnings beside it yourself. And where you cannot know a figure - what a resale will fetch in two years, what electricity will cost - use an honest estimate, write down that it is one, and try the sum again with a worse figure to see whether the answer changes.

There is a second half to this, and it has a unit of its own. The same force that adds R3,200 to a fridge over two years works for you when the money is on your side of the table, and it rewards starting early far more than starting big. Working out what that is worth, and why the early years cannot be bought back later, is its own arithmetic and its own problem.

T5: The Guardrails

A few truths that keep the true number in view.

The traps - and how to stay ahead of them

• The small monthly number is the number-one trap. It is designed to make expensive things feel cheap. Always multiply it out to the real total before you decide - your first and best defence.

• Running costs left out. Electricity, fuel, data, insurance, the annual licence, the service. On anything that plugs in or burns fuel these often beat the borrowing cost, and they are invisible on the day you sign.

• What it is worth at the end, forgotten. Two things with the same price are not the same cost if one is worth R3,000 in two years and the other is worth nothing. Subtract the resale - honestly, and low rather than high.

• "0% interest" is a sales tactic, not a gift. The cost is usually somewhere else - a higher sticker price, a once-off "initiation" fee, or harsh penalties if you miss one payment. Read the fine print and total it yourself.

• Two options compared on different bases. If you total one over its whole life, total the other one the same way, over the same years, with the same lines. Otherwise you are choosing between an x-ray and a photograph.

• The true number treated as the whole decision. It tells you the cost, not the value. Something expensive that earns can be worth it; write the earnings beside the cost and decide with both.

You now have x-ray vision for the cost of anything you buy and keep. You can see through the fog of any "friendly number" to what it will really take out of your pocket, over the whole life of the thing - and that turns you from the target of a sales pitch into somebody making a decision with the facts in front of them. Multiply the payment out, add the costs that arrive later, subtract what is left at the end, and walk calmly away from any deal that does not truly serve you. (Working with an AMU Facilitator?
