Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Zodwa, head of infrastructure maintenance for a local municipality. You are in the annual budget meeting. On the screen is a slick architect's rendering of the proposed new flagship library and civic square - full of vision, growth and community pride. When its R80 million is approved, the room breaks into applause.

Then it is your turn. Your presentation has no renderings. It has photographs of weeping joints on a 40-year-old water main that supplies the oldest suburb, and a crumbling roof on the clinic that serves 15,000 people. Your request is R10 million - just to keep the existing network from failing. The committee chair thanks you: "We appreciate the detail, Zodwa, but funds are tight after the flagship project. We'll see what's left."

Six months later you are standing in the rain as a section of the clinic roof collapses, water pouring into the dispensary, destroying medicine and records. The clinic is closed indefinitely, and a young mother with a sick baby is turned away at the gate. You warned them. You showed them the numbers. But the invisible work of keeping a roof sound lost to the visible glory of a new building.

The new build gets the budget and the applause while the network we already have is left to rot - how do I win the funding fight for the upkeep nobody sees?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

At first you think the problem is that they simply do not understand the risk. So you present the data harder - a more complex risk matrix, a detailed financial model, asset depreciation curves, probability-weighted outcomes. The decision-makers' eyes glaze over: they deal in stories and visible progress, and your numbers, however accurate, cannot compete with a picture of a shiny new building. Then comes a darker thought - let a small thing fail so they learn. You stop yourself: there are no small failures. A pothole wrecks a delivery driver's axle and costs him a day's wages; a burst pipe floods a small business. People get hurt, the mission fails, and when the inquiry comes the blame lands on you - it is a betrayal of your duty to serve. Finally you try to play their game - you rebrand the roof-repair programme as "Project Nightingale" with a fancy logo. It feels hollow, and it loses anyway: a new roof is still not a new library, and the real, unglamorous backlog only grows.

You realise you are asking the wrong question. It is not "how can I make my spreadsheets better?". So before you read on: your success is invisible - how do you make it visible?

Presenting the RISK DATA harder (a bigger matrix, depreciation curves, probability-weighted outcomes) just glazes their eyes - decision-makers run on stories and visible progress, and no spreadsheet beats a picture of a shiny new building. LETTING A SMALL THING FAIL 'to make the point' is a betrayal of duty - there are no small failures (a pothole wrecks a driver's axle, a burst pipe floods a business), people get hurt and the blame lands on me. PLAYING THEIR GAME (rebranding 'roof repairs' as 'Project Nightingale' with a logo) is hollow and still loses - a new roof is not a new library - while the real backlog grows. I am asking the wrong question. My success is INVISIBLE: how do I make it visible?
Maintenance loses the funding fight because its success is INVISIBLE - a bridge that does not fall, a pipe that does not burst, a clinic that simply stays open: nothing happens, no ribbon, no photo, no credit - while a new build is visible and rewarded, so rational people chase ribbons and starve upkeep until the asset rots and you pay twice. Five moves: (1) SEE THE BIAS - we reward the visible and ignore the invisible, so do not blame the decision-makers, change what they SEE. (2) MAKE THE INVISIBLE VISIBLE - tell the story of the disaster you PREVENTED (the failure that did not happen, the service kept running), with a face, a photo of the people served, and a number. (3) REFRAME upkeep from 'a cost' to PROTECTING VALUE ALREADY BOUGHT - you already paid for this asset; deferring maintenance does not save money, it borrows from the future at a brutal interest rate (a small repair now vs a full rebuild later). (4) MAKE THE TRADE-OFF EXPLICIT - one ribbon today versus these specific assets failing within a few years; show whole-life cost, not just build cost. (5) BUILD A CONSTITUENCY - the people who depend on the existing service are your allies; give the quiet, working assets a human voice. The most valuable assets are the ones already working that nobody notices.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you realise maintenance is not a 'cost' - it is the work of protecting a promise already made to the public, and its success is that NOTHING happens: the water keeps running, the lights stay on, the clinic stays open, the bridge does not fall. Your job is to make that "nothing" a visible, celebrated achievement. Five moves:

  • See the bias - we reward the visible, ignore the invisible: the breakthrough is to stop seeing this as the decision-makers being foolish or hostile and to see the bias clearly: humans reward what they can SEE and ignore what they cannot. A new build is visible, photogenic, finite and creditable - there is a ribbon to cut and a name on a plaque. Maintenance done well produces NOTHING visible: the water keeps running, the clinic stays open, the bridge does not fall. So perfectly rational people chase the ribbon and starve the upkeep - it is not malice, it is incentives. Once you accept that, you stop wasting energy being angry at the room and start working on the only thing you can change: what they see. The fight is not for their conscience; it is for their attention.
  • Make the invisible visible - tell the prevention story: stop showing pictures of cracked pipes and rusty bolts - that is the language of cost and decay, and it loses to a rendering every time. Instead, make the PREVENTED disaster visible: tell the story of the failure that did not happen. Show a photo of a schoolchild at a working water fountain with the line, 'we protected this: a R500,000 length of pipe replaced prevented a R20 million failure that would have closed three schools.' Give the success a face (the people served), a story (the catastrophe averted), and a number (the cost avoided). You cannot cut a ribbon for a flood that never came - so you must narrate it, vividly, until the quiet success becomes as real in the room as the shiny new thing.
  • Reframe upkeep from cost to protecting value already bought: change the language from spending to protecting. Maintenance is not a new cost competing with new projects; it is the insurance premium on assets the public has ALREADY paid for. 'We have already invested R25 million in this clinic - my R1 million for the roof is not a new cost, it is what protects that R25 million asset and the health of 15,000 people.' And be honest about deferral: putting off upkeep does not save money, it BORROWS it at a brutal interest rate - a R1 million roof today becomes a R15 million rebuild and a closed service tomorrow. Once maintenance is framed as guarding value already bought rather than asking for something new, it stops being the easy thing to cut.
  • Make the trade-off explicit and honest: do not let the choice stay hidden as 'fund the exciting thing, and maintenance gets what is left'. Put the real trade-off on the table, concretely and without spin: 'we can fund the new statue in the park for R5 million, or use that R5 million to keep these three bridges open that will otherwise close within two years - one ribbon-cutting now, or 30,000 people a day moving safely.' Show whole-life cost, not just the build cost (the new library will itself need upkeep forever - who funds that?). You are not arguing against the new; you are insisting the choice be made with eyes open, in daylight, owning its consequences - which is the decision-makers' actual job.
  • Build a constituency - give the working assets a voice: you should not be the lone voice for the invisible network - the people who depend on it are your natural allies, and they are far more persuasive than any engineer. Find the nurse whose clinic stays open, the principal whose school has water, the trader whose road carries customers, and give them a platform to speak about how much the EXISTING service matters to their lives. A councillor can wave away a maintenance manager's spreadsheet; it is much harder to wave away a community whose daily life depends on the thing you are trying to protect. Give the quiet, working assets a human voice, and the funding conversation stops being about your costs and becomes about the people you both serve.

See it transform Zodwa's next budget round. She stops showing cracked pipes and starts showing a schoolchild at a working water fountain: "we protected this - a R500,000 length of pipe replaced prevented a R20 million failure that would have closed three schools." She reframes the clinic roof not as a cost but as the insurance premium on a R25 million asset and the health of 15,000 people. She lays the trade-off out in daylight - "one statue for R5 million, or three bridges kept open for 30,000 people a day" - and she brings the head nurse and the school principal to speak for the services they depend on. The conversation changes: it is no longer about her costs but about the services protected, the failures prevented, the people served. She does not get everything, but she wins the critical funding for the roof and the water main. Maintenance loses the funding fight because its success is invisible - a bridge that does not fall, a pipe that does not burst; there is no ribbon to cut for a failure that did not happen. So make the prevention visible: reframe upkeep from a boring cost into the vital work of protecting what people already paid for, give a face and a story and a number to the disaster you avert every day, put the real trade-off in daylight, and let the people who depend on the service speak for it. The most valuable assets are the ones already working that nobody notices - make their quiet success impossible to ignore.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This is not only about municipalities - the same bias runs a household. A family buys a brand-new, top-of-the-range TV on credit: exciting, visible, enjoyed by all. Meanwhile the car is overdue a R3,000 service, there is a small dark patch where the roof leaks, and a tooth has started to ache.

See the same moves at home

Ignored, the boring upkeep fails catastrophically: the engine seizes on the motorway (a R40,000 repair); the small leak becomes a deluge in a storm, ruining the ceiling and furniture (R60,000); the toothache becomes an abscess and a painful root canal (R8,000). Now apply the moves:

• Make the invisible visible: a sticker on the dashboard - "a R3,000 service now PREVENTS a R40,000 breakdown".
• Reframe the cost: "fixing the roof for R5,000 is not a cost - it is protecting our R2 million house".
• Make the trade-off explicit: "we can get the new TV this month, or service the car - if we choose the TV we accept the risk of being stranded next month; is that a good trade for our family?"

By making the consequences visible and reframing the cost, the boring upkeep suddenly feels as important as it truly is.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look around your own organisation. Where is the quiet, essential work of upkeep being starved for the glamour of the new? It might be the server that runs your whole company, the delivery fleet, the customer database, or the building's air-conditioning - something everyone takes for granted, until it fails. Your task is to become the guardian of that invisible work.

  1. Pick one 'boring' asset - one critical system or piece of equipment being quietly neglected.
  2. Tell its prevention story. Who and what depends on it? What is the failure you are preventing? Find a photo of the people it serves, not the rust on its bolts.
  3. Calculate the brutal interest rate. The small preventative repair today versus the catastrophic full failure tomorrow - framed as "cost of action vs cost of inaction".
  4. Find its voice. Get a quote from someone whose work would stop dead if it failed: "if this server goes down, our entire sales team is blind for the day."

This was never about cynicism, and the decision-makers are not villains - they are human, wired to reward what they can see, and the fix is not to despair of them but to change what they see. It is not about winning an argument; it is the dignified, essential work of making sure the promises made yesterday are still kept tomorrow - the work of keeping things working, for the people who quietly depend on them.
