Not last year, plus ten

T1: The Ache

Every year the maintenance budget is set the same way: take last year's number, add a few percent, done. It feels safe and it takes an afternoon. But the number has quietly lost all connection to what the assets actually need. You are still funding a monthly inspection contract nobody needs any more, because it was in last year's figure - while the intake screens that are visibly failing go unbudgeted, because they were not.

And then finance asks for a cut, and you do the only thing an incremental budget lets you do: shave everything by the same percentage. So the trivial repaint and the safety-critical dosing system take the same haircut - and the one thing that keeps the town's water safe is now a little less funded, for no reason anyone chose. Your budget is a ghost of last year's, and it is protecting the wrong things.

Our maintenance budget is just last year plus a percentage, so we fund work nobody needs while critical work goes unbudgeted - and when we must cut, we cut everything equally. How should an asset budget actually be built?

Before you read on, decide. The budget took an afternoon: last year's figure, plus six per cent. Inside it, still funded, is a quarterly inspection contract nobody can now explain — the person who set it up left in 2019. Not inside it at all: the intake screens, which are failing and which nobody budgeted for, because they were not in last year's number to be adjusted. Then the instruction arrives to cut by eight per cent, and you do what everybody does — shave every line equally. So the depot repaint and the chlorine dosing system take the same haircut, and the thing keeping the town's water safe is now slightly less funded, for no reason anybody chose. Write down how a budget should be built — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the base is carrying

• “Argue for a bigger budget.” Even granted, it would be last year's base plus more — carrying the same unexplained contract forward and still omitting the screens, because nothing about how the number is built has changed.

• “Add the intake screens as an extra line.” Fixes the one omission you happened to notice, and the method that hid it is intact. Next year's newly necessary thing will be equally invisible.

• “Cancel the inspection contract.” Probably right and worth checking first — nobody remembers why it exists, which is not the same as knowing it is useless. And it addresses one line out of a base full of them.

• “Protect the critical items from the cut.” The right instinct with no basis behind it. Protected on whose judgement, and against what? Without a stated ordering it becomes an argument about who is most persuasive.

• “An equal cut is the fairest way.” This is the moment worth stopping on. It feels even-handed, and it silently decides that the repaint and the dosing system matter equally. A flat cut is not fairness, it is an abdication.

• The default method is a quiet trap because it never questions the base. Whatever was in last year's figure rides forward untouched — every past inefficiency, every contract that outlived its purpose, every line nobody remembers — while anything newly necessary is invisible, because it was not there last year to be adjusted. It assumes the world does not change, and asset bases change constantly, so the approach that feels safest is the one that slowly disconnects your spending from what your assets actually need. Three corrections work together. Break the inheritance by justifying each item from need rather than precedent, so nothing is funded merely because it was funded before. Let what the assets are actually doing — their condition and behaviour — drive the amounts, which is what ties the budget to reality rather than to memory. And when the money is short, as it always is, cut by consequence rather than uniformly, because the flat cut is exactly the moment your budget stops protecting anything in particular.

You build it from need and risk, not from history. Let us learn the methods.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you build an asset budget from what the assets need and the risk of not meeting it - breaking the incremental habit, letting condition drive the spend, and prioritising by risk when the money runs short.

1. The incremental trap: last year plus a percent

The default way almost everyone sets a budget is INCREMENTAL: take last year's figure and adjust it up or down a little. It is quick, it feels stable, and IT IS A QUIET TRAP, BECAUSE IT NEVER QUESTIONS THE BASE. Whatever was in last year's number rides forward untouched - every past inefficiency, every contract that has outlived its purpose, every line item nobody remembers the reason for - WHILE ANYTHING THAT HAS NEWLY BECOME NECESSARY IS INVISIBLE, because it was not there last year to be adjusted. Incremental budgeting ASSUMES THE WORLD DOES NOT CHANGE; asset bases change constantly. So the very method that feels safe is the one that SLOWLY DISCONNECTS YOUR SPENDING FROM YOUR ASSETS' ACTUAL NEEDS - still funding the inspection contract nobody needs while the failing intake screens go unbudgeted.

2. Zero-based budgeting: justify every rand from need

The first way to break the inheritance is ZERO-BASED BUDGETING: instead of starting from last year, you start from ZERO, and EVERY RAND MUST BE JUSTIFIED FROM NEED before it goes into the budget. Nothing is funded because it was funded before; each activity earns its place by showing what it is for and what happens without it. This is where the legacy inspection contract gets caught - asked to justify itself from scratch, it CANNOT, because the work is now done in-house. Zero-based budgeting is MORE EFFORT than adding a percentage, and few organisations do it fully every single year - but it is THE RIGHT DISCIPLINE WHENEVER A BUDGET HAS DRIFTED, because it is the only method that scrutinises the BASE and not just the change. It does not assume the past was right; it asks the past to prove itself.

3. Condition-based budgeting: let the assets tell you

The second method decides WHAT to fund from the assets themselves. In CONDITION-BASED BUDGETING, the spend is driven by the measured CONDITION of the assets - the inspection and monitoring data that says which assets are degrading, how fast, and when they will need work - rather than by a fixed calendar or by tradition. You fund the pump that the data shows is deteriorating and DEFER the one that is still healthy, regardless of when each was 'due'. This avoids the TWO OPPOSITE WASTES: doing work TOO EARLY (spending on an asset that did not yet need it) and doing it TOO LATE (letting an asset fail because its slot on the calendar had not come up). THE CONDITION DATA, NOT THE ALMANAC, SETS THE BUDGET - which is what connects the money to the real state of the assets rather than to the passage of time.

4. Risk-based prioritisation: when you cannot fund it all

Here is the truth that makes the final method unavoidable: even a perfectly justified, condition-driven list of needs will cost MORE THAN THE MONEY AVAILABLE. IT ALWAYS DOES. So the last method is RISK-BASED PRIORITISATION: when the budget cannot fund every justified need, you rank the needs by RISK - THE LIKELIHOOD OF FAILURE TIMES ITS CONSEQUENCE - and you fund the ones that BUY DOWN THE MOST RISK FIRST. This beats the two bad instincts. DO NOT cut evenly across the board: an across-the-board trim weakens everything a little and protects nothing, quietly starving the safety-critical dosing system by the same percentage as a cosmetic repaint. And DO NOT simply fund the loudest voice or the newest shiny thing. FUND WHERE THE RISK IS HIGHEST - and measure that risk not only in rands but in CONSEQUENCE TO PEOPLE AND TO THE SERVICE, because the highest-risk item is usually the one whose failure would harm people or stop the water. Say it plainly: A CONSTRAINED BUDGET IS A REALITY, NOT A FAILURE. THE DISCIPLINED RESPONSE IS TO PROTECT WHAT MATTERS MOST.

5. The three together - and it is not about spending more

These are NOT rival philosophies to choose between; THEY ARE PARTNERS IN ONE BUDGET. A good asset budget is CONDITION-INFORMED (what do the assets actually need?), ZERO-BASED IN SPIRIT (justified from need, not from history), and RISK-PRIORITISED (the highest-risk needs funded first when money is short) - all at once. And notice what this is NOT: it is NOT a plea to spend more. Zero-based and condition-based budgeting very often spend LESS, or the same amount REDIRECTED - cutting the work that habit funded and moving it to the work that risk demands. THE GOAL IS NOT A BIGGER BUDGET; IT IS A BUDGET POINTED AT THE TRUTH. Build it from what your assets need and the risk of failure, not from what you spent last year - and when you cannot fund it all, spend where the risk is highest, not where the habit is deepest.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. Onalenna has R2,100,000 for next year's maintenance and renewal at Corathyl. Last year's budget was R2,000,000. The needs on the table:

ItemCostCondition / note
Legacy monthly inspection contractR180,000Duplicates work now done in-house
Dosing-system overhaul (keeps water safe)R400,000Condition: degrading; failure risks unsafe water
Pump B renewalR650,000Condition: failing fast; high consequence if it stops supply
Pump A overhaulR500,000Condition: healthy; not yet needed
Intake screens repairR520,000Newly failing; unbudgeted for years
Office block repaintR150,000Cosmetic; low consequence
  1. Spot the incremental error. Which item would "last year plus 6 percent" wrongly keep funding, and which newly-critical item would it miss?
  2. Apply condition. Name one item the condition data says to defer, and one to bring forward.
  3. Prioritise by risk to fit R2,100,000. Choose what to fund and what to defer so the total fits the money - and for each item state, in a few words, its likelihood and consequence.
  4. Defend it. Say why this beats trimming every item by the same percentage.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the incremental error: "last year plus 6 percent" (R2,120,000) would keep funding the legacy inspection contract (it was in the base) and would miss the intake screens (a new need that was never in the base) - the two exact failures of budgeting from history.

Second, condition: defer the Pump A overhaul (healthy, not yet needed - saving R500,000) and bring forward the Pump B renewal (failing fast). The data, not the calendar, decides.

Third, a risk-prioritised plan that fits R2,100,000: cut the legacy contract (zero-based, R0); fund the dosing overhaul R400,000 (likelihood: degrading; consequence: unsafe water - very high), pump B R650,000 (likelihood: high; consequence: supply stops - high), intake screens R520,000 (likelihood: failing now; consequence: intake blockage - high) = R1,570,000; defer pump A (likelihood: low; consequence: none yet) and the repaint R150,000 (likelihood: n/a; consequence: cosmetic). That leaves headroom within R2,100,000 for contingency. The mark of a real answer is that every funded item is justified by likelihood and consequence, and the highest-consequence-to-people item (the dosing system) is protected.

Fourth, why not even cuts: an across-the-board 6 percent trim would shave the dosing system and pump B by the same percentage as the repaint - weakening the safety-critical and the trivial equally, protecting nothing, and still funding the useless legacy contract. Risk-based prioritisation protects what matters most; even cutting protects nothing.

T5: The Guardrails

Budget for what the assets need and the risk of not meeting it, not for what you spent last year - and when you cannot fund it all, spend where the risk is highest, not where the habit is deepest.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Last year plus a percent. The comfortable default never questions the base and slowly disconnects spending from need. Build from need, not from history.

• Never questioning the base. Incremental budgeting only ever adjusts the change, never the inherited whole. Zero-base it periodically to catch the waste that rides forward.

• Funding by calendar, not condition. Doing work because it is "due" wastes money on healthy assets and misses failing ones. Let the condition data set the spend.

• Across-the-board cuts. Trimming everything equally starves the safety-critical alongside the trivial and protects nothing. Cut by risk, not by percentage.

• Funding the loudest voice or newest shiny thing. Attention is not risk. Rank by likelihood times consequence, and fund the highest first.

• Treating the methods as rivals. Condition, zero-based and risk-based work together in one budget - informed by condition, justified from need, prioritised by risk.

• Thinking zero-based means spend more. It usually means spend less, or redirected - cutting habit-funded work and moving it to risk-driven work.

• Measuring risk only in rands. The highest-risk item is often the one whose failure hurts people or stops the service, not the one with the biggest invoice.
