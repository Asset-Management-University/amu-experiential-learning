If my business is clearly making a profit, how can I keep running out of money - and what do I do about a good month that leaves me broke?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Aziz, and you run a market stall selling beautiful handmade blankets - and you're doing well. This last month was your best ever: add up all your sales, take away the cost of the wool and your other expenses, and the number is clear, you made a good profit. You should be celebrating.

But it's the first of the month, and you feel sick with panic. Your stall rent is due today; your wool supplier needs paying today; together that's R20,000 - and the money you actually have on hand is almost nothing. How is this possible, when you're succeeding? The catch: your biggest and best customers, the local hotels, always pay you later - they're holding R25,000 of your money, but won't pay it until the 20th. And you had to buy all the wool upfront to make the blankets. So here you are, a profitable business owner who can't pay his bills, succeeding and failing at the very same moment.

If my business is clearly making a profit, how can I keep running out of money - and what can I possibly do about a good month that leaves me broke?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to work even harder - "I just need to sell more!" But if you sell more blankets to the hotels on the same "pay you later" terms, the problem gets worse: you'd buy even more wool upfront, digging a deeper hole for the 1st. More success would mean more panic. Your next thought is to borrow from a friend just to get through the day - a plaster on a deep cut; it might work this month, but what about the next, and the next? Finally you start to doubt yourself - "maybe I'm just bad at this" and think of giving up. But you ARE profitable; the whole month's numbers prove it. You're not bad at business; you're caught in the most common trap there is.

You're measuring your success by the month, but your survival is decided on the day. So before you read on: you make more than you spend over the month, yet you're broke on the 1st - what are you measuring wrong, and what actually decides whether you can pay on the day?

And I AM profitable - the month's numbers prove it, so that's not the problem. I make more than I spend over the month yet I'm broke on the 1st - so what am I measuring wrong, and what actually decides whether I can pay on the day?
Profit is measured over a period; cash is what's in your hand on a day - and money leaves fast (rent, stock, upfront) while it arrives slow ('pay you later'), so a profitable month can still leave you broke on the wrong day. The problem is timing, not effort - doing more on the same terms widens the gap. So MAKE THE TIMING VISIBLE: map money-in and money-out by date for the weeks ahead and see the danger days coming. Then close the gap fairly: PULL income in sooner, PUSH bills out later, or PAUSE an optional outflow.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

A profitable business can die on a single day it can't pay a bill - and that's not your fault; it's because profit and cash are two different things. Managing the timing of your money isn't greed - it's how you survive to keep serving. Five moves:

  • See the trap - profit is not cash: profit is a calculation over a period (a whole month): the story of your success. Cash is the actual money in your hand on a specific day: the fuel you need to operate. Aziz's stall made about R5,000 profit over the month (R25,000 of sales minus R20,000 of costs) - and yet on the 1st he needed R20,000 in cash he didn't have. A profitable business can still die on a single day it can't pay a bill.
  • The problem is timing, not effort: the trouble isn't too few sales - it's that money LEAVES fast (wool bought upfront, rent on the 1st) but ARRIVES slow (hotels paying on the 20th), and that gap strands you. Working harder on the same terms only makes the gap WIDER. (This is different from growing too fast and taking on big new costs - see bp-0044 - this strands even a steady, profitable business.)
  • Make the timing visible ahead: stop looking only at the monthly total - get a calendar or notebook for the next four weeks. On the exact dates, write down all the money you expect to ACTUALLY come in ('20th: R25,000 from hotels') and all the money that MUST go out ('1st: R8,000 rent, R12,000 wool'). Now you can SEE the dangerous days coming long before they arrive - you were watching overall gain; watch instead 'enough on the exact day'.
  • Pull your money in sooner, push your bills out later: once you can see the gap, act on it - fairly. PULL income forward: ask new customers for a deposit, invoice the day you deliver, or offer a small discount for paying now. PUSH payments back: as a good, regular customer, ask your supplier honestly, 'my customers pay me on the 20th - could I pay you on the 21st rather than the 1st?' These are honest negotiations that help everyone keep trading, not strong-arming.
  • Pause an optional outflow - and you don't need all three: if a cost isn't essential right now (the new display stand you wanted), PAUSE it - wait and buy it next month, after the money you're owed has landed. And you don't have to do all three moves: even one small, fair change can close the gap, so you have the cash on the day you need it and your profitable business survives.

Watch it rescue Aziz. He stops staring at the proud monthly total and writes the next four weeks on a calendar: out on the 1st, R8,000 rent and R12,000 wool; in on the 20th, R25,000 from the hotels. The danger day leaps off the page - and now he can act on it, fairly: he offers new customers a small early-payment discount and asks for deposits (pull), asks his long-standing wool supplier to take payment on the 21st instead of the 1st, when the hotel money has landed (push), and holds off on the new display stand until then (pause). One or two of those, and the 1st is survivable - the same profitable business, no longer ambushed. Profit is measured over a period, but cash is needed on a day - so a good month can hide a fatal mismatch in timing; don't just do more, make the timing visible ahead, then pull income in, push bills out, or pause an outflow.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about market stalls - the same timing gap strands anyone paid on a different clock from their bills. Watch a freelancer.

See the same move with a freelance writer

Nour, a freelance writer, has a fantastic month and finishes three big projects, sending invoices worth R30,000. She's clearly profitable. But every invoice says "payment in 30 days" - and this week her tax payment is due, her rent needs paying, and her design-software subscription renews. Profitable on paper, no cash to pay the bills.

Her wrong instinct: rush to find a fourth project - which would also be "paid in 30 days" and do nothing for this week. Her right move:

• Make the timing visible: she maps her bill dates against her payment dates and sees the 30-day gap will leave her short.
• Pull: for her next project she changes terms to "50% deposit to start, 50% on completion".
• Push: she arranges to pay her tax bill in two smaller instalments.
• Pause: she holds off buying a new laptop until a big invoice lands.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own business, job, or side-hustle. Does the money you earn arrive on a different timetable from the money you must spend?

  1. Separate profit from cash, and resist "just do more". Profit is what you earn over a whole month or year; cash is what's in your hand on any given day - and survival depends on cash. If your work has a built-in lag between spending and earning, doing more of it can make the squeeze worse before it helps.
  2. Map your timings. Get a simple calendar and, for the next month, write down when money will actually land in your hand and when your bills are actually due. Find the danger days before they ambush you.
  3. Choose one fair move - pull, push, or pause. Can you ask for a deposit or offer a small early-payment discount (pull)? Agree a payment date with a supplier or landlord that matches when you get paid (push)? Delay a non-urgent purchase until your cash is stronger (pause)?

Being profitable yet stranded for cash is not a sign you are failing - it is the single most common and most bewildering trap for good small businesses, and it catches the successful ones precisely because they're busy succeeding. The month's proud total was never a lie; it just couldn't tell you what mattered most - whether there was enough in your hand on the exact day a bill fell due. Learn to watch that, and to pull, push and pause with honesty and care, and your business stops lurching from panic to panic. Managing the timing of money is simply how you make sure the mission - the reason you started - survives to serve people tomorrow, and next month, and next year. Cash is the oxygen that lets you keep showing up.
