One at a time is how you miss the interaction

T1: The Ache

You are Adaeze, and you are reading a trial report from two years ago.

It is careful work. Somebody wanted to reduce liner wear in the transfer chute, and they tested the two proposals properly, one at a time, against a measured baseline.

Baseline wear: 73 mm. Harder liner alone: 51 mm - a saving of 22. Steeper chute angle alone: 59 mm - a saving of 14.

So the report recommended both, and projected 37 mm. The capital case was approved on that number.

The chute was rebuilt. It runs at 49 mm.

Nobody made an arithmetic error. Nobody was careless. Both changes genuinely helped, and the chute is genuinely much better than it was. But the number in the business case was wrong by twelve millimetres from the day it was written, and the person who wrote it spent a year being asked about it.

Two changes that each help, that together help less than the sum. How do I design a trial that would have shown me that before I built the business case?

Before you read on, decide. Two changes to the transfer chute. The harder liner was trialled on its own and cut wear depth by a measured amount. The steeper angle was trialled on its own and cut it by a measured amount too. Somebody added the two improvements together, projected 37 mm, and the capital case was approved on that number. The chute was rebuilt. It runs at 49 mm — twelve millimetres adrift. Nobody made an arithmetic error and nobody was careless; both changes genuinely helped and the chute is genuinely much better than it was. But the number in the business case was wrong from the day it was written, and the man who wrote it spent a year being asked about it. Write down how a trial could have shown this beforehand — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what sequential trials cannot see

• “Run each trial for longer.” More precise measurements of each change alone. Both individual figures were right — the error was in putting them together, and no amount of extra duration on a single-factor trial touches that.

• “Be more conservative — discount the projection.” Guesses at a correction without knowing its size or direction, and next time the two changes might amplify each other and you would be short the other way.

• “Somebody should have checked the arithmetic.” The arithmetic was correct. Adding the two numbers is exactly what the trials appeared to license.

• “Model it instead of trialling it.” A model would need to know how the two mechanisms interact — which is the very thing nobody knows, and the reason the projection failed.

• “Trial the combination as well, afterwards.” Genuinely better and it is a third trial after two, at three times the cost and time — and it still tells you nothing about a fourth change somebody proposes next year.

• The cause is structural: changes tested one at a time cannot reveal how they behave together. Two things that each improve a situation may, in combination, deliver less than the sum — because they act partly on the same mechanism, or one alters the conditions the other depends on. That relationship is invisible to any number of one-at-a-time trials, so rigour in the individual tests cannot rescue the addition. What makes it visible is varying the factors together in a structured set of combinations rather than singly. Two levels of each is enough — a deliberate economy, since two levels answer whether something matters and in which direction, which is the question at this stage. Run across the full set, you recover the separate contributions and the extent to which the factors interfere or amplify — which is precisely what a business case needs. Two disciplines keep it honest: run in random order, so anything drifting over time cannot attach itself to a factor and pose as its effect; and if budget forces fewer runs, state exactly which effects you can no longer tell apart — acceptable when declared, dangerous when discovered later by whoever is reading your conclusion.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the design - the factors and levels, the full matrix run in random order, the effects by subtraction, the interaction that the old trial could not see, and the honest accounting if you are only allowed half the runs.

1. State what you will change, to which two levels, and what you will measure - and why an experiment rather than the records

An experiment earns its cost only when the records cannot answer the question, so say why first. Mine: the transfer chute wears through liners faster than budgeted, and three things have been proposed as the fix. The maintenance history cannot separate them, because they have never been varied independently - everything that has been tried was tried during a shutdown when two other things also changed. That is the condition under which an experiment is the right instrument rather than an indulgence. Now name the factors and pin each to exactly two levels. A - liner material: current grade, or the harder grade. B - chute angle: 55 degrees or 65 degrees. C - impact-bar spacing: 300 mm or 450 mm. Response: maximum wear depth in millimetres after 500 kilotonnes through the chute. Two levels each, not three, and this is a deliberate economy - two levels answer 'does it matter and in which direction', which is what you need first, at a fraction of the runs. And choose the levels wide enough to produce a difference you could actually detect, but inside what the plant will genuinely operate; a level nobody would ever run tells you something true about a machine that does not exist.

2. Build every combination - all eight - and randomise the order you run them in

Three factors at two levels is eight combinations, and you run all eight. Write them in standard order with each factor as minus or plus: (1) - - -, (2) + - -, (3) - + -, (4) + + -, (5) - - +, (6) + - +, (7) - + +, (8) + + +. Every combination appears exactly once, and - this is the property that does the work - each factor is at its high level for exactly four runs and its low level for four, and this is true of every factor simultaneously. That balance is why you will be able to separate their effects with nothing more than subtraction. Now randomise the order you actually run them in. Not the matrix - the sequence. If you run all four low-material combinations in March and all four high-material in September, then material is entangled with everything else that changed over six months: the ore, the moisture, the crew, the belt speed. Randomisation does not remove those things. It stops them lining up with your factors, which is the difference between a nuisance and a wrong answer. Write the random order into the plan before you start, because a run order decided on the day drifts, quite innocently, towards whatever is convenient.

3. Compute the main effects - the arithmetic is subtraction, and it is meant to be

Here are the eight results, in standard order, in millimetres of wear: 73, 51, 59, 49, 77, 55, 63, 53. The overall mean is 60. Now the main effect of each factor: average the four runs where it was high, subtract the average of the four where it was low. That is the whole calculation. Material (A): the high-level runs are 51, 49, 55, 53, averaging 52; the low-level runs are 73, 59, 77, 63, averaging 68. Effect = -16 mm. The harder grade removes sixteen millimetres of wear. Angle (B): -8 mm - the steeper chute wears less. Spacing (C): +4 mm - the wider bar spacing wears slightly more. Notice what you have just done with eight runs. Every one of those three numbers is computed from all eight results, not from a pair - so each effect is an average across the other factors rather than a single comparison at one setting. An eight-run design gives you three effects each estimated with four-fold replication. Three separate one-at-a-time trials would have given you three effects each estimated from two runs, for the same eight runs of plant time. The factorial is not more work. It is the same work, arranged so that every run counts towards every answer.

4. Compute the interaction - and check whether it changes the recommendation

An interaction is what one factor does to another factor's effect, and it is invisible to any trial that changes one thing at a time. Compute it exactly as before, using the product of the two sign columns: material by angle (AB) = +6 mm. The other two interactions come out at zero. Now see what that costs somebody who did not look. Run the one-at-a-time version on the same chute. Baseline is 73. Change the material alone and you get 51 - a saving of 22. Go back to baseline, change the angle alone, you get 59 - a saving of 14. So do both and expect 73 - 22 - 14 = 37 millimetres. The actual result of doing both is 49. The trial promised 37 and the plant delivered 49, and nobody did anything wrong. The two improvements overlap: the harder liner earns less on a steeper chute, because part of what the steeper angle fixes is what the harder material was compensating for. Both changes are still worth making - 49 is much better than 73. But a business case built on 37 was going to be twelve millimetres short from the day it was written, and the person who wrote it would have spent the following year being asked why.

5. If you must halve the runs, say exactly what you have given up - by name

Eight runs is often four runs more than a plant will give you, so you will be asked to halve it. You can - but you must say what you are buying it with. A half-fraction runs four of the eight, chosen so the factors stay balanced. The cost is that each effect you estimate is no longer one effect. It is a sum. In this design, the estimate of spacing (C) becomes C plus the material-by-angle interaction (AB), and there is nothing in the four runs that can separate them. Watch what that does here. The true spacing effect is +4 and the true AB interaction is +6. The four-run design reports +10 - and it reports it cleanly, with no warning, because the arithmetic is perfectly correct. You would conclude that bar spacing matters two and a half times as much as it does, and you would spend money on it. So the rule is not that fractional designs are wrong; they are often the only design you will be permitted to run. The rule is that you write down, before you run it, which estimates are sums and of what - and you say it in the report, in those words. An engineer who says 'this estimate is the spacing effect plus any material-by-angle interaction' is trusted. One who reports +10 is trusted too, right up until the chute is rebuilt.

T3: The Blueprint

A factorial experiment is not more work than testing one thing at a time. It is the same work, arranged so that every run counts towards every answer - and so that interactions become visible instead of arriving later as a shortfall. Five moves:

  • Justify the experiment, then fix the factors at two levels. Two levels, not three. "Does it matter and in which direction" is the first question, and it is cheap.
  • Run every combination, in random order. Balance and randomisation. Balance is what makes the arithmetic work; randomisation stops the calendar lining up with your factors.
  • Main effects by subtraction. High average minus low average. Each effect uses all eight runs, so each is estimated four-fold.
  • The interaction. Multiply the sign columns. This is the thing one-at-a-time testing structurally cannot see.
  • If you must halve it, name what you gave up. Confounding, declared. Not "fractional designs are unreliable" - "this estimate is C plus AB, and I am telling you so".

The arithmetic, start to finish. Eight runs in standard order give 73, 51, 59, 49, 77, 55, 63, 53. Mean 60. Material -16, angle -8, spacing +4, and the material-by-angle interaction +6; the other two interactions are zero. Now the old report: it changed material alone (saving 22), changed angle alone (saving 14), and predicted 73 - 22 - 14 = 37. The true answer at both settings is 49. The shortfall is the physical signature of the interaction, and the one-at-a-time design had no place to record it.

Why the interaction is physical, not statistical. This matters more than the arithmetic. The harder liner earns less on a steeper chute because part of what the steeper angle fixes is the same thing the harder material was compensating for - the material is sliding further and dwelling longer at the shallower angle. Once you have said that out loud, the +6 stops being a correction term and becomes a fact about the chute. An interaction you can explain is a finding. An interaction you can only compute is a warning that you do not yet understand your own machine.

The economy that is easy to miss. Eight runs of one-at-a-time testing gives three effects, each from a single pair of runs. Eight runs of a full factorial gives three effects each averaged over four comparisons, plus all three two-factor interactions, plus the three-factor one. Same plant time. Same tonnage. The difference is entirely in how the runs were arranged, which costs an afternoon at a desk.

An honest boundary. This is a screening design and it does what screening designs do: it tells you which factors matter and in which direction, at two levels. It does not find an optimum - for that you need more levels and a response surface. It assumes the response is roughly linear between your two levels, which is why the levels must be chosen inside the plant's real operating range. And a single unreplicated factorial gives you no independent estimate of experimental noise, so a small effect and a quiet fluctuation look identical. Say which of your effects are large enough that you are confident, and which you are not.

T4: Your Turn

Rethabile is designing a trial on filter-element life in a hydraulic system. His constraint is not knowledge - it is access.

  1. How many runs would the full four-factor design need? State it.
  2. Evaluate his plan. He drops D and runs a full factorial in three factors. What does he get, and what has he actually given up - be precise about where D's effect goes.
  3. The alternative. Describe the other way to reach eight runs while keeping all four factors, and say in one sentence what it costs.
  4. The suspicion. He specifically suspects an A-by-B interaction. Under the alternative in question 3, that interaction is confounded with another two-factor interaction. Say why that is a problem for him specifically, and what would make it acceptable.
  5. Choose, and justify it in the terms this unit uses. There is a defensible answer either way - what matters is what you declare.
  6. Write the sentence that must appear in his report, whichever he chooses.

Work it before you turn over. The point of this one is that you will almost never be given the runs you want, and the skill is choosing which ignorance you accept.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. The full design. Four factors at two levels is 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 = 16 runs. He is allowed eight, so something has to give.

2. His plan, evaluated. Dropping D gives him a clean full factorial in three factors: all three main effects, all three two-factor interactions, and the three-factor interaction, every one of them unconfounded. That is a genuinely good design. But D does not disappear. Whatever oil is in the machines during the trial is now a fixed condition, and every result he reports is true at that oil grade only. If oil grade interacts with micron rating - which is plausible, since viscosity affects loading - he will not see it and will not know he has not seen it.

3. The alternative. Run a half-fraction of the four-factor design: eight runs, all four factors retained, main effects estimable. The cost: each two-factor interaction is confounded with another two-factor interaction, so he can estimate the pairs but cannot say which member of each pair is doing the work.

4. Why that is a problem for him specifically. Because A-by-B is his hypothesis. He does not want to know that an interaction exists somewhere in a pair - he wants to know whether the finer element pays at the longer interval, which is a purchasing decision. An estimate of "A-by-B plus C-by-D" leaves him unable to answer the one question he designed the trial for. What would make it acceptable: if he can argue on engineering grounds that C-by-D is implausibly small - that breather type and oil grade have no mechanism to interact - then the pair is effectively A-by-B alone. That argument must be written down, because it is doing real work in the conclusion.

5. A defensible choice. On these facts, drop D and run the clean three-factor design. His central question is the A-by-B interaction, and the half-fraction is precisely the design that muddies it. The three-factor design answers his actual question cleanly, and oil grade can be a second trial if the first one justifies it. The other choice is defensible too - if the plant will never grant a second trial, retaining all four factors with declared confounding may be worth more than one clean answer. What is not defensible is choosing either one without saying which ignorance came with it.

6. The sentence for his report. "All results are reported at the current oil grade, which was held constant; any interaction between oil grade and the tested factors is outside this trial and was not estimated."

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Testing one factor at a time and trusting a clean-looking result. One-at-a-time testing is structurally unable to see an interaction, and it will not warn you that it missed one.

• Running the matrix in whatever order is convenient. An unrandomised run order lets anything that drifts over time - the ore, the crew, the season - masquerade as a factor effect.

• Halving the design without saying what got confounded. Every estimate in a fractional design is a sum unless you declare otherwise. Report which effects are sums, and of what.

• Treating an interaction as a statistical correction rather than a fact about the machine. An interaction you can explain physically is a finding. One you can only compute is a warning that you do not yet understand your own equipment.

What you can do now that you could not before. Fix factors at two levels and justify an experiment over the records. Build a balanced matrix and randomise the run order. Compute main effects and interactions by subtraction. Recognise an interaction as a physical fact about the machine rather than a correction term. And when you are given half the runs you need, choose which ignorance you accept - and name it in the report.

The engineer who wrote that trial report two years ago did nothing wrong. They tested carefully, one thing at a time, exactly as they had been taught.

One at a time is not a sloppy method. It is a rigorous method that is structurally unable to see the thing that cost twelve millimetres - and it will not tell you that about itself.
