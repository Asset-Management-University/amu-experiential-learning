Three weeks, two days of work

T1: The Ache

A simple repair - a job that is two days of actual work - takes three weeks to get from the fault being reported to the machine running again. Everyone is busy. Everyone is working hard. And nobody can say why it takes three weeks, because no single person has ever seen the whole process at once.

It lives in fragments: the fault report sits with one team, the approval with another, the parts with a third, the scheduling with a fourth. Each person sees only their own piece, does it as fast as they can, and passes it on. The waiting - between the pieces, in the queues, at the handoffs - is where the three weeks actually goes, and the waiting is invisible, because it belongs to no one. So the blame starts to fly: the stores are slow, the planners are slow, the crews are slow. The real culprit - a broken process that traps good people in delay - never appears, because nobody can see it.

A two-day job takes three weeks and everyone is working flat out, so where is the time actually going? How do I make an invisible process visible, and then fix the right thing?

Before you read on, decide. The job contains about two days of actual work. It takes three weeks. The storeman picks the parts the morning he gets the list. The planner writes the pack the day it reaches him. The crew do the work in two days once they have it. Everybody is working flat out and doing their own piece as fast as they can — and the blame is circulating: stores say planning sits on things, planning says stores are slow, the crews say both. Every accusation is reasonable from where the accuser stands. Nobody in this works has ever seen the whole flow at once. Write down where the time is actually going, and how you would find out — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the time that belongs to nobody

• “Speed up the slowest department.” Which one? Each is fast at its own step. You could double everybody's speed on their own piece and take perhaps a day off a three-week job, because the work was never the problem.

• “Set service-level targets for each team.” Optimises each fragment separately, which is what they are already doing. It also makes each team defend its own numbers, which will harden the blaming rather than end it.

• “Get everyone in a room to sort it out.” Closer — and with nothing in front of them it becomes the same argument with better catering, because each person can only describe their own fragment.

• “Follow the official procedure properly.” The official procedure is a tidy path nobody actually walks. Enforcing it would enforce a description of a process that does not exist.

• “Buy a better system.” Digitises the same flow, including the waits. Things would sit in electronic queues instead of physical ones, faster to see and just as long.

• Two days of work inside three weeks means roughly thirteen of the fifteen working days are waiting — in queues, at handoffs, between the pieces. And that waiting is invisible because it belongs to nobody: each person sees only their fragment, does it fast, and passes it on, so nobody owns the gaps and nobody is even measured on them. You cannot improve what you cannot see, and this process exists only in fragments inside people's heads. So draw the actual flow onto one page — the steps, the handoffs, the decisions and crucially the waits. The value is not the drawing but the shared sight: the moment four departments who each saw one piece look at the same single reality, the mystery becomes a picture with the delay sitting plainly in it — impossible to miss, and impossible to keep blaming on each other. One discipline governs it: map the process as it truly is, not as the manual says it should be, because the real one is full of workarounds, extra approvals and waits that appear in no document. Only then choose the size of the change — improve what exists where the flow is sound, or rebuild it where the shape itself is the problem.

Here is how you see it, and then improve it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you turn an invisible process into an improvement - seeing it at all, choosing the map that reveals your problem, reading the value stream, choosing the right size of change, and justifying it while blaming the process, not the person.

1. You cannot improve what you cannot see

START WITH THE REASON THE THREE WEEKS WAS A MYSTERY: A PROCESS THAT LIVES ONLY IN PEOPLE'S HEADS, IN FRAGMENTS, IS A PROCESS NO ONE CAN SEE WHOLE - AND YOU CANNOT IMPROVE WHAT YOU CANNOT SEE. Each person in the work-order chain saw only their own piece, did it as fast as they could, and passed it on; nobody had ever seen the whole flow at once, so the waiting BETWEEN the pieces - the real culprit - was invisible, belonging to no one. A PROCESS MAP FIXES EXACTLY THIS: it draws the ACTUAL flow of work - the steps, the handoffs, the decisions, and CRUCIALLY THE WAITS - onto one page, so that for the first time the whole team looks at the same single reality. THE VALUE OF THE MAP IS NOT THE DRAWING; IT IS THE SHARED SIGHT: the moment four departments who each saw only their own fragment finally see the whole, the three-week mystery becomes a picture with the waste sitting right there in it, impossible to miss and impossible to keep blaming on each other. ONE DISCIPLINE ABOVE ALL: DRAW THE PROCESS AS IT TRULY IS, NOT AS THE MANUAL SAYS IT SHOULD BE. The official flowchart shows the tidy intended path; the real process is full of workarounds, extra approvals, and waits the manual never mentions - and the GAP between the two is very often exactly where the problem lives. Map the reality, with the people who live it, and the invisible becomes visible; and once it is visible, it can be fixed. THE SINGLE HARDEST TRUTH: A PROCESS MAP MAKES THE INVISIBLE SYSTEM VISIBLE, SO YOU CAN FIX THE SYSTEM, NOT BLAME THE PEOPLE.

2. Choose the map for the problem - no single right map

HERE IS THE MISTAKE TO AVOID: THINKING THERE IS 'A' PROCESS MAP. THERE ARE FAMILIES OF MAP, EACH BUILT TO REVEAL A DIFFERENT THING, AND THE WRONG ONE SHOWS YOU THE WRONG PROBLEM. Know three, and when each is right. A HIGH-LEVEL SCOPING MAP - SUPPLIERS, INPUTS, PROCESS, OUTPUTS, CUSTOMERS - draws the BOUNDARY: what goes in, what comes out, who supplies and who receives. You draw it FIRST, always, to make everyone agree WHAT PROCESS YOU ARE EVEN TALKING ABOUT before anyone argues about the detail - it is the alignment tool, and skipping it is why teams end up mapping different processes and talking past each other. A SWIMLANE MAP puts the process into lanes, one per team or role, so WHO DOES WHAT and WHERE THE HANDOFFS ARE jump off the page - you reach for it when the problem is TANGLED OWNERSHIP, dropped balls between departments, wrong things delivered, and delay at the handoffs, because the lanes make accountability and the handoff moments unmissable. A VALUE-STREAM MAP shows WHERE THE TIME GOES - it separates the value-adding steps from the waiting and the waste, tracks the cycle times, and exposes the bottleneck - and you reach for it when the problem is that things simply TAKE TOO LONG. THE SKILL IS NOT MASTERING ONE NOTATION; IT IS CHOOSING THE MAP THAT REVEALS THE PROBLEM YOU ACTUALLY HAVE. The quick rule: scope FIRST to align, then a SWIMLANE if the pain is handoffs and ownership, or a VALUE-STREAM if the pain is time and waste. For the work-order mystery, the complaint is TIME, so the value-stream map is the one to reach for - which is the next box.

3. Read the value stream - where does the time really go?

DRAWN FOR THE WORK-ORDER PROCESS, THE VALUE-STREAM MAP IS BRUTAL AND CLARIFYING, AND IT MEASURES THE ONE THING THAT MATTERS WHEN THINGS ARE SLOW: THE RATIO OF WORK TO WAIT. You write, under each step, the actual VALUE-ADDING WORK TIME (diagnosis, repair, test) and, between the steps, the WAITING TIME (in the queue, for approval, for parts, for the next department to pick it up). The result for the work order: of the 15 WORKING DAYS the job takes end to end, ONLY ABOUT 1.5 DAYS IS ACTUAL VALUE-ADDING WORK. THE OTHER 13.5 DAYS - 90% OF THE TOTAL - IS NON-VALUE-ADDING WASTE, almost all of it WAITING. THIS IS THE SINGLE MOST COMMON AND MOST SHOCKING FINDING IN PROCESS WORK: the value-adding time is a tiny fraction of the total, and nearly all the elapsed time is WAITING, NOT WORKING - which is precisely why blaming the people doing the 1.5 days of work never speeds anything up, because they were never the delay. The map also NAMES THE BOTTLENECK - the step where the queue is longest, where the work piles up - and this matters enormously for the fix: SPEEDING UP A STEP THAT WAS NOT THE BOTTLENECK CHANGES NOTHING, because the job just waits longer at the real constraint instead. So the value-stream map does two jobs at once: it proves how little of the time is real work (which reframes the whole problem away from people's effort) and it points at the exact bottleneck where any improvement must aim. It is the manufacturing and asset-process mapping method for exactly this reason: when the complaint is time, this is the map that finds where the time is actually going.

4. Optimise or re-engineer - the right size of change

NOW THE SHARPER HALF, BECAUSE MAPPING IS NOT THE POINT - IMPROVING IS - AND ONCE YOU CAN SEE THE PROCESS THERE ARE TWO SIZES OF CHANGE, AND CHOOSING BETWEEN THEM IS THE REAL JUDGEMENT. OPTIMISE (INCREMENTAL, CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT): keep the process and make it better - remove the waste, smooth the flow, cut the worst step, attack the bottleneck. It is CHEAPER, SAFER, FASTER AND SUSTAINABLE, and it is the RIGHT choice when the process is BASICALLY SOUND AND JUST CLOGGED. For the work orders, that is exactly the case: remove two needless approval steps, pre-stage the common parts, and cut two of the four handoffs, and the FIFTEEN DAYS BECOMES ABOUT THREE - a fivefold gain from a tidy, not a rebuild, at low cost and low risk. RE-ENGINEER (RADICAL REDESIGN): throw the existing process out and redesign it from a BLANK SHEET around the OUTCOME you actually want. It is EXPENSIVE, DISRUPTIVE AND HIGH-RISK, and it is JUSTIFIED ONLY when the process is so fundamentally broken that patching it is polishing something wrong: when it GREW BY ACCIDENT into a shape no one can follow, when PERFORMANCE IS SO POOR that no amount of tidying can close the gap, or when the TECHNOLOGY HAS CHANGED so much (a paper process in a digital plant) that the old shape is obsolete. THE TWO EXPENSIVE MISTAKES ARE RE-ENGINEERING WHAT ONLY NEEDED A TIDY (enormous cost and disruption for a change you could have made incrementally) AND ENDLESSLY OPTIMISING SOMETHING THAT SHOULD HAVE BEEN TORN UP (polishing a process wrong in its bones, forever). YOU JUSTIFY RE-ENGINEERING BY THE SIZE OF THE GAP AND THE BROKENNESS OF THE DESIGN, NEVER BY AN APPETITE FOR A DRAMATIC PROJECT. Map to see; then choose the right size of change for what you saw.

5. Justify the choice - and map with people, not about them

END ON JUSTIFICATION AND DIGNITY, BECAUSE THIS IS THE HEART OF THE UNIT AND THE PLACE IT IS MOST EASILY ABUSED. FIRST, THE JUSTIFICATION: you defend the CHOICE of change on evidence - the value-stream map showed 90% waste in a sound process, so OPTIMISE; or the design is obsolete and no tidying can close the gap, so RE-ENGINEER. You justify re-engineering BY THE SIZE OF THE GAP AND THE BROKENNESS OF THE DESIGN, not by how exciting a big project sounds. NOW THE DIGNITY, AND IT IS NON-NEGOTIABLE. A PROCESS MAP DIAGNOSES THE SYSTEM, NOT THE PEOPLE. IT MAKES INVISIBLE DELAYS AND BROKEN HANDOFFS VISIBLE, SO YOU CAN FIX THE PROCESS, NOT BLAME THE PERSON CAUGHT IN IT. The three-week job was not caused by slow stores or slow planners; it was caused by a DESIGN full of approvals, queues and handoffs, and the people were running as fast as the design allowed - a map that becomes a stick to expose who is slow has been turned exactly backwards, because it exists to move the blame OFF the person and ONTO the system, where it belongs and where it can be fixed. TWO MORE TRUTHS RIDE WITH THIS. RE-ENGINEERING IS A JUDGEMENT ON THE PROCESS'S DESIGN, NOT THE PEOPLE'S ADEQUACY - IT IS THE RECOGNITION THAT A PROCESS HAS BEEN OUTGROWN, NOT THAT THE PEOPLE WHO RUN IT ARE DEFICIENT; the people who ran the old process well will run the new one well. AND YOU MAP WITH THE PEOPLE, NOT ABOUT THEM: the people who do the work are the experts in how it is REALLY done, workarounds and all, so mapping is a COLLABORATIVE ACT OF DISCOVERY with them at the table, never an external audit performed upon them. Get the size of change right, justify it on the evidence, and keep the blame on the design - and you have done the whole of process improvement as it should be done.

T3: The Blueprint

A process map makes the invisible system visible, so you can fix the system, not blame the people. Map to see. See to choose. Choose the right map for the problem, and the right size of change for the process. Five questions:

  • Can I see the process at all? A process map makes the invisible flow visible - the steps, handoffs, decisions and waits - so the whole team sees one reality instead of fragments.
  • Which map reveals my problem? Scope map for the boundary; swimlane for who and the handoffs; value-stream for where the time goes. The wrong map shows the wrong problem.
  • Where does the time go? The value-stream map - value-adding steps against the waiting and the waste, the cycle times, the bottleneck. Drawn when things take too long.
  • How big a change? Optimise, or re-engineer. Keep and improve a sound process; blank-sheet redesign a broken one. Most need the first; a few need the second.
  • How do I justify it, and to whom? Justify by the gap, not the drama - and map with the people, not about them. The map diagnoses the system, not the person.

You cannot improve what you cannot see. Start with the reason the three weeks was a mystery: a process that lives only in people's heads, in fragments, is a process no one can see whole - and you cannot improve what you cannot see. A process map fixes exactly this: it draws the actual flow of work - the steps, the handoffs, the decisions, and crucially the waits - onto one page, so that for the first time the whole team looks at the same reality. The value of the map is not the drawing; it is the shared sight: the moment four departments who each saw only their own piece finally see the whole, the three weeks stops being a mystery and becomes a picture with the waste right there in it. Draw it as the process truly is, not as the manual says it should be - the gap between the two is often where the problem lives.

Choose the map for the problem - there is no single "right" map. Here is the mistake to avoid: thinking there is a process map. There are families of map, each built to reveal a different thing, and the wrong one shows you the wrong problem. A high-level scoping map - suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers - draws the boundary: what goes in, what comes out, who supplies and who receives. You draw it first, always, to make everyone agree what process you are even talking about, before anyone argues about the detail. A swimlane map puts the process into lanes, one per team or role, so who does what and where the handoffs are jump off the page - you draw it when the problem is tangled ownership, dropped balls between departments, and delay at the handoffs. A value-stream map shows where the time goes - it separates the value-adding steps from the waiting and the waste, tracks the cycle times, and exposes the bottleneck - and you draw it when the problem is that things simply take too long. The skill is not mastering one notation; it is choosing the map that reveals the problem you actually have: scope first, then handoffs or time, depending on what hurts. For the work-order mystery, the complaint is time - so the value-stream map is the one to reach for.

Read the value stream - where does the time really go? Drawn for the work-order process, the value-stream map is brutal and clarifying. Of the 15 working days the job takes end to end, only about 1.5 days is actual value-adding work - the diagnosis, the repair, the test. The other 13.5 days - 90% of the total - is non-value-adding waste: the job sitting in a queue waiting for approval, waiting for parts, waiting for a slot, waiting for the next department to pick it up. This is the single most common and most shocking finding in process work: the value-adding time is a tiny fraction of the total, and almost all of the elapsed time is waiting, not working. It also names the bottleneck - the step where the queue is longest - which is where any improvement must aim, because speeding up a step that was not the bottleneck changes nothing. The value-stream map is the manufacturing and asset-process mapping method precisely because it measures the thing that matters when things are slow: the ratio of work to wait.

Optimise or re-engineer - the right size of change. Now the sharper half, because mapping is not the point - improving is - and once you can see the process there are two sizes of change, and choosing between them is the real judgement. Optimise (incremental, continuous improvement): keep the process and make it better - remove the waste, smooth the flow, cut the worst step, attack the bottleneck. It is cheaper, safer, faster and sustainable, and it is the right choice when the process is basically sound and just clogged. For the work orders, that is exactly the case: remove two needless approval steps, pre-stage the common parts, and cut two of the four handoffs, and the fifteen days becomes about three - a fivefold gain from a tidy, not a rebuild. It is expensive, disruptive and high-risk, and it is justified only when the process is so fundamentally broken that patching it is polishing something wrong - when it grew by accident into a shape no one can follow, when performance is so poor that no amount of tidying can close the gap, or when the technology has changed so much (a paper process in a digital plant) that the old shape is obsolete. The two expensive mistakes are re-engineering what only needed a tidy (enormous cost and disruption for a change you could have made incrementally) and endlessly optimising something that should have been torn up (polishing a process wrong in its bones). You justify re-engineering by the size of the gap and the brokenness of the design, never by an appetite for a dramatic project.

A process map diagnoses the system, not the people. It makes invisible delays and broken handoffs visible, so you can fix the process, not blame the person caught in it. The three-week job was not caused by slow stores or slow planners; it was caused by a design full of approvals, queues and handoffs, and the people were running as fast as the design allowed. A map that becomes a stick to expose who is slow has been turned exactly backwards - it exists to move the blame off the person and onto the system, where it belongs and where it can be fixed. Two more truths ride with this. Re-engineering is a judgement on the process's design, not the people's adequacy - it is the recognition that a process has been outgrown, not that the people who run it are deficient; the people who ran the old process well will run the new one well. And you map with the people, not about them: the people who do the work are the experts in how it is really done, so mapping is a collaborative act of discovery with them at the table, never an external audit performed upon them.

Does it scale? A person who feels permanently overwhelmed can map their own week - the actual flow of a day - and almost always finds the same thing the work-order map found: a small amount of real work drowning in waiting, switching, and small frictions nobody had ever made visible. And they face the same choice: tidy the week (optimise) or rethink the whole shape of it (re-engineer). Seeing the invisible, then choosing the right size of change, is how you improve a plant and a life alike.

T4: Your Turn

Here are two process problems at the works.

Problem A: issuing new safety equipment is tangled across three departments and often ends with the wrong gear delivered.

Problem B (a different process): a value-stream map of the spares-issue process shows a total lead time of 10 days, of which 1 day is value-adding work; the single longest wait is 5 days sitting in the approval queue.

  1. Choose the map. For Problem A (tangled ownership, wrong-gear errors), would you start with a value-stream map or a swimlane map? Justify in one sentence.
  2. Read the map. For Problem B, what percentage of the 10 days is non-value-adding waste, and what is the biggest single source of it?
  3. Choose the change. Suppose Problem A's equipment-issuing process turns out to be built around a paper catalogue from ten years ago and makes no sense in a digital works. Optimise, or re-engineer? Justify against this unit's principles.
  4. Honour the person. A manager says, "the delays are because the stores team is slow." Rephrase that as a system failure, not a people failure.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the map: a swimlane map. The problem is tangled ownership and handoff errors (the wrong gear), and the swimlane's lanes-per-department make exactly that visible - who does what, where the handoffs are, and where the ball is being dropped. A value-stream map answers "where does the time go?", which is not the question here.

Second, the reading: of the 10 days, 9 days are non-value-adding waste - 90% (only 1 day is value-adding). The biggest single source is the 5-day approval queue, which alone is half the total lead time and the obvious first target.

Third, the change: re-engineer. A process built around a ten-year-old paper catalogue in a digital works is not a sound process that needs tidying - it is fundamentally obsolete, its very shape defeated by a technology change, and optimising it would be polishing something wrong in its bones. This is one of the genuine cases the unit names for radical redesign: not appetite for drama, but a design that has been outgrown. (Note: it is the design that is obsolete, not the people.)

Fourth, the rephrase: something like "The equipment-issue process traps the stores team in a design full of handoffs and a slow approval queue, so good people are delivering slowly because the system makes fast delivery almost impossible." The delay is in the process, not the effort - and naming it that way is both more accurate and the only framing that leads to a fix instead of a scapegoat.

T5: The Guardrails

When a job that is two days of work takes three weeks, the fault is almost never in the people doing the two days - it is in the thirteen days of waiting nobody could see.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Never mapping. A process that lives in fragments keeps its waste invisible, and invisible waste never gets fixed. Draw the whole flow onto one page first.

• Choosing the wrong map. A value-stream map answers "where does the time go?"; a swimlane answers "who does what?". The wrong map reveals the wrong problem. Match the map to the complaint.

• Skipping the scope. Without agreeing the boundary first, people map different processes and argue past each other. Draw the high-level scope map before the detail.

• Mapping the process as it should be. The official flow hides the workarounds and the real waits. Map it as it truly is, with the people who actually do it.

• Re-engineering what needed a tidy. A blank-sheet rebuild of a basically-sound process is huge cost and risk for a change you could have made incrementally. Optimise unless the design is genuinely broken.

• Optimising what should be torn up. Endlessly polishing a process that is obsolete in its bones never fixes it. When the design has been outgrown, re-engineer.

• Blaming the slow person. The delay is in the design - the approvals, the handoffs, the queues - not the effort. A map diagnoses the system, not the person caught in it.

• Mapping about people, not with them. The people who do the work know where it really breaks. Mapping is a collaborative discovery, not an audit performed upon them.

A two-day job took three weeks and everyone was blamed, because the process lived in fragments and no one could see the thirteen days of waiting hidden in the handoffs. So see it first: a process map makes the invisible flow visible so the whole team shares one reality; choose the map that reveals your problem - scope to agree the boundary, swimlane for who and the handoffs, value-stream for where the time goes; read the value stream and find that the value-adding work is a fraction of the total and almost all the elapsed time is waiting at a bottleneck; and then choose the right size of change - optimise a sound-but-clogged process to remove the waste, or re-engineer one whose design has been fundamentally outgrown, justifying the choice by the size of the gap and the brokenness of the design, not by appetite for a dramatic project. Map to see, then choose the right size of change - and remember always that a process map diagnoses the system, not the people, so you fix the design and never blame the person the design has trapped. The next time a simple job takes far too long, do not ask who is slow - draw the flow, find the waiting, and fix the process that was slowing everyone down.
