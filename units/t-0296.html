There is a 6 mm crack in a 25 mm plate and I have to say whether it waits fourteen months - how do I turn a crack size into a date I can defend?

T1: The Ache

It is the second day of a shutdown at Amandla Colliery and you are standing under the CV-04 transfer chute with a torch, looking at a crack.

The inspector found it an hour ago at the toe of the weld where a support bracket joins the chute frame. He has sized it: 6 mm deep, in a 25 mm plate. It was not there at the last inspection, or nobody saw it.

The plant comes back on line on Thursday. Repairing this properly means grinding out the weld, re-welding, re-inspecting - a three-day outage that would have to be taken separately, at about R2.1 million of lost production, because the shutdown crew and the coded welder needed are already committed elsewhere this week. The next planned shutdown is in fourteen months.

So the question you are being asked, standing there with a torch, is whether that crack can wait fourteen months.

And every way you have of answering it is a feeling. It is only 6 mm of a 25 mm plate - three-quarters of the wall is sound, and that sounds like a lot of margin. It is also true that the crack was not there before, that it has been growing for a while under a conveyor that starts and stops all day, and that a crack does not politely grow at the rate it grew last year. The engineering manager, Palesa Mokoena, is not going to accept "it looks all right to me" and she is not going to accept "let us not risk it" either - the first is a guess and the second costs R2.1 million to avoid a risk nobody has measured.

You have a crack with a size. You have a conveyor with a duty. What you do not have is any way to turn those into a date.

There is a 6 mm crack in a 25 mm plate and I have to say whether it waits fourteen months. How do I turn a crack size into a date I can defend?

Before you read on, decide. A 6 mm crack, a 25 mm plate, a three-day outage worth R2.1 million, and a planned shutdown fourteen months away. Write down how you would decide - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does with fourteen months

• "Six of twenty-five is under a third - there is plenty of wall left." The reassuring arithmetic, and it treats the remaining wall as the answer when the question is how fast the crack crosses it. A crack that has taken years to reach 6 mm does not take the same years again to reach 12.

• "It was not there last inspection, so it grew that much in eighteen months - it has fourteen months of margin." The most defensible-sounding of the five and it assumes the growth rate is constant. It is not: the rate depends on the crack's own depth, so the next millimetre always goes faster than the last one.

• "Repair it now - it is a crack in a structure, you do not gamble." Nobody will ever criticise you for it, and it spends R2.1 million to avoid a risk that has not been measured, which is the same decision quality as ignoring it, pointing the other way.

• "Grind it out and see how deep it really is." A genuinely good instinct, because the crack size is the input everything else depends on - and on Thursday you have neither the time nor the welder to close what you open.

• "Put a monitor on it and watch it." Sensible alongside a decision and not a decision itself: watching tells you it is growing, which you already know, and gives you no date.

• What the first five miss is that the growth rate is a function of the crack's current depth, so it accelerates - and that means it can be calculated forward. Given the depth, the stress swing and the number of swings a year, you can compute how fast the crack is growing right now, how much faster it grows as it deepens, and therefore how many cycles it has left before it reaches a depth you will not tolerate. That is a date. It is also, and this is the part that decides the answer here, extremely sensitive to how well the inspector sized that 6 mm.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the calculation, run under the chute with the numbers off the inspection report.

Step 1 - Fix the four inputs, and be honest about which one you trust least. Crack depth now, a = 6 mm. The depth you will not tolerate: the assessment takes 12.5 mm, half the 25 mm wall, as the limit at which the remaining section can no longer be relied on. The stress range at the weld toe, from the strain gauges the reliability team fitted last year: 85 MPa. And the number of damaging swings a year: the conveyor starts, loads, and stops around 50,000 times a year. Palesa asks which number he is least sure of, and the answer is the first one - the inspector sized the crack at 6 mm and would not argue with 3 mm or 9 mm.

Step 2 - Work out how hard the crack tip is being driven, right now. A crack grows because each stress swing tugs at its tip, and how hard depends on the stress range, the crack's depth, and its shape. That combination has a value you can compute - written ΔK - and here it is 1.12 x 85 x the square root of (π x 0.006 m), which comes to 13.1 in MPa-root-metre. On its own that number means nothing. Its whole purpose is to go into the next step.

Step 3 - Convert that into millimetres per cycle - and check your units twice. The growth law says the crack grows by a constant times ΔK cubed, each cycle. The published constant for this steel in air is 3 x 10-13, and here is where careers get embarrassed: that constant is quoted for ΔK in N/mm1.5, not in MPa-root-metre. The two differ by a factor of 31.6, and because the law cubes it, using the wrong one puts the answer out by about thirty thousand times. Converting properly, 13.1 MPa-root-metre is 413 N/mm1.5, and the growth rate comes to 2.1 x 10-5 mm per cycle - about a fiftieth of a millimetre every thousand swings.

Step 4 - Grow it forward, and watch it speed up. You cannot multiply that rate by the remaining 6.5 mm, because the rate is not constant: at 12.5 mm the same calculation gives 6.4 x 10-5 mm per cycle - three times faster. So you step the crack forward in small increments, recomputing the rate at each depth, and add up the cycles. From 6 mm to 12.5 mm comes to 174,000 cycles. At 50,000 a year, that is about 3.5 years - 42 months.

And notice where those cycles go: half of them are used up crossing the first 2.4 mm, from 6 to 8.4. The second half of the journey is nearly twice the distance and takes the same time. The crack is at its most predictable now and its least predictable later.

Step 5 - Test the input you trust least, before you give anybody a date. The inspector said 6 mm and would not argue with 3 or 9. So run all three:

If the crack is really...Cycles to 12.5 mmTime at 50,000 a yearAgainst a 14-month wait
3 mm409,0008 years (98 months)comfortable
6 mm (as sized)174,0003.5 years (42 months)three times the wait
9 mm70,0001.4 years (17 months)three months of margin

There is the answer, and it is not the one the calculation alone would have given. On the sized crack the chute waits fourteen months easily. But the honest range of that measurement runs from eight years of margin down to three months, and three months is not a margin - it is the difference between the inspector's ruler and yours. So what goes into Palesa's report is not "it will last 42 months". It is: defer the repair to the planned shutdown, and re-size this crack in six months with a method that can be trusted to a millimetre. If it comes back at 7 mm, the plan holds. If it comes back at 9, the outage is taken early and R2.1 million is well spent. The calculation did not make the decision - it converted a feeling into a date and then told you which measurement the date rests on.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the last one is what turns the number into advice.

  • 1. Fix the four inputs: the crack depth now, the depth you will not tolerate, the stress range at the crack, and the cycles a year. Say which you trust least.
  • 2. Compute the driving force at the current depth - the crack-tip quantity that combines stress, depth and shape.
  • 3. Convert it to growth per cycle, with the constants in the units they were published in.
  • 4. Step it forward in small increments, recomputing at each depth, and total the cycles.
  • 5. Re-run for the range of the input you trust least, and give a re-inspection date rather than a life.

The method is fatigue crack-growth assessment, and the growth law is the Paris law: the growth per cycle rises with the cube of the crack-tip driving force, which is written ΔK - the stress-intensity-factor range, the amount by which each stress swing tugs at the crack tip. The single fact that makes the whole thing work, and the one worth carrying away, is that ΔK depends on the crack's own depth. That is why a crack accelerates without anything about the loading changing, why last year's growth rate is not next year's, and why you must step the calculation forward rather than multiply.

It answers a question the fatigue curves cannot. An S-N fatigue life assumes a sound weld with no known flaw; the moment an inspection finds a crack, that calculation no longer applies and this one takes over. The two are not competing estimates - they are answers to different situations, and the inspection report decides which situation you are in.

Three limits, and the first is the one this case turned on. The answer is dominated by the crack size, because ΔK goes with the square root of depth and the rate with its cube - so the growth rate rises with depth to the power of about one and a half, and a crack sized 50 per cent too small gives an answer roughly twice too long. Sizing accuracy is not a detail of the inspection; it is the accuracy of your answer. Second, the constants are unit-specific and the units are a genuine trap: the same published constant means two things thirty thousand times apart depending on whether ΔK is in N/mm1.5 or MPa-root-metre, and the wrong choice produces a perfectly plausible-looking number. Write the units beside every constant and check the growth rate against something physical - a rate of 10⁻⁹ mm per cycle means the crack effectively never grows, which for a crack somebody just found is a result to disbelieve. Third, this is air and ordinary temperature; a corrosive environment or high temperature changes the constants and sometimes the mechanism.

Two neighbouring questions sit just beyond this one. Whether a crack that finally goes through the wall will leak detectably before it runs - which is what makes a 12.5 mm limit a sensible place to stop rather than an arbitrary one. And what happens when the load history is a real spectrum rather than one repeated swing, where the same cycles in a different order give a different life.

T4: Your Turn

Work each one before opening its answer.

Task 1 - Why not just multiply? A colleague says: "The rate is 2.1 x 10⁻⁵ mm per cycle and we have 6.5 mm to go, so that is 310,000 cycles - call it six years." What is wrong with that, and is the error in the safe or the unsafe direction?

What good looks like

It uses the rate at the current depth for the whole journey, and the rate rises as the crack deepens - three times faster by 12.5 mm. The true answer, stepping forward, is 174,000 cycles, not 310,000. The error is in the unsafe direction: it nearly doubles the life you report. Good answers say why the rate rises - ΔK grows with the square root of depth and the rate with its cube - and note that the mistake always overstates life, never understates it.

Task 2 - The units trap. An engineer computes ΔK = 15 MPa-root-metre, takes the published constant 3 x 10⁻¹³ with m = 3, and reports a growth rate of 1.0 x 10⁻⁹ mm per cycle and a remaining life of "several thousand years". What has gone wrong, and what is the first thing that should have made them suspicious?

What good looks like

The constant is published for ΔK in N/mm1.5; they used MPa-root-metre, and cubing a factor of 31.6 puts the answer out by about 31,600 times. Correctly: 15 MPa-root-metre is 474 N/mm1.5, giving 3.2 x 10⁻⁵ mm per cycle. But the number that should have stopped them is the answer, not the arithmetic: a crack somebody has just found, in a structure that cycles, does not have a life of several thousand years. Good answers say that a result which means "this crack effectively never grows" is a result to disbelieve and re-derive.

Task 3 - Which measurement buys the most? You have money for exactly one thing: a better crack-sizing technique (halving the sizing uncertainty), or a strain survey to pin the stress range down to within 5 per cent. The current answer is 42 months and the sizing uncertainty spans 17 to 98 months. Which do you buy, and why?

What good looks like

Buy the crack sizing. The decision currently spans 17 to 98 months on that one input alone, and 17 months against a 14-month wait is not a margin. A 5 per cent improvement in the stress range moves the life by roughly 15 per cent, which changes nothing about the decision - the answer is still "wait" at every value in that band. Good answers name the test: an input is worth measuring better only when the range it currently spans crosses the decision, and here only one of the two does.

Now take a real crack of your own - or a component you are being asked to run on with a known flaw - and work the five moves.

1. Crack depth now: ___ . Depth you will not tolerate, and where that limit came from: ___ . Stress range at the crack: ___ . Cycles a year: ___ . Which do you trust least: ___ .

2. The driving force at the current depth, with your shape factor and the square root of the depth in metres.

3. The growth rate per cycle - and write the units of your constant next to it, in the units it was published in. Then sanity-check: does this rate mean the crack grows a plausible amount in a year?

4. Step it forward in increments of a few tenths of a millimetre, recomputing the rate each time, and total the cycles. Divide by your cycles a year.

5. Now re-run steps 2 to 4 at the top and bottom of your least-trusted input. Does the decision change anywhere in that range? If it does, your output is a re-inspection date, not a life - and say what would have to come back for the plan to change.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the date honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The current rate multiplied by the distance left. The commonest error and always in the unsafe direction, because the rate rises as the crack deepens. Step it forward and recompute at each depth.

• The constant used in the wrong units. The same published number means two things about thirty thousand times apart depending on whether ΔK is in N/mm1.5 or MPa-root-metre. Write the units beside the constant every single time.

• A growth rate nobody sanity-checked. Before trusting the life, ask what the rate means in a year. If it says a crack somebody just found effectively never grows, the arithmetic is wrong somewhere, however tidy each line looks.

• The crack size taken as exact. It is the input the answer is most sensitive to, and inspectors will tell you their uncertainty if asked. Run the top and bottom of the range; if the decision changes inside it, you have not finished.

• Last year's growth used as next year's. "It grew 3 mm in eighteen months so we have eighteen months" assumes a constant rate, which is the one thing this method exists to say is untrue.

• An S-N life quoted for a component with a known crack. Those curves assume a sound weld. Once a crack is found, that number does not apply to this component any more, however carefully it was computed.

• A life reported instead of a re-inspection date. The output people can act on is when to look again and what would change the plan. A single number invites somebody to run to it.

• The tolerable depth chosen by habit. Half the wall is a common convention, not a law. Say where your limit came from, because everything before it is scaled to it.

You stood under that chute with a torch, a 6 mm crack and a R2.1 million question, and no way to turn a depth into a date. You can leave with one: 174,000 cycles from 6 mm to 12.5 mm, about 42 months at this conveyor's duty - and with the more useful half of the answer, which is that the same calculation gives 98 months if the crack is really 3 mm and 17 months if it is really 9. The crack accelerates as it deepens, and your date is only as good as the millimetre somebody measured. So the report says defer to the planned shutdown, re-size in six months, and here is what would change the plan - which is a decision Palesa can sign, rather than a number she has to trust.
