The customer I can't afford to lose

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and on paper Tank You is thriving - because of the Baraka Kitchen contract. It was the deal that changed everything, your proudest win, and over the years it has grown into the biggest part of everything you earn.

Which is exactly why your stomach is in a knot. Lately Baraka pays later and later - sixty days, then ninety. Last month they asked for a "loyalty discount," and you gave it. This week their manager mentioned, lightly, that "a couple of other suppliers have been in touch." And you said nothing, smiled, and absorbed it - because the one thing you cannot do is lose them.

And there it is, the trap you built with your own success: Baraka keeps Tank You alive, and because they keep you alive, they can ask for anything, and you must say yes. They have quietly become your boss. Worse - lie awake and feel it - if Baraka walked away tomorrow, more than half your income would vanish overnight. Wages, the loan on the bakkies, all of it. You are one phone call from collapse, and they know it.

My biggest customer keeps me alive - and that's exactly the trap; they know it, so I can't refuse them anything. How do I stop one customer from holding my whole business hostage?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Three answers come to mind and two are dangerous. Keep appeasing them? That just teaches them they can push harder. Confront them, or threaten to walk? Madness - they're half your income; you'd be cutting your own throat. Hope they stay? Hope is not a plan. There's a fourth way, and you've actually met this exact problem before - just smaller.

Before you read on: think back to when ALL your income came from one place, and what you did about it. The danger is the same shape now, one level up. So what is the move - and notice you can't use the dangerous two (fight or shrink the big customer)?

I keep giving Baraka whatever they ask - discounts, late payment - because I can't lose them. But that's the whole problem: because I can't say no, they can say anything. My biggest customer has quietly become my boss.
I can't safely shrink them or fight them - they're half my income. The only safe move is to make them a SMALLER slice of a BIGGER pie: go and win other customers, so no single one can sink me.
And I must do it NOW, while Baraka's money still funds the effort - not after they leave. Win enough others that no one customer is more than about a third, and I get back both my safety and my power to say no.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

This is the same lesson you once learned about your own income - never let everything hang on one thread - but now at the level of your whole business. And it has a sharper edge: a single big customer doesn't just threaten to leave, they squeeze you while they stay. This is the crucial difference - it isn't only the risk of them one day leaving; it's the slow erosion of your power, your profit and your dignity for as long as they stay.

  • The big customer is a lifeline AND a captor: when one customer is most of your income, two dangers grow together - and they feed each other.
  • Danger 1 - they hold the power: they know you can't afford to lose them, so they can push: late payment, discounts, demands. You can't say no - your biggest customer has quietly become your boss.
  • Danger 2 - they hold your survival: if they leave or fail, most of your income vanishes overnight - payroll and all. You are one phone call from collapse.
  • The only safe fix: GROW the others (don't confront, don't beg): you can't safely shrink them or fight them. You grow AROUND them - deliberately win several smaller customers, NOW, while their money still funds it - until no single one is more than about a quarter to a third of your income.
  • Do it BEFORE the storm - and win back two things: build the lifeboats while the sun shines. Diversifying gives you SAFETY (you survive any one leaving) AND POWER (you can finally negotiate as an equal - even walk away).

The key is that you do not fix this by attacking the problem customer - you fix it by growing past them. You make Baraka a smaller slice of a bigger pie. And the timing is everything: you must do it now, while Baraka's money is still flowing and can fund the hunt for others. The worst time to go looking for new customers is the day your biggest one leaves; the best time is today, when you don't yet have to.

And be fair as you do it: Baraka aren't villains - they're a customer using the strength they have, exactly as any business would. You aren't foolish either - landing them was a real triumph. The danger simply crept in quietly while you were busy being grateful. This is about building a stronger shape, not blaming anyone.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad and Ibraaheem work out the one number that had been hiding the danger - and then go to work on it.

See the number that changes everything

The single riskiest number: Baraka = about 55% of Tank You's income. THAT is why Muhammad can't say no to the discounts and the late payments - over half his world depends on one handshake.

The move (over several months): Muhammad puts real effort into winning smaller customers - the new housing development, a few corner shops, two restaurants. He doesn't drop Baraka or cut what they buy by a single cylinder. He tasks Sipho with keeping the new delivery routes to the development and the restaurants running smoothly, so service never slips anywhere while they grow.

The shift: the total income GROWS, and within that bigger total, Baraka is now about 30%. Notice: Baraka is buying exactly as much as before in rands - it's everything ELSE that grew around them.

What that 30% buys back: when the late-payment talk comes again, Muhammad can hold his terms calmly - he no longer NEEDS to fold. And if Baraka ever did walk, Tank You would have a hard month, not a funeral. Same business, completely different power - because of one number going from 55 to 30.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Your greatest success had quietly become your greatest risk - and the cure wasn't to tear it down, but to outgrow your dependence on it. This applies anywhere too much rests on one thing:

  1. Find the riskiest number. What share of your income (or hours, or sales) comes from your single biggest customer, client, or source? Divide that one by your total. That percentage is the danger.
  2. If it's too high (more than about a third), grow the others - now. Not by fighting or dropping the big one, but by deliberately winning several smaller ones while you still have the breathing room.
  3. Build before the storm. The time to reduce a dependence is while it's still comfortable - never wait for the loss to force it.
R

Work out your biggest one's share this week, and if it's too high, name three smaller customers to chase. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

A big customer can feel like you've arrived. But a business that lives or dies on one relationship hasn't arrived - it's been captured. Real strength, and real freedom, is serving many, so that no single one can ever own you.
