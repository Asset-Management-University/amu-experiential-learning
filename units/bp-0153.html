I did everything right and had a backup - so how was it dead at the one moment I needed it, and how can I ever trust a backup again?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Imran, and you run a cold-storage business - the chilly warehouse where local restaurants keep their expensive meat and fish. Your promise to them is simple: your freezers will always be freezing. You know the biggest threat is a power cut, so you were careful and did the responsible thing - you spent good money on a big backup generator that sits in the yard, a metal box of reassurance. If the main power ever goes, you fire it up and keep your promise.

Then one Tuesday a storm rolls in, the lights flicker and die, and the freezers fall silent. This is the moment you planned for. You grab the keys, run out into the rain, and turn the key - nothing. Again - a sad click; the engine won't even turn over. It's sat there looking perfectly fine for over a year, but it's stone dead: a flat starter battery. By the time the power returns next day, the stock is ruined and your promise is broken.

I had a backup - I did everything right - so how was it dead at the one moment I needed it, and how can I ever trust a backup again?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is money: "I'll buy a newer, better generator!" - but if you treat it the same way, letting it sit untouched, you've just bought a more expensive dead-thing-in-waiting. So you think, "I'll check it more often" - but what did checking mean? You glanced at it weekly; it looked fine (not leaking, not on fire) the whole time it was silently broken. Looking at a backup isn't the same as testing it. So then, "I'll buy a backup for my backup - a second generator!" - but what if the stored fuel for both had gone stale, or both were wired through the same switch that blew? You wouldn't have two layers of safety; you'd have two dead backups, killed by the same single cause.

A backup does nothing at all until the bad day. So before you read on: what would actually tell you, in advance, whether it will work - and could one single thing kill your "two"?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The betrayal comes from a hidden truth: some things break silently. A backup generator, a fire extinguisher, a spare tyre - their job is to do nothing, most of the time - so when one fails there's no alarm, no sign, just a dormant failure waiting for the worst moment. This disaster needs two things at once: the main thing fails AND the backup is already broken - and you can't stop the storm, but you can stop the backup being broken when it arrives. Five moves:

  • See the hidden failure: some things exist only to act WHEN something else fails - a backup, a spare, a safety cut-off. Because they do nothing most of the time, when one of them breaks there's no alarm, no stopped work, no sign at all. The failure is invisible and dormant - it can sit dead for months and you'd never know, waiting for the worst possible moment to be discovered.
  • The real risk is how long a dead backup hides: this disaster needs TWO things at once: the main thing fails (the power cut) AND the backup is already broken. You can't stop the storm - but you CAN stop the backup from being broken when the storm arrives. The danger isn't really how often the bad day comes; it's how long a broken backup is allowed to sit undetected. Your job is to shrink that hiding time.
  • Looking isn't testing - make it do its job: you can't know a parachute works by looking at it in its bag, and you can't know a generator works by glancing at it in the yard - it looked perfectly fine while it was dead. The only test that proves a dormant thing works is to actually RUN it under real load - start it, put the freezers on it, watch it carry the work for a real spell - then set it back ready.
  • Test on a schedule set by the risk: how often? It depends on how bad the failure would be. For a cold store where a failure means thousands of rand of ruined stock and broken promises, you might run the generator under load once a month; a home smoke alarm, twice a year. The schedule's whole job is to find a silent failure long before the day you actually need it - test often enough that a dead backup can't hide for long.
  • Two that share one cause aren't two: real safety needs INDEPENDENCE. If your main and your backup can both be killed by the same single thing - the same fuel that went stale, the same fuse that blew, the same drive, the same supplier, the same flood - you don't have two protections, you have one wearing a costume of two, and a third copy on that same cause changes nothing. Find the thing they both rely on and break that shared weakness.

See how it saves Imran next time. He can't stop the power cuts - but he can stop a dead generator from hiding: once a month he actually runs it under real load, putting the freezers on it for a spell and watching it carry the work, so a flat battery or stale fuel is caught in the yard on a calm day, not in the storm. And he hunts the shared weakness: he finds the main supply and the generator both feed through one old switch - so "two" was really one - and gives the generator its own independent line. A backup you don't test is a promise you haven't kept: looking isn't testing - make it do its job; the real risk is how long a dead backup is allowed to hide; and two things that die from the same single cause were never really two. A real backup isn't an object you own - it's a capability you've proven.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Silent backups hide everywhere, not just in a yard. Watch two everyday ones.

See the same trap in a smoke alarm and a backup file

The smoke alarm sits on the ceiling looking fine - but if its battery is dead, it has failed silently; it's a useless piece of plastic. The only way to know it works is to press the test button and make it do its job - scream. That's exactly why we're told to test them, not just look up at them.

The backup file: you're careful, so you copy your important files - but you save the copy in another folder on the same hard drive. The day that drive dies, you lose the original AND the backup: they shared one single, fatal weakness, so you thought you had two and really had one. The fix both times: prove it works, and make your two independent.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at the safety nets in your own life and work - the backup power, the spare equipment, the emergency contact list, the second source of income. When did you last not just look at it, but actually make it do its job?

  1. Prove it, don't assume it. Have you ever started the generator under load, restored the backup file to check it opens, pumped up the spare tyre? A backup is a capability you've tested, not an object you own.
  2. Set a schedule so a dead one can't hide. Put a recurring date in the calendar to test each safety net - often enough for how badly its failure would hurt - so a silent failure is found on a calm day, not the worst one.
  3. Hunt the shared cause. Ask: what single thing could kill BOTH my main and my backup? Same power, same fuel, same location, same supplier, same password. Find that one weakness and break it - give them different sources, places, or hands.

Having a backup at all was the responsible, caring thing - it was never the mistake; the only mistake was trusting it without proof. A protection you never exercise quietly rots, and two protections that lean on one thing are secretly one - and both betray you at the very moment someone is counting on you most. So make your safety real: prove it works, keep proving it, and make sure no single blow can take both your first line and your last. That is how a piece of metal in the yard becomes a true promise - the promise that, whatever the day throws at you, you can keep serving the people who trust you to be there.
