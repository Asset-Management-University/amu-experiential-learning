Step 1 of 5

Our compressed air is costing a fortune and the machines say they are loaded all day, but where is the air going? I cannot hear the leaks over the compressor roar, and the early electrical arcs in the switchroom are silent to me too. How do I find faults that make a sound too high and too soft for my ears?

T1: The Ache

You are walking the compressed-air line at a cement works, a clipboard in one hand and the monthly electricity bill folded in your pocket. The air compressors run almost flat out, day and night, to clean the bag filters and push material through the pneumatic lines. The compressor room is loud - a steady roar that fills the whole building, so loud you cannot hear anything small over it.

And small is exactly the problem. The plant engineer stopped you this morning, holding the bill: "Our compressed air is costing us a fortune. The machines say they are loaded most of the day, but we are not making more product. Where is the air going?" You both know the answer in general terms: leaks. A compressed-air system this size usually leaks 20% to 30% of everything it makes, straight into the air, doing no work. Here that is roughly R18,000 a month of electricity blowing out through holes you cannot see and cannot hear - a pinhole leak in a fitting three metres up hisses far too high and far too soft for your ears over that roar.

And the air system is only half of it. In the medium-voltage switchroom next door, an early electrical fault - a tiny arc, surface tracking across a dirty insulator - gives off the same kind of faint, high sound long before it shows up as heat or trips anything. You cannot hear that either, and you do not want to be standing in front of that panel when it finally lets go. Your hands and ears simply cannot find these faults.

I need to hear what is too high and too soft for human ears - to walk the plant, point at a fitting or a panel, and know whether it is leaking or arcing before it costs money or hurts someone.

Before you read on, decide. Compressed air is costing a fortune, the machines say they are loaded and no more product is coming out, and next door there may be an early fault singing away in a switchroom you would rather not stand in front of. Your ears cannot find either. Write down how you would go looking. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one can find

• “Walk the plant on a Sunday when it is quiet and listen.” Genuinely useful and it has a hard ceiling: a pinhole leak makes most of its noise above what a human ear can hear at all, silence or no silence. You will find the big obvious ones, which are rarely where the money is.

• “Soap-solution every joint.” It works, one fitting at a time, on the fittings you can reach. On a system this size that is weeks of work, and the leak three metres up in a roof space will still be leaking when you finish.

• “Use the thermal camera — it finds electrical faults.” It does, and it finds them when they are already hot. An early arc or surface tracking sings for weeks before it has enough heat to show, which is the entire window you are trying to buy.

• “Open the panel and look for tracking.” Please do not. This is the answer that hurts somebody: getting closer to a live medium-voltage panel is the opposite of what the situation calls for, and whatever you are looking for is exactly the thing you do not want to be standing in front of.

• “Get the compressor serviced — it is working too hard.” Reasonable, and it treats the symptom. The compressor is loaded because something downstream is consuming air that does no work; service it and it will run just as hard, slightly more efficiently, filling the same holes.

The two problems look unrelated and they are the same problem: a small leak and a small spark both make their noise above what people can hear — too high, and too soft, and hiding under the roar of a working plant.

One handheld instrument does it, in two ways. Here is how to use it.

T2: The Tool in Action

A person hears up to about 20 kHz, but a small high-pressure leak or a tiny electrical spark makes most of its noise above that, in the ultrasound range - sound too high for us to catch. An ultrasound detector listens there (around 40 kHz) and does two friendly things: it shifts the pitch down into your headphones so you can hear it, and it shows the loudness as a number on a screen so you can compare and record. So you do not guess - you point, you listen, you read. Watch it walk a plant.

Step 1 - Plan, set up, and tune out the roar

Dimpho, certified to ISO 18436-8, plans the full compressed-air ring main at its normal 7 bar and running, and notes the figure to beat: about R18,000 a month, the system assumed to leak 25%. The compressor room roars at 92 dB(A) to the ears, but through the 40 kHz detector's headphones that roar almost vanishes - the instrument passes only the high ultrasound band, so she can 'hear' in a room far too loud for ears.

Step 2 - Sweep, pinpoint, and size the leak in money

Sweeping Drop 4, Dimpho's reading climbs from a 12 dB background to 38 dB; moving in and dropping the gain, the sound peaks at a threaded fitting below the regulator at 41 dB at 0.3 m. The detector's leak-estimate reads about 2.0 CFM (cubic feet per minute); at the plant's rule of thumb of R650 per CFM per year, that one fitting wastes about 2.0 x R650 = R1,300 a year. Working the whole ring main: 14 leaks, about 19 CFM, roughly 19 x R650 = R12,350 a year - most of it in five threaded joints and two perished couplers that cost almost nothing to replace.

Step 3 - Read the signature, not just the loudness

In the medium-voltage switchroom, Dimpho listens through the louvres from the safe standing distance. Panel 3 carries a crackling, frying sound - uneven, like bacon in a pan - at 27 dB against 5 dB on the quiet panels. The erratic crackle (not a steady corona buzz) says surface tracking on a dirty insulator, early-stage. On a condensate line, a contact probe pressed to a steam trap gives a continuous 34 dB rush that never stops - the trap is passing live steam, a hidden loss.

Step 4 - Stay safe, hand the fault to the qualified person

Dimpho does not open Panel 3. She records 'Panel 3 - erratic crackle, 27 dB vs 5 dB baseline - pattern consistent with surface tracking, early-stage' and raises a priority work request for a qualified electrician to de-energise, inspect and clean it under the correct procedure. Ultrasound found the fault weeks before heat would have shown it; the qualified person makes it safe.

Step 5 - Tag, log, and total the win

Dimpho tags each leak and logs it - 'Leak 7 - Drop 4 regulator fitting - 41 dB at 0.3 m - about 2.0 CFM - about R1,300/yr - fix: re-seal the threaded joint' - and the same for the panel and the trap. The shift's tally: 14 air leaks worth about R12,350 a year, one early electrical fault routed to an electrician before failure, one failed steam trap found by touch - most of the air loss in joints and couplers that cost almost nothing to put right.

See what one instrument did in a single shift: fourteen invisible air leaks found and given a price, an early electrical fault caught from a safe distance, and a failed steam trap found by touch. And see the mission in it, because this technique serves three things at once, and never trades one for another. The first is stewardship of what is scarce. Compressed air is one of the most expensive things a plant makes, and a quarter of it can vanish through holes nobody can see or hear - so the tool does not just find the leaks, it prices them, turning a vague "we are wasting money" into a costed list a manager can act on, where the biggest wins are often perished couplers worth almost nothing to replace. That is money and energy and carbon, all saved by listening. The second is the safety of a person. An early arc in a switchroom sings in ultrasound weeks before it is hot enough for a thermal camera, and long before it fails in someone's face - so the fault is caught early, and caught from the rated safe distance, never by opening a live panel to get closer. The third is the humility to know where your job ends. The inspector finds the fault and characterises it; the inspector does not fix it. A live medium-voltage panel is handed, every time, to the qualified electrician who can make it safe. The aim is a plant that wastes less and a person who goes home whole - because someone listened for what the ears alone could never catch.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the survey in five steps.

  • 1. Plan, set up, tune out the roar. Route, baseline cost, mode; the detector filters the noise.
  • 2. Sweep, pinpoint, size the leak in money. High gain to find, low gain to fix the spot, dB to a rand cost.
  • 3. Read the signature, not just the loudness. The sound's shape names the fault, against a baseline.
  • 4. Stay safe, hand the fault to the qualified person. Never open live gear; find and characterise, do not fix.
  • 5. Tag, log, total the win. A row per find; total it into a number a manager can act on.

The ideas underneath. Sound reaches the detector two ways, and it does both. Airborne ultrasound travels through the air, caught from a distance with no contact - this finds a pressurised gas or air leak (escaping gas turns turbulent and roars in ultrasound) and finds arcing, tracking and corona in electrical gear from a safe distance. Structure-borne ultrasound travels through solid metal, caught by pressing a contact probe to the equipment - this listens inside a bearing, a steam trap or a valve. The three electrical faults, plainly: arcing is electricity jumping a gap (a spark, dangerous, worsens fast); tracking is electricity creeping across the surface of a dirty or damp insulator (an early warning); corona is the air next to a high-voltage part breaking down (wasteful, a sign of trouble) - and all three crackle in ultrasound long before they are hot enough for a thermal camera, which is why ultrasound catches electrical faults earliest. It needs a competently trained operator who knows the healthy baseline sound. Its honest limits: a submerged or sealed leak may make no airborne sound; background ultrasound can confuse a reading (aim, shield, compare); and it tells you a panel is arcing, not the safe repair - that is the electrician's.

Two disciplines make the survey worth running. First, price the fault, do not just hear it - a decibel reading changes nothing; a leak with a rand-per-year tag on it gets fixed, because now a manager can weigh the cheap re-seal against the money pouring out, and the survey ends as a costed repair list, not a folder of readings. T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your survey true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Price the leak, do not just hear it. A decibel does not pay a bill. Convert it to a leak rate and a rand-per-year cost, so a manager can act on a costed list, not a folder of readings.

• Read the shape, not just the loudness. A steady rush is a leak; a steady buzz, corona; an erratic crackle, tracking or arcing. The number is how loud; the signature is what.

• Judge against a known-good baseline. A strange sound only means something next to a healthy one. Without the baseline, background ultrasound and busy machines will fool you.

• Never open live gear to get closer. The whole point is finding it from the rated safe distance. Inspect through louvres and ports; the closer reading is not worth your life.

• Find and characterise; do not fix. A live medium-voltage fault goes to the qualified electrician to de-energise and repair. You caught it weeks early - that is your job, done.

You came onto the floor with a roaring room, a fortune leaking out of holes you could not see, and a switchroom hiding a fault that could one day let go in someone's face. You can leave with a costed list: fourteen leaks worth about R12,350 a year, most of them cheap couplers and joints; a panel caught crackling weeks before heat and handed to the electrician; a steam trap found passing by touch. Hear what your ears cannot - find the leak and price it, catch the arc weeks before the heat, and hand what you are not qualified to touch to the person who is. That is what an ultrasound survey is for: not a clever gadget, but less waste and a safer plant, because someone walked the line and listened in the band above hearing for the faults that were costing money quietly and the ones that could have hurt somebody loudly.
