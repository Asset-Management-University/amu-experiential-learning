Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

Before you begin - a note of care. This unit deals with the theft of money a community saved together, and the broken trust that comes with it. If this is part of your life right now, go gently, and take a break whenever you need to. Nothing here is a judgement of anyone who trusted a person they had every reason to trust. If money has been taken from you, you can open a case at any police station and ask for the case number; keep whatever records you have.

You are Bongi. For a year, you and eleven neighbours have been a team: every month each of you put R200 into your savings club, which you named "Kelvarn". You all dreamed of the payout just before the holidays - for you, new school shoes for your two children without borrowing; for your neighbour, a little stock for her stall; for everyone, a bit of breathing room. R28,800, built together, month by month. One person everyone liked and trusted was the treasurer; they collected the cash and kept it in their own bank account to keep it "safe", and you all had a little notebook with neat columns showing who had paid.

Now, a month before the payout, the money is gone. The treasurer has disappeared - or has a story about a robbery, or a family emergency, that no one can check. The story barely matters; the money isn't there. All you have is that worn notebook: it proves what everyone put in, but says nothing about where the money is. The grief is doubled - the lost savings, and the shattered trust in your own small community.

We trusted a good person and the money still vanished - was the only answer to find a more honest person, or was something missing all along?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to go after the treasurer - find them, confront them, demand it back. But that's recovering the past, and it feels hopeless; it turns neighbour against neighbour, and even if they truly had a crisis you'll never know for sure. Your next thought is to blame yourselves - "we should have picked someone better" - and vow to only ever trust a truly honest person next time. But you did trust this person: relying on one person's honesty was the very thing that failed, and even the most honest person can be robbed, make a mistake, or pass away suddenly, leaving the money tangled and unreachable. Or you give up completely - saving together is too risky, too much heartbreak - and in doing so you lose the whole point: the power of pooling to lift each other.

So before you read on: the treasurer failed, yes - but what was missing that would have protected the money no matter who held it?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The lesson is not to find a more honest person - it's to build a system that stays honest even when a person doesn't. Five moves:

  • See the real problem - trust was doing a system's job: the club's money sat in one person's private account, movable by them alone, with the balance visible only to them and nothing written down about how it ran. That made the treasurer a single point of failure - so when they failed (theft, a robbery, an honest mistake, even a sudden death), everything failed at once. The flaw wasn't simply who you chose; it was that trust was carrying the whole load a system of checks should carry.
  • Separate recovering the past from protecting the future: chasing the lost R28,800 may be hopeless, and confronting the treasurer mostly turns neighbour against neighbour - that's the past. But protecting every cent you save from here on is completely in the group's hands - that's the future. Grieve the past honestly, then put your energy where it can actually work: the system from now on.
  • Write the rules down, and give the money a home in the group's name: agree a simple one-page written agreement - what the club is for, how much you each save, what happens if someone needs to leave or something goes wrong - so the rules, not any one person's memory or goodwill, are the real boss. And open a separate account in the GROUP'S name (the 'Kelvarn Savings Club'), not any individual's, so the money has its own home, never tangled with one person's finances or reachable by them alone.
  • No one moves it alone: set the account so that moving any money out needs two members to approve it - more than one hand on every payment. No single person, not even the new treasurer, can ever move the money by themselves. This one rule removes the single point of failure entirely: a single bad day, bad actor, or honest slip can no longer empty the pot.
  • Records everyone can see - which protects the honest keeper too: keep simple records every member can check any time - the new treasurer shares the bank balance with the whole group each week; no secret notebooks. Everyone sees what's in, what's out, the running total. Sunlight keeps everyone safe - and it clears the honest custodian of all suspicion, because no one can ever accuse them of taking what everyone can see. Good rules protect everyone, including the person you trust.

See how it would have saved Kelvarn. The R28,800 never sat in one private account where one person could move it unseen; it lived in an account in the club's own name, needing two members' approval for any payment, with the balance shared to everyone each week and one page of rules agreed at the start. In that world, the treasurer's disappearance costs the club nothing - because no single person was ever the whole system. And the same protections clear an honest keeper of every suspicion. Trust was doing the work a system of checks should do - so build a system that stays honest even when a person doesn't: separate recovering the past from protecting the future, put the money in the group's name where no one can move it alone, and keep records everyone can see - which protects the honest custodian too.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about savings clubs - any shared pot in one person's hands carries the same risk. Watch a school-trip fund.

See the same four protections with a class-trip fund

One parent kindly offers to collect the trip money from all 30 children, keeping it in her purse or her own bank account. Everyone trusts her. But what if the purse is stolen? What if she hits an emergency and dips in meaning to repay, then can't? The risk is identical: trust is doing the work a system should do.

The same four protections fix it: a separate school account or a lockbox counted and sealed by two parents together (a home in the group's name + no one alone); a one-line note of how it works (written rules); and a simple paid/not-paid list on the classroom door (records everyone can see). The money is never in one person's sole control - which also protects that kind parent from ever being doubted.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at any shared pot of money you're part of - a family collecting for a parent's gift, an office tea fund, a collection for a sports team's kit, a savings club:

  1. Ask if trust is doing a system's job. Is the money sitting in one person's private account or wallet - movable by them alone, visible only to them? And separate the past (money already collected) from the future (money from now on), putting your energy where it can work.
  2. Give it a home and shared hands. For all new money, propose a home in the group's name (not a person's), a rule that no one can move it alone, and one page of simple written rules.
  3. Make it visible. Set up records everyone can see and check at any time - no secret notebooks.

Pooling your money to lift each other is one of the most generous, powerful things a community can do - it was never the mistake, and trusting a neighbour was human and right. The flaw was only ever a missing system, not your goodness. So don't let this loss teach you to trust no one or to stop saving together; let it teach you to make trust safe. Build the system that stays honest even when a person can't - and you protect not only the money and the mission, but the very person you trust, who never again has to carry that weight, or that suspicion, alone.
