Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Thulani, the quality manager at a large dairy plant that pasteurises and bottles fresh milk. The email lands with a thud, even though it makes no sound. It is from your biggest supermarket client. Subject: URGENT - batch rejection. They are refusing the last three days of deliveries. Their intake-lab report is attached, showing a reading for a harmless but indicative bacteria creeping up, now just over their strict threshold.

A cold feeling spreads in your chest. You pull up your own team's weekly quality report. It is a sea of green: "batches shipped on time: 100%", "customer complaints last week: 0", "production vs target: 102%". By all your measures, you were having a great week. Your report shows the results - the final score. Theirs shows the process starting to fail. They saw the problem coming before you did. You, the quality manager, were the last to know.

You look at the gleaming steel of the plant outside your window. The data was there, in the daily lab logs - but you were not looking for a trend. You were only looking for a failure that had already happened.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fix the reporting. You track the lagging numbers even harder - a new, more detailed end-of-week quality scoreboard, more charts on completed batches and final test results. After a month you realise it is just a better history book: it tells you with beautiful precision what went wrong last week, and gives you no clue what might go wrong tomorrow. It is still a scoreboard after the game is over. Then you measure absolutely everything - a new dashboard with fifty dials: pasteuriser temperature, chiller pressure, flow rates, line speed. The screen glows like a spaceship cockpit, but nobody knows what to focus on; a critical signal is just one of fifty blinking lights, drowned in noise. Because everything is important, nothing is. Finally you decide to just pass the next audit - weeks preparing documents, drilling the team on the right answers. It feels wrong: you are outsourcing your own job, relying on an outsider to inspect quality into your system instead of building it in yourselves, every single day.

You are still counting the crashes, drowning in noise, or waiting for someone else to tell you that you are failing. So before you read on: how do you stop measuring the past and start seeing the future?

Stop thinking about results and start thinking about WARNINGS. A lagging indicator is the autopsy - it tells you why the patient died (a customer rejection, a fine, a breakdown, an audit finding - it counts what already went wrong, and an outsider usually notices first). A leading indicator is the check-up - the few upstream signals that move BEFORE the failure, giving you the one thing the lagging number never can: TIME to act. Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop thinking about results and start thinking about warnings. A lagging indicator is the autopsy: it tells you why the patient died. A leading indicator is the check-up: it measures the vital signs that predict future health. Your customer's complaint was the autopsy - you need to start doing your own check-ups. Five moves:

  • See the difference - the autopsy vs the check-up: the breakthrough is to stop thinking about results and start thinking about warnings. A lagging indicator is the autopsy: it tells you, precisely and far too late, why the patient died - the customer rejection, the fine, the breakdown, the audit finding. It counts what has already gone wrong, the harm is already done, and an outsider is usually the one who noticed. A leading indicator is the check-up: it measures the vital signs that predict future health BEFORE the crisis. Your green scoreboard was all autopsy - a perfect history of a week that had already happened - while the small creep in a daily lab reading was the check-up nobody was reading. Once you see that lagging counts the crash and leading predicts it, you understand why managing only by the scoreboard guarantees you always learn too late, and from someone else.
  • Find the few leading indicators: for the failure that hurts, ask the question that turns hindsight into foresight: 'what small, measurable thing moves BEFORE this goes wrong?' At the dairy the team finds three: the number of overdue preventative-maintenance tasks on the chillers; the trend in the daily lab-sample counts even while they are still within spec; and the number of short temperature deviations in the pasteuriser. None of these is a failure yet - each is a finger of smoke before the fire. The skill is choosing the few signals that genuinely lead the outcome rather than just correlating with it, and resisting the urge to track everything. A handful of true leading indicators is worth more than a hundred dials, because you can actually watch and act on them.
  • Watch them yourself, before any outsider does: the failure was not a lack of data - it was that nobody was watching the right data, so the customer became the early-warning system. Fix that by owning your own foresight: scrap the fifty-dial cockpit and put the few leading indicators (plus one or two key lagging ones, like batches shipped and rejections) on a simple board, updated daily, in front of the people who can act. The morning meeting stops being a post-mortem of last week's failure and becomes a ten-minute huddle at the board - 'the chiller maintenance is overdue, let us clear it this morning; the lab trend is creeping on line two, let us check the cleaning cycle.' You are no longer waiting to be told; you are looking for the smoke yourself, every single day.
  • Set a trigger that forces action in time: a leading indicator that nobody acts on is just another lagging one - it will faithfully record the drift right up until the failure it predicted arrives. So pair each signal with a clear, agreed trigger that forces a response while there is still time: if overdue maintenance tasks go above two, a senior engineer must sign off and schedule them; if the lab trend rises three days running, the process is halted for inspection before anything ships. The threshold is decided in the calm beforehand, not argued about in the moment, so the warning automatically becomes an action rather than a number someone might notice. That is what converts seeing-it-coming into actually-stopping-it.
  • Keep a few of each in balance: this is not about throwing away the scoreboard - you still need the lagging numbers (rejections, on-time delivery, incidents) to know whether you truly won the war and to keep yourself honest about outcomes. The shift is what you MANAGE the day and the week by: lead with the leading indicators so you are never blindsided, and keep just a couple of lagging ones for the true result. And keep the whole set small - this is bp-0158's focus-on-the-vital-few applied to measurement: a board of five signals people watch and act on beats fifty that glow and get ignored. Balance gives you both foresight to prevent the fall and the honest final score to prove it worked.

See it transform the plant. The team names the few signals that move before a batch goes bad - overdue chiller maintenance, the daily lab-count trend even while still in spec, short temperature deviations in the pasteuriser - and you scrap the fifty-dial cockpit for a simple whiteboard showing just those three, plus batches-shipped and rejections. Each gets a trigger agreed in the calm beforehand: overdue tasks above two and a senior engineer signs off; the lab trend up three days running and the process halts for inspection. The morning meeting stops being a post-mortem of last week and becomes a ten-minute huddle at the board. Two weeks later the board shows a temperature trend starting to drift; the team catches a failing thermostat and replaces it in thirty minutes - it costs R500, and it saves a R200,000 batch from being spoiled. You did not wait for the auditor. You did not wait for the customer. You saw it coming, and you acted. A lagging indicator is the autopsy; a leading indicator is the check-up. Do not wait for the auditor, the regulator or the customer to tell you that you are sick - they always arrive too late. Find the few vital signs that move before the fall, watch them yourself with a trigger that forces action in time, and keep just enough lagging numbers to know you truly won - and you will have the one thing a lagging number can never give you: time to act.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - think about your own health.

See leading and lagging in your own body

A heart attack, a stroke, a diabetes diagnosis - these are lagging indicators. They are the final, painful result of a long process; they tell you that you have failed, after the harm is done.

Your blood pressure, your weight, your cholesterol, how many flights of stairs you can climb without getting out of breath - these are leading indicators, the upstream signals that move BEFORE the crisis. You do not manage your health by waiting for a heart attack; you manage it by watching your blood pressure and deciding to walk more, or seeing your weight creep up and choosing a better meal.

You act on the leading indicators to prevent the lagging disaster. Your work is no different - the few vital signs, watched in time, beat the diagnosis that arrives too late.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think about your own world. Where do you only find out you are failing when an outsider tells you - a safety incident, a budget overrun, a failed audit, a key project quietly going off the rails? Stop waiting for the autopsy; start doing the check-up.

  1. Name the failure. What is the one lagging indicator that hurts most when it arrives - the one that makes you feel you were the last to know?
  2. Find your leading indicators. Ask yourself and your team: "what small, measurable things move BEFORE this failure happens?" - near-misses, overdue tasks, small deviations, rising backlogs. Pick three or four.
  3. Watch them yourself. Choose the vital few, put them on a simple board or report, look every day or week, and set a clear trigger: "if this number goes above X, we will do Y."
  4. Balance your view. Keep your key lagging indicator to know if you are winning the war, but use the leading ones to win the daily battles.

Take the wheel and own your own foresight: lead with leading indicators, and you protect the people who depend on what you make, long before any outsider could.
