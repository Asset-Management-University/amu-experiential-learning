T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and a certification audit is coming. You decide to test your own evidence before the auditor does.

The machinery is good. There is a governance charter with decision rights, a meeting schedule, an engagement plan. All of it was designed properly and all of it ran.

So you pull a year of records to demonstrate that it ran, and you cannot.

Eleven governance meetings. Nine sets of minutes. Scored against what a minute is required to carry, 23 of 55 elements are present. Five record a decision with a named owner. Two record a deadline. Thirty-one actions were raised and eight have any evidence of closure.

Then you look at the engagement side. Fourteen engagements. Six records. One that says what changed because somebody spoke.

The work happened. The evidence that it happened does not exist - and for those fourteen conversations, neither does the answer to the only question the community actually asked.

Everything we were supposed to do, we did. I simply cannot prove any of it, and the people we consulted have no way of knowing whether it mattered. How do I write a record that is evidence rather than a memory?

Before you read on, decide. You did all of it. The committee met, the decisions were taken, the community was consulted. Now you have been asked to demonstrate it. The minutes say things like “the committee held a robust discussion regarding the pump replacement options, considering cost, operational risk and lead times” and “a decision was agreed on the path forward”. Of the actions raised, eight have any evidence of closure. Of fourteen community engagements, six have a record, and one says what changed because somebody spoke. Nobody was negligent; this is how minutes are written everywhere. Write down what a record has to contain to be evidence — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and who the record is actually for

• “Write longer, more detailed minutes.” More words about the discussion. A fuller account of a conversation is still an account of a conversation, and the auditor's question is not what was said.

• “Record the meetings and keep the audio.” Perfect fidelity to the thing that does not matter. Nobody will listen to fourteen hours of recordings to reconstruct a decision, and a successor certainly will not.

• “Get the chair to approve the minutes properly.” Approval of a document that records nothing testable. You would have a formally adopted record that still cannot show what was decided or on what basis.

• “Use a template with standard headings.” Closer, and headings alone will be filled with the same prose. Something has to change about what is written into them, not merely what they are called.

• “Chase the open actions.” Worth doing today, and it addresses this year's list rather than the reason the loop is invisible — next year's actions will decay the same way.

• The idea that changes every drafting choice is deciding who the record is for. Everybody in the room already knows what happened; they will never read it. The record exists for three people who were not there: the successor who inherits the decision in two years and must not unpick it blindly, the auditor testing whether governance actually operated, and the future decision that will be built on this one and needs to know what it rests on. That gives you one test for every sentence: could an outsider reconstruct the decision from this document alone? Run it over your two sentences. The robust discussion passes as a description of a meeting and fails completely as evidence — an outsider learns only that a conversation occurred. “A decision was agreed on the path forward” is worse, because it sounds like a decision and contains none. What must survive is the decision, its basis, its owner and its deadline. And for anything involving people outside the organisation, one thing more: what changed because they spoke — because without it you have not recorded a consultation, you have recorded some talking.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the governance record - who it is written for, the spine it must carry, the decision rather than the discussion, the action loop, and the outcome that closes an engagement.

1. Write for the person who was not in the room

THIS IS THE ONE IDEA THE WHOLE UNIT RESTS ON, AND IT CHANGES EVERY DRAFTING CHOICE THAT FOLLOWS. EVERYONE IN THE ROOM ALREADY KNOWS WHAT HAPPENED. They do not need the record and they will never read it. THE RECORD EXISTS FOR THREE PEOPLE WHO WERE NOT THERE: THE SUCCESSOR who inherits the decision in two years and must not unpick it blindly; THE AUDITOR who must test whether governance actually operated; and THE FUTURE DECISION that will be built on this one and needs to know what it rests on. SO APPLY ONE TEST TO EVERY SENTENCE YOU WRITE: COULD AN OUTSIDER RECONSTRUCT THE DECISION FROM THIS DOCUMENT ALONE? Not the mood of the meeting, not who spoke most - the decision, its basis, its owner and its deadline. Run that test over a real minute and watch what fails. 'The committee held a robust discussion regarding the pump replacement options, considering cost, operational risk and lead times' passes as a description of a meeting and FAILS COMPLETELY as evidence: an outsider learns that a conversation occurred and nothing else. 'A DECISION WAS AGREED ON THE PATH FORWARD' is worse, because it sounds like a decision and contains none. THE ABSENT READER IS NOT A COURTESY. THEY ARE THE ONLY AUDIENCE THIS DOCUMENT HAS.

2. Give the minute its required spine

THE MANAGEMENT-SYSTEM STANDARDS SHARE A HARMONISED HIGH-LEVEL STRUCTURE, and the management review clause is common across them - so this spine is not a house style, it is what the record is REQUIRED to carry, and you will meet the same requirement in quality, environmental, safety, information-security and asset management systems alike. MINUTES ARE THE MANDATORY DOCUMENTED INFORMATION OF THAT CLAUSE, and at minimum they carry FIVE THINGS. DATE AND ATTENDEES - including who was absent, because a decision taken without the person accountable for it is a fact the absent reader needs. THE INPUTS ANALYSED - named and dated, so the evidence base can be retrieved rather than remembered; 'the condition report' is not retrievable, 'the pump condition report of 14 March' is. CONCLUSIONS BY TOPIC - one conclusion per matter, not one narrative for the meeting. DECISIONS OR OUTPUTS WITH AN OWNER. AND A DEADLINE WITH A FOLLOW-UP INDICATOR. THE ORGANISATION SHALL RETAIN THIS AS EVIDENCE OF THE RESULTS OF THE REVIEW - retention is part of the requirement, not an afterthought. AND NOTE THE TWO ELEMENTS THAT ARE ALMOST ALWAYS THE ONES MISSING: THE OWNER AND THE DEADLINE. A CONCLUSION WITH NO OWNER IS A CONVERSATION, AND A DECISION WITH NO DEADLINE CAN NEVER BE LATE, WHICH IS WHY NOBODY EVER CHASES IT. At Corathyl, of nine minutes on file, five record a decision with a named owner and TWO record a deadline.

3. Record the decision - including what was rejected and what was deliberately not decided

NOW WRITE THE DECISION ITSELF, AND WRITE FOUR PARTS OF IT. WHAT WAS DECIDED, specifically enough to act on: not 'proceed with the pump replacement' but 'proceed with full refurbishment at R1.2 million, funded from the current capital allocation'. ON WHAT EVIDENCE, named and dated. WHAT WAS CONSIDERED AND REJECTED, AND WHY - and this is the part most often cut for brevity and most costly to lose, because an option with no recorded rejection COMES BACK EVERY QUARTER, and each time somebody spends a week re-analysing it. 'Replacement in kind was rejected because the lead time for the required model is three years, which the committee judged an unacceptable operational risk' costs one sentence and saves that week for a decade. AND FOURTH, WHAT WAS DELIBERATELY NOT DECIDED, AND WHY - the deferred matter, the question sent away for more work, the thing everyone assumed somebody else would pick up. THE DEFERRED DECISION IS THE ONE THAT SURPRISES EVERYONE LATER, precisely because it left no trace. ONE DISCIPLINE THAT KEEPS ALL OF THIS HONEST: RECORD WHAT WAS DECIDED, NOT WHAT SHOULD HAVE BEEN DECIDED. It is tempting to tidy a muddled decision on the page, and the record then disagrees with what the organisation actually did - which is worse than a poor decision recorded plainly, because it destroys the evidentiary value of every other record you write.

4. Close the loop on actions - carried forward, with closure evidence

THE STATUS OF ACTIONS FROM PREVIOUS REVIEWS IS ITSELF A REQUIRED INPUT TO THE NEXT ONE, so the record is a loop rather than a sequence of unconnected documents. CARRY EVERY OPEN ACTION FORWARD, with its original date, owner and deadline intact - not restated, not quietly re-dated, because a re-dated action hides how long it has been open, which is usually the most useful thing about it. AND CLOSE ACTIONS ON EVIDENCE, NOT ON ASSERTION. 'Completed' in a status column is a claim; the closure record says WHAT WAS DONE and points at the thing that proves it - the issued order, the signed procedure, the amended register entry. AT CORATHYL, 31 ACTIONS WERE RAISED ACROSS THE YEAR AND 8 HAVE A RECORDED CLOSURE - 25.8 PER CENT - SO 23 ARE CARRIED WITH NO EVIDENCE OF WHAT HAPPENED TO THEM. Be clear about what that is and what it is not. IT IS NOT ADMINISTRATIVE UNTIDINESS. It means the organisation cannot demonstrate that anything it decided was ever done, and it means the next committee will make its decisions on the assumption that the previous ones took effect. Some of those 23 were certainly completed and simply not recorded - and that is exactly the problem, because from the outside a completed-but-unrecorded action and an abandoned one are the same document.

5. The engagement record - and what changed because somebody spoke

AN ENGAGEMENT RECORD IS NOT AN ATTENDANCE SHEET. It states WHAT WAS HEARD, FROM WHOM, and WHAT HAPPENED NEXT - and the last of those is the part that is almost always missing. At Corathyl, 14 ENGAGEMENT EVENTS WERE HELD, 6 HAVE A RECORD, AND ONE RECORDS WHAT CHANGED AS A RESULT. THIS IS NOT AN ADMINISTRATIVE FAILING. Engagement without a documented outcome is CONSULTATION THEATRE: it builds cynicism and destroys social licence, and stakeholders who learn that their input is performatively solicited and substantively ignored move from cooperation to opposition. SILENCE IS NOT A NEUTRAL OUTCOME - IT IS A MESSAGE OF DISMISSAL. SO EVERY FORMAL RECORD OF STAKEHOLDER ENGAGEMENT MUST CONTAIN A DEDICATED SECTION TITLED 'OUTCOMES AND ACTIONS'. THIS SECTION MUST STATE, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE: (A) THE KEY FEEDBACK, CONCERNS OR PROPOSALS RECEIVED. (B) THE SPECIFIC DECISION OR ACTION TAKEN BY THE ORGANISATION IN RESPONSE TO THE INPUT. (C) WHERE NO ACTION WAS TAKEN OR A PROPOSAL WAS REJECTED, THE SPECIFIC, HONEST REASONS FOR THAT DECISION. THIS SECTION IS NOT COMPLETE UNTIL THE OUTCOME HAS BEEN COMMUNICATED TO THE STAKEHOLDERS WHO PROVIDED THE INPUT. Note what (C) protects. You will often be unable to do what was asked, and saying so plainly - here is what we heard, here is why we cannot do it, here is what we can do instead - treats people as adults with a right to an answer. IT IS THE ABSENCE OF THE ANSWER, NOT THE ANSWER ITSELF, THAT TEACHES A COMMUNITY THAT SPEAKING CHANGES NOTHING.

Box 1 is the idea the rest follows from, and box 5 is the one that has consequences outside the building. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A governance record is not a memory of a meeting for those who were present. It is the official evidence of a decision for those who were not. Five questions:

  • Who is this actually for? The absent reader - successor, auditor, and the future decision built on this one. One test per sentence: could an outsider reconstruct the decision from this alone?
  • What must it carry? The required spine - date and attendees, inputs analysed, conclusions by topic, decisions with owner, deadline and follow-up. Common to the harmonised management-system standards, so you will meet it everywhere.
  • What is the decision? Decision, not discussion - what was decided, on what evidence, what was rejected and why, and what was deliberately left undecided.
  • What happened to the last set? Closure on evidence, not assertion. The status of prior actions is itself a required input.
  • What changed because somebody spoke? Outcomes and actions - the section that turns engagement into something other than theatre.

The arithmetic that shows what is actually missing. Twenty-three of fifty-five elements present is 41.8 per cent, but the aggregate hides the diagnosis. Look at which elements survive: date and attendees are nearly always there, and the owner and the deadline are nearly always absent - five minutes of nine name an owner, two name a deadline. That is not carelessness distributed evenly. It is a consistent stopping point: the record is being written up to the moment of decision and abandoned at the moment of accountability. Fix that one habit and 41.8 per cent moves a long way without anybody writing more words.

And what the open actions really mean. Thirty-one raised, eight closed on evidence, twenty-three carried. Some of those twenty-three were certainly done - and that is exactly the problem, because from outside the organisation a completed-but-unrecorded action and an abandoned one are the same document. The next committee will then decide on the assumption that the last committee's decisions took effect, which is a guess dressed as continuity.

The part that reaches outside the building. Fourteen engagements, one record of an outcome. Engagement without a documented outcome is consultation theatre: it builds cynicism and destroys social licence, and people who learn their input is performatively solicited and substantively ignored move from cooperation to opposition. Silence is not a neutral outcome. It is a message of dismissal. So the record must say what was heard, what was done about it, and - where nothing was done - the specific honest reason, communicated back to the people who raised it. You will often be unable to do what was asked. Saying so plainly treats people as adults with a right to an answer, and it is the absence of the answer, not the answer itself, that teaches a community that speaking changes nothing.

One honest boundary. You produce the record. You do not make the decisions in it, and you must not improve them on the page - a tidied-up version of a muddled decision means the record disagrees with what the organisation actually did, which is worse than a poor decision recorded plainly, because it destroys the evidentiary value of every other record you write.

Does it scale? A surgeon's operation note is not written for the surgeon. It is written for the doctor who sees the patient at three in the morning six weeks later, knowing nothing, needing to understand exactly what was done and why. Nobody in that theatre needed the note. Your minute is that note.

T4: Your Turn

Here is a minute from an asset governance committee. It is immaculately formatted. Every required section is present and correctly headed.

SectionAs written
Date and attendeesRecorded in full, including apologies
Inputs"Report AM-2026-043 (Pump Condition). Presentation by the Head of Operations."
Conclusions"A robust discussion was held regarding the pump replacement options, considering cost, operational risk and lead times."
Decisions"The committee agreed on the path forward for the pump replacement."
Actions"Head of Operations to progress the pump replacement."
What you also knowDetail
The options on the tableReplace in kind, or full refurbishment
RefurbishmentR1.2 million, contractor available, six weeks
Replacement in kindR1.6 million, and the required model now carries a three-year lead time
FundingCurrent capital allocation has R1.4 million uncommitted
Also raised, not resolvedWhether the duty-standby arrangement should change once the pump returns
  1. Score it. Run the five required elements. What does it score, and what does the score miss?
  2. Apply the test. Could an outsider reconstruct the decision from this document alone? Say precisely what they could not recover.
  3. Rewrite the decision. Include what was rejected and why.
  4. Rewrite the action. Make it closeable on evidence.
  5. The loose end. Something was raised and not resolved. What do you do with it, and what happens if you leave it out?

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, on the score. It arguably scores five out of five - every required section exists and is populated. That is the point of the exercise. If your instinct was to add the missing fields, there are none to add. Compliance with a template is not the same as being evidence, and a record can satisfy an auditor's checklist while telling a successor nothing.

Second, on the rewrite. A defensible version reads: "Decision: proceed with full refurbishment at R1.2 million, funded from the uncommitted balance of the current capital allocation. Replacement in kind was rejected because the required model now carries a three-year lead time, which the committee judged an unacceptable operational risk." And the action: "Head of Operations to place the refurbishment order. Deadline 31 August. Closure evidence: the issued order number, reported at the next meeting." Notice the rejected option cost one sentence - and without it, replacement in kind returns to the agenda next quarter and somebody spends a week re-analysing a three-year lead time that has not changed.

Third, step 5 is the one most groups skip, and it is the most valuable. The duty-standby question was raised and not resolved. It must be recorded as deliberately deferred, with a reason and an owner - because an unrecorded loose end does not stay loose, it becomes an assumption. Six months on, the pump returns, the standby arrangement is unchanged, and nobody can say whether that was decided or merely never revisited. The deferred decision is the one that surprises everyone later, precisely because it left no trace.

T5: The Guardrails

Governance records fail quietly, and the failure only surfaces when somebody needs them.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Minuting the discussion. "A robust discussion was held" tells an outsider that a conversation occurred and nothing else. Record the decision.

• A conclusion with no owner. That is a conversation with a heading on it.

• A decision with no deadline. It can never be late, so nobody ever chases it. This and the missing owner are where records almost always stop.

• Unnamed inputs. "The condition report" cannot be retrieved. "The pump condition report of 14 March" can.

• No record of what was rejected. The option returns every quarter and is re-analysed every time, at a week apiece.

• No record of what was deliberately not decided. An unrecorded loose end becomes an assumption, and nobody can later tell a decision from an oversight.

• Closing actions on assertion. "Completed" is a claim. Closure names what was done and points at the thing that proves it.

• Re-dating a carried action. It hides how long the action has been open, which is usually the most useful thing about it.

• Improving the decision on the page. The record then disagrees with what the organisation did, and every other record you write loses its value too.

• An engagement record with no outcome. Consultation theatre. Silence is not a neutral result; it is a message of dismissal.

• Omitting the honest no. "We heard you, we cannot do this, here is why" respects people. Saying nothing teaches them that speaking changes nothing.

• Treating the record as administration. It is the evidence the organisation will be judged on, produced by whoever happened to be taking notes.

Onalenna had done everything and could prove none of it. Write for the person who was not in the room, and test every sentence by asking whether an outsider could reconstruct the decision from it alone; give the minute its required spine, and notice that the owner and the deadline are the two elements almost always missing; record what was decided, on what evidence, what was rejected and why, and what was deliberately left undecided; close actions on evidence rather than assertion, because from outside, a completed-but-unrecorded action and an abandoned one are the same document; and give every engagement record a section that says what changed, or says honestly why nothing did - and tell the people who raised it. A governance record is not a memory for those who were there. It is evidence for those who were not, and the community waiting to hear back is one of them.
