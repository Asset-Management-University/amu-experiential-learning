The plant has drifted away from its own records - the drawings and the register no longer match what is actually installed, and I cannot trust the map; how did we lose control of our own plant, and how do I get it back?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Mxolisi, the engineering manager at a water treatment works. It is 2am on a Tuesday and your phone is ringing: the main clarified-water pump, P-101, has failed. The night maintenance team is on it, but they are in trouble. You drive to the site.

The team checked the asset register and the engineering drawing - P-101 is a "Type-X" pump. They fetched the Type-X seal replacement kit from stores - it cost R8,000 - but it will not fit. Nothing lines up. The plant is now four hours into an unplanned shutdown, the storage tanks are running low, and in another four hours you will have to cut supply to the town. The cost of lost service and emergency water tankers is climbing at R10,000 an hour.

A veteran fitter, about to retire, squints at the pump. "That is not a Type-X," he says. "A few years back, the old one failed and the day-shift super grabbed a quick 'like-for-like' from a local supplier to get us running fast. It is a different make - a Type-Y." He is right. Someone, years ago, made a "small" change: swapped the pump for a similar but not identical model, got the plant running, and never told anyone. Never updated the drawing. Never updated the register. Never updated the spares list.

The team scrambles to find a Type-Y seal. The plant is down for a total of seven hours - R70,000 in lost service, plus the R8,000 seal kit you cannot use. But the money is not what keeps you awake. It is the cold realisation as you stare at the drawing that lied: if the record for this critical pump is wrong, what else is wrong? Which valve will fail to isolate in an emergency? Which pipe is not where you think it is? Which safety device has been quietly bypassed?

The plant has drifted away from its own records and I cannot trust the map any more - how did we lose control of our own plant, and how do I get it back?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fix the map. "We will do a full site audit - walk down every pipe, check every machine, update every drawing until they are 100% accurate." You spend a fortune on contract surveyors and drafters and, six months later, you have a perfect as-built set. But a week after the project ends, a team replaces a corroded valve with a slightly different type because it was available, and does not update the drawing. The drift begins again. An audit is just a photograph; it does not stop the plant changing the second after you take it. "Then no more changes - the plant is locked down, nothing changes without my personal sign-off." The rule lasts a month: plants must evolve, small improvements are essential, so the changes just go underground - 'temporary' fixes that become permanent, bypassing a system that is too rigid. The problem gets worse, because now it is hidden. "Fine - I will just remind everyone: please update the paperwork, it is important." People nod and agree, but when a fitter is under pressure at 3am to restore supply, a form is the last thing on their mind; good people with good intentions always route around a process that feels like a burden. Exhortation is not a system.

So before you read on: the real question is not how to STOP change - it is how to make CONTROLLED change the easiest path.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop seeing the plant and its records as two separate things. A plant is not just its steel and wires; it is the steel and wires PLUS the trustworthy body of knowledge about what is installed. The information is part of the asset - so a change to the asset is not complete until the information is changed too. Five moves:

  • See uncontrolled change as the silent killer: the danger that nearly cost you the town's water was not one big, obvious change - it was one small, quiet one, years ago, repeated invisibly across the whole plant. A pump swapped for a similar model, a pipe re-routed around a trip hazard, a control setpoint nudged, a 'temporary' bypass that quietly became permanent: each looks harmless on its own, and each widens the gap between what the records SAY is installed and what is ACTUALLY installed. The killer is the ACCUMULATION - hundreds of undocumented changes adding up, over years, into a plant that nobody alive fully understands any more, where the only true knowledge lives in the heads of a few veterans about to retire. Naming this is the first move: the threat is not change itself (plants must evolve) but UNCONTROLLED change, and it is silent precisely because no single step ever looks dangerous enough to stop.
  • A record is only worth what you can trust it to be: hold up the invoice for the seal kit that did not fit, because it teaches the whole lesson: a record that no longer matches reality is worse than no record at all. With no drawing, the night team would have walked to the pump, read its nameplate, and ordered the right part. With a WRONG drawing, they confidently ordered the wrong one, lost hours they did not have, and nearly cut a town's supply - because a record's entire value is that people TRUST it and act on it without re-checking. So the deepest shift is to stop thinking of the plant and its information as two separate things: the trustworthy knowledge of what is installed - the drawings, the register, the procedures, the spares list - is PART OF THE ASSET, as real and as critical as the steel. A pump you cannot correctly identify is only half an asset; the other half is the truth about it, and that half had been allowed to rot.
  • Put a gate before the change: since the threat is uncontrolled change, the cure is to put a simple, proportionate gate in FRONT of every change - a moment of thought before any metal is cut. Make it a quick Request-for-Change, and scale the rigour to the risk: a genuinely trivial, like-for-like swap can be auto-approved in seconds, while a bigger change - a different pump model, a re-route, a setpoint that affects a safety function - gets a short review by an engineer who asks the question the day-shift super never did: what are the knock-on effects on spares, on procedures, on capacity, on the safety case? Most disasters from change are not the change itself but its unconsidered ripples - a 'like-for-similar' substitution that quietly needs a different seal, defeats an interlock, or invalidates a procedure. The gate is not there to say no; it is there to make sure someone competent has SEEN the change and its consequences before it becomes reality.
  • Close the loop after the change: this is the single most important move, and the one almost everyone skips: the change is NOT finished when the physical work is done - it is finished when the records have been updated to match the new reality. The commonest failure mode in the world is a perfectly good physical change followed by a drawing, a register and a spares list that were never touched, and that is exactly how P-101 became a lie. So build the loop-closing into the work itself: the work order cannot be CLOSED in the system until the person confirms 'the drawing, the asset register, the procedure and the spares list have been updated to match what I just installed'. Give them the tools to do it on the spot - red-line the drawing on a tablet at the pump, there and then - so updating the record is part of the job, not a form for 'later' that never comes. An open loop is a future near-miss waiting silently for its moment.
  • Make the controlled path the easy path: the reason the old system failed is not that people were careless - it is that the honest path was the hard path, so good people under pressure routed around it. The fix is to flip that: make the CONTROLLED path the EASIEST one to take. If the change request is a 90-second form, if trivial like-for-like jobs are approved instantly, if the record can be red-lined on a tablet at the asset in a minute, then it becomes quicker to USE the system than to dodge it - and people stop experiencing it as bureaucracy and start experiencing it as simply the proper, supported way to do their work. A heavyweight, slow, punishing MoC process is not 'more control'; it is LESS, because it guarantees its own evasion. Proportionate, fast and genuinely helpful is what keeps the records matching reality for years, not a thick procedure nobody opens. Make the right thing the easy thing, and the drift stops.

See it change things for Mxolisi. A pipe is re-routed to remove a trip hazard - but this time the change is proposed, the impact is checked, it is approved, and when the work is done the fitter red-lines the drawing on a tablet on the spot before the work order will close. The system works; the gap between the plant and the records begins to shrink, change by controlled change. For the first time in years, Mxolisi starts to trust the map again - and sleeps a little easier knowing the next 2am call will find a record that tells the truth. Every change to a plant changes two things: the physical asset, and the truth of what you know about that asset. The job is not finished when the metal is cut and welded - it is finished when the drawings, records, procedures and spares are updated to match the new reality. Control the change with a proportionate gate before it, and close the loop after it, because the gap between the plant-as-documented and the plant-as-built is where accidents, failed repairs and bad decisions are born. The information is part of the asset; keep it true.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - picture a family's treasured, handwritten recipe book.

See the stew that drifted from its own recipe

The book holds the original recipe for Grandma's famous stew. Over the years your dad found it tasted better with more thyme, but never wrote it down; your aunt discovered a different cut of meat made it more tender, but never wrote it down; you worked out it needs twenty minutes longer, but never wrote it down. Then a cousin who has never made it follows the recipe in the book exactly - and the result is okay, but it is not right, not the stew everyone loves. The "as-cooked" reality has drifted away from the "as-written" recipe, and the book no longer tells the truth.

The fix is simple: everyone agrees one rule - if you improve the recipe, you update the master copy in the book immediately. A gate before the change ("does this really make it better?") and a closed loop after ("it does, so I am writing it down now"). The book stays a living, trustworthy guide for anyone who picks it up. A plant, a procedure, an asset register - they are all that recipe book, and they are only worth the truth you keep in them.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Your world is full of "recipe books" - drawings, procedures, asset lists, network diagrams, even the contact list on your phone. How many still match reality? Where has the drift happened, and where are you confidently using a map that lies? You can start to close the gap today:

  1. Pick one critical area and re-baseline it. Survey it once to get a true as-built picture - and know this is only a starting point, not the cure.
  2. Put a simple gate before changes. For any change in that area, however small, ask: "What are the knock-on effects?" It need not be heavy - a mental check for a small change, a written review for a big one.
  3. Close the loop. Make it a personal rule: a change is not "done" until the record is updated. Change a process, update the procedure at the same time; install new software, update your master list before you close the ticket.
  4. Make it easy. Do not build a slow, heavy process - a simple checklist or a note in a shared document that people actually use beats a perfect system nobody opens.

They are all trusting that your map matches the territory. Keep it true, and do not let them down.
