The plan was broken before the week began

T1: The Ache

By Wednesday, the week's schedule is fiction. Again.

You are Onalenna, and the pattern never varies. The schedule is built properly on Friday. On Monday a pump fails, a crew is pulled, and the jobs that get bumped are the routine preventive ones - because they are the only jobs that can be bumped. Nobody is careless. Everyone works hard all week. And on Friday the urgent work is done, the preventive work is carried over, and the same schedule is built again.

You are not short of scheduling skill. You can build a proper logic network, find the critical path, level the resources. So you sit down and do the arithmetic you have somehow never done, and the answer is humiliating in its simplicity.

Six artisans, forty hours: 240 hours on paper. Call it 180 once travel, breaks and everything that is not a spanner in a hand are honestly subtracted. And you have been scheduling 180 hours of planned work into it - into a plant that has produced, week in and week out for years, about 36 hours of emergencies.

216 hours of real demand, pushed into a 180-hour week. Every week. The plan was never broken by the breakdowns. It was 120 per cent loaded before Monday morning, and it was broken by its own arithmetic.

My schedule is fiction by midweek, every week, and it is not because my people are slow. How do I choose the rules that decide what goes first - and build a schedule that survives the work I know is coming?

Before you read on, decide. Your crew has 180 hours in a week. You planned 180 hours of work into it. This plant has produced, week in and week out for years, about 36 hours of emergencies — and the records showing that have been there the whole time. By Wednesday the schedule is fiction and everyone blames the breakdowns or the pace of the fitters. And this morning you have four jobs and one crew: A takes 2 days and is due day 4; B takes 1 day, due day 10; C takes 4 days, due day 6; D takes 3 days, due day 8. You will just pick an order. Write down how you would build a schedule that survives the week — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two decisions nobody knew they were making

• “Push the crew — they need to work faster.” They would have to work a fifth again faster, permanently, to absorb what you have loaded. The plan is not failing because of pace, and telling them it is will cost you the people.

• “Reduce the emergencies — fix the underlying reliability.” Right, over a year or two, and it is not a scheduling answer. Meanwhile every week between now and then is planned the same broken way.

• “Reschedule as things come up — stay flexible.” This is what already happens, and it is why the plan is fiction by Wednesday. Continuous re-planning is not resilience; it is the absence of a plan, performed daily.

• “Overtime.” Buys the missing hours at a premium and makes the shortfall permanent by concealing it — the plan still says 180 and the truth is now 216 plus fatigue.

• “Prioritise better — do the important jobs first.” Nearly the right instinct with the crucial word undefined. Important by what measure? As the next paragraph shows, “important” conceals three different objectives that cannot all be served.

• Two decisions are being made here and nobody realised they were decisions. First, capacity: 180 hours of planned work plus 36 hours of work you know is coming is 216 hours pushed into 180 — 120 per cent loaded before Monday morning. The plan was never broken by the breakdowns; it was broken by its own arithmetic. The remedy is not exhortation but an explicit reserve sized from your own history, which turns a permanent overrun into a plan that can be met. Second, the order. The rule deciding what goes first is never neutral, because the objectives genuinely conflict: finishing the average job soonest, protecting every committed date, and being visibly fair are three different aims bought at each other's expense. Take your four jobs. Shortest-first gives B, A, D, C — the average job done in 5.00 days, and one date blown by four. Earliest-due-first gives A, C, D, B — average 6.75 days, worst lateness one. Same jobs, same crew, same week. A planner who has never named the objective has still chosen one, silently, and will defend the order without being able to say what it bought.

A robust schedule is two deliberate choices, not one hopeful list. Here they are.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the robust weekly schedule - the same crew, the same plant, designed for the week that actually happens.

1. What is this schedule FOR? (name the objective before choosing a rule)

Before any rule, name the objective, because the objectives CONFLICT and you cannot have them all. Do you most need THROUGHPUT (clear a swollen backlog, get the average job finished soonest, free the crew), or DUE-DATE PROTECTION (no single job badly late, commitments kept, statutory dates safe), or FAIRNESS (visible, defensible order in a regulated or contested setting)? Each is legitimate and each costs the others. A planner who has never named the objective has still chosen one - by habit, silently - and will defend a rule without being able to say what it buys. The objective this week at Corathyl: protect the commitments. That single sentence decides the next box.

2. Which rule serves it? (the sequencing rule encodes the objective)

Four jobs, one crew. A: 2 days, due day 4. B: 1 day, due day 10. C: 4 days, due day 6. D: 3 days, due day 8. SPT (shortest processing time first) gives B, A, D, C - completions 1, 3, 6, 10, so MEAN FLOW TIME 5.00 days, but lateness of -9, -1, -2, +4, so MAXIMUM LATENESS 4 days. EDD (earliest due date first) gives A, C, D, B - completions 2, 6, 9, 10, so MEAN FLOW TIME 6.75 days, with lateness -2, 0, +1, 0 and MAXIMUM LATENESS 1 day. SAME JOBS, SAME CREW, SAME WEEK. SPT finishes the average job 1.75 days sooner and blows one date by four; EDD gives up that speed and protects the dates. Also on the shelf: CRITICAL RATIO = time remaining until due / processing time remaining (lowest first; below 1 means already behind), which suits dynamic re-prioritising; MINIMUM SLACK = time until due minus remaining work, when the absolute buffer matters more than the ratio; and FCFS where fairness must be visible. We named due-date protection, so we take EDD - and we can now say what it cost.

3. Is the work even schedulable? (rules rank only what is ready)

A sequencing rule ranks work that is READY. It cannot conjure readiness, and this is where most so-called scheduling failures actually live. Before any job enters the sequence it must pass four gates: are the PARTS on site and reserved; is the PERMIT obtainable for that day; is there ACCESS - an agreed shutdown or isolation window when the plant will release the equipment; and is a COMPETENT person available with the right ticket. A job failing any gate is not a low-priority job, it is a NOT-YET-SCHEDULABLE job, and putting it in the sequence corrupts the whole week - the crew arrives, the part is not there, and everything behind it slips. Schedule the ready work, and put the unready work into planning, where its missing gate has an owner and a date.

4. What are you leaving for the work you know is coming? (load to the reserve)

Start from real capacity, not headline hours. Six artisans x 40 hours = 240 GROSS crew-hours. Apply an honest efficiency factor for travel, breaks and non-wrench activity - 75 per cent here - giving 180 available WRENCH-HOURS. Now load planned work to 80 per cent of that: 144 HOURS PLANNED, 36 HOURS RESERVED. And here is what the works had been doing instead: scheduling the full 180 planned hours into 180 available hours, in a plant whose emergent work has averaged 36 hours a week for years. That is 216 hours of real demand pushed into a 180-hour week - a schedule that was 120 per cent loaded before Monday, and therefore mathematically certain to fail. It was never broken by the breakdowns. It was broken by its own arithmetic.

5. Freeze it, measure it, re-tune it (the reserve must become evidence)

FREEZE the week once agreed, admitting only genuinely critical break-ins - a freeze that everyone may override is not a freeze, and the reserve exists precisely so that most emergent work no longer needs to break anything. MEASURE SCHEDULE COMPLIANCE: work orders completed in the week they were scheduled, divided by the work orders committed for that week; 90 per cent or above indicates a well-run programme, and the old system's 80 per cent came from completing only 144 of the 180 committed hours. Then RE-TUNE: record the emergent hours that actually arrived and resize the reserve from that record, so 36 is EVIDENCE rather than a guess and moves as the plant changes. Watch one thing above all - WHICH work gets displaced. It is almost always preventive work, because it is the only work that can be moved; and displacing it is how this month's shortage becomes next month's breakdown.

Before you read on, look only at the two sequences in box 2 and decide which is the better schedule. Argue it with yourself: one gets the average job done sooner, the other keeps its promises. Now look at what each one costs. That trade is the whole of this technique - and it is not a flaw in the rules, it is the reason you must choose one on purpose. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A schedule is not a prediction of a perfect week. It is a budgeted plan for a real one, resting on two choices: the rule that sequences the work, and the reserve that absorbs the shocks. Five questions:

  • What is this schedule for? Name the objective before touching a rule. Throughput, due-date protection and fairness conflict, and a planner who has not named one has still chosen one - by habit, silently.
  • Which rule serves it? A dispatching rule encodes the objective. SPT (shortest processing time) reduces work-in-progress and mean flow time - good for clearing an overloaded queue of small jobs when dates are flexible. EDD (earliest due date) is the most effective single rule for minimising maximum lateness - it protects commitments. Critical ratio, time remaining until due divided by processing time remaining, sequences lowest-first and flags anything below 1 as already behind, which suits dynamic re-prioritising. Minimum slack, time until due minus remaining work, is the same idea as an absolute buffer rather than a ratio. FCFS earns its place where fairness must be visible and defensible.
  • Is the work even schedulable? Rules rank only what is eligible: parts on site, permit obtainable, access or shutdown window agreed, competent person available. A job failing a gate is not low priority, it is not-yet-schedulable - and sequencing it corrupts everything behind it.
  • What are you leaving for the work you know is coming? Compute available capacity from headcount and hours with an honest efficiency factor - typically 75 to 85 per cent - then set schedule loading at roughly 80 to 90 per cent of that, holding the rest as explicit reserve for urgent corrective work.
  • How does the reserve become evidence? Freeze the week once agreed and admit only genuinely critical break-ins; measure schedule compliance - completed in the scheduled week over committed, with 90 per cent or above indicating a well-run programme; then re-size the reserve from the emergent hours that actually arrived.

One honest boundary. You are choosing and justifying the set of heuristics - the sequencing rules and the capacity reserve - that make a schedule robust. You are not building the logic network or finding the critical path, not operating the scheduling software, and not planning an individual job's scope. Those are separate techniques, and each of them will produce a schedule that shatters on Wednesday if this one has not been done.

Does it scale? A hospital theatre list is never filled to the last minute. They do not treat emergencies as a surprise; they treat them as a statistic, and they leave the capacity open on purpose. Doing anything else would not be ambition - it would be a decision to cancel someone's operation, made in advance, by arithmetic. Your maintenance week is the same list.

  1. Capacity. Compute gross hours, available wrench-hours, planned-work hours and the reserve - for the week, and per day.
  2. Eligibility. Which jobs are schedulable this week and which are not? Say what each unschedulable job is waiting for, and who should own that.
  3. Objective and rule. Name the objective for this week and recommend a sequencing rule for the eligible jobs, justifying it against the alternatives.
  4. Sequence and check. Give the day-by-day sequence, confirm it fits inside the planned hours, and state honestly which due dates you meet and which you miss.
  5. Compliance. State the schedule-compliance target you would commit to, and how you would use this week's result.

The test of a real answer: job R's date is fixed in law, not in preference - and this is where a rule applied without thinking will quietly betray you. Sequence the four eligible jobs by earliest due date and R lands on days 3 and 4, finishing a day after the date the law set. The rule did nothing wrong; it was never designed to protect a single immovable date, only to minimise the worst lateness across a set. R is not a candidate to be sequenced, it is a constraint to be scheduled around: block its 48 hours first, then sequence what is left into the space that remains.

And then face what that costs, because the honest answer is not free. Block R across days 2 and 3, put T on day 1, and V is pushed to day 4 - one day past its due date. You cannot meet everything this week, and pretending otherwise is how the fiction starts. The right answer is to miss V by a day, deliberately, having said so out loud on Friday - not to discover it on Thursday. If your sequence met every single date, check it again: 120 hours of eligible work into 120 planned hours leaves you no room to be lucky.

T5: The Guardrails

Schedules fail in ways that look like bad luck and are almost always arithmetic.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Loading to 100 per cent of capacity. The original sin. A schedule with no reserve is not ambitious, it is a plan that has already decided which work will be sacrificed - it just has not told you which yet.

• Using gross crew-hours as capacity. Headcount times hours is not wrench-time. Without an honest efficiency factor every schedule is overloaded before the first rule is applied.

• Choosing a rule by habit. If you cannot say what the schedule is optimising, you cannot defend the sequence - and you are still optimising something, just not on purpose.

• Believing one rule is universally best. SPT and EDD both win, at different things. The trade is real and permanent; the skill is choosing which side of it you want this week.

• Sequencing work that is not eligible. The week then fails on parts, permits and access rather than on priorities, and the failure gets blamed on the schedule instead of on planning.

• A freeze everyone can override. If every break-in is admitted the freeze is decorative. The reserve exists so that most emergent work does not need to break anything at all.

• Never measuring schedule compliance. Then the reserve is a guess that never improves, and nobody can tell a bad week from a bad system.

• Letting a heuristic near a hard date. Statutory, safety and legally fixed dates are constraints, not candidates. Block them first and sequence around them.

• Always displacing the preventive work. It is the only work that can move, so it always moves - and that is precisely how a reactive spiral starts: this month's displaced PM is next month's emergent hours, which displace more PM.

Onalenna's people were never slow. Her schedule was 120 per cent loaded before Monday and no amount of effort could rescue it. Name what the schedule is for; choose the sequencing rule that serves that objective and be able to say what it cost you; sequence only work that is genuinely ready; compute capacity honestly and hold back an explicit reserve for the emergencies you can already predict; then freeze the week, measure compliance, and let the real emergent hours re-size the reserve. A schedule loaded to the last hour is not ambitious - it is fiction with a start date. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take last week's real schedule and do the two sums: available wrench-hours, and the hours of emergent work that actually arrived.
