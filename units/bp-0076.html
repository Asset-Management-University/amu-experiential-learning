Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Nkosazana, the sustainability manager for a large commercial property group. You are standing in the marble lobby of head office. To your left is a huge, beautifully designed banner: "Halving our energy use by 2030." It is your project, your promise - you helped write the press release, and you feel a surge of pride every time you see it.

Your phone buzzes: the quarterly capital-spend report, hot off the press. You scan the approved replacements and your eyes land on one line - "Building 7: replace 4 rooftop air-conditioning units." You recognise the supplier and the model instantly: the cheapest on the market, and notoriously inefficient, known for guzzling electricity. They will lock in high energy use for the next fifteen years.

The pride you felt moments ago curdles in your stomach. The beautiful promise on the banner and the hard-edged decision in the spreadsheet on your phone exist in two completely different universes. They have never met. One is a hopeful dream; the other is the expensive, energy-wasting reality your company just bought.

We made a proud public promise, and then quietly bought the exact opposite - how do I make our big commitment actually change what we buy and do?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fight words with words: "we need a better report, a bigger campaign." You spend a month on a stunning new brochure with bolder graphs and an internal awareness week. People say it looks great - but the engineer in Building 7 still has a form to fill out, and the most important box on it is "upfront cost." The brochure does not change the form. Your next attempt is to plead: you email managers and procurement, "please remember to think sustainably." You get polite replies - "will do, Nkosazana" - but "think sustainably" is a vague wish, and when a buyer has a target to cut 5% off upfront cost and a choice between an R80,000 standard unit and an R110,000 efficient one, your wish loses every time. Finally you fight for months and win a small, high-profile solar showpiece on the head-office roof: a ribbon-cutting, a great photo. It feels like a win, but a hollow one - while everyone admires ten panels on one roof, the other fifty buildings quietly replace failing kit with the same cheapest-first models. You have built a green chapel while the rest of the city goes up in the same old grey bricks.

So before you read on: what is the point of a louder promise or a single symbol if the system underneath keeps deciding the opposite? What actually closes the gap?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you realise a promise is not delivered by announcements, but by the thousands of small decisions it changes. A strategy is just a rumour until it shows up as a line in a budget, a field on a form, or a rule in a buying manual. Your promise to "halve energy" cannot live on a banner - it has to live in the spec sheet for the next air-conditioning unit. You have to connect the planets. Five moves:

  • See it - strategy is delivered by decisions, not announcements: the breakthrough is to realise a promise is not delivered by the announcement but by the thousands of small decisions it changes. A strategy is just a rumour until it shows up as a line item in a budget, a field on a form, or a rule in a purchasing manual. 'We will halve our energy' cannot live on a banner in the lobby; it has to live in the spec sheet for the next air-conditioning unit, the next pump, the next vehicle. The boardroom planet (the promise) and the boiler-room planet (the purchase order) have simply never met - so the asset base keeps quietly delivering the opposite of what you publicly committed to. Everything that follows is about connecting those two planets: making the promise reach the exact moment a real choice is made.
  • Translate the promise into a decision criterion: a promise that stays a slogan changes nothing; it has to become a concrete, non-negotiable rule that bites at the point of choice. Translate 'halve our energy' into something a buyer can actually apply: 'every new asset over a set value is selected on its whole-life cost - the purchase price PLUS the projected energy and running cost over, say, ten years - not the sticker price alone.' Now the vague aspiration is an instruction. The same works for any promise: 'safety first' becomes a specific spec the new tool must meet; 'cut our water' becomes a litres-per-unit threshold on new equipment. The test of a real commitment is whether you can write down the decision rule it implies - if you cannot, it is still just a wish.
  • Put it where the decision is made, and give it an owner: a rule that lives in a report nobody on the floor reads is no rule at all. Put the criterion exactly where the decision actually happens: build the whole-life-cost calculation into the capital-request form as a mandatory field so a request cannot even be submitted without it; write it into the procurement rules and the equipment specs; place it on the approval gate. And give it a named owner - the head of engineering personally signs off the whole-life-cost comparison for any major purchase, so it is part of someone's job, not everyone's vague good intention. This is bp-0069's discipline of embedding a standard into the real system: the promise has to be designed into the workflow, not bolted on as an afterthought.
  • Measure the asset-level reality and show the gap: stop publishing glossy reports about intentions and start measuring choices. Replace the brochure with a simple one-page monthly dashboard sent to the operations leadership: 'new assets this month: 12; assets meeting the whole-life-cost rule: 9; gap: 25 percent.' Suddenly the say-do gap is visible to everyone who can do something about it, in the language of actual decisions rather than slogans. Measure what was BOUGHT, not what was promised - the percentage of purchases that met the criterion, the projected energy locked in this quarter. This is the partner of bp-0150 (lead indicators that warn you before the auditor does): a visible gap is a gap that gets closed, because no leader wants to keep signing off a number that contradicts the banner in their own lobby.
  • Align the incentives with the promise: this is the move without which the others quietly fail: if the person making the choice is still rewarded only for speed and lowest upfront cost, the promise loses every single decision, no matter how good the rule or the dashboard. So change what 'a good decision' is measured as. If procurement's bonus is 100 percent based on upfront savings, a slice of it must shift to the whole-life savings achieved across the portfolio - so the efficient unit that costs more today but saves far more over its life becomes the choice that is rewarded, not punished. People deliver what they are measured on; until the promise changes how 'a good job' is judged, it is asking them to act against their own interest, and that is a fight the banner will always lose.

See it transform the group. "Halve our energy" becomes a hard rule: every new asset over R50,000 is chosen on whole-life cost - purchase price plus ten years of projected energy - not the sticker price. The calculation is built into the capital-request software as a mandatory field, signed off by the head of engineering; a plain monthly dashboard ("new assets: 12; meeting the rule: 9; gap: 25 percent") goes to all of operations leadership; and a slice of procurement's bonus shifts from upfront savings to whole-life savings. Now when the next air-conditioning unit needs replacing, the form forces the engineer to compare the R80,000 unit with the R110,000 efficient one - and shows that the cheap unit will cost an extra R250,000 in electricity over ten years. The choice is suddenly obvious; the efficient unit is approved. The promise is kept - not by a banner, but by a purchase order. A strategy is only as real as the decisions it changes. To make a promise matter, translate it from a slogan into a non-negotiable criterion inside the real systems - the spec sheet, the budget form, the purchase order - that shape everyday choices; put it where the decision is made with a named owner; measure the choices, not the slogans, and show the gap; and align the incentives so the right choice is the rewarded one. Connect the boardroom planet to the boiler-room planet, or your promise is just empty space.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - think about a personal promise like "I'm going to get healthy this year."

See where the promise actually has to live

On the first of January, "get healthy" is your big, bold strategy. But it is just words. The decision does not happen on the first of January - it happens at seven o'clock on a tired Tuesday evening, standing in the supermarket aisle with a frozen pizza in one hand and a bag of salad in the other. That is the real asset decision.

Your promise to "get healthy" is useless in that aisle unless you have translated it into a concrete rule for that exact moment - something like "no processed food on weekdays" or "half my basket must be fresh vegetables." Without that specific rule embedded at the point of choice, the promise is a vague hope that habit and convenience will beat every single time. It only becomes real when it changes what you put in the basket - not what you declared on New Year's Day.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at a promise or core value in your own organisation - "safety first", "we are customer-obsessed", "zero harm". Now ask yourself, honestly: does that promise live on a different planet from your team's everyday decisions?

  1. Translate it. What does "safety first" mean for the choice of a new contractor, tool or system? Write it as a clear, simple decision rule.
  2. Embed it. Where is that decision actually made? Put the rule on the checklist, in the tender document, on the approval gate - make it impossible to skip.
  3. Measure it. Do not measure slogans, measure choices: track "percentage of new tools purchased that meet the highest safety spec," and make the gap visible.
  4. Align it. Is the person choosing rewarded for speed and cost, or for honouring the promise? Fix the incentive so it rewards the promise.

This was never about a careless procurement team - they were doing exactly what the form and the bonus told them to do; the failure was a promise that never reached the place the decision was made. A promise is not a statement of fact - it is a commitment to a new way of deciding, and your job is to build the bridges that let those new decisions actually happen, connecting the world of what you say with the world of what you do.
