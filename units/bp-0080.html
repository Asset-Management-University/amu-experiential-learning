Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Ayabonga, the asset planning lead at a manganese mine and ore-processing site out on the dry inland plains. You walk into the boardroom and the air-conditioner is humming too loud against the heat already pressing on the windows. On the table in front of you is a single bound document, 38 pages, with a blue cover: "Beneficiation Plant - Capital Approval Submission, R420 million." A half-drunk cup of rooibos sits beside it, going cold.

The plant would take your raw manganese ore and upgrade it into a higher-value product before it leaves the gate. Right now you sell raw ore. Beneficiated ore sells for much more - when the market is hot. When the market is flat, the extra processing barely pays for itself. Across the table sits Zweli, the commercial director. He taps the blue cover. "The board wants a yes or a no on Thursday," he says. "Not a maybe." He has staked his year on getting this expansion moving.

The submission runs a single calculation: build the full plant now, assume the manganese price holds at $4.80 per dry metric tonne unit, discount the cash flows at 12%, and you get a net present value of plus R36 million. Positive. Approve it. The number sits there in bold, looking certain. But you have watched this price for eleven years - you have seen it at $7.20 and seen it crash to $2.90 inside eighteen months. The $4.80 is not a fact; it is a guess dressed up as one. And the whole R420 million goes in up front, before anyone knows which way the price will move.

You also know three things the single calculation does not capture. You do not have to decide today - you hold a signed option on the land and water rights for two more years. The plant could be built in two phases: a R180 million first module handling half the ore, and a second R260 million module added later only if the first proves the market - a total staged cost of R440 million, a R20 million premium over the all-in R420 million build, which is the price you pay for the flexibility it buys. And if the price collapses after Phase 1, you could mothball the module and recover some money. The blue document prices the plant. It does not price your freedom to wait, to start small and grow, or to walk away - and that freedom feels like it is worth real money you just cannot see on any page.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The temptation is right there in your hand: the NPV is positive, the deadline is Thursday, Zweli wants a clean yes - just sign off the R36 million number and be done. "Argue the forecast - maybe $4.80 is too optimistic, push it to $4.20 and re-run." But haggling over the single guess keeps you inside the trap: one fixed number is still one fixed number, and you have watched this price swing by more than double - no single value is honest. "Trust the NPV - it is positive, that is what the tool is for." But a static NPV silently assumes you make every decision today and then sit still whatever the price does, and that is simply not true: you can wait, you can stage, you can stop. "Give the board the clean yes they asked for." But the clean yes commits R420 million up front and rides whatever the market does - a 25% kind of chance of a ruinous loss with no way out, sold as certainty.

So before you read on: the question is not "is the NPV positive?" Name what the blue document leaves out, and what your freedom to wait, grow or stop is actually worth.

The blue document runs a STATIC net present value: one fixed forecast, one path, build-everything-now versus do-nothing. NPV is a sound tool, but used this way it treats the future as a single known road and quietly assumes you make ALL your decisions today and then sit still, whatever the price does. Reality is not like that - you hold three rights you can use LATER, after seeing more: the right to WAIT (the two-year option), the right to EXPAND (add the second module only if Phase 1 proves the market), and the right to ABANDON (mothball and recover something if the price collapses). These rights have a name: REAL OPTIONS - managerial flexibility to defer, expand or abandon. The reason the static NPV mispriced the project is that it left the value of these options out entirely: a right you can use only when things go your way, and decline when they do not, ALWAYS has positive value. So do not argue about whether $4.80 is the right guess - replace the single guess with a rough spread of futures, and value each course of action across them. The question is not 'is the NPV positive?' but 'what is my freedom to wait, grow or stop actually worth?'
Five moves turn the trap into a decision. (1) FRAME THE STATIC-NPV TRAP - name that the submission prices one fixed forecast, all-or-nothing, and wrongly assumes every decision is made now and then you sit still. (2) MAP THE PLAUSIBLE FUTURES - replace the single $4.80 guess with a rough weighted spread: Strong 35% at $6.50, Flat 40% at $4.80, Weak 25% at $3.20; the point is not a perfect forecast, it is to stop pretending the future is one number. (3) IDENTIFY YOUR REAL OPTIONS - name the rights the static NPV ignores: WAIT (the two-year option), EXPAND (the second module only if Phase 1 pays), ABANDON (mothball and recover). (4) VALUE THE FLEXIBILITY GAP - score each course across the futures and take the chance-weighted average AND the worst case. Full plant now: +R210m / +R36m / -R190m, expected value +R40.4m, but a 25% chance of a -R190m WOUND with no way out. Staged Phase 1: +R175m / +R28m / -R45m, expected value +R61.2m, worst case a -R45m BRUISE. The roughly R21m gap is, literally, the value of flexibility - the worth of expanding only in good futures and stopping in the bad. (5) STRESS-TEST THE CRITICAL ASSUMPTION - the staged worst case leans entirely on recovering R70m by selling the mothballed module to a sister operation; set that as a success line in advance, then test it the cheapest honest way (phone the buyer), because a weaker, true case beats a stronger, false one. The true value of a big investment is not in a single forecast but in the priced-in flexibility to wait, grow or stop as the real future unfolds.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough is to stop arguing the forecast and start pricing your freedom. The blue document ran a static net present value - one fixed guess, one path - which quietly assumes you decide everything today and then sit still. But you hold rights you can use later, after seeing more, and those rights have a name: real options. Five moves:

  • Frame the static-NPV trap: the first move is to name exactly what is wrong with the blue document. It runs a STATIC net present value - one fixed price forecast, one path, build-everything-now versus do-nothing - and although NPV is a sound tool, used this way it treats the future as a single known road. Worse, it quietly assumes the team will make ALL its decisions today and then sit perfectly still, no matter what the manganese price does over the years the plant runs. That assumption is the trap: it is false to how real projects unfold, and it is the reason a fragile guess ($4.80, a price you have personally watched swing from $7.20 to $2.90) can masquerade as a confident R36 million 'yes'. You do not defeat this by arguing the guess should be $4.50 or $5.10; you defeat it by rejecting the shape of the question. The decision is not 'all in, now, on one number, or nothing' - it never was. Naming that frees you to value what the document made invisible: your freedom to act later, after you have seen more.
  • Map the plausible futures: you cannot value flexibility against a single number, so replace the one guess with an honest, rough picture of uncertainty. Working with the commercial team, you say: over the next couple of years the price will land in roughly one of three states - a Strong market (about a 35% chance, around $6.50), a Flat market (about 40%, around $4.80), or a Weak market (about 25%, around $3.20). This is deliberately rough, and that is the point: you are not trying to forecast the future perfectly, you are simply refusing to pretend it is one fixed number. Three crude futures with rough chances that sum to a hundred are enough - they convert a false certainty into an honest spread. And once the future is a spread rather than a point, something becomes visible that the single number hid completely: your choices behave DIFFERENTLY in the good futures and the bad ones, and that difference is where flexibility lives.
  • Identify your real options: now name the rights the static NPV left out - the things you can legally choose to do LATER, once the market has shown more of its hand. There are three. The right to WAIT: you hold a signed two-year option on the land and water rights, so you can watch before you commit. The right to EXPAND: the engineers can build in two phases - a R180m first module that handles half the ore, then a second R260m module added later ONLY if Phase 1 proves the market (a total staged cost of R440m if fully built, a R20m premium over the all-in R420m build - part of the price you pay for flexibility). And the right to ABANDON: if the price collapses after Phase 1, you can mothball the module and recover some of the cost. These rights have a name - they are REAL OPTIONS, managerial flexibility to defer, expand or abandon, valued the way a financial option is valued. And the key to why they are worth money is this: a right you exercise ONLY when it pays you, and decline when it would not, can only ever add value - you keep the upside and shed the downside. Ignoring them underprices good staged projects and overprices 'all-in now' ones, every time.
  • Value the flexibility gap: now do the arithmetic that makes flexibility visible in money. Score each course of action across all three futures, then take the chance-weighted average (the expected value) AND look hard at the worst case. OPTION A - build the full R420m plant now: Strong +R210m, Flat +R36m (the submission's number), Weak -R190m (you run at a loss, locked in). Expected value = 0.35x210 + 0.40x36 + 0.25x(-190) = +R40.4m. It looks fine on average - but a 25% chance of a -R190m WOUND, with no way out. OPTION B - build Phase 1 (R180m), then decide Phase 2 once the market reveals itself: Strong, build then expand, +R175m; Flat, build but do not expand, +R28m; Weak, abandon and mothball, -R45m (a bruise). Expected value = 0.35x175 + 0.40x28 + 0.25x(-45) = +R61.2m. Staged wins on the average AND turns the -R190m wound into a -R45m bruise. The roughly R21m gap between B and A IS the value of flexibility, named in money - the worth of being able to expand only in good futures and stop in the bad. Flexibility is not free (staging earns less in the single strong forecast, +R175m vs +R210m, and costs a little more per tonne to build in two goes) - but that is a small known price paid to remove a large unknown one.
  • Stress-test the critical assumption: a spreadsheet that prefers Option B is not yet a decision that survives the real world. So before the board, find the ONE assumption your best option's worst case rests on, and test it. Here it is plain: staging's -R45m bruise leans entirely on recovering R70m by selling the mothballed module to a sister operation. Set that as a success line in advance and write it down so you cannot move the goalposts: 'staging only wins if that R70m recovery is real.' Then test it the cheapest honest way - not by building anything, but by phoning the sister operation's planning lead and asking plainly: if we mothball this in eighteen months, would you actually buy it, and at what price? The answer is the surprise: they would commit only about R45m, in writing - the R70m was an engineer's replacement-cost ESTIMATE, not a price anyone would pay. Re-run with R45m: Option B's worst case worsens to about -R70m and its expected value eases to about +R55m. The decision STILL holds (+R55m beats +R40m; a -R70m bruise still beats a -R190m wound) - but the margin is thinner than the clean spreadsheet promised, so you change not the recommendation but the CASE: you build the board's number on the R45m the sister operation will actually commit to (with a letter of intent on file), not the soft R70m. A weaker, true case beats a stronger, false one, every time.

See it come together for Ayabonga. You build the picture across three rough futures - Strong (35%, $6.50), Flat (40%, $4.80), Weak (25%, $3.20) - and value each course in money. The full R420m plant averages +R40.4m but carries a 25% chance of a -R190m wound with no exit. Staged Phase 1 averages +R61.2m and turns that wound into a -R45m bruise; the roughly R21m gap is the value of flexibility, named at last. Then you test the one assumption it rests on - the R70m mothball recovery - by phoning the sister operation, and learn they would commit only R45m in writing; the staged case eases to about +R55m but still clearly beats all-in, so you change not the recommendation but the case you make for it, backing the board's number with a real letter of intent instead of a soft estimate. On Thursday you do not give the blue document's clean yes; you give a better one: approve R180 million for Phase 1 now, with Phase 2 and the mothball route written in as decisions to take in twelve to eighteen months once the market shows its hand. A big decision priced as all-in-now on a single forecast leaves out the worth of your freedom to wait, grow or stop. Replace the one guess with a spread of futures, name the rights you can use only when they pay, and the gap in money between staged and all-in is the price of your flexibility - then test the one assumption your worst case rests on before you commit, because a weaker, true case beats a stronger, false one. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - forget the mine, and picture a house.

See the same trap wearing a very different coat

You have just been offered a good job in a city three hours away, and you and your partner love the look of one suburb there. A property agent sends a clean one-pager: a R1.6 million townhouse, bond repayments that "work out cheaper than renting over ten years", and a note that prices in that suburb "have only ever gone up". The pull is strong - buy now, settle in, stop paying someone else's bond. Renting first feels like throwing money away.

But look at what the one-pager rests on, against the blue document at the mine. It stands on ONE forecast - prices keep rising, the new job works out, you will want to stay - and it quietly assumes you decide everything now: city, suburb, house, all in one signature. That is the same shape as "build the full plant on a $4.80 guess". And where are your rights to act later? Renting for a year IS your right to WAIT - you keep your money free while you learn whether you even like the city and the job. A shorter lease or a smaller place is your right to START SMALL AND GROW. And leaving at the end of a lease - instead of being stuck selling a townhouse into a flat market - is your right to STOP AND RECOVER. Picture the three futures: Strong (you love it, prices rise - the buyer wins this one), Flat (it is fine, prices drift - buying and renting come out close, but the buyer is locked in and the renter is free), Weak (the job sours or you hate the winters, and the buyer must sell a R1.6m house at a loss while the renter simply gives notice and walks). Renting earns "less" in the strong future - the small known price of lost upside - but it turns a possible WOUND into a BRUISE. A large, hard-to-reverse commitment dressed up by a single confident forecast is the same trap whether it is a R420 million plant or a R1.6 million house. The coat is different; the body underneath is the same.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The trap you just felt is not really about a manganese plant. It is this: a big decision was priced as if it had to be made all at once, on one guess about the future, with no room to change course - so the value of being able to wait, grow or stop was left out. That pattern lives almost everywhere capital and commitment meet uncertainty. Find it in your own world:

  1. Name a current "all-in now" decision. One large, hard-to-reverse commitment you are weighing - a machine, a building, a system, a hire, a course of study, a market, a house - and write down the single forecast being used to justify it.
  2. Surface the future you are pretending is fixed. The one number or assumption that could go either way - sketch three rough futures (strong, middling, weak) and a rough chance for each. You are not predicting; you are admitting the spread.
  3. List the rights you actually hold. Could you WAIT? Could you START SMALLER AND GROW only if it works (build half, pilot first, lease before you buy)? Could you WALK AWAY part-way and recover something? Each is a piece of flexibility the single forecast ignored.
  4. Compare staged against all-in, and put the trade-off honestly. Where does the all-in worst case hurt most, and does staging turn that wound into a bruise? What does staging cost you in lost upside - and is that a small known price worth paying to remove a large unknown one?

The world changes; the trap, and the way out, do not. The true value of a major investment is not found in a single forecast, but in the priced-in flexibility to expand, abandon or wait as the real future unfolds - so commit the smallest sensible first slice, buy yourself a real decision point, and keep the right to grow or to stop.
