Speak money

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have just done some of your best work. A critical pump is failing too often, and you have found the answer: a new one - more efficient, more reliable, better throughput. Your engineering case is airtight. You recommend buying it outright for R1,200,000, walk into the budget meeting confident, and watch the finance director reject it flat.

Not because your engineering is wrong. She does not even argue the engineering. She rejects it because, to her, your recommendation was a black box with a big number on it. You never said what spending R1.2m in one go would do to the company's cash this year - cash that is already tight. You never mentioned that the pump would sit on the books as an asset, slowly losing value for years. You never offered her the obvious alternative - leasing it - that would have kept the cash free. Your best technical work died on the table, and you walked out stung, realising the problem was not your engineering at all.

My engineering was right and it still got rejected - because I could not explain what it would do to the company's money. How do I make the same technical case in a way that finance will actually approve?

Before you read on, decide. The pump is the right pump. Nobody in the room disputed a single technical point. It costs R1,200,000, and the finance director said the company cannot take that much cash out this year — and that was the end of it. She was not hostile and she was not wrong. You never mentioned that the same pump could have been had another way, because it did not occur to you that how it was paid for was your business at all. Write down what you would put in front of her next time — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the three questions she was actually asking

• “Make the technical case more strongly.” She agreed with the technical case. Repeating a point nobody contested, louder, is the most common response to this rejection and the least useful.

• “Quantify the risk of not doing it — show what a failure costs.” Genuinely necessary, and it answers “is this worth doing”, which was never the objection. Her objection was that the money is not there this year, and a bigger benefit does not create cash.

• “Find a cheaper pump.” Solves the wrong problem and usually creates yours — you would be trading away the engineering that was the one uncontested part of the proposal.

• “Split it across two budget years.” Closer, and it is a workaround improvised on her side of the table rather than an option properly put. It also often does not survive contact with how the spend has to be accounted for.

• “Escalate — go over her head.” Spends your credit to overrule the person whose job is precisely this judgement, on a question you have not yet shown you understand.

• Here is what she was actually weighing, and it is three separate questions, not one: how much cash leaves the business and when; what it does to reported profit each year; and what appears on the books as something owned. The same pump answers those three very differently depending on how it is funded. Buy it and a large sum leaves now, the profit statement takes only a slow annual charge across its life, and the company owns an asset. Rent its use instead and far less cash moves at any one time, the full annual amount hits profit every year, and nothing goes on the books — flexibility instead of ownership. Neither is right; they are different shapes of the same commitment, and which shape suits depends on which constraint is tightest this year, which is her expertise and not yours. Two honesties make it a case rather than a sales pitch: spreading the cash costs more in total, and you must say so; and since the engineering is identical either way, putting both routes in front of her strengthens your case — you are no longer asking her to approve a purchase, you are handing her a decision she is equipped to make.

There is a simple one-page tool that turns a technical recommendation into a financial one. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the page that gets good engineering approved - the same pump, laid out as a money decision, so finance can see exactly what each choice does to cash, profit, and the balance sheet.

1. The asset - and the two ways to fund it

The same asset, two ways to fund it: a critical new pump, P-701, priced at R1,200,000. You can BUY it outright (CAPEX) or LEASE it (OPEX). The engineering is identical either way - but the money lands completely differently.

2. Route 1 - BUY (CAPEX)

Route 1 - BUY (CAPEX, capital expenditure): R1,200,000 cash out up front. It is CAPITALISED - it goes onto the balance sheet as an asset you own - and then DEPRECIATED: spread as a R200,000/year expense on the profit statement over its 6-year life. You own it at the end.

3. Route 2 - LEASE (OPEX)

Route 2 - LEASE (OPEX, operating expenditure): R250,000 per year, EXPENSED in full each year straight through the profit statement - nothing capitalised, no big upfront cash. You own nothing, but you stay flexible: upgrade or walk away.

4. The impact compared - cash, profit, balance sheet

Side by side. CASH: buying drains R1.2m now; leasing drains R250k a year. PROFIT: buying lowers reported profit by only R200k/year (the depreciation); leasing lowers it by R250k/year. BALANCE SHEET: buying adds a R1.2m asset; leasing adds nothing. (Note the total: leasing over 6 years = R1.5m vs buying R1.2m - smoothing the cash has a price.)

5. The recommendation - in the language of money

The recommendation in MONEY language, not just 'it is the better pump'. For example: 'With cash tight this year, I recommend the OPEX lease - it preserves R950,000 of cash now and keeps us flexible, at a cost of R50,000 more profit impact a year. If cash were strong and we wanted the asset long-term, buying wins on total cost.' THAT is the case finance will approve.

Look at what it does. It does not change the engineering at all - the pump is the pump. It simply shows the same asset funded two ways, and what each way does to the things finance actually watches. Onalenna's recommendation was not wrong; it was just silent on all of this. Now let us walk through the language.

T3: The Blueprint

The big idea is simple and powerful: how you fund an asset changes everything for the company's financial reports, even when the asset itself is identical. There are two routes, and they have names. Each part answers a question:

  • How do we pay - all at once, or smoothly? Buying outright is CAPEX (capital expenditure): a big single cash payment. Leasing is OPEX (operating expenditure): a smooth, regular cost.
  • Where does it live in the accounts? A CAPEX purchase is capitalised - it becomes an asset on the balance sheet (the list of what the company owns). An OPEX lease is expensed - it just runs through the profit statement as a cost and is gone; nothing is owned.
  • What does it do to our profit? Here is the part that surprises everyone. The R1.2m purchase does not hit this year's profit by R1.2m. It is spread out as depreciation - R200,000 a year over the pump's six-year life. The lease, by contrast, lowers profit by its full R250,000 every year.
  • What does it do to our cash? And here is the twist that catches people out: cash and profit are not the same thing. The purchase drains R1.2m of cash right now, but only dents profit by R200k this year. The lease drains cash gently - R250k a year - matching its profit hit. A company can be profitable and still run out of cash; this is exactly how.

So which is right? Neither, always. Buying builds an owned asset but ties up a lot of cash now and costs R1.2m. Leasing keeps cash free and flexible but costs more over time (R250k x 6 = R1.5m) and leaves you owning nothing. The "right" answer depends on the company's cash position, its tax, and its strategy - and your job is to lay that trade-off out clearly.

Does it scale? The choice between buying this pump or leasing it is the exact same decision a business makes when it chooses to buy its fleet of vehicles or lease them, or to build its own data centre or simply pay for cloud computing - the same CAPEX-versus-OPEX call, everywhere money meets equipment.

T4: Your Turn

Now make a funding call of your own. You need a new high-performance laptop for your final two years of study, and there are two ways to get it:

  • Option 1 - buy it outright (CAPEX-like): R30,000 cash, now. You own it.
  • Option 2 - a 24-month subscription (OPEX-like): R1,500 a month. You own nothing, but you can upgrade.

Build a one-page decision sheet: the two routes side by side, comparing the upfront cash, the monthly budget impact, the total cost, and what you own at the end. Then recommend one - in the language of money.

Check yourself: the subscription totals R1,500 x 24 = R36,000 - that is R6,000 more than buying. So the honest case is a trade-off: "I recommend buying to save R6,000 overall and own it" OR "I recommend the subscription to keep my R30,000 free now and stay flexible, accepting R6,000 more over two years." Either can be right - what matters is that you said why in cash-and-cost terms.

T5: The Guardrails

Speaking money is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you make your next funding case.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Confusing cash with profit. The single biggest one. A R1.2m purchase hits cash by R1.2m now, but profit by only R200k this year (the depreciation). They are not the same number, and a company can be profitable yet cashless. Always show both.

• Thinking one route is always better. CAPEX is not "bad" - it builds an owned asset base. OPEX is not "good" - it can cost more over time and builds no ownership. The right choice depends entirely on the company's cash, tax, and strategy.

• Forgetting depreciation is a real expense. It moves no cash, but it genuinely lowers reported profit (and the asset's book value) every year for the asset's whole life. It is not free just because no money leaves the bank.

• Quoting only the sticker price. The lease looks cheaper each year (R250k vs R1.2m) but over six years it costs more (R1.5m vs R1.2m). Show the upfront cash, the yearly impact, and the total - the trade-off lives in the gap between them.

Onalenna's pump was the right pump. What failed her was not her engineering but her silence on the money - and that is a fixable thing. Now you hold the tool: take any asset decision, lay the funding routes side by side, and show what each does to cash, to profit, and to the balance sheet, then recommend in those terms. Do that, and your good engineering stops dying in budget meetings - because you will have learned to carry it across the one bridge that always decides: the language of money.
