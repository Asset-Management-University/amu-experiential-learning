Signal or noise

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you are staring at a screen full of jitter. A vibration sensor on a critical pump feeds you a reading every minute, and it never sits still - it bounces around 4.0 all day, up to 4.6, down to 3.5, up to 5.0, back again. Noise. Always noise.

And you are trapped between two ways of failing. Every time it jumps to 5, your instinct is to send a crew - but the last three times you did, they found nothing, and now they sigh when you call. Cry wolf often enough and people stop coming. But the opposite terrifies you more: that somewhere in all this scatter, the baseline is slowly creeping upward as a bearing quietly dies - a real, developing fault - and you will wave it off as "just noise" until the day the pump tears itself apart. You have learned to find hidden failures and to trend a signal over time, but here, eyeballing this restless chart, you genuinely cannot tell a real threat from a ghost.

My sensor data is so noisy that a real developing fault and pure random scatter look identical in any single reading. How do I tell when the asset's behaviour has ACTUALLY changed - so I act on the real shifts and stop chasing the noise?

Before you read on, decide. The chart bounces all day. Last time you called it, you stopped a machine on a spike and found nothing — and it cost you, because now your colleague waves everything off as noise. Looking at today's chart you can see a lone reading of 5.0 that leapt out and dropped straight back, and a stretch further along sitting around 6.5 and staying there, against a long history that hovers near 4.0. One of those is a ghost. One of them is a bearing that will tear the pump apart. Write down how you would tell which is which — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the eye reliably picks the wrong one

• “Set an alarm limit and act whenever it is crossed.” This is what burned you. Random scatter will step over any limit you draw from time to time — that is what random means — so you will keep stopping machines and finding nothing, and each time it costs you a little more of the credibility you need for the real one.

• “Take more readings, more often.” More data is genuinely worth having and it does not answer the question. A denser chart of the same noise still shows you spikes, and now more of them.

• “Smooth it — average it out so the trend shows.” Closer, and it buys clarity with delay: averaging hides the scatter and it hides the beginning of the real shift too, which is exactly the part you needed early.

• “Watch it a while longer and see what develops.” Sometimes right, and as a policy it is how the second failure happens — waiting has no end condition, so “let us see” quietly becomes “we never acted”.

• “Trust the judgement — I know this machine.” The eye goes to the biggest spike, and the biggest spike is usually the meaningless one. That is not a failure of experience; it is what noise does to everybody's eye.

• The way through is to stop judging readings and start judging against what normal actually looks like here — not just its usual value but how much it ordinarily wanders, which lets you draw a band of ordinary behaviour. Inside the band, the thing is breathing. But the band on its own is not enough, because scatter will occasionally step outside it by chance, and that is precisely the excursion that gets chased. So the second half of the test is persistence: a real change does not step out and come back, it goes and stays. Your 5.0 leapt and returned. Your 6.5 went and stayed. Drama is not evidence; duration is. And what that gives you is better than an alarm — it is a verdict with a date attached: the behaviour changed here, and normal is now this.

There is a discipline that answers exactly this, and turns a jittery chart into a clear verdict. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the one page that turns the jitter into a decision - it draws the line between "normal noise" and "something genuinely changed", and tells you exactly when to act.

1. The noisy signal - and the two ways to fail

The signal is noisy - it bounces around all day. Two ways to fail: react to every random spike (cry wolf, lose trust), or let the noise hide a slow real shift (miss the developing fault). The whole skill is telling one from the other.

2. The baseline - what is normal here?

What is NORMAL here? The historical mean (4.0 mm/s) and the band of normal noise around it - the control limits, here mean +/- 3 standard deviations = 2.8 to 5.2 mm/s. Anything inside this band is just the asset breathing.

3. The persistence test - outside the band, AND staying?

Is a reading OUTSIDE the band - AND does it STAY there? A single point poking out (a lone 5.0 is still inside; even a one-off spike) is noise. A run of points that stay high is the tell. Persistence, not drama, is the signal.

4. The change-point - the real shift

The change-point: the moment the underlying behaviour truly shifted - here a sustained rise to about 6.5 that holds, well beyond the 5.2 limit. That is not noise; the asset is now living in a new, worse regime - a developing fault.

5. The verdict and action

The verdict: 'A genuine change in behaviour was detected - the baseline has shifted from 4.0 to 6.5. ACTION: inspect the pump bearing.' And the lone 5.0 spike: 'Noise, within the band - ignore.' Act on the real shift; ignore the wiggle.

See what it does. It does not try to explain every wiggle. It establishes what normal looks like, draws a band around it, and then watches for the one thing that matters: a move that goes beyond the band and stays there. The lone spike to 5.0 - ignored. The sustained climb to 6.5 - caught, named, acted on. Onalenna's restless chart just became a clear yes-or-no. Now let us walk through the discipline.

T3: The Blueprint

This is time-series change-point detection - the discipline of telling signal from noise. It is the analytics layer that sits on top of everything you have already learned: the data you can query, the signal you can trend, the hidden failures you have learned to fear. It answers the one question all of those raise - "is this trend real?" Each step is a question:

  • What is normal here? Establish the baseline - the historical mean the signal sits at (here, 4.0). You cannot spot a change until you know what it is changing from.
  • What is the range of normal noise? Draw the control limits - a band around the baseline that captures the asset's ordinary breathing (here, the mean plus or minus three standard deviations of the noise, giving 2.8 to 5.2). Anything inside this band is just the asset being itself.
  • Is this beyond the band - and does it PERSIST? Here is the whole secret. A single point poking past the limit means almost nothing; noise does that all the time. What matters is persistence - a run of points that go beyond the band and stay there. Persistence, not drama, is the signal.
  • So has the behaviour genuinely changed? If yes, you have found a change-point - the moment the asset moved into a new, different regime (here, a sustained jump to 6.5). That is not a louder version of normal; it is a new normal, and usually a worse one - a developing fault.

One trap is worth its own warning, because it is the heart of the skill - call it the two-chart test. Chart A shows a single, dramatic spike that leaps far past the limit and instantly drops back. Chart B shows a smaller, duller shift that barely clears the limit but stays for many readings. Which asset is in trouble? The untrained eye points at the dramatic spike in A. It is wrong. B is the real fault - the quiet, persistent one. Discipline trumps drama, every time.

Does it scale? Exactly the same logic that flags a sustained shift in this pump's vibration flags a sustained change in a patient's heart rate as a medical alert, or a persistent change in spending as possible bank fraud - one baseline, one band, one test of persistence, everywhere data is watched for a real change.

T5: The Guardrails

Change-point detection is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust a verdict.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Chasing single spikes. Reacting to one dramatic reading - crying wolf - until no one trusts your alarms. One point outside the band is not a change-point; wait for persistence.

• Normalising the slow creep. The opposite, and the deadlier one: a genuine, gradual rise that you keep explaining away as "just a bad day" until the fault matures. A sustained drift is a change-point, even when it is undramatic.

• A band too tight, or too wide. Too tight and normal noise constantly trips it (false alarms, lost trust); too wide and a real fault hides comfortably inside it (missed signals). The band must reflect the asset's true normal spread.

• Mistaking a cycle for a change. A predictable daily or seasonal swing - hotter at midday, busier on Mondays - is not a fault. Know your asset's natural rhythms, or you will "detect" the same change every single day.

• Forgetting the baseline can legitimately move. After a repair or an upgrade, "normal" genuinely resets. Re-establish the baseline, or you will keep measuring against a past that no longer exists.

Onalenna stopped being trapped by her own data the moment she stopped judging it one reading at a time. Now you hold that judgement - the one that decides when all the data you have learned to gather is finally worth acting on. Chase persistence, not drama, and you will catch the quiet faults that matter while everyone else is still flinching at spikes.
