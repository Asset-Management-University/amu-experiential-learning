I'm responsible for this but I can't ORDER anyone to do anything - so how do I get people who don't answer to me to commit, when pushing has only made it worse?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Kagiso, the works planner at a busy factory, responsible for getting the main packing machine overhauled this month. The whole site is counting on you; your name is on the plan. But you have no power to command the people you depend on. Operations won't release the machine - "we're behind on orders, we can't stop the line". The skilled artisans want to do the overhaul their own way, slower but more thorough - "this is how we guarantee our work". And your manager just looks at you and says, "Kagiso, make it happen."

You've put together three different schedules. Each was agreed in a meeting - and then torn apart within days as people drifted back to their own priorities. You are stuck in the middle, with all the responsibility for the outcome and none of the power to make it happen, and pushing harder has only made everyone dig their heels in.

I'm responsible for this but I can't ORDER anyone to do anything - so how do I get people who don't answer to me to commit, when pushing has only made it worse?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to push harder - a firmer email, quoting the manager, pulling rank you don't really have. But you've already learned it: a decision broadcast at people is not a commitment from them; they nod in the meeting to make you go away, then back on the floor they do what protects them, and force without real power just breeds resentment. Or you decide it yourself and impose the schedule - you have the formal "right" - but because the others didn't choose it, they don't defend it, and the first small problem becomes the reason to abandon it; an imposed plan is one nobody owns, so they let it fail. Or you give up and escalate everything to your manager - and "make it happen" comes straight back down, only now you've shown you can't handle the middle seat that is, after all, your job.

Pushing a decision at people has failed three times now. So before you read on: what would turn their reluctant nods into promises they actually keep?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You can't command them - so stop trying. Stop pushing a decision at people and start building a commitment from them: a plan people choose together is one they defend; a plan you impose is one they let fail. This is a craft, not a personality - and it's a particular one: not the gap where nobody owns the outcome, nor learning to lead your own team by enabling them, but the middle seat where you own the outcome and the people you need answer to someone else entirely. Five moves:

  • See the trap - responsible, but not in command: you own the outcome but not the people, and your formal 'right' to decide is useless if they don't act - a decision announced is not a decision done. Pushing harder on people you can't command is like shoving a locked door: it only jams the lock further. The first move is to accept this honestly - the power you wish you had isn't coming, so the craft has to replace the command.
  • Find what each is really protecting: people don't fight for what they DEMAND, they fight for what they need to PROTECT. Operations aren't just difficult - they're protecting their order book and promises to customers; the artisans aren't just slow - they're protecting their pride, their safety, the quality their name rests on; the manager is protecting a promise they made upward. The demand is the position; the protection is the real need - you can rarely reconcile positions, but you can often meet needs.
  • Put the hidden trade-off on the table: stop solving the puzzle for everyone in private - bring the real trade-off into the open and let the group choose with the true costs visible: 'if we overhaul next week we lose three days of orders; if we wait, we risk a breakdown in the month-end rush that could stop us for three weeks - neither is good, but which risk do we, together, choose?'. A trade-off the group makes together is owned; one you make for them is resented.
  • Convert agreement into named, owned promises: a nod in a room evaporates the moment people leave it - so turn the shared decision into specific, written, named, owned promises: operations (Anelisa) releases the machine Monday 8am; the artisans (Sibu) finish phase one by Wednesday 5pm; you have the parts ready - and everyone knows that a missed promise visibly delays the restart by a day. Not a vague 'we agreed', but who does what, by when, and what it costs if missed.
  • Accept the honest cost: this path is slower, and sometimes costs more, than simply ordering it would - but ordering it was never actually available to you, and a slower plan that HOLDS beats a faster plan that collapses for the fourth time. Influence is the long road that actually arrives. And it isn't a trick: surfacing what people protect and sharing the real trade-off is honest alignment, which is why the plan it builds is one people defend rather than abandon.

Watch it hold for Kagiso. He stops pushing and starts listening, and learns what each side is really guarding; he brings them together and lays the real trade-off on the table - three days of orders lost now, against the risk of a three-week breakdown in the month-end rush - and lets them choose which risk to carry; then he turns their choice into named promises with a visible cost if missed. The fourth schedule isn't torn up - because for the first time it's theirs, not his. A decision broadcast at people is never a commitment from them - so find what each is really protecting beneath what they demand, put the hidden trade-off on the table and let the group choose with the costs visible, and convert the agreement into named, owned promises a nod can't wriggle out of. It's slower than an order - but the order was never yours, and a plan that holds beats a faster plan that collapses.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only for factories - the middle seat is everywhere. Watch a parent coordinating a child's holiday between two divorced co-parents who don't get along.

See the same craft with no authority at all

You're responsible for your child's happiness but can't command either parent; pushing harder just makes them dig in. So run the same moves:

• What's really protected: one parent isn't just demanding Saturday - they're protecting their only guaranteed weekend time; the other isn't just demanding Christmas morning - they're protecting a family tradition they feel slipping away.
• The shared trade-off: put the child's need on the table, not your preference - "if we hold rigidly to the schedule, she misses her cousin's party; if we flex a few hours, she gets to go" - so the question becomes "what's best for her?", not "me versus you".
• Named, owned promises: "you collect her from the party at 4pm Saturday; in return you get three extra hours next Sunday" - specific, agreed, fair.

No authority, the same result: a commitment that holds because everyone chose it.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of something you're responsible for that depends entirely on people you have no power to command - a project across departments, a family event, a volunteer team, a community plan. Stop trying to push your solution at them; that path has failed. Instead, build a commitment from them:

  1. See the trap, and listen differently. Accept you're responsible but not in command - then ask questions until you understand what each person is truly protecting beneath their demand.
  2. Make the choice shared. Put the real, hard trade-off on the table where everyone can see it, and let the group choose which cost to bear together.
  3. Make the agreement real. Turn the decision into specific, named, owned promises with a visible cost when broken - so a nod in a room becomes a commitment in the world.

Being stuck with all the responsibility and none of the power - and pushing, and watching it fail - was never weak leadership; it's the near-impossible bind of the middle seat, and the instinct to push is the most natural thing in the world. The missing piece was never your strength of will; it was a craft you can learn. And it isn't a trick or a way to quietly get your own way: finding what people are protecting and laying the real trade-off open is honest alignment, which is exactly why the plan it builds is one people defend instead of abandon. It is slower than an order - but the order was never yours to give, and leading this way does something command never could: it delivers the outcome and keeps the people whole, treated as partners rather than obstacles. A plan that holds is the only plan worth having.
