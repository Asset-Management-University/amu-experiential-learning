The fight that was not about the pump

T1: The Ache

Three weeks now, the same fight in the scheduling meeting. Operations will not release the number-three pump for its overhaul. Maintenance says it has to come out now. Both sides arrive with their reasons sharpened, and by the end nobody has moved and the room is colder than when it started.

You are the planner. You have no authority over either of them, and you keep trying to settle the argument about the pump - a date, a compromise, a split of the difference. And it never stays settled. Next week the same two people are back, angrier, having the same fight in slightly different words.

Here is what you have started to suspect, standing at the whiteboard with two crossed-out dates on it: the fight is not about the pump. It is about something underneath the pump that nobody has said out loud. And you have been settling the wrong thing.

Two people are locked in a conflict I have no power to end by ordering it ended. How do I get them from fighting each other to solving a problem together - and get a decision they will actually keep?

Before you read on, decide. Two crossed-out dates on the whiteboard. Operations wants the pump taken out next week; maintenance wants it the week after. You split it — a compromise date — and both of them left the room unsatisfied, which you told yourself was the sign of a fair settlement. A week later they are back, angrier, having substantially the same argument in different words. Neither reports to you. Somewhere in the middle of it the maintenance planner said, half under his breath, that nobody asked him before the overtime budget was cut in March. Nobody followed that up, including you. Write down how you would get these two solving a problem together — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the settlement keeps coming undone

• “Split it again — meet in the middle.” The move that produced this. Splitting the difference feels most reasonable and is frequently a quiet lose-lose: neither concern is actually met, so nothing holds the agreement up and it comes apart the moment it is tested.

• “Escalate — let their managers decide.” Produces a decision and a loser, and the loser is still here tomorrow, working alongside the winner, on the next version of the same question.

• “Stay out of it — it is not my fight.” Feels civil and safe, and it lets the thing fester underground, where it grows and returns as something worse than a date dispute.

• “Decide it myself on technical grounds and tell them.” You have no authority to compel either, and a technically correct answer to a question that is not really technical will be complied with and quietly undermined.

• “Get them to be more professional about it.” Addresses the temperature and not the cause. Both of them are being professional; that is why the real disagreement is being conducted through a date.

• First notice how you were about to handle it, because most people default to one of the worst options without ever choosing. There are broadly five ways to engage a conflict — pushing hard for yourself, giving way, stepping back, splitting, or working for an outcome that meets both concerns — and each has its moment. But two defaults wreck ongoing work: avoiding, which buries it, and fighting to win, which manufactures a loser you still have to work with. And splitting the difference, the one that feels fairest, is often the quiet lose-lose you have just demonstrated twice. What works is to separate the people from the problem — soft on the people, hard on the problem — so both of them can attack the difficulty instead of each other. Then look under the stated positions for the interests: what each actually needs, which is usually not what they are demanding. Two incompatible positions very often rest on interests that are not incompatible at all. And listen again to what the planner said half under his breath about March. You have been settling a date twice, and the fight is not about the pump.

Here is how you do it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you turn a fight into a shared problem - choosing how to engage it, protecting the people while going hard at the problem, finding what it is actually about, building an agreement they will keep, and breaking a deadlock when it comes.

1. Choose how to engage - not avoiding it, not winning it

BEFORE YOU DO ANYTHING ABOUT A CONFLICT, NOTICE HOW YOU ARE ABOUT TO HANDLE IT, BECAUSE MOST PEOPLE DEFAULT TO ONE OF THE TWO WORST OPTIONS WITHOUT CHOOSING IT. There are, broadly, FIVE WAYS PEOPLE ENGAGE A CONFLICT, sitting on two axes: how hard you push for your own concern, and how hard you work to meet the other person's. COMPETING pushes hard for yourself and not at all for them - win it, someone loses. ACCOMMODATING gives in - their concern over yours. AVOIDING does neither - you step back and hope it goes away. COMPROMISING splits the difference so each side gives something up - often a quiet lose-lose where nobody is really satisfied. COLLABORATING works for an outcome that actually meets both concerns - the problem-solving mode. EACH FITS SOME MOMENT: you accommodate on things you do not care about, you avoid a trivial spat that will pass, you compete when safety demands an instant call. BUT THE TWO DEFAULTS THAT WRECK ONGOING WORK ARE AVOIDING AND FIGHTING-TO-WIN. Avoiding feels safe and civil, and it lets the conflict fester underground where it grows and comes back later as something worse. Fighting to win produces a loser, and the loser takes it out on the work, the relationship, or the next meeting. BOTH TREAT CONFLICT AS A THREAT TO SURVIVE RATHER THAN A PROBLEM TO SOLVE. So the first act is a deliberate choice: for anything that matters and where you still have to work with this person tomorrow, REACH FOR COLLABORATING - decide, on purpose, to treat this as a shared problem the two of you are going to solve together, not a fight one of you has to win and not a fire to back away from. Everything else in this unit is how you make that choice real.

2. Be soft on the people, hard on the problem

THIS IS THE MOVE THE WHOLE UNIT TURNS ON, AND IT SOUNDS LIKE A CONTRADICTION UNTIL YOU SEE THAT THE TWO HALVES MAKE EACH OTHER POSSIBLE: BE SOFT ON THE PEOPLE AND HARD ON THE PROBLEM. SOFT ON THE PEOPLE means respectful, calm, attentive - you protect the other person's dignity, you assume they are reasonable, you do not attack, belittle, or score points. This is not weakness and it is not giving in on the issue. HARD ON THE PROBLEM means you go at the actual issue with everything you have - you are rigorous, you push, you do not settle for a bad answer to keep the peace. People get this backwards all the time: they go SOFT ON THE PROBLEM (avoiding the hard question to avoid the tension) and HARD ON THE PEOPLE (letting frustration leak out as sharpness, sarcasm, or blame). Flip it. THE KEY THAT UNLOCKS THIS IS SEPARATING THE PERSON FROM THE POSITION. The pump-out date is a POSITION. The person holding it is not the problem - they are a colleague who wants something, for reasons that make sense to them. YOU CAN DISAGREE COMPLETELY WITH WHAT SOMEONE WANTS AND STILL TREAT THEM AS SOMEONE YOU RESPECT. And here is why this is not just good manners but the load-bearing wall of the entire method: THE MOMENT A CONFLICT BECOMES ABOUT THE PEOPLE RATHER THAN THE PROBLEM, IT STOPS BEING SOLVABLE. Now there is a face to defend and a score to settle, pride is in the room, and no schedule, no data, no clever option will fix it, because the fight is no longer about the thing on the table. Protect the person, attack the problem, and keep those two straight even when you are frustrated - especially when you are frustrated.

3. Find what it is actually about - ask, do not diagnose

HERE IS THE SHARP ONE, THE THING THAT ENDS THE FIGHT THAT KEEPS COMING BACK: THE CONFLICT IS RARELY ABOUT WHAT IT SAYS IT IS ABOUT. Two people fighting about the schedule are very often really fighting about RESPECT, or workload, or fear of who carries the blame, or an old grievance nobody has ever named. And this is why the planner kept failing: YOU CANNOT RESOLVE A FIGHT ABOUT RESPECT BY ADJUDICATING THE SCHEDULE. You settle the date, and it comes back next week wearing a different costume, because you fixed the symptom the conflict was wearing and never touched the thing driving it. So the real work is to get underneath the STATED POSITION to the INTEREST beneath it. A POSITION is what someone says they want: 'do not release the pump.' An INTEREST is why they want it: 'I am judged on production and I am frightened of an overrun that lands on me.' Operations says do-not-release (position); underneath is fear-of-an-overrun (interest). Maintenance says take-it-out-now (position); underneath is fear-of-a-catastrophic-failure (interest). THE POSITIONS ARE LOCKED HEAD TO HEAD. THE INTERESTS ARE NOT OPPOSED AT ALL - both people are just trying not to be the one holding a disaster. NOW THE DISCIPLINE THAT KEEPS THIS HONEST AND KEEPS IT KIND: YOU DO NOT DIAGNOSE - YOU ASK. Deciding, from the outside, that you know what someone is 'really' upset about is not insight; it is a guess wearing the mask of insight, and people can feel it, and it insults them. Instead you form a quiet HYPOTHESIS and then you TEST it with a question that lets them confirm or correct you: 'It sounds like the worry here is less the pump itself and more the risk of an overrun on your numbers - is that right?' Then you listen to the answer and you believe it. The person tells you what the conflict is about. You never tell them.

4. Build a decision they will keep - and refuse the false one

WHEN YOU MOVE FROM UNDERSTANDING THE CONFLICT TO DECIDING WHAT TO DO, KNOW EXACTLY WHAT YOU ARE AIMING AT, BECAUSE THE OBVIOUS TARGETS ARE THE WRONG ONES. CONSENSUS IS NOT UNANIMITY, AND IT IS NOT EVERYONE GETTING THEIR FIRST CHOICE. IT IS A DECISION EVERYONE CAN LIVE WITH AND COMMIT TO, reached after a genuine effort to meet everyone's interests. That is a real and reachable bar: not 'everyone loves this' but 'everyone can get behind this and will not undermine it.' It is stronger than a bare majority vote precisely because a vote leaves a defeated minority sitting in the room, outvoted and quietly waiting to reverse the decision the moment it wobbles - whereas a consensus everyone helped shape is one everyone will defend. BUT THERE IS A COUNTERFEIT YOU MUST LEARN TO REFUSE, AND IT IS THE MOST DANGEROUS OUTCOME IN THE WHOLE UNIT: FALSE CONSENSUS, WHERE SILENCE IS MISTAKEN FOR AGREEMENT. A DECISION 'AGREED' IN A MEETING BUT SABOTAGED IN THE HALLWAY WAS NEVER A CONSENSUS. It was compliance, or exhaustion, or people who had given up on being heard. GENUINE CONSENSUS REQUIRES THAT DISSENT WAS ACTIVELY SOUGHT, GENUINELY HEARD, AND ADDRESSED. IF YOU HAVE NOT HEARD ANY DISAGREEMENT, YOU HAVE NOT TRIED HARD ENOUGH TO FIND IT - go looking for it, ask the quiet person directly, make it easy and safe to say 'I am not sure about this.' AND WHERE THERE IS A POWER GAP IN THE ROOM, THE BURDEN SHIFTS ONTO WHOEVER HOLDS THE POWER: THE PERSON WITH MORE POWER IN A CONFLICT, THROUGH RANK, EXPERIENCE OR POSITION, HAS THE PRIMARY RESPONSIBILITY TO MAKE IT SAFE FOR THE OTHER PERSON TO SPEAK. IF YOU HAVE POWER, YOUR JOB IS NOT TO WIN THE ARGUMENT. YOUR JOB IS TO FIND THE TRUTH, AND THE TRUTH OFTEN LIVES WITH THE PERSON WHO IS MOST AFRAID TO SPEAK IT. MISTAKING COMPLIANCE FOR CONSENSUS IS A FAILURE OF LEADERSHIP. Do not force a false harmony, do not paper over a real disagreement to end the meeting on time, and never win by rank - the disagreement you suppress today is the one that sinks the decision tomorrow.

5. Break a deadlock by returning to the shared goal

SOMETIMES, EVEN WHEN YOU HAVE DONE EVERYTHING RIGHT, IT LOCKS. A DEADLOCK IS TWO POSITIONS JAMMED HEAD TO HEAD, AND THE ONE THING THAT WILL NOT FREE IT IS PUSHING HARDER ON EITHER POSITION - that only tightens the jam. THE WAY OUT IS ALMOST ALWAYS TO GO BACK TO THE SHARED GOAL BOTH SIDES FORGOT THEY HAD. Underneath almost every deadlock is a goal neither party actually disputes, buried under the heat of the argument. So you name it, and you put it up where both can see it - literally on the wall, above the two positions - and you ask: what serves THIS? For operations and maintenance, the shared goal was never in question: the plant runs well and neither of them ends up carrying a disaster. Standing on that shared ground instead of behind their two positions, an option appears that nobody had proposed while they were fighting - a short planned outage in the low-production window in nine days, with a condition check now to confirm the pump can safely wait that long. NEITHER SIDE GOT THEIR STATED POSITION. BOTH GOT THEIR INTEREST. Operations is protected from the overrun; maintenance is protected from the failure. That is what living on the shared goal instead of the positions produces: not a compromise where each loses something, but a third option that serves what each actually needed. THE TECHNIQUE, THEN, IS A LADDER: when two positions lock, climb up to the interests beneath them, and if those still will not meet, climb higher to the shared goal above them, and from that height look back down for the option you could not see from inside the fight. AND ONE LAST HONEST NOTE: SOMETIMES, AFTER EVERY GENUINE EFFORT, A GROUP CANNOT AGREE AND A DECISION STILL HAS TO BE MADE. When that happens, do not fake a consensus that is not there - NAME OPENLY WHO DECIDES AND ON WHAT BASIS. An owned, transparent decision is not a failure of the method; it is what a mature team does when consensus is genuinely out of reach and the work still has to move. What you never do is dress up one person's rank as everyone's agreement.

T3: The Blueprint

The conflict is not the people; it is a problem the people share. Once you see that, five questions:

  • How am I going to engage this? Not by avoiding it and not by winning it. Both feel safe; both lose. Choose to solve it together.
  • Am I soft on the people and hard on the problem? Protect the relationship; go at the issue with full force. The moment it becomes about the people, it stops being solvable.
  • What is this actually about? The interest under the stated position. The schedule fight is rarely about the schedule. Ask - do not diagnose.
  • Will they keep this decision? Consensus is what everyone can live with and commit to - not unanimity, and not a silence you mistook for agreement.
  • What if we deadlock? Go back to the shared goal both sides forgot. The lock is between two positions; the way out is above them.

Avoiding and winning are the two traps. Most people handle conflict in one of two losing ways. They avoid it - which feels safe, and lets the conflict fester underground where it grows and comes back later as something worse. Or they fight to win it - which produces a loser, and the loser takes it out on the work, the relationship, or the next meeting. Both treat conflict as a threat to survive. There are, broadly, five ways people engage a conflict, along two axes: how hard you push your own concern, and how hard you work to meet the other's. Competing pushes hard for yourself and not at all for them - win it. Accommodating gives in - their concern over yours. Avoiding does neither - step back. Compromising splits the difference, and each side gives something up - often a quiet lose-lose. Collaborating works for an outcome that meets both - the problem-solving mode. Each fits some moment, but for anything that matters and where the relationship continues, collaborating is the one to reach for - and it is the one avoiding and fighting both skip.

Be soft on the people, hard on the problem. This is the move the whole unit turns on. Soft on the people means respectful, calm, attentive - not weak, not giving in. Hard on the problem means you go at the issue with everything you have while protecting the person on the other side of it. The two are not in tension; they are what make each other possible, because you can disagree completely with what someone wants and still treat them as someone you respect. This requires separating the person from the position - the pump-out date is a position; the person holding it is not the problem. And the reason it matters is simple and unforgiving: the moment a conflict becomes about the people rather than the problem, it stops being solvable, because now there is a face to defend and a score to settle, and no schedule on earth will fix that.

The conflict is rarely about what it says it is about. This is the sharp one. Two people fighting about the schedule are very often really fighting about respect, or workload, or an old grievance nobody has named - and you cannot resolve a fight about respect by adjudicating the schedule. You will "settle" it, and it will be back next week wearing a different costume. So the real work is to find what the conflict is actually about, which is almost always the interest sitting underneath the stated position. Operations says "do not release the pump" (a position); underneath is "I am judged on production and I am afraid of an overrun that lands on me" (an interest). Maintenance says "it must come out now" (a position); underneath is "I am judged on reliability and I am afraid of a failure that lands on me" (an interest). The positions are locked head to head. The interests are not opposed at all. But here is the discipline that keeps this honest: you do not diagnose; you ask. Deciding you know what someone is "really" upset about is not insight, it is a guess dressed up as one, and it will insult them. You form a quiet hypothesis, and then you test it with a question that lets them confirm or correct you: "It sounds like the worry here is less the pump itself and more the risk of an overrun on your numbers - is that right?" The person tells you what it is about. You do not tell them.

Consensus is what people will keep, not what everyone loves. When you move to a decision, know what you are aiming at. Consensus is not unanimity, and it is not everyone's first choice. It is a decision everyone can live with and commit to, reached after a genuine effort to meet everyone's interests. It is stronger than a bare majority vote precisely because the minority is not left outvoted and plotting to reverse it in the corridor. But there is a counterfeit you must refuse: the most dangerous outcome is false consensus, where silence is mistaken for agreement. A decision "agreed" in a meeting but sabotaged in the hallway was never a consensus. Genuine consensus requires that dissent was actively sought, genuinely heard, and addressed. If you have not heard disagreement, you have not tried hard enough to find it.

One honest boundary. Your work is to make the conflict productive, not to suppress it. You do not force a false harmony, you do not "win" it by rank, and you do not paper over a real disagreement to make a meeting end on time. And when there is a genuine power gap in the room - a senior engineer and a junior planner - the burden shifts: the person with more power in a conflict, through rank, experience or position, has the primary responsibility to make it safe for the other person to speak. If you have power, your job is not to win the argument. Your job is to find the truth, and the truth often lives with the person who is most afraid to speak it. Mistaking compliance for consensus is a failure of leadership.

Break a deadlock by returning to the shared goal. Sometimes it locks anyway. A deadlock is two positions jammed against each other, and you will not free them by pushing harder on either one. The way out is almost always to go back to the shared goal both sides forgot they had - put it on the wall, above the two positions, and ask what serves it. For operations and maintenance the shared goal was never in dispute: the plant runs well and neither of them carries a disaster. Standing on that shared ground, an option nobody had proposed appears - a short planned outage in the low-production window in nine days, with a condition check now to confirm the pump can safely wait that long. Neither side got their stated position. Both got their interest. And if, after every honest effort, a group genuinely cannot agree and a decision must be made, then name openly who decides and on what basis - an owned, transparent decision is not a failure of consensus, it is what you do when consensus is not available and the work still has to move.

Does it scale? Two friends arguing about where to eat are rarely arguing about food - one is tired and wants somewhere close, one is broke and cannot say so. Fight about the restaurant and you lose the evening; ask what is really going on and you find the option that works for both. The move that ends a plant dispute and the move that saves a friendship are the same move.

T4: Your Turn

Here is a real one, and it has a power gap in it on purpose. In the planning meeting, a senior reliability engineer insists a critical gearbox is stripped and inspected this month - "we are not waiting for it to fail." A junior planner - new, half the room's experience, and clearly uneasy - says quietly that the only slot is a week the site is already short-staffed for the audit, and then stops talking. The engineer, impatient, says "then we make time, this is more important than paperwork," and the room goes quiet. It reads as agreed. It is not agreed.

  1. Name the trap in the room. The meeting is about to "reach consensus." Why is the quiet that just fell not consensus - and who carries the responsibility for that?
  2. Find what it is actually about. Write the position and the likely interest underneath it for the engineer and for the junior planner. Then write the exact question you would ask each of them to surface that interest - remembering you ask, you do not diagnose.
  3. Make it safe for the smaller voice. Write the specific thing you - or the senior person - would do or say so the junior planner's objection is genuinely heard, not overridden by rank. What makes it safe for them to finish the sentence they stopped halfway?
  4. Build the live-with-it decision, or break the deadlock. Propose an outcome both can commit to. If they lock, name the shared goal you would put on the wall above both positions to free it.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the trap. The quiet is not consensus because it is compliance, not agreement - the junior planner did not say "yes," they stopped talking after a senior person pushed back, and silence bought by rank is exactly the false consensus that gets sabotaged later, or worse, that lets a real staffing risk go unspoken. And the responsibility for fixing that sits with the person holding the power: the senior engineer, by rank and experience, owns the job of making it safe for the planner to speak. A planner who stays quiet has not agreed; they have been outranked, and the decision is now built on a fault line.

Second, what it is about. The engineer's position is "strip it this month"; the interest underneath is almost certainly fear of an unplanned catastrophic failure on a critical machine landing on their reliability record - a real and legitimate fear. The planner's position is "there is no good slot"; the interest is protecting a site that is genuinely stretched, and quite possibly the fear of being the junior who caused the audit to fail. Neither is being unreasonable. Good questions test, not accuse: to the engineer, "Help me understand the risk if this waits three weeks - is it the failure mode itself you are worried about, or that we would not see it coming?" To the planner, "You went quiet - what is the thing you are seeing about that week that you do not think the rest of us are seeing?"

Third, safety for the smaller voice. The senior person says, plainly and first, some version of: "I pushed hard just now and I want to be sure I did not talk over a real problem. You know the staffing that week better than I do - walk me through it, and I will hold my position until I have heard it." That does three things: it names the power imbalance out loud, it explicitly invites the objection back into the room, and it signals the position is genuinely still open. It is the opposite of "then we make time" - which closed the room.

Fourth, the decision. The live-with-it outcome almost certainly exists once both interests are on the table: perhaps the inspection is booked but pulled forward or back by one week to clear the audit, or a condition check is done now to confirm the machine can safely wait the extra week, so the engineer gets their protection-against-failure and the planner gets their protected audit week. If they still lock, the shared goal on the wall is the one neither disputes - the plant stays reliable and the site is not set up to fail - and from that ground you ask what serves both, which is a question with a different answer than "your week or mine." What a real answer never does: declare the engineer right because they outrank the planner.

T5: The Guardrails

Conflict over ideas is the engine of good decisions. A team that never disagrees is not a harmonious team; it is a silent one - and silence is where the real problems hide until they are too big to fix.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Avoiding the conflict so it festers. Stepping back feels safe and civil. Underground, the conflict grows and comes back worse. An unaddressed conflict is not a resolved one.

• Fighting to win it. A win produces a loser, and the loser takes it out on the work or the next meeting. You can win the argument and lose the relationship you need for everything after it.

• Making it about the people, not the problem. The instant it becomes personal it stops being solvable. Be soft on the people and hard on the problem - protect the relationship, go at the issue.

• Adjudicating the stated position while the real conflict survives. Settle the schedule fight that was really about respect, and it returns next week in a new costume. Find the interest under the position first.

• Diagnosing instead of asking. Deciding you know what someone is "really" upset about is a guess that insults them. Form the hypothesis quietly, then test it with a question they can correct.

• Splitting the difference when you could have collaborated. A compromise where each gives something up is often a quiet lose-lose. When it matters and the relationship continues, look for the option that meets both interests.

• Mistaking a quiet room for agreement. Silence is not consensus; it is often disagreement that has given up on being heard. If you have not heard the disagreement, you have not looked hard enough for it.

• Winning by rank. If you hold the power, compliance is not agreement, and the truth usually lives with the person most afraid to speak. Your job is to make it safe for them, not to end the argument by outranking it.

• Forcing a false harmony. Papering over a real disagreement to make the meeting end on time is not resolution. The disagreement you suppress is the one that sinks the decision in the hallway.

Two people were locked in a fight about a pump, and the planner kept settling the pump and kept watching it come undone - because the fight was never about the pump. See the conflict as a problem the two people share, not a battle one has to win or avoid; choose to solve it together rather than fighting it or fleeing it; be soft on the people and hard on the problem, so the relationship survives the disagreement; find what it is actually about by asking for the interest under the position, never by diagnosing it; build a decision everyone can genuinely live with and commit to, refusing the false consensus that silence pretends to be; and when it locks, return to the shared goal both sides forgot and find the option above both positions. Your work is to make the conflict productive, not to suppress it - and if you hold the power in the room, your first job is to make it safe for the person most afraid to speak, because that is usually where the truth is. The next time a meeting ends in the same fight for the third week running, do not settle the argument on the table. Ask, gently, what the argument is really about - and watch the wall you have been pushing against turn out to have a door in it.
