Our new R4.2m control system passed every one of its 217 contracted tests and the acceptance certificate is signed - yet last night two thousand homes went dry because it has no alarm nobody ever asked for, and a twelfth change order is already coming; do I just send the urgent fix, or is something deeper broken?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sinazo, the asset engineer for a small regional water utility - the one that pumps drinking water up to four hillside towns. It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday and you are standing on the steel walkway above the pump house, a clipboard going damp in your hand. The new telemetry and control system went live eleven days ago. It cost R4.2 million. The controls contractor switched it on, ran their tests, got your manager's signature, and drove their bakkie out the gate.

On the clipboard is a single printed page: the acceptance certificate, already signed. Next to it, the morning's alarm log - fourteen pages, printed because the screen scrolls too fast to read. Lwandiswa, your most experienced operator, is leaning on the rail beside you. She has run this pump house for nineteen years. She is not angry. She is worse than angry - she is quiet. "It does everything they said it would," she says. "Watch." She taps the panel. The high-level alarm for Reservoir 3 fires exactly as promised. The valve closes. The screen turns the right shade of red. Every line item on the contract works. "Now watch this."

She points at the trend. Last night, between 02:10 and 02:40, the pressure on the upper main dropped to 1.8 bar - low enough that, in the old days, she would have walked down and started the backup pump by hand before the towns woke up to brown water. The new system did not warn her. It had no alarm for slow pressure loss on that main. Nobody had asked for one. So nobody built one. So at 04:30 two thousand homes got a trickle, and your phone is full of messages from the municipal manager. You flip through the annexure: it lists 217 "system functions", every one ticked. None of them is keep water in the towns through the night, because that was never written down as a thing the system must do. It lived only in Lwandiswa's head, and in her legs, and in nineteen years of walking down at 02:00. "When they were building it," you ask, "did anyone sit with you?" "A young man came once. He showed me a screen and asked if I liked the colours."

You turn to the change-order log stapled to the contract: eleven changes during the build, each a small war - the contractor saying that was not in scope, your manager saying but obviously we needed it, both right, both paying for it. R610,000 and four months. And the alarm gap you are now staring at will become change number twelve. You pull out your phone to message the contractor - add a low-pressure alarm, urgent - your thumb over send. But Lwandiswa is watching, and you stop, because you know that if you send this one you will be sending number thirteen next week, and fourteen the week after, forever - chasing each missing thing one panicked message at a time, with no way of knowing how many more are hiding in nineteen years of legs and habit. You put the phone down on the damp rail.

The system passed all 217 contracted tests and the certificate is signed - yet two thousand homes went dry because of an alarm nobody asked for, and a twelfth change order is coming; do I just send the urgent fix, or is something deeper broken?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The contractor did exactly what the contract said, and you still got a system that fails the real need. "Send the urgent fix - add the low-pressure alarm this morning." But that treats one symptom and teaches you nothing about the other gaps hiding in nineteen years of habit; you'll send number thirteen next week and fourteen the week after, forever. "Blame the contractor and withhold payment." But they delivered every one of the 217 functions and got a signed acceptance - they met the contract; the contract met the boxes, not the need. "Have your manager approve change twelve and move on." But that is the eleven-changes war again - another argument, another invoice - and it still leaves you unable to say how many needs are still unwritten. So before you read on: name what was never created between "what the towns need" and "what the contractor built", and the cheapest way to find out how many more needs are still hiding.

I am Sinazo, the asset engineer for a small regional water utility that pumps drinking water up to four hillside towns. Our new R4.2 million telemetry and control system went live eleven days ago - the controls contractor switched it on, ran their tests, got my manager's signature on the acceptance certificate, and drove off. Lwandiswa, my most experienced operator (nineteen years running this pump house), is not angry; she is quiet, which is worse. 'It does everything they said it would,' she says - the high-level alarm for Reservoir 3 fires exactly as promised, the valve closes, the screen turns red; all 217 contracted 'system functions' are ticked. 'Now watch this.' Last night between 02:10 and 02:40 the pressure on the upper main dropped to 1.8 bar - low enough that in the old days she'd have walked down and started the backup pump by hand before the towns woke to brown water. The new system did NOT warn her: it has no alarm for SLOW pressure loss on that main, because nobody asked for one, so nobody built one - so at 04:30 two thousand homes got a trickle. None of the 217 functions is 'keep water in the towns through the night', because that was never written down; it lived only in Lwandiswa's head, her legs, and nineteen years of walking down at 02:00. The contract already has eleven change orders (R610,000, four months), each a small war over 'scope', and this alarm gap is obviously number twelve. My thumb is over 'send' on an urgent message to the contractor - but I stop, because I know I'll be sending number thirteen next week, chasing each missing thing one panic at a time. Where do I start?
Don't send the panic message. Sending it teaches you nothing about the other gaps still hiding in nineteen years of legs and habit - one fix at a time is the same mistake repeated forever. The contractor did exactly what the contract said and you still got a system that fails the real need, so the cause is not a bad contractor: it is that the real NEED ('keep water in the towns through the night') was never translated into a written, verifiable REQUIREMENT before the engineering started. The 217 'functions' are the contractor's product list; none of them is a statement of what the people who use the system must be able to DO. So set a success line and run the cheapest probe: pilot the needs-capture process on ONE subsystem (the upper main) for ONE day - sit with the operators and the municipal manager (not a screen, not 'do you like the colours?') and ask, many ways, 'what does this system have to do for you to trust it?' Capture NEEDS, not features. Then turn each need into a sentence you could TEST on acceptance day - 'when pressure falls below 2.0 bar for more than 5 minutes, the system shall raise a priority alarm within 10 seconds' - and trace each one to the part that delivers it. The real question is not 'which alarm do I add this morning?' but 'why did the thing we built satisfy the order and not the need - and how do I capture the needs still hiding before they become change thirteen?'
Five moves turn an endless change-order war into a system that is right, not just working. (1) REFUSE THE PANIC FIX, PILOT THE DIAGNOSIS - log the alarm as a known gap (the towns have water now; the cause waited years, it can wait an hour) and set a success line: a one-day pilot on one subsystem should surface at least one real need the 217 functions missed, or you're wrong about the cause. (2) SURFACE THE NEEDS, NOT THE FEATURES - sit with the people who do the job and ask what the system must DO for them to trust it; out come needs no contract held (warn of SLOW pressure loss, hold each town above 2.0 bar overnight, start the backup pump from the panel in 30 seconds). (3) EXPOSE AND RESOLVE CONFLICTS ON PAPER - the surprise: the backup-pump need COLLIDES with another (starting it on low pressure from a burst main downstream would pump treated water onto the street), two needs in direct conflict, neither on the contract; you settle it in twenty minutes around a table with an explicit condition, while it is still cheap words, not change thirteen and an outage. (4) TRANSLATE NEEDS INTO VERIFIABLE REQUIREMENTS - not 'keep the water flowing' (uncheckable) but 'when X for >Z, the system shall do Y within T' - a sentence that can be argued before the build and TESTED on acceptance day (watch it fire, or don't sign). This is requirements analysis: the bridge between what people need and what engineers build, done BEFORE design. (5) BUILD THE TRACE - need -> requirement -> component -> who-feels-it-if-it-fails - so 'what breaks if we change the Reservoir 3 valve logic?' is answered in a minute, and the knowledge that lived only in Lwandiswa now lives in a table the whole utility owns. The rule that earns its keep: a gap found in a requirement costs a sentence; the same gap at acceptance costs a change order; in service it costs a town's water.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You did not send the message - not because the alarm wasn't needed, but because sending it would teach you nothing about the other gaps still hiding. One panic fix at a time is not a plan; it is the same mistake repeated. Here is what you and Lwandiswa actually did, in five moves:

  • Refuse the panic fix; pilot the diagnosis: you refuse to send the message - not because the alarm isn't needed (it is) but because sending it would teach you nothing about the other gaps still hiding, and one panic fix at a time is the same mistake repeated. So you log the upper-main alarm as a known gap and leave it for an hour; the towns have water by now, and the cause had been waiting for years - it can wait one morning. Then, before spending days on a plant-wide process on faith, you set a success line out loud, in advance: if capturing needs properly is the cure, then a one-day pilot on a single subsystem - the upper main - should surface at least one real need that the 217 ticked functions missed; if it surfaces nothing new, you're wrong about the cause, and you'll just send the alarm fix and move on. That is the test, and it is cheap and falsifiable: you will either find hidden needs by the end of the day, or you won't, and you'll know which. The discipline is to treat the dry-town morning not as a fire to stamp out but as a symptom whose cause you can diagnose - on one subsystem, in one day, before betting the whole plant on the answer.
  • Surface the needs, not the features: you run the cheapest honest probe: one subsystem, one day. You sit down with Lwandiswa, the two night operators and the municipal manager - not with a screen, not asking about colours, but with one question asked many ways: what does this system have to do for you to trust it, on the upper main alone? You write down every answer in plain words, and out come things no contract had ever held: warn the operator of SLOW pressure loss on a main, not just sudden high-level, because slow loss at night is how towns go dry; hold each town above 2.0 bar through the 02:00-05:00 low-demand window; let one operator start the backup pump from the panel within 30 seconds, without walking down. These are not features - they are NEEDS, statements of what the people who use the system must be able to do, Lwandiswa's nineteen years of legs finally on paper. And your success line is met within the first hour: the pilot has already surfaced needs the 217 functions never named. The move that matters here is the question itself - not 'what do you want the screen to look like?' but 'what must this do for you to trust it?' - because the first asks about the product and the second asks about the need, and only the need tells you what the system is actually for.
  • Expose and resolve conflicts on paper: the day holds a surprise that changes the plan. You had expected the gaps to be MISSING alarms - things nobody asked for - but the third need breaks that expectation. When the night operators describe starting the backup pump, the municipal manager objects: starting it when pressure is low for a DIFFERENT reason - a burst main downstream - could pump treated water straight onto a street and waste a whole reservoir. Two stakeholders, two needs, in direct conflict - and neither was on the contract. This is not a gap you can close by adding a line; the plan you walked in with, 'collect the missing needs and write them up', cannot survive a need that fights another need. So you course-correct: the process now has to do something harder than capture wishes - it has to make conflicts visible and RESOLVED on paper, before the build, with the people who hold both sides in the room. You write the backup-pump need with an explicit condition: start within 30 seconds - UNLESS downstream flow exceeds the burst threshold. The conflict that would have become change number thirteen, an outage and a blame argument is settled in twenty minutes around a table, because the pilot forced it into the open while it was still cheap words. The deepest gaps in a requirement set are not only the needs nobody mentioned; they are the needs that COLLIDE - and a contract full of un-reconciled wishes is a war waiting to happen.
  • Translate needs into verifiable requirements: this is the move that changes everything: you turn each need into a written, TESTABLE requirement. Not 'the system should keep the water flowing', which nobody can check, but: 'when pressure on the upper main falls below 2.0 bar for more than 5 minutes, the system shall raise a priority alarm at the operator panel within 10 seconds.' That sentence can be argued over before anyone builds it, and it can be TESTED on the day of acceptance - you either watch the alarm fire at 2.0 bar within 10 seconds, or you do not sign. It is specific, it is verifiable, and it belongs to the need, not to the contractor's product list. What you are building with Lwandiswa is a REQUIREMENTS SPECIFICATION - the bridge between what people need and what engineers build - and the whole disaster came from one missing structure: nobody had translated stakeholder needs into written, verifiable requirements before the engineering started. The contractor did not fail the contract; the contract failed the need, because the need was never captured. This step has a name in asset and systems engineering - requirements analysis - and its job is exactly this translation, done BEFORE design, so that design has something true to aim at. A need you cannot test on delivery day is still a wish; a need written as 'when X, it must do Y within Z' is a requirement, and the difference is whether anyone can ever hold the build to account.
  • Build the trace, and price the silence: finally you build the thing nobody had: a trace. You draw a simple table - left column, each need (protect the towns through the night); middle, the written requirement(s) that satisfy it (the 2.0-bar alarm; the 30-second backup start); right, the component or logic block that delivers it (alarm rule A-47; valve logic V-12). This is REQUIREMENTS TRACEABILITY, and it is boring and it is gold: now, when someone asks 'what breaks if we change the Reservoir 3 valve logic?', you read down the right column to V-12, follow it left to its requirement, then to the need, and you can say in one minute exactly what is at risk and who will feel it - knowledge that used to live only in Lwandiswa, now living in a table the whole utility owns. And you price the silence honestly: capturing needs and writing verifiable requirements for the upgrade properly would have taken about three weeks of engineer and operator time up front - call it R180,000 in labour and lost availability - before a single cable was pulled; that feels like delay when everyone wants the new screens, but the eleven change orders cost R610,000 and four months, with a twelfth coming and a dry-town incident the municipality will not forget. The rule that earns its keep: a gap found in a requirement costs a sentence to fix; the same gap found at acceptance costs a change order; found in service, it costs a town's water and your manager's trust. Requirements analysis will not promise you think of everything - Lwandiswa may still surprise you in month six - but every need you DO capture becomes a sentence anyone can test and trace, so the surprises are fewer, found earlier, and never again an argument about whose fault the silence was. (A missing interface requirement is one kind of missing requirement - see bp-0070; and a client capable of writing requirements is the discipline of bp-0089.)

See where it lands. The towns had water by mid-morning; one buried stakeholder conflict was settled around a table for the cost of twenty minutes instead of an outage; and the knowledge that lived only in Lwandiswa's legs now lives in a table the whole utility owns and can test. Then you sent the contractor a message - not "add an alarm", but "here is the need, the verifiable requirement, and how we will test it." A different message entirely. A system that does everything the contract says can still fail the real need, because the need lived only in someone's head and habits and was never written down. So before anything is built or bought, surface the real needs from the people who do the job, resolve the conflicting ones on paper while they are still cheap words, write each as a verifiable requirement you can test on delivery day - "when X, it must do Y within Z" - and trace it to the part that delivers it and the person who feels it if it fails. A gap found in a requirement costs a sentence to fix; the same gap found at acceptance costs a change order; found in service, it costs a town's water. That is how "it works" finally becomes "it's right".

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Let's take one step together - the same trap, in a place that looks nothing like a pump house.

See the cafe whose spotless checklist still lost the regulars

Khanyo owns a small cafe near a taxi rank. For years the morning shift was run by Asisipho, who knew without thinking that the rank fills at 06:15, so the koeksisters and the big urn of coffee had to be ready and out by 06:10, not started at 06:10. He never wrote that down. He just did it. Last month Asisipho left, and Khanyo promoted Sibusisiwe, her best baker. She handed her the same morning checklist the cafe had always used: unlock, switch on ovens, bake the day's bread, stock the fridge, open till, flip the sign at 06:00. Sibusisiwe does every item, perfectly, every morning. The checklist is spotless. And takings are down 18% in three weeks, because at 06:15 she is only just sliding the first tray in, and the regulars from the rank are buying their coffee somewhere else.

Khanyo's first instinct is the same as Sinazo's on the walkway: fire off one fix - "come in earlier." Don't. Diagnose it the way you diagnosed the pump house. Look at the checklist: every item is done, so the checklist isn't WRONG - it's INCOMPLETE. It lists the tasks, but not the NEED: "the rank crowd must be able to buy hot coffee and koeksisters the moment they arrive at 06:15." That need is the whole point of the morning shift, and it was never written down - it lived only in Asisipho's head and his hands, exactly the way keep water in the towns through the night lived only in Lwandiswa's. The handover lost it because nobody asked Asisipho the one question - what must the morning do for the cafe to keep its regulars? - and wrote the answer as a sentence you could check. A spotless checklist hid the gap, just as 217 ticked "system functions" hid the utility's: a thing that "works" is the most convincing disguise a missing need can wear. So turn the need into a verifiable requirement: "hot coffee and koeksisters must be ready on the counter by 06:10 every weekday, so the rank crowd can buy from 06:15." Now Khanyo can stand in the cafe at 06:10 and SEE whether it's met - and trace it: the need (keep the rank regulars) -> the requirement (ready by 06:10) -> the part that delivers it (start baking by 05:30, not 06:10) -> who feels it if it fails (the regulars, then the takings). When the real need lives only in someone's head and habits, a handover that copies the tasks will still lose the job; capture the need as a written, testable, traceable sentence, and "it works" finally becomes "it's right".

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The trap is not really about water or telemetry. It is letting the real job live in someone's head and habits instead of on a page everyone can check - and then discovering, too late, that what got built satisfies the order but not the need. That pattern is everywhere people commission, buy, or build something on someone else's behalf. Find it where you are:

  1. Locate your Lwandiswa. Who carries critical knowledge of what something must do only in their head, their hands, or their years of habit - knowledge that has never been written down where others could test it? Name the person and the knowledge.
  2. Find a recent "it works but it's wrong". Something delivered to you - a system, report, service, build, or a hire's brief - that ticked every box you wrote and still failed what you actually needed. What was the unwritten need nobody turned into a checkable statement?
  3. Turn one need into a verifiable requirement. Rewrite a single thing you need from a wish ("it should be reliable") into a sentence you could test on delivery day ("when X happens, it must do Y within Z, measured by..."). Notice how much harder - and how much more honest - the second sentence is.
  4. Build a two-line trace, and price the silence. For one component or clause you own, say what need it serves and what would break if it changed; then estimate what your last scope argument or late change actually cost - against the hour it would have taken to write the need down beforehand.
R

Run the smallest test this week: take the one need you wrote, sit with one person who does the job, on one subsystem or task, and ask "what must this do for you to trust it?" - then count the needs that surface that were nowhere in the written order. The yes-sign, set in advance: the count is more than zero. If it is, you've proved - in your own world - that the silence in your brief is already costing you. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring the unwritten need you found and build it into a requirement and a trace you could defend at acceptance.)

The case was a water utility; yours might be a software brief, a maintenance contract, a new hire, a renovation, a supplier agreement. The trap is identical: needs that were never translated into written, verifiable, traceable requirements before the work began.
