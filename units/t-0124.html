Six sensors, three kilometres

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and the pressure in the three-kilometre buried main to the reservoir is dropping. Somewhere out there, water is going into the ground instead of to the town.

You have six good sensors on that line. Every one of them reports normal.

And slowly a horrible realisation arrives: they are not wrong, and they are not broken. They are simply not there. Each sensor tells you the truth about the few metres of pipe it is bolted to - perhaps thirty metres of pipe between all six of them, out of three thousand. About one percent. The leak is in the other ninety-nine, and your instruments will go on calmly reporting "normal" while the road above it washes out.

So you price up covering it properly - a sensor every few metres, with cabling and power to each - and the number is absurd. It is not a budget you argue for; it is a budget nobody would ever approve. You are standing on top of three kilometres of pipe you cannot see, holding a monitoring system whose honest summary is that it proves almost nothing about the asset - and worse, whose green lights are quietly reassuring you.

My sensors are all reporting normal, and the pipe is leaking - because 99% of it has no sensor on it and never could. How do I watch an asset that is far too long to cover?

Before you read on, decide. Six sensors. Three kilometres of buried main. It is leaking, and every one of those six is reporting normal — correctly, because the leak is nowhere near any of them. Each sensor meaningfully watches a few metres of pipe. Somebody in the meeting last month pointed at the green dashboard as evidence the main was in good order. To cover this line at the density you actually have would take thousands of sensors, which is not a budget you argue for; it is a budget nobody would ever approve. Write down how you would watch an asset far too long to cover — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the argument you cannot win

• “Make the case for more sensors.” Do the arithmetic before the meeting. At a few metres each, three kilometres needs sensors in the hundreds or thousands — an argument you will lose, and should lose, because the cost is out of all proportion to the asset.

• “Move the six to the highest-risk points.” Sensible housekeeping and it changes almost nothing: six times a few metres is six times a few metres wherever you put them. It also assumes you know where the risk is, which is the thing you are trying to find out.

• “Use better sensors — higher sensitivity.” A better instrument watching the same few metres. Your problem is not resolution, it is everything in between; you do not need a better microscope, you need a map.

• “Walk the route and inspect it.” Worth doing, and it is buried — so you would be inspecting the surface above a pipe you cannot see, on a cycle, which is neither continuous nor conclusive.

• “Accept it — you cannot monitor everything, and run to failure.” Honest about the limit and wrong about the options, and it leaves the worst feature of the present arrangement untouched: those six green lights are still telling everybody the main is fine.

• Start with the part that is actually dangerous. The system does not merely miss things — it reassures, and a green dashboard covering well under one per cent of an asset is read by everybody as a statement about the asset. So count the fraction and write it down, because a number makes the gap arguable instead of invisible. Then stop treating sensing as one activity and split it by function. Some methods exist to screen: they trade fine resolution for reach, examining long stretches from a single accessible point, and they answer the question your point sensors cannot — is there anything anywhere along here. Others make a continuous medium into the instrument itself, so coverage stops depending on how many devices you buy. And point measurement stays, but is redeployed: no longer a failed attempt at coverage, but a precise local measurement aimed where screening has already pointed. One discipline holds it together — any claimed screening reach must be proven on this line, buried, coated and full of water, rather than taken from a brochure.

The answer is not more sensors. It is a different kind of sensing altogether. Let us show you the strategy - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the sensing strategy for that main - written by someone who has stopped trying to win an argument they cannot win with more point sensors.

1. The coverage gap - what fraction can you actually see?

Count it honestly. Six point sensors on a 3,000 m main, each meaningfully watching a few metres of pipe - about 30 m in total. That is roughly 1% watched and 99% unwatched. The sensors are excellent; the problem is everything between them. You do not need a better microscope - you need a map.

2. Screen wide - guided-wave testing from access points

A ring of transducers at ONE access point launches low-frequency waves along the pipe wall, screening tens of metres in BOTH directions and flagging loss of cross-section. At about 50 m each way that is 100 m per access point - roughly 30 access points for 3 km, instead of thousands of sensors. But de-rate honestly: this line is coated, buried and full of water, all of which sap the wave, so the real reach must be proven on site, not taken from a brochure.

3. Watch continuously - fibre-optic sensing

Lay a fibre along the route and the cable becomes the instrument. FBG (fibre Bragg gratings) are discrete point sensors written INTO the fibre at chosen critical locations, turning strain or temperature into a wavelength shift, many multiplexed on one line. DAS (distributed acoustic sensing) goes further: using Rayleigh backscatter, the ENTIRE fibre becomes a continuous sensor - thousands of effective listening points over kilometres.

4. What each cannot do - the honest limits

Guided wave SCREENS, it does not size - and echoes from welds, supports and branches can mask a defect lying near them. DAS senses acoustic and strain EVENTS, not wall thickness: it may hear a new pinhole leak or feel ground movement, but it cannot size a stable corrosion pit. Both need a baseline to compare against, and DAS needs a costly interrogator plus real processing capacity.

5. The two-stage plan - and the level it honestly buys

Two stages, not two rivals. Screen wide to shortlist the suspect spots, then excavate and measure locally to size what is there - screening exists to make local measurement targeted and affordable. Honestly declared, this buys reliable detection across the whole line, and localisation to the resolution of the method - not severity, and not remaining life.

Notice the shape of it. The first box is not a technology at all - it is an honest count of how little is currently watched, because that number is what justifies everything after it. And notice that the fourth box is entirely devoted to what these clever methods cannot do, written down before anyone gets the chance to assume otherwise. Now let us name the idea underneath.

T3: The Blueprint

Here is the sentence that reframes the whole problem: the problem was never the quality of your six sensors - it was the metres between them. A point sensor, however excellent, only ever tells you about the point it is fixed to. On a long asset that is a losing arithmetic, and no amount of sensor quality wins it. What you need is not a better microscope. You need a map.

So the paradigm changes. Two families of method buy you coverage, each by giving up some detail:

  • Long-range screening - guided-wave testing. A ring of transducers at a single access point launches low-frequency ultrasonic waves that travel along the pipe wall itself, reaching tens of metres in both directions and returning an echo where the cross-section has been lost to corrosion. One access point can screen a hundred metres of pipe. Its honest limits: it screens rather than sizes, and the wave attenuates - coatings, burial, soil contact and the product inside all sap it, so the real reach on your line must be proven on site, never quoted from a brochure. And pipe features - welds, supports, branches - each throw back their own echo, which can mask a defect sitting near them.
  • Distributed sensing - fibre optics. Lay a fibre along the asset and the cable becomes the instrument. FBG (fibre Bragg gratings) are point sensors written into the fibre at chosen locations, converting strain or temperature into a wavelength shift, with many multiplexed along a single line. DAS (distributed acoustic sensing) goes further: it turns the whole fibre into a continuous sensor - thousands of effective channels over kilometres. Its honest limits: an expensive interrogator, very large data volumes, real processing effort - and, crucially, it senses acoustic and strain events, not wall thickness. It might hear a new pinhole leak begin or feel the ground shifting; it cannot tell you the depth of a corrosion pit that is sitting there quietly.

Which leads to the professional answer, and it is a partnership rather than a contest: screen wide, then measure locally. The screening method's job is to turn three kilometres into a shortlist of three suspect spots. The local method's job is to go to those spots and find out how bad they actually are. Screening exists to make careful local measurement affordable and targeted; local measurement exists to turn a screening hit into a decision. Neither replaces the other, and a screening indication is a shortlist entry, never a measurement.

And be honest about what this buys you on the capability ladder: reliable detection across the whole line, and localisation to the resolution of the method - not severity, and not remaining life. Those still need what they always needed.

Does it scale? Your six point sensors are cameras on a few specific doors of a long perimeter wall. Guided-wave testing is a guard who can patrol a whole section of that wall from one position. Distributed fibre makes the entire wall itself the sensor, reporting anyone who touches it anywhere.

T4: Your Turn

Take a long or spread-out asset you actually know - a fence line, a water pipe across a campus, a long cable run, a stretch of road, a boundary wall.

  1. Count the coverage. If you checked it the way it is checked now, what fraction are you truly watching? Be brutal - a monthly walk past three points is not coverage.
  2. Choose a paradigm. Would screening from a few access points suit it better, or something continuous along its whole length? Say why, in terms of the asset, not the technology.
  3. Write what it cannot do. Name at least two things your chosen method would not tell you. This box is compulsory.
  4. Design the second stage. When it flags something, what will you actually do to find out how bad it is?

The arithmetic to feel: at roughly 50 m of reach each way, one guided-wave access point screens about 100 m - so three kilometres needs around thirty access points, against thousands of point sensors for the same line. That is the whole argument for changing paradigm, in one sum.

T5: The Guardrails

These methods are powerful and easy to oversell - including to yourself. Glance through these before you specify one.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Believing more point sensors will fix it. On a long asset this is arithmetic you cannot win. Doubling six sensors to twelve takes you from about 1% coverage to about 2%. The answer is a different paradigm, not a bigger order.

• Quoting the brochure range. Guided-wave reach is cut hard by coatings, burial, soil contact and product. The effective range on your line is something you establish on site - assuming otherwise leaves long stretches you believe are screened and are not.

• Forgetting that features mask defects. Welds, supports and branches return their own echoes. A defect sitting beside one of them can hide in that clutter, so know where your blind spots are and cover them another way.

• Treating a screening hit as a measurement. Screening produces a shortlist, not a size. Acting on a screening indication as though it were a wall-thickness reading is how good methods get blamed for bad decisions.

• Assuming distributed sensing measures thickness. It does not. DAS senses acoustic and strain events - it may hear a leak starting, but it cannot size a quiet corrosion pit. Know exactly which question your instrument answers.

• Buying coverage you cannot act on. A continuous system generating data nobody has the capacity to process, or flagging spots nobody is resourced to excavate, is an expense rather than an improvement. Plan the response before you buy the sensing.

Onalenna's instruments were never lying to her; they were answering a question about six points while she was asking a question about three kilometres. Now you hold the shift: count what you can actually see, choose sensing by the coverage the risk demands rather than by the sensitivity on a datasheet, write down plainly what your method cannot tell you, and pair every wide screen with a local measurement that turns a suspicion into a fact. Do that and the long, buried, unglamorous assets that quietly serve whole towns stop being invisible.
