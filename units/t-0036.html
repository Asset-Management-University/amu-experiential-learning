T1: The Ache

You are in the control room at a district substation, an 88/11 kV site that feeds a textile mill, two clinics and about 9,000 homes. Out in the yard sits TX-2, a 40 MVA oil-filled power transformer, 22 years old, humming under a thin morning mist. It is the only spare-less transformer on the feeder. If it goes, the mill stops and the clinics run on generators.

In your hand is the lab report from the oil sample you drew last week. The transformer's insulating oil has been tested for the gases dissolved in it - because a transformer that is quietly cooking or sparking inside makes gas long before it trips. The report is a column of names and numbers - hydrogen, methane, ethylene, acetylene, carbon monoxide - each with a figure in parts per million (ppm), which just means how many gas molecules there are for every million molecules of oil.

Standing next to you is Nandipha, the area maintenance manager. She is not angry, but she is firm. "I have a planned outage window on Saturday - three hours. I either open TX-2 up then, or I wait six months for the next window. Tell me - is something wrong inside it, and how serious?" She taps the paper. "These numbers mean nothing to me. They have to mean something to you."

The ethylene reading has jumped since last quarter. But a single high number does not tell you what is wrong - a transformer that is overheating makes a different mix of gases than one that is sparking, and the two need completely different responses. You feel the pull to just compare each gas to a "limit" in a table and call the one over the line. But that tells you a gas is high, not what is breaking.

The lab report gives me a column of numbers, but the manager needs one diagnosis to act on. How do I turn these gases into a single, defensible story about what is breaking inside my transformer?

Before you read on, decide. The lab report on TX-2 gives you, in parts per million: hydrogen 180, methane 320, ethane 95, ethylene 540, acetylene 12, carbon monoxide 420. Last quarter the ethylene was 300. The manager wants one diagnosis he can act on, not a column of numbers. Write down how you would turn this into that — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the mix matters more than any single gas

• “Compare each gas to its limit and report the ones over the line.” The natural first move, and it produces a sentence that says a gas is high. It cannot say what is breaking, which is the only thing the manager can act on.

• “Ethylene is the biggest number, so report ethylene.” Closer — the loudest gas is genuinely informative — and a single gas can be produced by more than one kind of fault. What separates them is what is alongside it.

• “Total the combustible gases and trend that.” Adds the six numbers into one and destroys the pattern, in exactly the way an overall vibration reading destroys a spectrum.

• “Take another sample to be sure.” Prudent, and the rate already tells you something urgent: ethylene has gone from 300 to 540 in a quarter — up about 80% — so this is active and worsening, and waiting is itself a decision.

• “Send it to a specialist.” Reasonable, and you will get back a diagnosis you cannot check, on an asset you are responsible for.

• The idea underneath is simple and it settles everything: different faults cook the oil at different temperatures, and each temperature produces its own mixture. So you read the mix, not any single number — and you read it more than one way, then check the readings against each other. Here the ratios all say the same thing: acetylene against ethylene is 12/540 = 0.02, essentially nothing, so there is no arcing; methane against hydrogen is 320/180 = 1.78, which points to heat rather than a low-energy discharge; ethylene against ethane is 540/95 = 5.7, high, which puts the heat at the top of the range. Three independent readings agreeing is what turns a column of numbers into one diagnosis somebody can sign — and had they disagreed, that disagreement would itself have been the finding.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to read this column of gas numbers and turn it into one clear answer - what kind of fault is growing inside TX-2, and what to do about it. There is a standard way to do it; you just need it cold. Here it is, worked on TX-2.

T2: The Tool in Action

Dissolved Gas Analysis (DGA) reads the gases dissolved in a transformer's oil and turns them into a diagnosis of what is going wrong inside; the rulebook is IEEE C57.104, so two engineers reading the same report reach the same answer. Everything rests on one idea: different faults cook the oil at different temperatures, and each temperature makes its own mix of gases. DGA reads the mix, not any single gas - through three methods you run together and cross-check. Watch them on TX-2.

Step 1 - Confirm the signal

Lay out the gas readings (ppm): H2 180, CH4 320, C2H6 95, C2H4 540, C2H2 12, CO 420. Ethylene at 540 and methane at 320 are well above the watch thresholds (tens of ppm) - a real, active fault, not noise. And the rate: ethylene rose 300 -> 540 in one quarter, +80% - getting worse fast.

Step 2 - Read the key gas

Which combustible gas shouts loudest? Ethylene (540) dominates, far above the rest, with acetylene tiny (12). The key-gas pattern for 'ethylene dominant, acetylene low' is overheating of the oil at high temperature - a thermal fault, not an electrical arc.

Step 3 - Run the Rogers ratios

The comparisons: acetylene/ethylene = 12/540 = 0.022 (essentially zero -> no arcing); methane/hydrogen = 320/180 = 1.78 (above 1 -> heat, not a low-energy discharge); ethylene/ethane = 540/95 = 5.68 (well above 4 -> a high-temperature thermal fault). All three ratios agree.

Step 4 - Plot the Duval triangle and triangulate

Take the three triangle gases: CH4+C2H4+C2H2 = 320+540+12 = 872. As percentages: 36.7% methane, 61.9% ethylene, 1.4% acetylene - the point lands in T3 (a thermal fault above 700 C). Now triangulate: key-gas, Rogers and Duval all say the same thing - a hot thermal fault, no arc. Three independent reads, one diagnosis: a metal hot-spot, most likely a loose or oxidised connection cooking the oil. Sign it.

See what the methods did. The column of numbers that "meant nothing" became one story - not "ethylene is high", but a non-arcing thermal fault above 700 degrees C, worsening fast, most likely a loose or oxidised connection cooking the oil. No single method made that call; the power was in the agreement. One gas over a limit tells you a number is high; three independent methods landing on the same fault tells you what is breaking - and that is the difference between a guess and a diagnosis you can sign your name to. Now Nandipha has her answer: a real, fast-growing thermal fault - worth opening on Saturday, not in six months.

T3: The Blueprint

You read any DGA report in four moves.

  • 1. Confirm the signal. Are the gas levels above the noise floor (tens of ppm), so there is a real fault? And is the rate of change between samples rising? Do not diagnose noise - and the trend often matters more than the level.
  • 2. Read the key gas. Which combustible gas shouts loudest? Ethylene dominant = overheating oil; acetylene present = arcing; carbon monoxide rising = the paper insulation cooking. The quick read of the mix.
  • 3. Run the Rogers ratios. Compare pairs: acetylene÷ethylene (arcing?), methane÷hydrogen (heat vs low-energy discharge), ethylene÷ethane (hot vs cool thermal). The pattern of ratios names the fault type.
  • 4. Plot the Duval triangle and triangulate. The three Duval gases as a percentage of their sum land one point in one zone; then confirm all three methods agree - one diagnosis you can sign.

The gases tell the story by temperature: gentle breakdown makes methane and ethane (the cool gases); hotter faults make ethylene (the hot gas); a violent electrical arc is hot enough to make acetylene - the alarm gas, which barely appears otherwise, so a real amount almost always means sparking; hydrogen comes up across nearly every fault, so alone it tells you little; carbon monoxide points at the paper, not the oil. The Rogers ratios are named for the engineer who developed them; the Duval triangle (after Michel Duval) is the most reliable single method, because a point can land in only one zone - partial discharge, low- or high-energy arc, or a thermal band by temperature. DGA fits any oil-filled electrical asset - power transformers, reactors, bushings, tap-changers. Handle with care: a few ppm of everything can be normal (do not diagnose noise); a single sample is a snapshot, so the rate of change between samples often matters more; and DGA gives the type and rough severity, not the exact spot - confirming the location still needs an internal inspection. DGA points the torch; it does not open the lid.

Two disciplines turn a reading into a diagnosis. First, never trust a single method or a single sample: run all three reads, and look at the trend between samples, because a rising line is worth more than any one level. Second, let the methods argue - when key-gas, Rogers and Duval disagree, you do not have a diagnosis yet, you have a question; chase it (a re-sample, a lab error, two faults at once) until they agree. The confidence to tell a manager "open it Saturday" or "it can wait" comes not from one alarming number, but from three independent methods telling the same story.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your DGA honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not diagnose noise. A few ppm of everything can be perfectly normal in a healthy transformer. You need a real signal - levels clearly above the watch thresholds - before any pattern means anything.

• The trend beats the level. A single sample is a snapshot. A gas that is high but flat for years is very different from one rising fast. Where you can, judge the rate of change between samples, not just today's number.

• Read the mix, never one gas against a limit. "Which gas is over the line?" tells you a number is high; it does not tell you what is breaking. Overheating and arcing need opposite responses - only the pattern of several gases separates them.

• Make the methods agree. Run all three - key-gas, Rogers, Duval. When they agree, you have a diagnosis. When they disagree, you have a question, not an answer: re-sample or investigate before you act.

• DGA names the fault, not the spot. It gives you the type and rough severity, and it points the torch - but confirming exactly where, and how bad, still needs an internal inspection or electrical test. Do not promise the board a location the oil cannot give you.

The column of numbers that meant nothing to Nandipha became one defensible sentence - a hot thermal fault, worsening, most likely a loose connection - because three independent methods agreed, and you could show every step. That is what lets you stand in front of her boss and say "open it Saturday", and be right. One gas is a number; a pattern is a clue; three methods in agreement is a diagnosis you can sign. Read the mix, watch the trend, make the methods argue until they agree - and a sheet of gas readings becomes the early warning that keeps a spare-less transformer, a mill and two clinics running.
