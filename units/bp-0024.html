The money owed to me isn't here when my bills are

Movement 1 - The Simulation

It is the 24th of the month, just after seven in the morning, and you are sitting at the small steel table at the back of the Tank You filling yard in Lenasia. You are Muhammad. Ibraaheem is out doing the early run. The kettle has just boiled and the yard still smells faintly of gas from yesterday's fills. Your phone is face up on the table next to a mug you have not touched.

You are looking at three things, side by side.

The first is your bank balance: R8 400.

The second is your phone, open on a group chat, where four messages are already waiting. The landlord for the yard: "Rent due 1st - R6 500, please." The fuel station where you run a small account: "Account R3 200, settle by month-end." Your cylinder supplier, who fills your bulk stock: "Next bulk load R9 000 - booked for the 28th." And a short one from your wholesaler that just says: "Insurance debit going off the 1st, R2 100."

The third is a hand-written page from the order book. You add it up slowly. This has actually been a good month. You delivered to the new guest-house in Eldorado Park, you picked up the panel-beater who orders four cylinders a week, and the spaza customers were steady. The work is done. The cylinders are plugged in. On paper you have earned R31 000 this month - more than any month before. You should be celebrating.

But you go back to the bank balance. R8 400.

You start listing what is owed to you, the money sitting out there with your business customers, the ones you let pay on account because they order big and order often. The guest-house: R7 200, and they pay "around the 7th" of next month. The panel-beater: R4 600, and he is reliable but he pays when his own customers pay, usually mid-month. A small builder's yard: R3 500, who once took six weeks. Add it up and R15 300 is owed to you. Real money. Money you earned. Money that is simply not in the bank yet.

You can feel your jaw tightening. The numbers all work. You made a profit. So why does the 1st of the month feel like a wall coming towards you?

You do the cruel little sum on the back of the order page. Bills landing on or before the 1st: rent R6 500, fuel account R3 200, the bulk load R9 000 on the 28th, the insurance R2 100. That is R20 800 going out in the next week. You have R8 400. The money owed to you - the R15 300 that would more than cover it - does not arrive until the 7th, the 14th, whenever your customers feel like paying.

A voice in your head offers the easy way out, and it is tempting: just phone the panel-beater and the guest-house and ask them to pay early. You imagine the call. The guest-house owner was warm and grateful last week; the panel-beater sends you four orders without fail. You picture yourself, four days into a relationship, saying "sorry, can you pay me now, I'm short." Your stomach turns. That is your best, most loyal business. Lean on them in a panic and you teach them that ordering from you on account comes with awkward phone calls. You might keep the cash and lose the customer.

Ibraaheem's van pulls back into the yard. He swings the door, sees your face, and stops. "What's wrong? It was a good month."

That is exactly the problem. It was a good month. And you are about to be short by twelve thousand rand.

You earned more than you spent. The money exists. It is just not here when the bills are.

Where do you start? What is it you actually need to see that the order book and the bank balance, on their own, are not showing you?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Before you read how Ibraaheem and Muhammad worked their way out of this, stop and try it yourself. Make your best attempt first - it is worth more than reading the answer cold.

This is the trap: the month was profitable, and they were still about to run out of money. So profit, clearly, is not the same thing as money-in-the-bank. Hold that thought and work with these questions. You can check each one yourself against the facts above - you do not need our answer to know if you are right.

  1. Find what neither document carries. Look at the order book - does it show the day each payment lands? Now look at the bank balance - does it show the days money must go out? Write down the one thing both of them leave out. To check your answer, try to use each document, on its own, to answer this: "On the 14th, will I have enough?" Neither one can answer it - and the reason it cannot is exactly the missing piece. (If neither the order book nor the bank balance records timing, you have found it.)
  2. Make two short lists for the next two weeks: every amount of money going out (with the day it leaves) and every amount coming in (with the day it actually lands, not the day it was earned). What does laying them side by side, day by day, let you see that a single monthly total hides?
  3. Rank the gap-closers by what they cost. Phoning loyal customers to pay early is off the table. List 3 other ways to cover a two-week gap - for example: delay a supplier payment, a short overdraft, defer your own drawings, a small bridging loan. For each one, write its cost in rand and its cost in trust (score it 1-5). Now add the two costs together for each row. The best move is simply the row with the lowest combined cost - and you can see which one that is from your own scored list, before any solution. (Notice that deferring your own drawings usually costs R0 and 0 in trust, while a loan costs interest and an overdraft costs fees.)
  4. Test every fix you can think of with one question. Write down your proposed fix, then ask it this: "Does this change WHEN money arrives or leaves - or does it try to change HOW MUCH is earned?" A fix passes only if it works on timing - for example: invoice on delivery instead of month-end, ask for a deposit up front, negotiate longer supplier terms, keep a small buffer. If your fix needs higher income to work, it fails the test - because the scenario already proved that a good income month still crashed. Sort your own ideas into two piles, "timing" and "income", and throw out every income pile. What is left is your answer, and you decided it yourself.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Ibraaheem pulled up a crate and sat. They did not phone a single customer that morning. Instead they did something they had never done before: they stopped looking at the month as one number and started building the smallest instrument that would make the invisible visible - they started looking at it day by day.

Before they drew anything, Muhammad said out loud what he expected to find, so he could not fool himself afterwards. His success line: "If we really did earn more than we spent, the running balance should end the month above zero. If profit and cash are the same thing, it should never dip below zero at all." That was the test. If the line stayed above zero the whole way, the panic was just nerves. If it dipped, the dip - and exactly which day it fell on - was the real enemy.

Muhammad turned the order page over and drew two columns. On the left, money going out, with the date it leaves. On the right, money coming in, with the date it actually arrives - not the date the work was done, the date the cash truly lands. Then he added a running balance line underneath, starting from today's R8 400, and carried it forward one day at a time.

It looked like this:

24th (today)--R8 400
28th - bulk gas loadR9 000--R600
1st - rentR6 500--R7 100
1st - insurance debitR2 100--R9 200

~20th - builder's yard pays-R3 500+R2 900

The moment they drew it, the problem stopped being a fog and became a shape - and reality answered Muhammad's success line in two parts. The first part went as he hoped: the month ends at +R2 900. They really did earn more than they spent; the line came back above zero, just as he predicted it would. But the second part was the surprise that changed everything, and it was a real one - not a small slip, a wrong assumption about how the business worked. Muhammad had quietly believed that a profitable month was a safe month. The instrument proved it false in front of him: from the 28th to about the 7th, the running balance dives below zero and bottoms out at -R12 400 around the 2nd. A good month had a nine-day hole punched straight through the middle of it. That is the meaningful correction the test forced: the danger was never the size of the month, it was a valley in the middle, and the deepest point of that valley is the only thing that can actually sink them. Everything after the 7th heals itself.

That is the whole insight, and it has a name. What they had just built is a cash-flow forecast - a plain map of when money arrives against when it must leave. And what it revealed is the thing that had been hurting them all along: profit and cash are not the same. Profit asks did you earn more than you spent over the month? - yes, by R2 900. Cash asks is the money in your hand on the day the bill is due? - and for nine days, the answer was no. A good earning month had a cash valley hidden inside it, because the money owed to them arrived after the bills it was meant to pay. The gap between the two had simply never been mapped.

Once they could see the valley, they did not stop at the diagnosis - they went and found out, by the cheapest honest means, whether they could actually cross it. The fix had to be about timing, not earning. They did not need R12 400 forever. They needed to get a few thousand rand across a nine-day gap. Three options were on the table:

  • Phone loyal customers to pay early. Free in rand, but expensive in trust - exactly the move that turned Muhammad's stomach. They ruled it out.
  • Move one bill. The supplier had filled their bulk stock for two years. Rather than assume the answer, Muhammad set a tiny success line for the call - "if he says yes without it costing the relationship, this move is real" - and then made the cheapest possible test: he simply phoned and asked, calmly and in advance, "Can we take the 28th load on the 9th instead?" Because he asked early - not in a panic, not on the day - the supplier said yes without blinking. Reality confirmed the move. That single yes lifted the R9 000 out of the bottom of the valley. Re-drawing the map, the deepest point became about -R3 400 instead of -R12 400.
  • A small arranged buffer. For the remaining gap, they spoke to the bank about a modest overdraft - a few thousand rand they could dip into and repay within the same month when the customer money landed. It carries a small cost in interest, and that is the honest trade-off: you pay a little to bridge time you cannot otherwise bridge. But arranged calmly in advance, it is cheap insurance against a missed rent payment that could cost them the yard.

Between moving one bill and a small buffer, the valley flattened to something they could cross without touching a single customer relationship. So Muhammad made his call: he held the plan, with one change forced by the test - he kept the day-by-day map as his early-warning instrument, but he abandoned the belief that "good month = safe month" for good. The forecast, not his gut, would tell him when a valley was coming.

The deeper lesson they carried forward: a profitable month can still produce a crisis month if the timing is not mapped. From then on, on the 24th of every month, Muhammad drew the two columns and the running balance line forward two weeks. It took ten minutes. It turned "the 1st feels like a wall" into "the dip is on the 2nd, it's -R3 400, here's how we cross it" - a problem you can see coming is a problem you can plan around. And the trade-offs stayed honest: moving bills works only if you ask early and do not wear out the goodwill; a buffer costs interest; neither earns you a cent more - they simply line up when the money you already earned is in your hand.

The concept, named only now because the problem revealed the need for it: a cash-flow forecast, and the truth underneath it - profit is not cash; what matters is whether the money is there on the day the bill is due.

Do this yourself

You do not need software. You need a page, two columns and a running line. Here is the bare recipe Muhammad now runs in ten minutes:

  1. Write today's real bank balance at the top.
  2. List every amount going out, each on the date it actually leaves.
  3. List every amount coming in, each on the date it truly lands - not the day you earned it or were promised it.
  4. Sort the rows by date and carry the balance forward, one line at a time: previous balance - out + in.
  5. Find the lowest point and the day it falls on. That single number, on that single day, is the only thing you have to cross.
  6. Cross it with a timing move, not an earning move - and write down what each move costs you in rand and in trust.

Method & Finding

  • The Method (the lesson - reusable): Build the smallest instrument that makes the invisible visible - set a success line first ("the dip should/should not appear"), then map money out against money in day by day, and read where the truth actually is.
  • The Finding (in this story only): A genuinely profitable month (+R2 900) still hid a nine-day cash valley bottoming at -R12 400 - proving Muhammad's "good month = safe month" belief wrong and forcing a timing fix, not an earning one.
  • Your Check (how you will know in your world): Carry your own balance forward day by day for the next four weeks and read off your single lowest point and the exact date it falls on - that one number on that one day is your real test, not your monthly profit.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Good. You have seen the clean version - clustered business bills, money owed by customers, a clear valley. Now let us take one step together before you walk on your own, because the real test is spotting this when it is wearing a disguise.

Here is a different person, a different scale. Meet Mookho. She is not running a business. She earns a steady salary - R18 000 lands on the 25th, every month, like clockwork. Her bond, her debit orders and the groceries all fit comfortably inside that salary. Most months she is fine. She is, by any honest measure, living within her means.

This month, a single letter arrives on the 19th: her car licence renewal, R2 400, due by the 22nd. On the 19th her account holds R900 - the lean end of the month, just before payday. She stares at it and thinks: "Why am I short? I don't overspend. I earn enough. This is just bad luck - a one-off."

Take a beat. What do you notice about Mookho's situation that feels familiar?

(Pause here. Look back at Muhammad before you read on.)

Show me ▸

Here is the first hint. Ask the two questions you learned, separately. Over the month, does Mookho earn more than she owes? Yes - easily; R18 000 covers a R2 400 licence and everything else. On the 22nd, is the money in her hand? No - she has R900, and payday is the 25th. Notice that those are the exact same two questions that exposed Muhammad's valley. Earned enough; not there on the day.

So where does the trap hide here? Where do both Mookho's shortfall and Muhammad's shortfall actually come from?

(Pause. Say it in your own words before the reveal.)

Show me ▸

The reveal: the bill landed before the income meant to cover it. The licence is due on the 22nd; the salary that easily pays for it arrives on the 25th. That is a three-day gap - small, but it is the same shape as Muhammad's nine-day gap. Mookho's "one-off bad luck" is not bad luck at all. It is a timing gap that simply does not happen every month, so it ambushes her because it is rare. That is the disguise: when the gap is hidden inside a clustered bad month, you suspect a problem; when it shows up alone, dressed as one unlucky letter, you blame yourself for "being short" and miss the structure entirely.

Now finish it with the tool. Drop her into the same pro-forma. Today (19th): R900. Out: R2 400 on the 22nd → running balance -R1 500. In: R18 000 on the 25th → +R16 500. The valley is one line deep and three days wide. And the fix is, once again, a timing move, not an earning move - she earns plenty. She could phone the licensing office and ask to pay on the 26th; she could move one flexible debit order back four days; or, simplest of all, next year she can see this exact letter coming and set R200 aside each month so the R2 400 is already waiting. None of those make her richer. They line up when her money is in hand.

The deep-structure lesson: "I earn enough, this is just a one-off" is the most common disguise this problem wears. A timing gap does not have to be a recurring monthly valley to be real - a single bill that lands before its income is the same problem as a whole cluster of them. Once you can name it - period fine, short on the day - you can see it whether it comes as a crisis or as one stray letter. And you fix it the same way every time: with timing, and in advance.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Ibraaheem and Muhammad's crisis was a gas business, but the shape of it is everywhere: money you have genuinely earned, arriving after the obligations it was meant to cover. This same problem-type lives in your world too. Let us go and find it.

  1. Find your own valley. Take the next four weeks. On one side, list every amount of money that must leave you, with the date it leaves - rent or bond, accounts, debit orders, school fees, a debt repayment, stock you must buy. On the other side, list every amount coming in, with the date it actually lands - not when you earned it or were promised it, the day it truly hits your account. Carry a running balance forward day by day from today's real balance. Where is your lowest point, and on which day?
  2. Name the gap between earning and arriving. Where in your life or work does money you have already earned arrive later than the bill it should have paid? A salary that lands on the 25th against rent due on the 1st. A client who pays on 30-day terms. A grant or commission that comes in arrears. Pick the one that pinches hardest and write down exactly how many days the gap is.
  3. Separate the two questions. For one recent month, ask both questions honestly: did I earn more than I spent? and was the money in my hand on the day each bill was due? If the answers differ - earned enough, but short on the day - you have found this exact problem. Name it.
  4. Choose your timing move, not an earning move. If you saw a valley in question 1, list the ways you could cross it that do not depend on earning more: asking to move a payment date (early, calmly, before the crunch), arranging a small buffer in advance, shifting when you buy stock. For each, name the real cost - in rand, and in trust or goodwill. Which one crosses your gap most cheaply?
  5. Build the ten-minute habit. Decide on a day each month - the 24th worked for them - when you draw the two columns and the running line forward two weeks. What would have to be true for you to actually do it every month?

The honest transfer: this is not advice to spend less or earn more - it is a different skill entirely. It is the skill of seeing when money moves, so a timing gap stops ambushing you. The numbers, the people and the bills will be yours, not theirs.
