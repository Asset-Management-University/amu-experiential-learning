T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning and you are the new risk lead at a regional water utility - one dam, two treatment works, hundreds of kilometres of pipeline, serving about 120,000 people across a few small towns in the arid interior. You are in the small board room; the aircon is broken, the window is open, and you can hear a pump in the yard cycling on and off.

On the table are three documents, and they do not agree. The first, the operations risk log from the treatment works, rates a chlorine leak "High". The second, the finance risk note, rates the same leak "Medium" - because finance scored it only on money lost, not on people harmed. The third, the safety file, does not mention the chlorine store at all, but spends two pages on slippery floors. Three teams, one organisation, three different ideas of what "High" even means.

Zwelakhe, the board chair, leans in. He has read all three. "So which is it?" he asks - not angry, but not smiling either. "When you bring risks to this board, I need them to mean the same thing whoever wrote them. Right now I cannot compare any of these. I cannot tell you what our biggest risk is. And the auditor is coming in March." Your stomach tightens, because he is right. You have a stack of risk lists. What you do not have is a shared way to make them. The quick fix is tempting - merge the three into one big spreadsheet tonight - but you can already see it will not hold: next month a new team will score risks their own way again, and you will be back here.

Before you read on, decide. The same chlorine leak, scored High by one team, Medium by another, and not recorded at all by the third. The chair cannot compare them, cannot say what the biggest risk is, and the auditor comes in March. Write down what you would do. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one does next month

• “Merge the three lists into one spreadsheet tonight.” Quick, and you can already see it will not hold. You would be adding up numbers made three different ways, and next month a fourth team scores risks their own way and you are back in this chair with a longer document.

• “Get the three teams in a room and agree on this one.” It settles the chlorine leak and settles nothing else. The next disputed risk arrives with the same three people and the same three habits — and whoever argues hardest wins, which is what the chair is objecting to.

• “Take the highest score — safest when we disagree.” Cautious, and it makes the register useless in the other direction: if every disagreement resolves upward, everything becomes High, and a board that cannot rank cannot spend.

• “Score everything in rands so it’s comparable.” Genuinely tempting because money adds up. A chlorine leak’s consequence is not a cost — the team that scored it Medium was probably pricing the plant, and the team that scored it High was thinking about a person.

• “Buy risk software — it will standardise us.” It will standardise the boxes and not the judgements. Three people will still enter three different numbers, more neatly.

The chair asked for something narrower and harder than a better list: that a High should mean the same thing whoever wrote it. That is a question about rules, not about this leak.

The answer is not another list - it is one shared way for the whole organisation to find, judge and treat risk: the same rules everyone uses, anchored by a line the board draws. Here is that tool, worked on the very leak that started the fight.

T2: The Tool in Action

When every team judges risk its own way, you cannot add risks up, compare them, or trust them. ISO 31000 - the international guide to managing risk - fixes this by agreeing one shared way of working. At its heart is a single process run on every risk, and one number agreed at the top: the risk appetite, the line that turns a score into a decision. Watch the four boxes settle the disputed chlorine leak.

Box 1 - The risk, and what you most protect

The risk: a chlorine gas release from the store at the main works. What the utility most protects, ranked by the board: (1) public health, (2) continuous water supply, (3) staff and public safety, (4) legal compliance, (5) money - so this risk is scored on health and safety, NOT on money (which is exactly how finance mis-rated it 'Medium').

Box 2 - Score it on the ONE shared scale

On the one 5x5 grid: likelihood 3 (Possible - ageing valves, last clear check 14 months ago); consequence 4 (Severe - a release could cause lasting injury or worse to staff and nearby homes). Score = 3 x 4 = 12. The same rules give the same 12 whoever does the scoring.

Box 3 - Evaluate against the risk appetite (the line the board drew)

The board's appetite: 1-6 Low (monitor only), 7-12 Medium (a named owner + a watch date), 13-25 High (above appetite - must be treated, and the board must see it). 12 is Medium - BUT the built-in override (any consequence of 4-5 on health or safety escalates one band) lifts it to High/red. Above appetite. The argument is over: the rules decided.

Box 4 - Treat, own, and review

Four options, named honestly: Avoid (switch dosing method, ~R3.2m over 18 months - parked); Reduce (replace the valves + a gas detector and alarm + retrain staff = R180,000, dropping likelihood 3 to 2 so 2 x 4 = 8, back within appetite - CHOSEN); Transfer (insurance covers money, not lives - a top-up only); Accept (rejected - above appetite). Owner: the Works Manager. Review: 3 months. The board chair reads it and approves the R180,000 in two minutes.

See what just happened. The same leak that scored "High" and "Medium" and "ignored" now has one answer - and not because the loudest team won, but because the rules decided. Scoring it on health and safety (not money) gave a 12; the safety override lifted it above the board's appetite line; and a R180,000 plan brought it back. A shared scale plus a board-agreed appetite turns "High" from an opinion into a decision anyone can audit. The board chair who could not compare three documents now reads one register in two minutes and approves the spend - and the shared way will still be there after you leave.

T3: The Blueprint

You run any single risk through four steps - the repeatable process at the core of ISO 31000.

  • 1. The risk, and what you most protect. Name the risk, and rank what the organisation most protects (people, supply, safety, compliance, money) - because consequence is scored on the worst-affected matter, not money alone. That ranking, agreed at the top, settles arguments before they start.
  • 2. Score it on the one shared scale. Likelihood times consequence on the same 1-to-5 grid every team uses, so a score means the same thing whoever wrote it.
  • 3. Evaluate against the risk appetite. Compare the score to the line the board drew on the bands - below it, monitor; above it, act - plus any override (anything that can kill escalates a band, whatever the money). This is what turns a number into a decision.
  • 4. Treat, own, and review. For risks above appetite, choose honestly among Avoid, Reduce, Transfer and Accept, then give the risk one named owner and a review date.

That process is one of three parts of ISO 31000, and it helps to keep them apart. The principles are the qualities good risk management must have - above all that it must create and protect value (earn its keep, not just generate paperwork) and be part of how decisions are made, not a separate folder. The framework is the standing machinery that makes it run - who is accountable, the policy, the resources, and a Plan-Do-Check-Act loop: agree the policy and scale (Plan), run the process across the organisation (Do), see whether it is working (Check), and fix what is not (Act). And the process above is the steps on each risk. A simple way to hold them: principles are why and how well, the framework is the machinery, the process is the steps on each risk.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your risk framework honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Score on who gets hurt, not money alone. The leak was mis-rated "Medium" because finance counted only rands. Build health and safety into the consequence scale, and add an override so anything that can kill a person is escalated regardless of cost. People are not a line item.

• Without an appetite line, "High" is just a word. The board must draw the line - what is acceptable, what must be acted on - in advance. That line is what turns a score into a decision; without it, every rating is back to opinion.

• One scale for everyone, or you cannot compare. The moment two teams use two grids, your risks stop adding up. The same likelihood bands and consequence ladders must apply from the pump operator to the board.

• A framework nobody uses protects nobody. The point is the running machinery, not the document. Wire in the routines - one register template, red risks to the board, every risk with an owner and a date - and audit once a year whether people actually use it and whether the scores predicted reality.

• The matrix is a shared tool, not a truth machine. A 5-by-5 grid is right for most risks, but it hides its assumptions; for tangled, high-stakes risks where likelihood and consequence are not independent, know its limits and reach for a richer method.

The three arguing documents were never really about chlorine - they were about an organisation with no agreed way to think, so the loudest voice or the narrowest spreadsheet won. A shared framework takes that power away from volume and gives it to rules everyone helped set. A shared scale and a board-agreed appetite line turn "High" risk from a subjective opinion into an objective, auditable decision. Build it once, wire it in, and the board can finally see its real top risks - and put money where it protects water and people, not paperwork. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
