The canyon between the board and the plant

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have done the two things the standard asks. You have a compliant asset management policy - a clear one-page statement of intent. You have good asset management plans - costed, risk-based, one per asset group. You feel ready for the board.

Then the chair asks a fair question. "Our strategy is to absorb Stellaford and serve about forty per cent more demand over the next twenty years. Show us how your two-hundred-million-rand asset programme delivers that."

And you cannot. Your plans are excellent at keeping today's pumps reliable and say nothing about a twenty-year objective. Your policy is a page of fine intentions with not a single number on it. You are standing in a canyon: the board's strategy is on one rim, your plans are on the other, and there is nothing spanning the gap. Every plan you have is locally sensible and strategically blind, and you cannot show that one rand of your programme serves what the organisation is actually trying to become.

My policy says what we intend and my plans say what we will do to the assets - but nothing connects the two, so I cannot show the board that our asset decisions deliver their strategy. What is the document that spans the canyon?

Before you read on, decide. The board's strategy says three things: serve a growing region, keep the water safe and affordable, stay financially sustainable. Directions, in words, with no asset anywhere in them. Your plans say what will be done to which pumps, in which year, at what cost — tasks and dates, with no strategy anywhere in them. Both are good documents. Someone has just asked you how your programme delivers the strategy, and you cannot answer — not from nerves, but because the connection does not exist. Neither document can be derived from the other by reading it harder. Write down what would span that gap — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what actually has to happen in the middle

• “Put the strategy's objectives at the top of each plan.” Decoration. A heading quoting a board aim above a list of pump overhauls asserts a connection rather than making one, and the first person to test it will find nothing underneath.

• “Write a better policy.” Your policy is fine — it says what you intend. The gap is not on either rim; it is the span between them, and another statement of intent adds to one side.

• “Report on more measures so the board can see performance.” More reporting on activity nobody has connected to their aims. You would be sending better evidence that you are busy.

• “Ask the board what they want from the assets.” Reasonable, and it hands them a job they cannot do — they set direction in the language of the organisation, and converting that into what the assets must achieve is precisely your expertise, not theirs.

• “Justify each plan on its own merits — condition, risk, cost.” This is what you have been doing, and it is why every plan is locally sensible and strategically blind. Each is defensible alone and none of them adds up to anything the board recognises.

• What is missing is a document whose entire purpose is conversion, and the conversion is the substance rather than a preamble to it. Take each organisational intention — which you receive rather than invent, and which is not yours to argue with — and translate it into something the assets can be held to: with a number, a date, and a visible parent above it. That last requirement disqualifies most of what gets written. “Improve reliability” is not an objective, because nothing can be measured against it and nothing above it demands it. “Fifteen per cent more capacity by year eight, because the region grows and Stellaford must be absorbed” is one. Do that for each intention and you get a line of sight you can walk in both directions — down from a boardroom sentence to a specific action, and up from any rand of spend to the intention it serves. That is the difference between a programme that is reasonable and one that is defensible.

It has a name and a shape, and it fits on one page. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the Strategic Asset Management Plan - the SAMP - for the regional water board. Read it top to bottom: it starts at the boardroom and ends at a specific action, and you can walk the line between them.

1. What is the organisation trying to achieve? (you receive these, you do not invent them)

From the regional water board's strategic plan, three organisational objectives: (A) serve the growing region, absorbing the Stellaford scheme and meeting about 40% more demand over 20 years; (B) keep the water safe and affordable; (C) be financially sustainable. Note what these are: directions, in words, with no asset in them. They are the board's to decide. My job is not to argue them - it is to convert them into something the plant can act on.

2. So what must the assets achieve, with numbers and dates? (the conversion - the heart of the SAMP)

Each organisational objective CONVERTED into a measurable asset-management objective. From (A): treatment and distribution capacity +40% by year 20, with an interim +15% by year 8 to absorb Stellaford. From (B): meet the drinking-water standard on 100% of tests, and hold real tariff growth at or below inflation. From (C): cut non-revenue water from 32% to below 15% by year 5, and keep asset-renewal spend below R 50m a year in real terms. 'Improve reliability' is not an objective. '+15% capacity by year 8' is - it has a number, a date, and a parent above it.

3. How will the plans be built to deliver these? (the approach, not the plans themselves)

The approach the AMPs (see the AMP unit) must follow: one plan per asset class - raw water, treatment, distribution, electromechanical - each built to deliver the year-8 and year-20 objectives above, not just to keep today's plant running. Prioritise every candidate action by three criteria: its whole-life cost, the risk it retires, and how much it contributes to a stated asset-management objective. An action that serves no objective does not enter a plan. This box does not contain the plans; it sets the rules by which they are written.

4. The line of sight - can I trace it both ways?

DOWN: organisational objective A (absorb Stellaford, +40%) -> AM objective (+15% capacity by year 8) -> raw-water and treatment augmentation strategy -> AMP action: commission the Stellaford interconnector and a fourth filter by year 7. UP: a team proposes a R 12m distribution-main replacement - why? It traces to the non-revenue-water objective (below 15% by year 5), which traces to organisational objective C (financial sustainability). It earns its place. If a proposed action traces UP to nothing, the SAMP has just told you to question it. That two-way trace is the whole point of the document.

5. Over what horizon, and does it hold under different futures?

Horizon: a 20-year plan held within a 40-year view, because the assets last 40+ years and a 5-year horizon would hide the renewal wave. Then stress the objectives against three plausible futures: HIGH GROWTH (demand arrives faster - can year 8 move to year 6?); FUNDING SQUEEZE (renewal budget cut - which objective yields, and who decides?); DROUGHT (supply, not demand, becomes the binding constraint). A SAMP that only works in one future is a forecast, not a strategy. The board reviews it every year and refreshes it fully every five - this document is alive.

Notice what it is not. It is not the policy - the policy is the intent above it. It is not the detailed plans - those are the operational work below it. It is the one document in the middle whose entire job is to convert what the organisation wants into measurable things the assets must achieve, and to make the connection traceable in both directions. That conversion, in box 2, is the heart of the whole thing.

T3: The Blueprint

The standard sets up three tiers, and you have already met two of them. The asset management policy is strategic intent - one page, no numbers. The asset management plans (AMPs) are operational detail - costed, per asset group. Between them sits the SAMP, and its job is defined precisely: it specifies how organisational objectives are converted into asset-management objectives, the approach for developing the plans, and the role of the management system in delivering them. It is the bridge, and without it the other two tiers cannot reach each other. Build it in five moves, each a question:

  • What is the organisation trying to achieve? Read the organisational objectives off the board's strategic plan. You receive these; you do not invent them. They are directions in words - "serve the growing region", "be financially sustainable" - with no asset in them yet.
  • So what must the assets achieve, with numbers and dates? This is the conversion, and it is the reason the SAMP exists. Turn each organisational objective into a SMART asset-management objective: specific, measurable, dated, and visibly derived from its parent. "Be financially sustainable" becomes "non-revenue water below fifteen per cent by year five." "Improve reliability" is not an objective - it has no number, no date, and no parent. A real asset-management objective always has all three.
  • How will the plans be built to deliver these? State the approach - the method and the decision criteria the AMPs must follow, not the plans themselves. Here: one plan per asset class, each built to hit the dated objectives, every candidate action prioritised by whole-life cost, risk retired, and contribution to a stated objective. An action that serves no objective never enters a plan.
  • Can I trace it both ways? This is the line of sight, and it is the test of whether the SAMP actually works. Down: from an organisational objective, through its asset-management objective, to a concrete action a plan will authorise. Up: take any proposed spend and justify it back to strategy. If an action traces up to nothing, the document has just told you to question it. A SAMP you cannot walk in both directions is decoration.
  • Over what horizon, and does it hold under different futures? Set a horizon matched to the assets - decades, not budget years - and stress the objectives against two or three plausible scenarios (faster growth, a funding squeeze, a drought). A plan that only works in one future is a forecast, not a strategy. Then name the review cycle, because the document is alive.

One honest boundary. You build the bridge. You do not write the organisation's business strategy - the board owns the organisational objectives, and inventing your own is overreach. You do not write the detailed costed plans - that is the AMP's job. You are the translator: you do not decide what the two sides want, you make it possible for them to act as one.

Does it scale? A translator in a negotiation does not choose either party's position; they make sure a sentence spoken on one side arrives whole on the other - which is the only way the two ever reach an agreement they can both act on.

T4: Your Turn

A different organisation: a university that runs its own campus estate - buildings, water, power, grounds. From its strategic plan, three organisational objectives:

  • grow student numbers by a quarter over ten years;
  • cut the campus carbon footprint by half by 2035;
  • protect the institution's finances against a volatile grant.
  1. Convert. Turn each organisational objective into a SMART asset-management objective - specific, measurable, dated, and clearly derived from its parent.
  2. Approach. Write one line of the approach: how would the estate's plans be prioritised to deliver all three at once when they compete?
  3. Line of sight. Trace ONE complete line, top to bottom: from an organisational objective, through its asset-management objective, to a single concrete action a plan would authorise.
  4. Horizon. Name one plausible future that would put two of these objectives in direct conflict, and say which one you would expect the board to protect.

The test of a real answer: read each asset-management objective you wrote and find its number, its date, and its parent objective. If any one is missing, you have written an intention, not an objective - rewrite it until you can trace it upward to something the board actually said.

T5: The Guardrails

The SAMP goes wrong in a few very recognisable ways, most of them a failure to stay in the middle tier.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Re-writing the policy or the plans instead of the bridge. If your SAMP reads like a page of intentions, you have written the policy again. If it reads like a costed task list, you have written an AMP. The SAMP is neither - it is the conversion and the line of sight between them.

• Asset-management objectives that are not measurable or not derived. "Improve reliability", "enhance sustainability" - no number, no date, no parent. Every asset-management objective must be traceable upward to an organisational objective and downward to something you can count.

• Writing the organisation's strategy yourself. Inventing objectives the board never set is overreach dressed as initiative. You convert the objectives you are given; if they are missing or unclear, you go and ask - you do not fill the gap with your own.

• A beautiful document with no line of sight. The test is not how it reads; it is whether you can trace any action up to strategy and any objective down to an action. If you cannot walk it both ways, it does not work, however polished it looks.

• One horizon for a multi-decade asset base. A five-year SAMP for assets that last forty years hides the renewal wave and the demand that has not arrived yet. Match the horizon to the assets, not to the budget cycle.

• A single-future plan. If the SAMP only holds when everything goes to plan, it is a forecast. Test it against a funding cut and a demand surge before you take it to the board, and say what gives in each.

Onalenna had the intent and she had the plans, and still could not answer the board, because the document that joins them was missing. Now you can build it: read what the organisation wants, convert it into measurable asset objectives, set the rules the plans must follow, prove the line of sight runs both ways, and hold it all over a horizon long enough to matter. That is the SAMP - not the grandest of the three documents, but the one that makes the other two worth having. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, bring one real organisational objective and convert it out loud - the number and the date are harder to find than they look.
