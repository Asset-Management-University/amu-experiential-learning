Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Faizel, the spares controller, and a piercing alarm cuts through the depot's hum - the main packing line is down, costing a fortune every minute and risking a major customer. The maintenance team radios in: a critical motor has failed on the C-101 packer. You spin to the keyboard - the system is your world - and pull up C-101: it's a "Model B". You find the motor on the parts list, check stock, and relief washes over you: three in stock, Aisle 7, Bin 4. You grab the picking note and run.

But Bin 4 is bare. Dust. Nothing. As you stand there in disbelief, a senior engineer jogs past: "The C-101 motor? That's a Model C now - we upgraded it last year. Different part. Special order." The floor drops away. The records were wrong two ways at once: they said you had a part you didn't, and described a machine that no longer exists. Every decision you just made was built on a lie - the wrong part is now being rushed in, the line is down two extra days - and a cold dread rises: what ELSE is wrong? What other orders and plans are silently corrupted?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

In the panic, the first instinct is to grab another, more official-looking record and act on that - but that's just gambling on the same broken system. The second thought is to give up on the system and keep everything in your own private notebook, trusting only what you've seen - but now you're the bottleneck, and the shared records everyone else uses rot on without you. The third idea is a huge heroic "clean-up day" to fix everything at once - but that won't restart today's line, you can't fix thousands of records in one go, and with no change of habit they'll be wrong again in six months.

The records are lying. So before you read on: in this emergency, what is the one thing you actually can trust - and what is the real disease that caused the emergency in the first place?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

When the foundational records are wrong, every plan built on them inherits the error - so you can't just patch the cracks, you rebuild trust from reality upwards. Five moves:

  • See two problems, not one - the emergency and the disease: today's stopped line is the EMERGENCY; the fact that your basic records don't match the real world is the DISEASE underneath. Treat them separately - if you only solve the emergency, the disease just causes another one next week. Naming both stops you pouring all your effort into the fire while the thing lighting the fires burns on.
  • Solve the emergency against reality, not the record: for the urgent problem, trust your feet and your eyes, not the screen. Walk to the machine, read the real model off its metal plate; look at the broken part to get its real number; go to the actual shelf and count what's truly there. Act on what is physically TRUE, not what's written in the faulty record. That gets the line running again on solid ground.
  • Find the single source of truth and correct the record: the cure for the disease is to make the records match reality - and for each thing the 'one true source' is the physical object itself (or the signed original, the real reading). Go to it, correct the record to match (the C-101 is a Model C; the real shelf count is what it is), and if you find two records for the same thing, delete the old one. The goal is simple: one record for each real thing, and that record must be true.
  • Fix the foundation by priority: you can't fix ten thousand records overnight - so start where a wrong record costs the most. Correct the records for the critical machines that stop the whole plant first, then the high-value spares, then the safety items, and work down. Fixing by priority means the errors that would hurt people and the mission most are made true first, not left to chance.
  • Stop the bleeding - owner and rule: to stop the records rotting again, give them an owner and one simple standing rule - for example, a named person responsible, and 'the record is updated the moment the real thing changes; one record per item'. Without this they silently rot and multiply right back. Trustworthy records are not a one-time clean-up - they're a promise you keep, every day.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about machines - any basic records drift from reality. Watch a phone's contact list.

See the same four moves with a contact list

Your contacts are a set of basic records. Over time people change numbers and move, and you end up with three entries for one person ("Jim", "Jim Work", "Jim Smith") - the records no longer describe reality.

• The emergency: you urgently need Jim about a family matter - you call the first number, and it's disconnected.
• Solve against reality: you don't just try the other two and hope - you text a mutual friend you trust and ask "what's Jim's number now?", then act on that real, verified answer.
• Fix the foundation: once it's sorted, you update the one contact for Jim Smith with the new number and delete the two old, confusing entries - one record for one real person.
• Owner and rule: you own your contacts; your rule is "when I get a new number, I update the card and delete the old one straight away."

Same disease, same cure: check reality, find the one true source, fix the record, and keep it true on purpose.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of a set of basic records you rely on - a customer or address list, a price list, a parts catalogue, your team's contact details. Have they drifted from reality - duplicates, old information, things just plain wrong?

  1. Separate the two problems. Deal with the immediate need (the emergency), but name the underlying issue as the faulty record (the disease) - and don't let solving one hide the other.
  2. Solve against reality, then correct the record. Find the one true source - the real person, the physical item, the signed contract - act on that truth, then fix that record to match and kill any duplicates. Start where an error would cost you most in time, money, or trust.
  3. Stop the bleeding. Decide who OWNS these records and the one simple RULE that keeps them corrected the moment reality changes.
R

Name the records, their one true source, and who'll own them. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Pick the costliest-when-wrong set to fix first together.)

That the records had rotted was never your failing - records drift everywhere the moment no one is keeping them, and trusting the system was the reasonable thing to do. The fault was in a foundation no one had been asked to keep true, and you can rebuild it from reality up. Trustworthy records aren't about being tidy; they're the foundation under every promise you make to a colleague or a customer - the right part ordered, the right person called, the right service delivered, every single time. Keep them true, and you keep faith with everyone who depends on what you decide.
