T1: The Ache

You are standing on the second platform of the C-201 distillation column at Cedar Bay Oleochemicals, a fatty-acid plant on a warm coastal industrial belt. In your hand is a marked-up P&ID - the piping and instrument diagram. The steel rail is warm from the morning sun; there is a faint sweet, waxy smell from the product line. The column runs at 3.5 bar and 240 C. Its design pressure is 6 bar.

Naseema, the plant manager, finds you on the platform. "The board wants this unit signed off as safe before we restart Monday. I need to know it will not blow a flange. Can you tell me it is safe?" Her tone is friendly, but she is not leaving without an answer.

You can feel the pull of the easy move: point at the pressure-control valve and the relief valve on the drawing and say "there are two protections, we are fine." It would close the conversation. But protection is only ever protection against something you have already thought of - and the accidents that happen on plants like this one are almost never the failure everybody was watching. They are the one nobody in the room had ever said out loud.

I can only protect against what I have imagined - so how do I find every way this column can go wrong, including the ways nobody here would ever think of?

Before you read on, try it. Look at what you know: a column at 3.5 bar with a 6 bar design limit, a condenser, a reflux drum, a pressure-control valve, a relief valve. Write down every way you can think of that this section could reach a dangerous state. Take a full two minutes and get as many as you can - your own list, in your own hand.

Now see what the shape of the list reveals

• A list like this usually runs to two or four items, and it nearly always includes the pressure-control valve failing or the reboiler over-firing. That is what the experienced operators on this plant produce too. It is a list built out of real working knowledge.

• Notice what those items have in common. They are the failures we have met before, read about, or been trained to watch for. A list like this is not a survey of what could happen; it is an inventory of what we already know to look out for. It is a search of memory, and memory is the only place any of us can search unaided.

• The problem is not the list. The problem is what is not on it. Nothing warns us about a hazard we have never imagined - there is no ache attached to it, no sense of a gap. That is why a plant can be run carefully for eleven years by good people and still surprise them.

• So the missing piece is not more expertise, and it is not a longer meeting. It is a way to be made to imagine departures we would not have volunteered - something outside memory that walks through the possibilities one at a time and refuses to let us skip the ones that sound silly. That is what the next screen does. The two findings it produces on this column were on nobody's list - not the operators', not the manager's, and not mine.

T2: The Tool in Action

Box one small piece of the plant. Write in one sentence what it is supposed to do. Then walk a fixed set of words across every parameter of that section - No, More, Less, Reverse, As well as, Part of, Other than - and say each combination out loud, including the ones that sound absurd. Watch it run on C-201.

Step 1 - Box a small piece of the plant and write what it is SUPPOSED to do

You put a box around the overhead system of the C-201 column - from the top of the column, through the condenser, to the reflux drum - and write the intent underneath it: 'Vapour leaves the column top at 3.5 bar and 240 C, condenses, and collects in the reflux drum.' One box, one sentence. Not the whole plant; the whole plant is where studies go to die. Naseema reads the sentence and nods - it is the first time anyone has written down what this section is actually for.

Step 2 - Walk the guidewords across every parameter, including the ones that sound silly

Pressure / More. Pressure / Less. Flow / No. Flow / Reverse - somebody snorts, because how would flow reverse in an overhead line? Composition / As well as - what could be in here that should not be? Temperature / More. You go across the whole grid, and the room's mood changes twice: once at Flow / Reverse, when the shift supervisor stops laughing and says 'actually, on a hot shutdown with the drum still full, it could'; and once at Composition / As well as, when a young process engineer says 'water carry-over from the wash - into a 240 C column.'

Step 3 - For each real deviation: what causes it, what it does, what already protects, what is still missing

Pressure / More. Cause: PV-201, the pressure-control valve, fails closed. Consequence - written plainly, not softened - 'pressure climbs toward the 6 bar design limit; possible rupture and flammable release next to the walkway.' Existing safeguards: relief valve PSV-201, high-pressure alarm PAH-201. Action: this one is serious enough that somebody must size the protection properly, and the answer needs a number - raised, owner named, and that is a different piece of work. Then Flow / Reverse and Composition / As well as get their own rows, and neither of them was on anybody's list an hour ago. That is the study's whole worth: the rows nobody would have written from memory.

See what the three steps did, and note that they did not need a cleverer engineer. The two findings that mattered - reverse flow on a hot shutdown, and water carry-over into a 240 C column - came out of the two combinations the room had laughed at. Nobody in that group was careless. No one can search their own blind spot unaided - that is what the method is for, and it is why it works as well for a room of twenty-year veterans as for anyone else. The guidewords are not a checklist of hazards. They are a machine for making a room imagine past the edge of its own experience, one square of a grid at a time - and what you carry off that platform is a list of things that could go wrong, each with an owner, that no amount of care would have produced from memory.

T3: The Blueprint

Three moves, and the second is the one people are tempted to hurry.

  • 1. Box a node and write its intent in one plain sentence - what flows, from where to where, at what conditions. Small sections; the whole plant at once is where studies go to die.
  • 2. Sweep every word across every parameter - No, More, Less, Reverse, As well as, Part of, Other than - and say each combination out loud, especially the ridiculous ones.
  • 3. For each credible deviation, record four things: cause, consequence in unflinching words, safeguards that genuinely exist, and an action with an owner where a gap remains.

This is a HAZOP - a Hazard and Operability study (IEC 61882) - and now that you have run one, its words are just names for what you did. The section you boxed is a node; the sentence you wrote is its design intent. The fixed words are guidewords, and a guideword applied to a parameter gives you a deviation - "More pressure", "Reverse flow" - which is why the method finds what memory cannot: you are not asked what you fear, you are handed each square of the grid in turn and made to answer for it. The rows you fill in are the worksheet, and the actions coming off it are the only part anybody will read next year.

The method fits any process handling pressure, heat, or flammable or toxic material, and it is at its most valuable exactly where a plant has run safely for years - because that is where memory is most confident and least complete. What it cannot do is tell you how much protection is enough: a HAZOP finds the scenario and says "this one is serious", and then stops. Putting a number on how much protection one serious scenario needs is a different question with its own method, and this study's job is to hand it over cleanly.

Two disciplines make the difference between a study that finds things and a meeting that happened. First, say the absurd combinations out loud anyway, and give the room permission to laugh - "reverse flow in an overhead line" is meant to sound silly, and the moment someone stops laughing and says "actually, on a hot shutdown..." is the moment the study earns its whole cost. Skipping the ridiculous squares to save time removes exactly the ones memory was never going to supply. Second, write the consequence in the words you would use at an inquiry. "Possible upset" protects nobody, and it will quietly shrink every decision made downstream of it; "rupture and a flammable release next to the walkway" is harder to write and is the only version that carries the weight it should.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your study true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• "There are some safeguards" is not an answer. Two valves on a drawing is a feeling about the failures you happened to think of. Never let the comforting version close the conversation before the grid has been walked.

• Do not skip the squares that sound stupid. The findings that justify the study almost always come from there. If a combination is genuinely impossible, it takes ten seconds to say so and write nothing - that is cheap. Not asking is what costs.

• Do not soften the consequence. A worksheet that says "possible upset" instead of "rupture, flammable release, potential fatality" will have every decision downstream sized to the softened words.

• Keep the node small. A node you cannot hold in your head produces a sweep nobody can concentrate through. Two tight nodes beat one loose one, every time.

• The output is the action list, not the worksheet. A study with no owners and no dates has found nothing, however thick the document. Ask at the close: who owns each row, and by when?

• Finding it is not sizing it. When a row is serious, resist the urge to settle on the spot how much protection it needs. That question deserves its own arithmetic, and answering it from the room's confidence is how good studies end in bad decisions.

You came onto that platform with a manager who wanted a yes and a drawing that could only offer a shrug - and with the list any of us would have written from memory, which is a list of the failures already known about. You can give Naseema something far better: a boxed node with its intent written down, every parameter swept word by word, and a row for each credible deviation with the consequence named plainly and an owner against it - including reverse flow on a hot shutdown and water carry-over into a 240 C column, neither of which was on anyone's list an hour before. This is how a plant stops relying on the imagination of whoever happens to be in the room, and starts finding the failure that nobody feared. That is what keeps the person on the walkway safe: not the reassurance, but the search that went past where the reassurance stopped. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
