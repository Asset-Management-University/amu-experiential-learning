Step 1 of 5

Is the demand real - or just kind words?

T1: The Ache

You're about to commit something scarce and hard to get back - your savings, weeks of time, a pile of stock, your good name - to a new idea. And everyone is cheering. "Great idea!" "I'd definitely buy that!" Likes, thumbs-up, warm words wherever you turn. It feels like proof. It feels like permission to go all in.

But a cold little fact keeps nagging: not one of those cheering people has actually done anything that cost them a cent. They've been kind - and kindness is free. You can feel the pull to commit on the strength of all that warmth... and a quiet fear that warmth might not be the same as truth.

Everyone says they love my idea - but is the demand REAL, or just kind words? How do I find out for sure before I bet everything on it?

Before you read on, decide. The brothers want to deliver gas to their neighbourhood. Everyone they have told loves it — the aunties, the neighbours, the man at the corner shop. Buying in and getting started will take real money they cannot afford to lose, and they want to know this week. Write down the test you would run, and how you would know from its result whether to go ahead — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the one variable none of them changes

• “Ask more people. Ten is nothing — go and ask a hundred.” The instinct that feels most like rigour, and it buys nothing: an opinion costs the person giving it exactly nothing, so a hundred free yeses is a hundred times zero. Scaling a worthless signal does not make it valuable, it just takes longer.

• “Name the price — ask whether they would pay R260 for a delivery.” Better, because a number makes it harder to be vaguely encouraging. But it is still a question about a future, and agreeing to a future purchase costs nothing today. People are kind, and they are kindest about money they have not yet been asked for.

• “Take names and numbers — build a list of everyone interested.” A faint signal, and a dangerous one, because it feels like progress. A page of names is a page of people who spent thirty seconds being encouraging. A list of interested people is not a list of buyers.

• “Start small — buy a few cylinders, run it for a month, and see.” This is a real test, and it will give a real answer. It is simply the expensive kind: it spends the money first and learns afterwards, which is the exact order the brothers cannot afford.

• “Just go. The response has been overwhelming — momentum matters more than certainty.” The answer all that warmth is quietly pushing toward, and the one that costs the most on the day it turns out to be wrong.

• Look at what separates those five. They differ in how many people are asked, and in how soon — never in what the “yes” costs the person giving it. That is the variable, and it is the whole method. Rank a yes by what it costs the giver: a thumbs-up costs nothing, a sign-up costs a little time, a deposit costs real money, paying in full costs the most — and the more it costs them, the truer it is. Then ask for one high enough up that ladder that only a real buyer would give it. And there is a second half without which it is still a hope rather than a test: decide the bar before you look. “Eight of the ten I ask will hand me R260 cash this week” is a claim that can come back wrong — which is what makes the answer worth having. Decide it afterwards and any result reads as encouraging.

There's a way to tell the difference cheaply, before you commit - by running a small, honest test. Watch how the brothers did it, and how the same test works far beyond gas.

T2: The Tool in Action

The brothers had the same cheering for the gas idea - everyone said it sounded wonderful. Instead of trusting the warm words, they designed a small test that asked for a costly signal - something only a real buyer would give. Here is the experiment they ran:

1. My claim (one that could be proven WRONG)

At least 8 of the 10 neighbours I ask will pre-pay R260 for a gas delivery this Saturday.

2. The test + the COSTLY signal I'll ask for

Go door to door this week and ask each neighbour for R260 CASH up front for Saturday - not a 'yes', not a thumbs-up, but real money in hand (the top of the ladder).

3. The 'yes' bar - decided BEFORE I look

If 8 or more of the 10 actually pay, it's a GO. 7 or fewer = NO (or rethink the offer). Decided now, before a single knock.

4. Result + decision (what people DID)

(After) Count who actually paid - not who smiled. Met the bar? Then build. Below it? A clear, cheap NO that just saved you from betting big on kind words.

The heart of it is the ladder of costly signals. Some "yeses" cost the giver nothing, and some cost a lot - and the more it costs them, the truer it is:

  • A like, a thumbs-up - costs nothing - worthless as proof
  • "I'd definitely use that!" - costs nothing - still worthless
  • An email or a sign-up - costs a little time - a faint signal
  • A deposit - costs real money - a strong signal
  • Full pre-payment - costs the most - the truth

The brothers climbed straight to the top: cash, up front. And it travels far beyond a bakkie. Picture wanting to build a new report for your work team, and everyone says "we desperately need this!" The costly signal isn't money - it's time: "To build it right, I need one of your people for a four-hour workshop next Tuesday." Decide the bar first: if a manager actually frees someone up by Friday, the need was real - GO. If no one does, the "desperate need" was just kind words. Same method; the currency of the costly signal simply changes.

T3: The Blueprint

A good demand test has four parts. Together they separate real demand (people give up something real) from free approval (kind words that cost nothing).

  • 1. A claim that could be proven WRONG. "My idea is good" can never fail - so it teaches you nothing. "At least 8 of 10 neighbours will pre-pay R260" can fail - so it can teach you the truth. Always start with a claim a test could break.
  • 2. Ask for a costly signal - climb the ladder. Don't ask "would you buy?" - ask them to actually buy, or put down a deposit, or commit real time. Reach for the most costly signal you reasonably can; that's where the truth lives.
  • 3. Decide the 'yes' bar BEFORE you look. Set the cold, clear pass line now - "8 of 10 = go" - before you run it. This is your shield against your own hope, which is a terrible bookkeeper and will always try to mark a fail as a pass.
  • 4. Run it small, and read what people DID. Keep it cheap enough that being wrong barely stings. Then ignore the smiles and count the actions - hands reaching for wallets, not thumbs going up.

That's the whole craft: approval is free; demand is costly. Design a small test that makes the difference visible, and decide what it means before you run it.

T5: The Guardrails

This is a tool for listening to reality, never for pressuring people. Hold these five guardrails - clarity over coercion, always.

The five guardrails of an honest test

• Clarity, not coercion. You're not tricking anyone into a costly signal. You're being honest that a real thing needs a real commitment, and inviting them to be part of it. Never high-pressure or guilt someone into a yes.

• Make "no" easy and safe. Your test must give people a comfortable way to decline. If they'd feel awkward saying no, your test is badly designed - and the yeses you get won't be honest.

• A "no" is a gift. A clear no saves you money, time and heartache - truth bought cheaply. Thank them for their honesty; never argue or try to convince them. Their action already gave you the answer.

• Honour the deal. If people give the costly signal - a deposit, a pre-order - you must be ready to deliver your side. This is an honest test of demand, never a way to take money and vanish.

• Don't move the bar. Once you've set the pass line, hold it. Hope will whisper "close enough" - don't listen. A test you can't fail isn't a test.

All that cheering was never a lie - people are kind, and kindness is lovely. It just isn't proof. The cure isn't to distrust people; it's to ask reality a clear question and let actions answer. Design a small, honest test, ask for a signal that costs something real, decide what it means before you look - and you'll know the truth while being wrong is still cheap. That's how you protect not just your money, but your hope and your good name, for every idea you'll ever have. (Working with an AMU Facilitator?
