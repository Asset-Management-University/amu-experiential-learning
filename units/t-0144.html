The number that sounded good

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and the board asks you a simple question: "how reliable are our pumps?" You have a CMMS full of work orders and, when it comes to it, no actual answer. So you reach for the number everyone reaches for and tell them the main pumps run about 1,400 hours between failures. It sounds excellent. Heads nod.

And it does not match reality at all. The plant manager keeps raising production losses from pump downtime - real interruptions, real lost water - and you cannot explain why a pump you just called reliable is causing so much pain. You have a number that sounds good and a plant that feels bad, and no way to reconcile the two.

The problem is that the number you quoted is the flattering one. "Time between failures" says nothing about how long each failure keeps the pump down - and that, it turns out, is exactly what has been hurting you. You have data, and no metrics; and the one metric you reached for is the one that hides the thing that matters.

I quoted a reliability number that sounds good while the plant keeps losing production - so the number must be hiding something. What are the metrics that actually tell me, and the board, how reliable an asset really is?

Before you read on, decide. You told the board the pump runs about 1,440 hours between failures — six failures in a year, and that is true. The operations manager then asked why, if it is that reliable, the plant keeps losing production. And she is right: those six failures cost you 120 hours of downtime between them. The number you quoted has nothing to say about that, and you picked it because it was the one you had and it happened to sound good. Write down what actually tells you, and the board, how reliable this pump is — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what one number cannot hold

• “Quote the failure count instead — six a year is clearer.” Same information in plainer words, and the same silence about the thing that is hurting you. Six short stops and six long ones look identical here.

• “Report the cost of the lost production.” Gets the board's attention, and it tells them how much it hurt without telling anybody what to fix. Cost is a consequence, not a diagnosis.

• “Compare it against last year.” A trend in a number that hides the mechanism is a trend that hides the mechanism. If repairs got slower and failures got rarer, this comparison would look like an improvement.

• “The number is honest — the manager has misunderstood it.” The number is honest and she has understood it perfectly. She asked how reliable the pump is; you answered a narrower question and let it stand for hers.

• “Get better data before quoting anything.” A real prerequisite rather than an answer, and worth holding on to: these are all computed from work orders, so a duplicated record inflates one and a mislabelled service deflates another. A metric on dirty data is a confident wrong answer.

• The trouble is structural rather than dishonest. How often something fails says nothing about how long it is out of action each time, and when the pain lives in the second, the first looks excellent throughout — a machine that fails rarely and takes days to recover can hurt far more than one that fails weekly and is back in minutes. No single number holds both, because two independent facts are involved. So reliability takes three numbers read together: how often it fails, how long it is down each time, and what those two produce — the proportion of time it is actually available, which is the question the operations manager was really asking. Two disciplines keep them honest. Define what counts as a failure before you count anything — a planned service is not one, a trip reset in two minutes might be — and apply that consistently, because the answer moves materially with the choice. And do the arithmetic on the time the pump was actually running, not on the calendar: it was not operating while it was down.

There are three headline numbers, not one, and together they fit on a single page. Here they are, worked for the pump.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the one-page reliability summary that turns a CMMS full of work orders into an answer the board can act on.

1. Is my data clean, and are my failures defined? (get the work history right)

From the pump's work history over one year (8,760 hours): count the FAILURES (events where it stopped doing its job) - 6 - and sum the DOWNTIME across those events - 120 hours. Define 'failure' before you count: a planned service is not a failure; a trip that was reset in two minutes may or may not be, but decide and be consistent. Dirty data poisons everything after this - a duplicated work order inflates the failure count, a mislabelled service deflates it. Clean first, then count.

2. How often does it fail? (MTBF)

MTBF - Mean Time Between Failures, for a repairable item like this pump. Operating time = 8,760 - 120 downtime = 8,640 hours. MTBF = 8,640 / 6 = 1,440 hours. So on average the pump runs 1,440 hours between failures. (For a non-repairable item you would speak of MTTF, mean time to failure - same idea, but you replace it rather than repair it.) Note: it is operating time divided by failures, not calendar time - the pump was not running while it was down.

3. How long is it down each time? (MTTR)

MTTR - Mean Time To Repair. Total downtime 120 hours across 6 repairs: MTTR = 120 / 6 = 20 hours. This is the number MTBF says nothing about, and it is often where the real pain lives. A pump that fails rarely but takes days to fix can hurt the plant far more than one that fails often and is back in an hour. MTBF tells you how often; only MTTR tells you how long.

4. What fraction of time is it actually available? (availability - the honest number)

Operational availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) = 1,440 / (1,440 + 20) = 98.63% (same as uptime/total, 8,640/8,760). Now the lesson: another pump fails the same 6 times a year but each repair takes 80 hours, not 20 - MTBF barely changes (1,380 h, 'about the same'), but downtime is 480 hours and availability drops to 94.5%. Same failure frequency, FOUR TIMES the lost time. MTBF looked identical; availability told the truth, because it combines how often it fails with how long it stays down. Availability is what the plant actually feels.

5. What does it cost, and which lever do I pull? (the management summary)

The one-page summary the board can act on: MTBF 1,440 h, MTTR 20 h, availability 98.6% - and the impact: 120 hours of lost pumping this year, and at the value of the water and power that is, say, R X of lost output. Then the diagnosis, which decides what to fix: if MTBF is low, the problem is FREQUENCY - improve reliability (design, condition monitoring). If MTTR is high, the problem is REPAIR TIME - improve maintainability (spares, access, planning). For our second pump, the fix is repair time, not the pump. Numbers without this last step are trivia; with it, they are a decision.

Box 4 is where the ache resolves. The pump Onalenna called reliable and the one causing pain can have almost the same MTBF - the number she quoted - and completely different availability, because one takes far longer to fix. MTBF flattered; availability told the truth. And box 5 turns the truth into a decision. Let us name the three numbers.

T3: The Blueprint

Reliability has three headline numbers, and the whole skill is computing them honestly from a work history and reading them together. Five questions:

  • Is my data clean, and are my failures defined? Every number below is only as good as the work history behind it. Count the failures, sum the downtime, and define what counts as a failure before you start - a duplicated work order inflates the count, a service mislabelled as a breakdown poisons the lot. Clean first, then count.
  • How often does it fail? MTBF - Mean Time Between Failures, for a repairable item - is total operating time divided by the number of failures. Operating time, not calendar time: the pump was not running while it was down. (A non-repairable item uses MTTF, mean time to failure - the same idea, but you replace rather than repair it.)
  • How long is it down each time? MTTR - Mean Time To Repair - is total downtime divided by the number of repairs. This is the number MTBF is silent about, and it is often where the pain actually lives. A rare failure that strands the plant for days can hurt far more than a frequent one fixed in an hour.
  • What fraction of time is it actually available? Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR), which is the same as uptime over total time. This is the honest number, because it combines how often it fails with how long it stays down - and it is the only one the business actually feels, as output it did or did not get. Teach the operational availability, from the real work history, because that includes the waiting and logistics time the plant really loses. This is where MTBF alone is exposed: two pumps with the same MTBF can sit at 98.6% and 94.5% availability because one takes four times as long to fix.
  • What does it cost, and which lever do I pull? The management summary: the three numbers, the business impact in rand of the downtime, and the diagnosis. If MTBF is low, the problem is frequency - improve reliability. If MTTR is high, the problem is repair time - improve maintainability: spares, access, planning. The metric points at the lever.

One honest boundary. You compute and interpret the three headline metrics from a work history. You are not doing advanced reliability modelling - Weibull life-data fitting and reliability block diagrams are the next level, a different tool. Your numbers are only as good as the cleaned data they came from. And a metric with no business impact and no recommendation is a number nobody acts on - the summary, not the calculation, is the deliverable.

Does it scale? A car with an impressive "miles between breakdowns" - a high MTBF - is useless if each breakdown takes a week to fix - a high MTTR; availability is the number that tells you whether you can actually depend on it, and it is the one MTBF alone will never show you.

T4: Your Turn

A backwash blower ran for a year (8,760 hours). Its work history shows 8 failures and 200 hours of total downtime.

  1. MTBF. Compute it from the operating time. (Operating time = 8,760 - 200.)
  2. MTTR. Compute it.
  3. Availability. Compute it, and state it as a percentage.
  4. The comparison. A second blower has the same MTBF but double the MTTR. Which blower would you rather rely on, and is its weaker sibling's problem one of failure frequency or repair time?

The test of a real answer: in step 4, notice that the two blowers have the same MTBF. If you picked between them on MTBF alone you could not tell them apart - it is availability, driven by MTTR, that separates them. If your reasoning did not reach for MTTR, re-read box 4.

T5: The Guardrails

Reliability metrics are simple to compute and easy to misuse, almost always by quoting one number and hiding the rest.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Quoting MTBF alone. The flattering-number trap, and the one that started the ache. MTBF says how often, never how long. Report it beside MTTR and availability or it misleads by omission.

• Confusing MTBF with MTTF. MTBF is for repairable items (time between failures); MTTF is for those you replace (time to failure). Using the wrong one signals you have not thought about what happens after the failure.

• Computing on dirty data. A duplicated work order inflates the failure count; a service logged as a breakdown ruins the downtime. Clean and define your failures first - every metric inherits the quality of the data beneath it.

• Using calendar time for MTBF. The asset was not operating while it was down. MTBF divides operating time by failures, or the number is quietly wrong.

• Forgetting that availability is what the business feels. Nobody in operations experiences MTBF; they experience the hours the asset was not there when they needed it. That is availability, and it is the number to lead with.

• A summary with no impact and no diagnosis. Three numbers on a page are trivia until you say what the downtime cost and whether the fix is reliability or maintainability. The recommendation is the point.

• Reading too much into too few events. Two failures do not make a trend. With small numbers, say so - a metric from thin data is an estimate, not a verdict.

Onalenna quoted a good-sounding number to a board while her plant bled production, because she reached for the metric that flatters. Now she can do better: clean the work history, compute how often it fails and how long it stays down, combine them into the availability the business actually feels, and put it on one page with what it costs and what to fix. Three numbers, honestly read, are the difference between sounding reliable and being able to prove whether you are.
