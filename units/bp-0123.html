How can I send my people to do hazardous work when the maps we give them - the drawings and records - can't be trusted?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Nkosana, an engineering supervisor at a processing plant. It is a planned shutdown, and your team needs to replace a seal on an acid circulation pump - critical work, but routine. You have the permit, the P&ID drawing from the controlled server, and a good team. The drawing is clear: close and lock the suction valve and the discharge valve. Your team does exactly that - they follow the procedure, tag out the valves, and test for zero energy. Everything looks right.

One of your fitters, Mpho, carefully cracks the flange on the discharge pipe. Instead of a few drips, a thin, hissing spray of residual acid shoots out, eating the paint off the concrete plinth. Everyone jumps back. Mpho is unharmed - only because he was in full PPE - but his face is pale. You find the cause a minute later: a small, un-tagged bypass line, barely thicker than your thumb, added years ago for sampling. It taps in after the main valve. It was never put on the P&ID. The official drawing, the one from the controlled server, was a lie - and that lie nearly cost Mpho his eyesight.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

First you think, "We must redraw everything." You get a quote to laser-scan the whole plant and produce perfect as-built drawings: R15 million and a two-year timeline. You know that by the time it is finished a hundred new small changes will have been made, and the new drawings will already be out of date. Too big, too slow, too expensive. Next you try to enforce discipline - "everyone must be more careful and double-check their work". But your team looks back at you with tired eyes: they were careful. They followed the official procedure and the official drawing. The system failed them, and blaming them for a faulty map breaks trust. Finally you hunt for the 'real' master drawings. The drawing office has a digital set; the senior planner swears his laptop PDFs are more current; an old artisan has a dusty paper roll covered in handwritten notes. You compare all three for that one pump - and they all differ. There is no single source of truth. You are only choosing which lie to believe.

You realise the problem is not the people, and not finding one magic drawing. So before you read on: what would a system need so the map is constantly corrected by the territory - and trustworthy at the moment it matters most?

So the question is not the people or one magic drawing - it is: how do I build a system where the MAP is constantly corrected by the TERRITORY, so it can be trusted when it matters most?
A drawing is a CLAIM about reality, not reality - and an unverified claim is just a guess wearing the authority of an official document. You do not need perfect drawings to be safe; you need a safe process for dealing with imperfect ones. Five moves: (1) SEE IT AS A CLAIM, THEN VERIFY - before any hazardous work (isolating, digging, cutting) physically walk the line and compare drawing to reality; trust your eyes on the asset, not the paper. (2) CLOSE THE LOOP - no modification is 'complete' until the drawing is updated; make it a mandatory sign-off item. (3) CAPTURE AS-BUILT NOW - teams carry red pens and mark every difference on a printed copy at the point of work, submitted with the job. (4) ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH - declare one controlled master, retire the rival copies, feed the red-lines back to it. (5) CONCENTRATE ON THE DANGEROUS FEW first - the safety-critical and high-use drawings, walked down and verified before the rest. The root is that CHANGE outpaces DOCUMENTATION; the fix is to close the loop between the territory and the map.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop seeing the drawing as reality. A drawing is a CLAIM about reality - and an unverified claim is just a guess, however official it looks. This changes everything: you do not need perfect drawings to be safe, you need a safe process for dealing with imperfect ones. Five moves:

  • See it as a claim, then verify: the breakthrough is to stop seeing the drawing as reality. A drawing is a CLAIM about reality, and an unverified claim is just a guess - however official it looks, however current the server says it is. So you do not need perfect drawings to be safe; you need a safe process for imperfect ones. Before any hazardous work - isolating, digging, cutting - the drawing is a guide, not gospel: the first step of the job is to physically walk the line and compare the paper to the real world. At the moment of danger, trust your eyes on the asset, not the map in your hand. This single shift - from 'the drawing is true' to 'the drawing is a claim I must check' - is what turns a near-miss into a discrepancy found safely, before the flange is ever cracked.
  • Close the loop - no change is done until the drawing is: the gap does not open by accident; it opens because CHANGE outpaces DOCUMENTATION. Every modification made on the plant but never drawn back widens it - a bypass added 'just for sampling', a valve relocated, a line re-routed. So close the loop: no modification project is COMPLETE until the engineering drawing has been updated to match. Make it a final, mandatory item on the project sign-off sheet - the job is not finished, and not paid out as finished, until the drawing tells the truth again. Stop the gap growing first; only then can you shrink it.
  • Capture as-built now, not 'someday': drawings rot because the truth lives in people's heads and gets written down 'later' - which never comes. So capture it AT THE POINT OF WORK: teams carry red pens, and any difference they find while doing a job is marked directly onto a printed copy of the drawing on site. That red-line drawing is submitted with the work order - a small, cheap habit that captures reality at the one moment someone is actually looking at it. The person with their hands on the asset is your best surveyor; give them the pen and a way to hand the markup back.
  • One source of truth: if there are five copies, there is no truth - only a choice of which lie to believe (the server set, the planner's laptop PDFs, the artisan's marked-up paper roll, all disagreeing). So declare ONE controlled master - say the digital server - as the single source, and retire or clearly stamp every rival copy as uncontrolled. The red-line markups from the field are scanned and attached to the work order, giving the drawing office one clear instruction to update the one master. Now 'the latest drawing' has a single, unambiguous meaning, and the corrections flow to one place instead of scattering.
  • Concentrate on the dangerous few first: you cannot fix twenty years of drift overnight, and you do not have to. Not every drawing can hurt someone. So start with the few that can: the high-pressure and high-energy systems, the acid and chemical lines, the main electrical feeds - the safety-critical and high-use drawings. Walk those down and verify them first, on a deliberate programme, before touching the rest. This is the same focusing discipline: your limited effort lands where a wrong drawing would do the most harm, so the most dangerous maps become trustworthy first.

See it transform Nkosana's work. Before a job, his team now walks the pipework with the P&ID in hand and a red pen, finding the discrepancies before a single flange is unbolted - the un-drawn bypass would have been caught at the rail, not at the spray. Every modification project now carries one last sign-off line: the drawing is updated, or the job is not done. The red-lined drawings flow back to the drawing office, the one master on the server grows more accurate every week, and the dusty rival copies are retired. The near-misses stop, and the stress of uncertainty is replaced by the confidence of a process people can trust. A drawing is an unverified claim about reality, not reality itself - so stop trusting the claim and start verifying it against the physical asset before you act. The gap opens because change outpaces documentation, so close the loop: every change in the plant must reach the drawing before the job is called done; capture as-built in red pen at the point of work; keep one controlled master, not five conflicting copies; and fix the dangerous few drawings first. At the moment of danger, trust your eyes on the territory, not the map on the paper.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This is not only about plant drawings - any map you navigate by works the same way. Think of using a map app to drive to a new address: the map is a drawing, a CLAIM about the roads.

See the same moves on a map app

The app says "turn right in 50 metres" - but as you reach the junction you see a brand-new, permanent "No Right Turn" sign. The road has changed; the map has not.

• See it as a claim, then verify: the app CLAIMED you could turn; your eyes on the territory (the sign) showed the claim was false - so you do not turn.
• Close the loop / capture as-built: you update your own memory of that junction, and if you are diligent you tap "report an error" so the master map can be corrected for everyone.
• One source of truth: you trust your direct observation over the data on the screen.
• Focus on the dangerous: you pay closest attention at the junctions, not the long straight stretches.

You did not crash because you trusted your eyes on the real world over the out-of-date drawing on your screen - exactly what this problem asks of you.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The drawings in your world - the P&IDs, the electrical single-lines, the buried-services plans - are the maps your people use to navigate dangerous territory. Are you sending them out with a faulty GPS? Four actions you can take this week:

  1. Find one. Pick one drawing for a safety-critical system that is scheduled for work soon.
  2. Walk it down. Take that drawing and physically walk the system, red-penning every place it does not match what you see.
  3. Add one step. On the next hazardous permit you issue, add a line: "physical walk-down completed to verify the drawing before isolation."
  4. Ask one question. At your next team meeting ask: "when we finish a modification, what is our process for getting the master drawing updated - and who is responsible?"

It is about professional stewardship: a small, powerful step to make work fundamentally safer for the people who rely on you, by giving them a map they can begin to trust again. People-safety comes before throughput, always - and a drawing you have not verified is just a guess wearing the authority of an official document.
