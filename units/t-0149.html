Everyone chasing their own theory

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and a critical pump has eaten its bearings for the third time this quarter. Your team is investigating - and the investigation is chaos.

One engineer is certain it is the lubricant, and has spent two days on oil analysis. Another blames the operators. A third quietly suspects misalignment, but checking alignment means stopping the machine and setting up laser gear, so it is awkward, so nobody has done it. Each person is chasing a favourite theory, or the easiest thing to test, and there is no shared list of what could actually be causing this and no agreement on what to look at first.

You watch them and see the real problem: they are busy, and they are not converging. They are investigating hard, in no particular order, and the way this is going, the "cause" will be whoever's theory is checked last standing - not the thing that is actually breaking the bearings. And the failure will come back a fourth time.

My team is investigating a failure with energy and no order - each chasing a pet theory or the easy test, with no shared list of possible causes and no agreement on priority. How do I turn this into a structured, prioritised hunt for the real cause?

Before you read on, decide. Third set of bearings. Four capable people, four theories: contaminated oil, a bad batch, vibration coming down the line from the compressor, somebody's fitting. The oil sample went off this morning because it was easy; the bearing batch is being traced because that is a phone call. Checking the alignment properly would mean stopping the machine and getting laser gear on it, so that one has been left for later — again. There is no list anywhere of what might be causing this. The way this is going, the “cause” will be whichever theory is last standing. Write down how you would turn this into an ordered hunt — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what must be kept out of the ranking

• “Let everyone finish their checks and compare notes.” The current plan, with a meeting at the end. Whatever the easy tests eliminate will look like progress, and the hard one still will not have been done.

• “Put the most experienced engineer in charge of deciding.” Replaces four hunches with one, more confidently held. It also buries the disagreement rather than resolving it, so nobody can say afterwards what was ruled out or why.

• “Do the cheap tests first, then work up.” The most reasonable-sounding answer here, and it is precisely the failure — it is what is already happening, dignified as a strategy. Cheapness has no relationship to likelihood.

• “Replace everything — bearings, oil, do the alignment, all of it.” Might stop the bleeding, and you will never know which one it was, so you cannot prevent it anywhere else and cannot tell whether it will return.

• “Fit monitoring and wait for the next one.” Buys data at the cost of a fourth failure, on a critical machine, when you already have three failures' worth of evidence nobody has organised.

• Two quiet failures are running here. First — and this is the commonest way such an investigation dies — the real cause may never be written down at all, and what was never a candidate can never be eliminated. So build the candidate list before anyone investigates anything, completely and together, because a list assembled after people are invested in their theories is a negotiation rather than an enumeration. Second, score each candidate on how likely it is and how much it would contribute, which turns four competing hunches into a shared basis people can disagree with openly. And now the discipline that decides this case: record how hard each is to verify, and keep that out of the ranking. The moment ease enters the priority, convenience takes over again — and the awkward candidate is very often the likely one, precisely because hard-to-check things involve stopping or dismantling something. Your alignment check scores high on both likelihood and contribution. It is also the difficult one. It goes first.

There is a worksheet for exactly this - the FMEA you know, turned around and pointed at a failure that has already happened. Here it is, for the bearings.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the problem-solving FMEA worksheet - every candidate cause, scored and ordered, with the evidence that settles each.

1. What could have caused THIS failure - all of it? (the candidate causal factors)

The critical pump has eaten its bearings for the third time. Before anyone investigates anything, list every candidate causal factor - completely, not one pet theory: MISALIGNMENT of shaft to driver; LUBRICANT CONTAMINATION; UPSTREAM VIBRATION from another machine; a BAD BEARING BATCH; OPERATOR or FITTING error; over-loading. Six factors, not one. The single most common way an RCA fails is that the real cause was never even written down - so the list must be complete before the arguing starts.

2. How likely, how serious, and how verifiable is each? (the three scores)

Score the LIKELIHOOD and CONTRIBUTION of each factor (1-5), and assess its VERIFIABILITY (e.g. easy, hard). This repurposes the FMEA's occurrence/severity/detection logic for a reactive investigation. Misalignment: likelihood 5, contribution 5, verify HARD (needs the machine stopped and laser alignment). Lubricant contamination: likelihood 3, contribution 4, verify EASY (an oil sample). Upstream vibration: likelihood 3, contribution 3, verify medium. Bad bearing batch: likelihood 2, contribution 4, verify easy. Operator/fitting error: likelihood 2, contribution 3, verify medium. The scores are a shared, explicit basis - not one engineer's hunch versus another's.

3. In what order should I investigate? (prioritise by likelihood x contribution)

Priority = likelihood x contribution (how probable AND how much it matters). Misalignment 5x5 = 25; lubricant 3x4 = 12; upstream vibration 3x3 = 9; bad batch 2x4 = 8; operator 2x3 = 6. So the order is MISALIGNMENT FIRST - even though it is the hard one to check. And there is the team's mistake laid bare: two days were spent on lubricant (priority 12, easy to test) while misalignment (priority 25, awkward) was ignored precisely because it was awkward. Verifiability tells you HOW to check a factor, never whether to - you investigate the likely and serious ones first, hard or not.

4. What is the worksheet, and what evidence settles each? (the FMEA worksheet)

The problem-solving FMEA worksheet, one row per factor: the factor, its likelihood/contribution/verifiability scores, its priority, and - the column that turns it into an investigation - the VERIFICATION ACTION. For misalignment: 'stop the pump, take laser-alignment readings; out-of-tolerance confirms, in-tolerance eliminates'. For lubricant: 'oil sample and particle count'. Every factor now has a defined piece of evidence that would confirm or kill it, and the team works down the list in priority order instead of in argument order.

5. What does the evidence actually prove? (investigate, verify, update)

The FMEA prioritised the hunt; only EVIDENCE ends it. Investigate misalignment first: the laser readings come back well out of tolerance - confirmed as a major contributor, and now the recurring failure makes sense. Update the worksheet: misalignment confirmed; test the next factors to see whether lubricant contamination was a secondary contributor or a red herring, and eliminate the rest. A high score bought misalignment the first look, not a conviction - the cause is what the evidence proves, not what topped the ranking. That discipline is what separates a structured investigation from a confident guess.

Look at box 3, because it is where the team's mistake becomes visible. They spent two days on the lubricant - priority 12, easy to test - while misalignment - priority 25, awkward - sat untouched precisely because it was awkward. The worksheet flips that: it says investigate the likely and serious factor first, hard or not. The tool did not solve the failure; it pointed the team at the right lead. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

You already know the FMEA as a preventive tool - listing what could fail before it does. A problem-solving FMEA takes that same worksheet and turns it reactive: after a failure, it structures the hunt for the cause. Five questions:

  • What could have caused this failure - all of it? List every candidate causal factor, completely, before anyone investigates anything. Not one favourite theory - all of them. The most common way an RCA fails is that the real cause was never written down, so it could never be found.
  • How likely, how serious, and how verifiable is each? Score each factor on three axes, borrowing the FMEA's structure: likelihood it is implicated (the occurrence idea), contribution to the failure (severity), and how easily it can be verified (detection). Now the arguing has a shared, explicit basis instead of one engineer's hunch against another's.
  • In what order should I investigate? Rank by likelihood x contribution - how probable and how much it matters. And here is the discipline that fixes the ache: verifiability tells you how to check a factor, never whether to. Misalignment scored highest, so it is investigated first even though it is the hard one - you do not skip the most probable cause because it is inconvenient to test.
  • What is the worksheet, and what evidence settles each? The worksheet is one row per factor: the scores, the priority, and - the column that makes it an investigation - the verification action, the specific evidence that would confirm or eliminate it. Now the team works down the list in priority order, not in argument order.
  • What does the evidence actually prove? The FMEA guides the hunt; only evidence ends it. Verify the top factor, confirm or eliminate it, and update the worksheet as you go. A high score bought a factor the first look - it did not convict it. The cause is what the evidence proves, not what topped the ranking.

One honest boundary. You are using the FMEA worksheet reactively, to list and prioritise candidate causes for a failure that has already happened. This is the opposite direction to the proactive design FMECA - same tool, pointed at a real failure rather than a possible one. And the worksheet prioritises the investigation; it does not conclude it. The number tells you where to look first; the evidence tells you what is true.

Does it scale? It is a detective's suspect board: you do not arrest the first face you see or interrogate the whole town - you list the suspects, rank them by motive and opportunity, and investigate the strongest lead first, but you still need evidence to convict.

T4: Your Turn

A control valve on a dosing line keeps sticking - three times in a month. The team is about to investigate.

  1. List. Write three or four candidate causal factors for a sticking control valve.
  2. Score one. Take one factor and score it on the three axes - likelihood, contribution, verifiability.
  3. Prioritise. Say which of your factors you would investigate first, and why - by likelihood and contribution, not by which is easiest to test.
  4. Verify. Name the specific evidence that would confirm or eliminate your first factor.

The test of a real answer: in step 3, if the factor you chose to investigate first is also the easiest one to check, pause - is it genuinely the most likely and serious, or did "easy" quietly win again? The whole point is to investigate the probable factor first even when it is the awkward one.

T5: The Guardrails

Structured investigation fails when the structure is skipped, in a few very human ways.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Fixating on the first plausible cause. Confirmation bias, and the classic RCA failure - seizing "it was the operator" and closing the case. The worksheet exists to force a complete list before anyone commits.

• Investigating the easy factors before the likely ones. Effort spent where it is comfortable, not where the cause probably is. Verifiability plans the method; likelihood and contribution set the order.

• An incomplete candidate list. If the real cause was never on the sheet, no amount of investigation will find it. Completeness first, priority second.

• Treating the top score as the proven cause. A high score is a lead, not a verdict. Nothing is the cause until evidence confirms it, and the highest-scored factor is sometimes eliminated on the first test.

• Scoring by gut with no shared basis. Numbers pulled from the air are just opinion in a table. Agree the basis for the scores, so the priority is defensible rather than personal.

• Confusing this with a design FMECA. The proactive version asks what could fail; this reactive one asks what caused a specific failure. Same worksheet, opposite direction - do not run one when you need the other.

• Never updating the worksheet. An investigation is live: factors get confirmed or killed, and priorities shift. A worksheet frozen at the start becomes a document instead of a tool.

Onalenna's team was investigating hard and getting nowhere, each chasing a theory or an easy test, with the most likely cause ignored because it was awkward. Now she can order the hunt: list every candidate cause, score each on likelihood, contribution and verifiability, investigate in priority order regardless of what is easy, and let evidence - not the ranking - name the cause. A problem-solving FMEA turns a chaotic, biased investigation into a structured, prioritised one; and a structured investigation is the one that actually stops the failure coming back.
