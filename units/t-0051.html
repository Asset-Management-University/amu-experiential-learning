T1: The Ache

It is late February, and you are at the small steel desk in the back of the Tank You filling yard. Ibraaheem is across from you. Between you is a single sheet with one number in blue pen: R180,000 - what you spent on maintenance last year, near enough, and the number Ibraaheem has just written for next year too, because, as he says with a shrug, "It was fine last year."

But last year is not this year. You have three delivery vehicles now, a filling pump, a compressor, two storage tanks and around forty cylinders in rotation; last year you had two vehicles and no filling plant of your own. So one number copied forward does not sit right. And you remember, with a small knot in your stomach, the week in October when the filling pump seized: you had nothing set aside, paid R22,000 in a panic on the card machine you use for customers, and did not sleep properly for three nights wondering if the cheque to the gas supplier would clear.

The bank wants a budget too. You are applying for a small expansion loan, and the loan officer - a careful woman named Palesa - asked you on the phone, politely but firmly, "And how did you arrive at your maintenance figure?" You said "experience." There was a pause on the line you did not enjoy. The temptation is to just write R200,000, round up from last year, add a bit for "the new plant", and go deliver gas. Thirty seconds.

Before you read on, decide. Palesa asked how you arrived at your maintenance figure, you said “experience”, and the pause on the line told you what she thought of it. Last October a seized filling pump cost R22,000 you had not saved for, on the card machine, and three nights’ sleep. Write down how you would build a figure you could defend. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one could survive being asked about

• “Take last year’s total, round up, add a bit for the new plant.” Thirty seconds, and it cannot answer the only question Palesa actually asked, which was how. It also carries last year forward whole — including whatever was in it that you are specifically trying never to repeat.

• “Use a percentage of asset value — that’s the industry rule.” It sounds authoritative and it is somebody else’s average. It cannot see that your plant is new, or that your vehicles do township distances rather than long haul, and when she asks which of your assets drives the number, the rule has nothing to say.

• “Add a contingency for the next surprise.” Sensible instinct, wrong shape. A pot for surprises is not a plan for the pump — and the October pump was not really a surprise; it was a known asset doing what known assets do, with nothing set aside because nobody had listed it.

• “Ask another operator what they spend.” Useful as a sense check afterwards and useless as a source. Their fleet, their distances, their plant — and you would be taking a number you cannot explain into a room where you will be asked to explain it.

• “Budget high to be safe.” It feels prudent and it is expensive twice: you would be asking the bank for money you do not need, paying interest on the padding, and you still could not say which line was the padding.

Every one of those starts with a total and works backwards. Hold on to the shape of the question you were actually asked: not what is the number, but where does it come from.

You need a way to build the budget up from the actual assets and the actual work they need - a number you can defend line by line to Palesa - and a way to check, every month, whether you are on track or drifting. Here is that method, worked on Tank You.

T2: The Tool in Action

There are two ways to budget. The top-down way is what Ibraaheem did: take last year's total, adjust a little, write one number - fast, but blind; it cannot see the new plant and cannot say why. The bottom-up way builds the total from the ground up: list every asset, list the work each needs, cost it, add it up - and the total falls out at the end. (Opex is just operating expenditure - the running cost of keeping what you own healthy - as opposed to capex, buying it in the first place.) Watch the four steps build Tank You's number.

Step 1 - List the assets and their work

3 delivery vehicles (service 3x/yr, tyres 1x/yr, brakes 2x/yr), the filling pump (service 2x/yr, seal 1x/yr), the compressor (service 2x/yr), 2 storage tanks (safety inspection 1x/yr each), and 40 cylinders (about 8 fall due for requalification a year). The whole filling plant did not exist in last year's single number - listing it is how it stops being invisible.

Step 2 - Cost it bottom-up

Vehicle service R1,800 x 3 x 3 = R16,200; tyres R6,000 x 1 x 3 = R18,000; brakes R2,500 x 2 x 3 = R15,000; pump service R6,000 + seal R4,500 = R10,500; compressor R4,000; tanks R7,000; cylinders R450 x 8 = R3,600. PLANNED-WORK TOTAL = R74,300.

Step 3 - Add an honest contingency, then total

Contingency (20%, because the plant is new): 0.20 x R74,300 = R14,860. TOTAL = R74,300 + R14,860 = R89,160, rounded to R89,200; spread is about R7,433 a month. Ibraaheem's guess was R200,000 - more than double the real need - so bottom-up did not just defend a number, it found a better one.

Step 4 - Track the variance each month

March: actual R11,600 (two vehicle services + a set of tyres bought early when one blew + a compressor service) minus planned R7,433 = +R4,167. Why? Mostly TIMING - the tyres were due later in the year, brought forward. Decision: accept it, and note the later tyre budget is now R6,000 lighter. No panic, no re-plan - just eyes open.

Look at the two numbers side by side: Ibraaheem's guess, R200,000; your bottom-up budget, R89,200. The guess was more than double what the assets actually need - you were about to ask the bank for a loan padded with R110,000 of fog. And the puzzle that more assets cost less than last year resolves once you see it honestly: last year's R180,000 was swollen by exactly the panic you are trying to escape - the R22,000 seized-pump repair and the one-off costs of standing the new plant up. Planned maintenance is cheaper than firefighting. Bottom-up did not just defend a number; it found a better, truer one - and every rand of it traces to a line Palesa can read.

T3: The Blueprint

The technique has two parts: build the budget once a year, then track it every month. The four boxes carry both.

  • 1. List the assets and their work. Every asset you maintain, the tasks each needs, and how often - asked of your people, the supplier manuals and your own memory. This is how a new machine stops being invisible in one lump sum.
  • 2. Cost it bottom-up. Cost each task once (parts + labour), then yearly cost = cost x times-per-year x how-many-assets. Add the lines for your planned-work total. The total is the output, never the starting guess.
  • 3. Add an honest contingency, then total. You cannot predict which thing breaks, but you can save for the fact that something will - 10-20% of planned work (the higher end for new or ageing kit). Sum, and spread across twelve months, putting lumpy items (tyres, a tank inspection) in the month they fall.
  • 4. Track the variance each month. Variance = actual - planned. For any real gap, ask why - timing, a real overspend, or skipped work - then decide: accept, fix, or re-plan.

The budget does three jobs. It is a defensible number - every rand traces to a real task on a real asset, so "how did you arrive at this?" has an answer you point at. It buys no surprises - known work is saved for on purpose, so a seized pump is a line you planned, not a crisis on the card machine. And it is a baseline - once you have a plan, the monthly variance tells you early when you are drifting. It fits when you have a known set of assets and a fair idea of their work (a fleet, a plant, a building); it is overkill for one or two simple assets with no history, and it cannot save you if the underlying maintenance plan is wrong - forget a task and the budget forgets it too. (Note: a negative variance - spending less than planned - is not always good news; first ask whether the work was actually done.)

T4: Your Turn

Now build it for your own assets - do not copy Tank You's numbers, copy the method. One row per task per asset: cost, times a year, how many, multiplied across. Then the contingency, the total, the spread - and from next month, the variance line.

The honest heart of the whole thing is Step 3's contingency - last year's number had none, which is exactly why October became three sleepless nights. Set the cushion on purpose, and the next seizure is a line you saved for, not a card-machine panic. Then do not skip the monthly variance: a budget you build once and never check is a guess again by March. When you can fill the last cell with a number you can read line by line, you have something a bank, a partner, or your own worried self can trust.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your budget honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The total is the output, not the input. The moment you start from "about R200,000" and work backwards, you are guessing again. Build from the assets and the tasks up, and let the number fall out at the end.

• Always fund the unplanned. A budget with no contingency is the one that turns a seized pump into a crisis on the card machine. Set aside 10-20% on purpose - the cheapest insurance you will ever buy is admitting that something, somewhere, will break.

• Planned beats reactive - and costs less. Undisciplined firefighting is dearer than a calm plan: you pay rush rates, you pay for the secondary damage, and you pay in lost sleep. A lower, planned budget is not a smaller commitment to maintenance - it is a smarter one.

• A variance is a question, not a verdict. Over-spending may just be timing (a cost brought forward); under-spending may mean work was skipped, which is worse. Always ask why before you act - and never re-plan the whole budget over a single month's gap.

• Garbage in, garbage out. The budget only costs the work you tell it about. If your maintenance plan forgets a task, your budget forgets it too - so the budget is only ever as good as the plan beneath it.

The pause on the phone was not really about money - it was about whether you knew your own business. A number you can defend line by line answers that, to the bank and to yourself. Build your budget from the work up, so every rand has a reason and a breakdown becomes a line item you saved for, not a crisis you pay for. Do that, and "experience" becomes evidence - and the next October is a plan, not a sleepless week. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring your asset list and last year's spend, and build the first three lines together.)
