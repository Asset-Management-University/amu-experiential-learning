Eleven decisions you did not make

T1: The Ache

You are Ntsika, in your second year, on placement at a water treatment works.

You have been asked to write the maintenance procedure for changing the mechanical seal on a raw-water pump. It took you an afternoon. Six steps, clearly written, technically correct.

Your supervisor read it and said it was fine.

Then, walking past the workshop, you heard the fitter who will actually use it - a man called Bhekizizwe who has been changing seals since before you were at school - ask a colleague, not unkindly: "Does this mean the small isolator or the panel?"

And you realised you did not know which one he meant either, because you had written "isolate the pump", and you had pictured somebody isolating the pump, and you had never once pictured somebody standing in front of two isolators at two in the morning deciding which.

He will get it right. He always gets it right. And if he is on leave and somebody newer is on call, they may not.

My procedure is correct and somebody still has to guess. How do I write instructions that a competent person can follow exactly - at night, in a hurry, with nobody to ask - and be safe?

Before you read on, decide. The phone went at two in the morning. Andries, twenty-odd years on the plant, calling from the pump house: “When you say isolate the pump — do you mean the local isolator or the panel?” And you realised you did not know which one you meant either, because you had written “isolate the pump”, and you had pictured somebody isolating the pump, and you had never once pictured a person standing in front of two isolators at two in the morning deciding which. He will get it right. He always does. And if he is on leave and somebody newer is on call, they may not. Write down how you would write instructions somebody can follow exactly, at night, with nobody to ask — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the decisions you cannot see

• “Fix that line — say which isolator.” Do it today. And it repairs the one ambiguity somebody happened to ring about; the others are still in there, and you cannot see them for the same reason you could not see this one.

• “Add more detail throughout.” More words is not fewer decisions, and it has a cost: instruct a man of twenty years in the use of his own spanner and he will stop reading the document properly, which is where the real content is hiding.

• “Tell people to ring if unsure.” He did ring, at two in the morning, and got you. The next person may not ring — the whole difficulty is that an ambiguity does not announce itself as one, so they will simply choose.

• “Only experienced people do this work.” Not a control you hold. Leave, illness and turnover decide who is on call, and a procedure that works only for the person who does not need it is not doing anything.

• “Add a photograph of the isolator.” Genuinely helps here, and it fixes one step by a method you will not apply to the other forty. It also still leaves the decision unnamed rather than removed.

• Start by naming the one person who will read this. Write down what they already know — this is where authors err in both directions at once, because telling a skilled tradesman how to hold a spanner insults him and he will notice, while omitting what only you know leaves the actual gap. And what only you know is almost never about competence: it is this specific machine on this specific plant — the second suction line added in a modification that never reached the workshop drawing, the non-return valve that has been passing since March, the flush that must be isolated before the discharge or the faces run dry. Then count the decisions you have left them rather than sensing them: every place your text permits two reasonable readings is a choice transferred from you, with drawings and daylight, to somebody at night with neither — and counting is what makes an invisible problem countable. Then walk it through with somebody who does the work, because re-reading cannot find these: you read your intention, and they read your words. And understand what the signature at the bottom asks — not a formality, but somebody staking their competence on this being followable exactly as written.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the procedure - who reads it, counting the decisions you left them, the rewrite, walking it through in the workshop, and understanding what the signature at the bottom is asking for.

1. Name the person who will do this work - what they know, what they cannot see, and what shift they are on

A procedure is written to one person, and you should know who they are before you write a word. Mine goes to a qualified fitter. Write down what they already know, because this is the half most engineers get wrong in one direction or the other. They know how to isolate equipment. They know how to use their tools. They have changed more mechanical seals than I have ever seen. Writing 'select a spanner of the correct size' insults a person who has been doing this for eleven years, and they will notice. Now write down what they cannot know without me. That this pump has a second suction line, added in a modification three years ago, which is not on the drawing in the workshop. That the discharge non-return valve has been passing since March. That the seal flush must be isolated before the discharge or the seal faces run dry. None of that is about their competence. All of it is about this specific machine on this specific plant. And write down when they will read it: at two in the morning, in the rain, with a torch, having been called out. That is not a reason to simplify the engineering. It is a reason not to make them hold six things in their head at once.

2. Read your draft as they will - and count every decision you have left them to make

Here is the honest test, and it takes twenty minutes. Read each step and ask: what would this person have to decide for themselves? My first draft had six steps. Step 1, 'isolate the pump' - five decisions: which electrical isolator, whether to lock it, which valves and in what order, whether the drain counts, and how to prove there is no energy left. Step 2, 'remove the coupling guard' - two: where the fasteners go while the job is open, and whether the alignment shims underneath must be kept. Step 3, 'withdraw the seal cartridge' - one: whether the flush line is disconnected first. Step 4, 'fit the new seal' - two: which torque, and in what sequence. Step 5, 'reassemble' - one: whether alignment is checked before or after the guard goes back. Step 6, 'return to service' - none, because by then it is obvious. Total: eleven decisions. Every one of those will be decided correctly by an experienced fitter on most nights, and that is exactly what makes them dangerous - a procedure that works because the reader is good is not a procedure, it is a memory aid, and it will be handed to a new person eventually. Count them. Write the number down. It is the single most useful number about your draft.

3. Rewrite so each step is one action, in order, with the resource named and nothing left to infer

Now rewrite, and the rule is one step, one action, nothing to infer. Six steps became fourteen, and the eleven decisions became zero. What changed. 'Isolate the pump' became five steps: open and lock the named isolator, close and lock the suction valve, close and lock the discharge valve, open the drain and confirm no flow, then verify zero energy at the test point. That last one is a verification step and it is new - it does not tell the reader to do something, it tells them how to prove the previous four worked. Every procedure needs these and most drafts have none. Three were added: zero-energy verification, seal-face condition check before fitting, and alignment confirmed before the guard is refitted rather than after. Name every resource in the step where it is needed, not in a list at the top: the seal part number, the 24 mm socket, the torque wrench and the value, the shim pack. And put the safety warning immediately before the step it protects, with its reason attached. Not a page of warnings at the front that nobody reads at two in the morning, but 'the seal flush is isolated first: opening the discharge first runs the faces dry' - a warning with a reason is one a competent person will follow even when they are in a hurry, because now they understand what it is for.

4. Walk it through with the person who will use it - before it is issued, not after it fails

Take it to the workshop and read it with them. This is thirty minutes and it is the difference between a document and a working procedure. Say plainly what you are asking: 'I have written this and I need to know where it is wrong.' Then listen, and resist the thing you will want to do, which is to explain what you meant. If you have to explain it, the document has already failed - because you will not be standing there at two in the morning. What mine came back with. The 24 mm socket will not reach past the guard bracket, so the bracket comes off first - a step I did not know existed. The torque figure I had taken from the manual is for a dry thread and they always assemble that joint lubricated, which changes it. And the seal part number I quoted supersedes to a different one, which stores has known for a year. Three corrections, none of which I could have found at my desk, all of which would have cost somebody an hour or worse at night. Then change the document. Not a note, not a verbal briefing - the document, because it is the only part that will still be there in five years. And say who walked it with you and when, in the procedure itself. That is not credit-sharing. It tells the next reader that a person who does this work has seen it.

5. Understand what you are asking them to sign - and write the procedure that deserves it

At the bottom of this document is a line where somebody signs to say they completed it as written. Be clear about what you are asking for. You are asking a person to put their name against a set of instructions you wrote, in a plant where the consequences are physical. If the procedure is wrong, they carried it out; if it is unclear, they interpreted it; and if somebody is hurt, the document is read out in the investigation. So run the last check, and run it honestly. Go through the fourteen steps and ask, of each one: would I be comfortable following this myself, at night, having been called out, with no author available? Anywhere the answer is 'I would ask somebody' - that step is not finished, and the person who will ask nobody, because it is two in the morning and they do not want to seem unsure, is exactly who the step will fail. This is not about protecting yourself from blame. It is that somebody is going to trust this document with their hands, and they will not have met you. The reason to make it clear enough to sign is that they are entitled to a document that can be followed exactly and still be safe - which is a thing you can actually give them, tonight, from your desk.

Box 2 is the twenty minutes that changes the document, and box 4 is the thirty minutes that makes it true. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

An executable procedure is not a correct description of a job. It is a document a competent person can follow exactly, alone, at night, and be safe. Five moves:

  • Know who reads it. What they already know, and what only you know. They know their trade. They do not know that this pump has a second suction line added in a modification.
  • Count the decisions you left them. The honest test. Six steps, eleven decisions. Write the number down.
  • One step, one action, nothing inferred. Plus verification steps - which prove the last action worked, and which most drafts have none of.
  • Walk it through in the workshop. Change the document, not the explanation. If you have to explain it, it has already failed.
  • Know what the signature means. Would you follow this yourself, at night, with nobody to ask?

The count that does the work. Six steps became fourteen, and eleven decisions became zero. The eleven were: five in "isolate the pump", two in "remove the coupling guard", one in "withdraw the seal cartridge", two in "fit the new seal", one in "reassemble", none in the last. Every one of them would be decided correctly by Bhekizizwe on most nights - and that is exactly what makes them dangerous. A procedure that works because the reader is good is not a procedure. It is a memory aid, and it will be handed to a new person eventually.

Verification steps, and why they are usually missing. Three were added: prove zero energy at the test point, check the seal faces before fitting, confirm alignment before the guard goes back rather than after. A verification step does not tell the reader to do something - it tells them how to know the last thing worked. Most drafts contain none, because the author knows the previous step works and so never thinks to prove it.

Warnings with reasons attached. "Isolate the seal flush first: opening the discharge first runs the faces dry." Compare a page of warnings at the front of the document, which nobody reads at two in the morning. A warning with a reason is one a competent person will follow even when they are in a hurry, because now they understand what it is for. A warning without one is a rule to be worked around by somebody who is cold and wants to go home.

An honest boundary. This produces a procedure that can be followed. It does not replace the permit system, the risk assessment or the isolation certificate - those are separate documents with their own authority and your procedure sits inside them. It does not make you the authority on the task; the person in the workshop is. And a procedure for a task you have never seen done is a document you are not yet in a position to write, however good your reading.

T4: Your Turn

Here is a real draft, written by a second-year student on placement. Four steps, for replacing a pressure transmitter on a dosing line.

StepAs written
1Isolate and depressurise the line.
2Disconnect the transmitter and remove it.
3Fit the new transmitter and reconnect.
4Restore the line and check for leaks.

What you also know about this job: the dosing line carries a chemical that is harmful on skin contact. There are two isolation valves, one at each end, and a bleed point between them. The transmitter is wired into a control loop, so removing it will send the loop a false reading unless it is put into manual first. The replacement transmitter must be ranged and its range is not the same as the factory default. And the fitting is a compression type with a specified torque.

  1. Count the decisions. Go step by step and list what the reader must decide because the writer did not say. Give a number.
  2. Find the missing step - there is one whole action absent from this procedure that is not a decision inside another step. Name it and say what happens if it is skipped.
  3. Verification. There are no verification steps. Name three that belong, and say what each one proves.
  4. Warnings. Write one warning, with its reason attached, and say which step it goes immediately before.
  5. Rewrite step 1 alone into as many steps as it needs. Count them.
  6. The workshop question. You take this to the artisan who does the job. Write the one question you ask - and say why it is not "is this alright?"

Work it before you turn over.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. The count. Step 1: which valves, in what order, whether to lock them, where to bleed, how to prove it is depressurised, and what protective equipment for a harmful chemical - six. Step 2: whether the loop goes to manual first, whether to isolate the wiring, and where the removed unit goes - three. Step 3: the ranging, the torque, and the wiring polarity - three. Step 4: how leaks are checked and at what pressure, and when the loop returns to automatic - two. Fourteen decisions in four steps.

2. The missing step. Ranging and verifying the new transmitter is buried inside "fit and reconnect", but it is a whole action - and if it is skipped the loop runs on a transmitter reading the wrong scale. The line will not leak and nothing will look wrong. The dosing will simply be incorrect, quietly, until somebody notices downstream. That is the worst kind of missed step: one whose absence produces no symptom.

3. Three verification steps. After isolation: open the bleed and confirm zero pressure - proves both valves are actually holding, not just closed. After ranging: apply a known pressure and confirm the reading - proves the range was set and the wiring is right. After restoring: confirm the loop is in automatic and the control reading matches the local gauge - proves the plant is back, not just the pipework.

4. A warning with its reason. "Wear chemical gloves and face protection before opening the bleed: residual product remains in the line between the valves even after isolation." It goes immediately before the bleed step - not at the top of the page, where it protects nobody at two in the morning.

5. Step 1 rewritten. (a) Put the control loop into manual and record the current output. (b) Fit chemical gloves and face protection. (c) Close and lock the upstream isolation valve. (d) Close and lock the downstream isolation valve. (e) Open the bleed point and drain to the collection vessel. (f) Confirm zero pressure at the bleed and leave it open. Six steps, from one. And notice that (a) came from the control loop, which the original step did not mention at all.

6. The workshop question. "I have written this and I need to know where it is wrong." Not "is this alright?" - because "is this alright" is a yes-or-no question asked by somebody who wants a yes, and a polite person will give you one. You are not asking for approval. You are asking for the three things they know that you do not, and you have to make it easy for them to tell you.

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Trusting the reader to make the right call, then treating that as proof the procedure works. A step that works because the reader is good conceals a decision, and the document will eventually reach someone who is not.

• Skipping the count of implicit decisions. "It reads fine" is not the same as "eleven decisions became zero" - count them explicitly, step by step.

• Explaining a confusing step out loud instead of rewriting it. If you have to stand next to the person and explain it, the document has already failed - change the document, not the explanation.

• Leaving out verification steps. A step with no way to check it worked gives a false sense that the job is done - add the check that proves the last action succeeded.

What you can do now that you could not before. Write for the specific person who will do the work. Count the decisions your draft leaves them. Split steps until each is one action with nothing inferred. Add the verification steps that prove the last action worked. Put warnings where they protect, with their reasons attached. And take it to the workshop before it is issued rather than after it fails.

Bhekizizwe would have got it right. He always gets it right, and a procedure that depends on that is a procedure that has quietly made one person's experience into a safety system.

The eleven decisions were not in the plant. They were in the gap between what you knew and what you wrote down, and that gap is the only part of this you can close from your desk.
