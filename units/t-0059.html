A reliability study we were proud of in 2019 says these pumps' bearings 'rarely' fail - but one has tripped three times in six weeks because the water got dirtier and nobody told the book. How do I keep the maintenance plan alive so it updates itself as the plant changes, instead of drifting into fiction?

T1: The Ache

It is a wet Tuesday morning at the Corathyl Water Treatment Works, the plant that cleans river water for about 120,000 people in your town. You are the maintenance planner, standing in the pump hall with a coffee going cold in your hand.

On the steel bench is a thick lever-arch file with a faded sticker: "RCM Study - High-Lift Pumps - completed March 2019." Five years ago a team spent three months working out exactly how each pump could fail and what jobs would keep it running. It was good work; everyone was proud. But this morning High-Lift Pump 3 has tripped again - the third trip in six weeks. The file says Pump 3 needs a bearing inspection once a year and that bearing failure is "rare". Your hand-written log says otherwise: three bearing call-outs since April.

Your shift fitter, Tshepang, leans on the rail and says it flat: "Boss, that book is out of date. We changed the river intake screens last year, there's more grit in the water now. The pumps are working harder. Nobody told the book." He is right, and it stings. The raw water is dirtier since the upstream mine reopened; you added two new variable-speed drives; the supplier discontinued the old bearing grade. None of that is in the file. The plan you are working to was true in 2019 and is slowly becoming a work of fiction - while Pump 3 sits idle, costing about R18,000 a day in extra pumping from the standby set.

You can feel the trap pulling at you: just redo the whole study from scratch, lock a team in a room for another three months. But you did that once already - and in five years it drifted out of date anyway. A one-off study, however good, goes stale the moment the plant moves on.

A reliability study we were proud of is now fiction - the water got dirtier and nobody told the book. How do I keep the maintenance plan alive, so it updates itself on a schedule as the plant changes, instead of drifting out of date until the pumps fail?

Before you read on, decide. The study was good in 2019. Since then the raw water got dirtier, two variable-speed drives went in, and the supplier discontinued the old bearing grade — and none of it is in the file. Pump 3 is idle at about R18,000 a day. Tshepang knew; the book did not. Write down what you would do. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one does about next time

• “Redo the whole study from scratch.” Three months in a room, and you have done this once already — and it drifted out of date within five years anyway. A better one-off produces a better fiction, more slowly, because the fault was never the quality of the study.

• “Fix the pump-3 problem and move on.” Necessary, and it treats the symptom you happened to notice. Three things changed and you know about one of them because a pump stopped — the other two are still sitting in the plan, still wrong, still waiting.

• “Tell people to raise a change whenever something changes.” Reasonable, and it depends on somebody recognising that a dirtier river is a maintenance matter. Nobody who changed anything here thought they were changing the maintenance plan, and an instruction does not fix that.

• “Review it annually — put it in the calendar.” Much closer, and a review with no defined trigger becomes a meeting where everybody agrees the plan looks fine. What would you actually look at? Without naming what to listen for, the calendar entry survives and the plan still drifts.

• “Ask the operators — they know what really happens.” Right, and it is the answer that saved you this time: not the file, but Tshepang on the rail. Make it a habit rather than a rescue and you have most of what you need — but it still has to arrive somewhere and change something.

The sentence to hold on to is his: the pumps are working harder, and nobody told the book. Whatever you build has to be the thing that tells the book.

You do not need a cleverer study. You need a reliable, repeating way to keep your maintenance strategies current - so that when the water gets dirtier, a pump fails more often, or a part is discontinued, the plan catches it on a schedule. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is an RCM Living Programme. RCM - Reliability-Centred Maintenance - asks, for each important asset, "how can this fail, what happens when it does, and what is the smartest job to stop it or catch it early?" A first study gives a good plan for the day you finish it; the failure here was treating it as a one-off project instead of a living thing. A living programme keeps the plan up to date on purpose, on a repeating cycle, listening for three triggers - a changed context, new failure data, and tasks that stop earning their keep. Watch it run on the high-lift pumps.

Step 1 - Set the cadence and the owner

The High-Lift Pumps are critical (no water to town if they all stop) and in a fast-changing context (dirtier water), so you set a review every 12 months (the office air-conditioning, low-harm and stable, gets one every 3 years). You name yourself the owner and Tshepang the technical reviewer, and put a recurring task in the system: 'RCM review - High-Lift Pumps - due 31 March each year.'

Step 2 - Gather the evidence (the three triggers)

Context: the upstream mine reopened, so raw-water grit roughly doubled (turbidity ~5 to ~11 NTU); two variable-speed drives were added to Pumps 3 and 4; the old bearing grade was discontinued. Failure data: Pump 3 had 3 bearing trips this year, Pump 4 had 1, Pumps 1 and 2 none - and the 2019 study predicted one failure every 5 years per pump, while Pump 3 is failing about once every 4 months, roughly 15 times more often. Performance: the yearly inspection is too rare to catch a 4-month fault, and the monthly vibration check is only on Pumps 1 and 2.

Step 3 - Screen for what actually changed

You sort the four pumps fast. Pumps 1 and 2: the context changed (water) but no new failures and no failing task - a light check. Pumps 3 and 4: context changed (water and the new drives), failing differently (the trips), and a task under-performing - a full re-analyse. You do NOT redo the whole 2019 study; you re-analyse only Pumps 3 and 4's bearing failure mode - the slice that tripped the triggers.

Step 4 - Re-analyse the changed slice, and update the plan

For the bearing mode on Pumps 3 and 4: a yearly inspection cannot catch a fault that grows in four months, so you move to a monthly vibration check (condition-based, catching it early), change the bearing to the available higher-grade sealed type, and add a quarterly intake-strainer clean. A cost check: the vibration check is about R600 per pump a month (R7,200 a year), and avoiding even 3 standby days (3 x R18,000 = R54,000) pays for it many times over. You record the reason: 'frequency yearly -> monthly and bearing re-specced because turbidity doubled and drives were added; trips rose to once per 4 months vs a predicted 5 years.'

Step 5 - Close the loop (next review, and to the doers)

You confirm the next high-lift pump review for 31 March next year, check the owner and reviewer are still in post, and hand the new tasks to the floor: brief the fitters, update the job cards, and get the monthly vibration check onto Tshepang's round sheet - not just into the file. Step 5 now points back at Step 1; next year, when the world changes again, the plan will catch it, because the catching is finally a habit.

See what the cycle did. It did not lock a team in a room for three months; it caught the few things that had really changed, fixed exactly those, wrote down why, and made sure the catching happens again next year. And see the mission in it: a maintenance plan is a promise to the 120,000 people whose water depends on these pumps - that the right care is happening, matched to how the asset actually fails today, not how it failed in a study five years ago. A plan left to drift is a quiet lie that holds until the day the pumps stop. And notice who saved it: not the file, but Tshepang on the rail, whose log and whose eyes knew the truth the book had lost. A living programme is, at heart, the discipline of listening to the people who do the work, and writing what they know back into the plan. The R18,000 a day is the measure of the drift, not the point; the point is care that stays true.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the living programme as a repeating loop of five moves.

  • 1. Set the cadence and the owner. How often each asset's plan is reviewed (harm times rate-of-change), and the named person responsible.
  • 2. Gather the evidence. The three triggers since the last review.
  • 3. Screen for what changed. Sort the changed few from the unchanged many.
  • 4. Re-analyse the changed slice, and update. Redo only the affected failure mode; change the tasks; record why.
  • 5. Close the loop. Schedule the next review and get the new tasks to the people who do the work.

The ideas underneath. A living programme is a maintenance strategy kept current on a repeating cycle, so it breathes with the plant; "living" means that when the plant changes, the plan changes too. It listens for three triggers: a context change (the asset's world - load, environment, raw materials, a law, a discontinued spare), new failure data (it is failing differently than the study predicted - the call-out log is the evidence), and performance feedback (a task too frequent, too rare, or simply not catching what it should). The shape is a circle, not a line: step 5 feeds back into step 1, and that is what makes it living. This has a formal home in SAE JA1011 (the standard for what counts as a proper RCM process), which requires the analysis to be revisited as the context changes. It fits any asset whose failures hurt (safety, supply, big cost) and whose operating context changes over time; it is overkill for something cheap and stable where run-to-failure is fine.

Two disciplines keep the programme alive rather than just tidy. First, let the call-out log overrule the study, every time - the plan is a prediction and the log is the truth; when a fitter's record says a "rare" failure is happening every few months, the record wins and the plan changes, because a study defended against reality is exactly how a plan rots into fiction. Second, the change is not real until it reaches the person holding the spanner - a revised frequency that lives only in the RCM file changes nothing; close the loop onto the job card, the round sheet, the briefing, or you have updated a document, not a maintenance plan.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your living programme true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A study is a snapshot; a programme is a habit. The best one-off RCM goes stale the day the plant moves on. Build the repeating review, not just the analysis.

• Give every plan an owner and a date. A review nobody owns and nothing schedules never happens. The dated, owned, recurring task is the whole difference.

• Re-analyse the slice, not the study. Screen first; redo only the failure modes that tripped a trigger. Redoing everything is how the cycle dies of exhaustion.

• The log beats the book. When the call-out record contradicts the study, the record is reality. Change the plan to fit the failures, not the failures to fit the plan.

• Close the loop to the floor. A new task that never reaches the job card and the round sheet has changed a file, not the maintenance. Hand it to the people who do the work.

You came in to a tripped pump, a proud old study, and a fitter telling you the book was a work of fiction. You can leave with something that cannot drift the same way: a dated, owned review; the three triggers gathered; only the changed slice re-analysed; the new tasks on the round sheet; and a date in the diary for next year. The result is a living plan that breathes with the plant - and the catching becomes a habit, not a project. That is what protects the 120,000 people downstream: not a cleverer study locked in a file, but a plan kept honest, year after year, by listening to the asset and the people who tend it.
