Step 1 of 5

The councillor asked what the network will look like in three years if we spend nothing, and I have 1,400 kilometres of condition grades and no way to answer - how do I turn a snapshot into a forecast?

T1: The Ache

Roodeplaat Local Municipality has 1,400 kilometres of water pipe under it and about 210,000 people on the end of it. You are the asset manager.

Last Thursday, in the infrastructure portfolio committee, a councillor asked you a fair question in nine words. "What will the network look like in three years?"

You had, in front of you, more condition data than the municipality has ever had. A three-year CCTV survey programme. Every section graded 1 to 5. The burst register going back eleven years. You could tell her how many kilometres are failing today to the nearest kilometre.

And you could not answer her question. Not because you lacked data. Because everything you had was a photograph, and she had asked about a film.

You said you would come back to her. The conversation that follows is the same one this department has every year: the current condition is presented, and a budget is requested, and nothing in between the two is a forecast. She is not being difficult. She is being asked by residents in Ward 6, who have had four bursts on one street since March, whether it is going to get better or worse - and "here is the current condition" is not an answer to that, however good the survey behind it.

The replacement programme runs at 18 kilometres a year. Somebody chose that number before you arrived. Nobody in the building can tell you whether it is catching up, holding, or losing.

I have the best condition data this municipality has ever had, and it cannot answer a question about next year. How do I turn a snapshot into a forecast?

Before you read on, answer the councillor. Fourteen hundred kilometres, every section graded, eleven years of bursts, and nine words to answer: what will it look like in three years? Write down how you would work it out - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one is able to say

• "Show her the current condition report." It is the best data in the building, it is accurate, and it answers a different question. A grade profile says what is true today; nothing in it moves.

• "Extrapolate the burst trend." Genuinely useful and it forecasts the symptom rather than the state. It also cannot tell you what would change if the programme doubled, because bursts respond to condition with a lag and the trend line knows nothing about grades.

• "Use the design life - pipes last 60 years, so work from installation dates." The instinct everybody reaches for, and it is a forecast about a catalogue rather than about this network. Two pipes laid the same year in different soil are not in the same state, and you have a survey that proves it.

• "Ask the engineers what they expect." Their judgement is real and worth having. It is also unwritten and unarguable, so it cannot be checked, handed over, or used to compare two budget options.

• "Model it properly - get consultants in." Might be right eventually, and it puts a nine-word question six months and a procurement away.

• What every one of these is missing is the one thing a snapshot cannot contain: a rate. The grades say where everything is. To say where it will be, you need to know how quickly things move from each grade to the next - and that number is not in the survey. It is in the difference between two surveys, which this municipality has and has never compared.

T2: The Tool in Action

Define the states so two people would grade the same asset the same way. Count how much sits in each one today. Then get the thing a single survey cannot give you - how fast assets move from each state to the next - by comparing two surveys separated in time. Roll it forward year by year. Watch it run on Roodeplaat's network.

Step 1 - Name the states so that two people would put the same asset in the same one

Karabo Venter writes the five grades on the wall with an observable test beside each: 1, no defects on camera; 2, minor cracking, no infiltration; 3, cracking with infiltration or displaced joints; 4, structural deformation or active leakage; 5, collapsed, or repaired more than twice in two years. Then she does the part that matters: she has two inspectors independently grade the same forty sections. They agree on thirty-four. The six they disagree on are all at the 3-and-4 boundary, so she tightens that one definition and re-runs those six. Without that check she would have had a forecast built on two different scales.

Step 2 - Count how much of the asset base sits in each state today

From the CCTV programme and the burst register, across 1,400 km: grade 1, 380 km; grade 2, 420 km; grade 3, 330 km; grade 4, 190 km; grade 5, 80 km. Karabo notes what this does and does not tell the councillor. It says 80 km is failing now, which everybody already knew from the bursts. It says nothing whatever about next year.

Step 3 - Measure how fast things move from each state to the next

Karabo compares the 2021 survey against this one, five years apart, section by section. Of the pipe that was grade 1, about 6 per cent per year moved to grade 2; grade 2 to grade 3, 8 per cent; grade 3 to grade 4, 11 per cent; grade 4 to grade 5, 14 per cent. Deterioration accelerates, which surprises nobody who has worked on pipes and had never been written down as a number anywhere in the department. She writes the sample size beside each rate, because a 14 per cent taken from a small grade-4 population is the softest figure in the study.

Step 4 - Roll the numbers forward year by year and read the answer

Year one: grade 1 keeps 94 per cent of 380 and gains nothing, so 357 km; grade 5 keeps its 80 and gains 14 per cent of 190, so 107 km. Karabo carries it three years and checks each year sums to 1,400. The answer to the councillor's question: grade 5 goes from 80 km to 164 km, and grade 4 and 5 together from 270 km to 378 km. Spend nothing for three years and the failing category roughly doubles. That is a sentence nobody in the department could produce a fortnight ago.

Step 5 - Read off the number that changes the budget conversation

About 27 km of pipe falls into grade 5 every year. The current programme replaces 18 km a year. So the department is not slowly catching up, as everyone had assumed from the fact that it replaces pipe every year - it is falling behind by about 9 km annually, and has been for as long as the programme has run at this level. Karabo puts one line in the report: 27 km a year is standstill; 18 km is managed decline; and anything above 27 is the only version that is actually recovery. Nobody argues with it, because it is their own inspection data and their own rates.

See what one comparison of two surveys unlocked. Spend nothing for three years and the failing grade goes from 80 kilometres to 164 - it roughly doubles - and that sentence comes entirely from the municipality's own inspection data, which had been sitting in two files nobody had put side by side. And then the number that actually changes the budget conversation, which nothing in a condition report can produce: about 27 kilometres falls into the failing grade every year, and the programme replaces 18. The department is not slowly catching up, which is what everybody had assumed from the fact that it replaces pipe at all. It is losing about 9 kilometres a year, and has been for as long as the programme has run at this level. Nobody argued with it, because it was their own data and their own rates - and for Ward 6, the difference between managed decline and recovery is now a number instead of a hope.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the third is the one that turns a photograph into a film.

  • 1. Define the states observably - and test the definitions by having two people grade the same sample independently.
  • 2. Count how much sits in each state today. Most organisations already have this.
  • 3. Measure the transition rates from two surveys separated in time - what share of each state moved up each year.
  • 4. Roll it forward one year at a time, checking the totals still add to your asset base.
  • 5. Read off the inflow to your worst state. That is the standstill rate.

What you have built is a multi-state condition model, and its vocabulary is only names for what you did. The grades are condition states; the share moving from one to the next each year are transition rates, and the whole grid of them is a transition matrix. Rolling it forward assumes what is called the Markov property - that where an asset goes next depends on the state it is in now and not on how long it took to get there - which is an approximation, and a good enough one for planning as long as you say you have made it. The inflow figure you read off at the end is the standstill rate: the annual renewal that holds the profile still, below which you are in managed decline whatever the programme is called.

It fits any population you grade and renew rather than run to failure - pipes, roads, roofs, bridges, poles, vehicles, plant, housing stock. Two limits belong in the same paragraph as the answer. It forecasts the profile of the population and cannot tell you which individual pipe will burst, so it is a budget instrument and never a work-order instrument. And it tells you how a population deteriorates, not what to do about it - deciding which intervention to apply in which state, at what cost, is the next question, and it takes a transition matrix like this one as its input.

T4: Your Turn

Take a population you are responsible for that gets graded and renewed - pipes, roads, roofs, vehicles, machines, buildings, IT equipment. Write your states down with an observable test beside each. Count what sits in each one now. Then find your second survey, however rough, and work out what share moved up per year.

Two disciplines decide whether the forecast is worth putting in front of a councillor. First, test your state definitions on two people before you trust a single rate. Two inspectors working to slightly different scales will produce a transition rate that measures the difference between them rather than anything about the asset - and it will look exactly like a real rate. Grading the same sample independently and arguing about the disagreements is an afternoon that protects everything downstream. Second, get your rates from your own two surveys, not from a published table. Deterioration rates depend on soil, water chemistry, pressure, traffic and climate, and a rate borrowed from another network is a forecast about that network wearing your asset names. If you genuinely have only one survey, say so and give a range rather than a number - the honest wide answer survives a challenge that the borrowed precise one does not.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the forecast honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A snapshot is not a forecast, however good it is. The best condition survey ever done says nothing about next year. Only a rate does, and a rate needs two surveys.

• Loose state definitions produce confident nonsense. If two inspectors would grade the same asset differently, your transition rate is partly a measurement of your inspectors. Test it on a sample first.

• Never borrow somebody else's rates. Soil, pressure, chemistry and climate decide these numbers. A published table is a forecast about the network it came from.

• Check the totals every year. The counts must still sum to your asset base. That one check catches nearly every arithmetic error this method allows.

• It forecasts the population, not the pipe. It cannot tell you which section will burst, so it belongs in a budget paper and never in a work order - and saying that plainly is what stops it being misused as a prediction about a particular street.

• Say that you assumed history does not matter. Rolling it forward treats an asset that has been grade 3 for a decade the same as one that arrived last year. That is usually acceptable for planning and it is always worth declaring, because it is the assumption a reviewer will find.

• Quote the softest rate with its sample size. The worst-state transition is usually taken from the smallest population and carries the most weight in the answer. Give it a range, and show what the forecast looks like at both ends.

• The forecast is an average, not a promise. Rolling rates forward gives you the expected profile; the real network will land somewhere around it, and a single wet year or one bad batch of pipe will move it. Present it as the central expectation with the softest rate's range beside it, so nobody quotes the middle number back at you in three years as though it had been a guarantee.

You left that committee owing a councillor an answer to nine words, holding the best condition data the municipality has ever had and unable to say anything about next year. You can go back with one page: the five grades defined so two inspectors agree, today's profile, the rates taken from your own two surveys, and the answer - spend nothing and the failing grade doubles in three years - and underneath it the number nobody had, that 27 kilometres a year is standstill and the programme has been running at 18. This is how a photograph of the network becomes a film, using data the organisation already paid for and had never compared. For four bursts on one street in Ward 6, the useful part is not the forecast. It is that "better or worse" finally has an arithmetic behind it. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring two condition surveys of the same population and work out the rates between them together.)
