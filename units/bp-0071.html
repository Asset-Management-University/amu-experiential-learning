Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Nkosenhle, the owner of a large commercial farm. You are standing in the rain, watching muddy water pour over the main access road, again. The culvert - the big pipe that runs under the road - is overwhelmed. It was designed for a 1-in-20-year storm, but you have had three of these "rare" storms in the last five years, and the road is washing away at the edges. Again.

Last year you spent R120,000 repairing this exact spot. You put it back precisely to the original specification - it felt like the right, solid thing to do. Now, a year later, it is failing again. The harvest is ready in the packing sheds, but your trucks cannot get out and your workers could not get in this morning.

And it is not just the rain. In summer the heat is different: the packing-shed cooling, top-of-the-line twenty years ago, cannot cope with week-long heatwaves, and you lost 10% of your stored crop to heat last season. The dam, sized for the rainfall of the last century, runs dangerously low by the end of the dry season. You fix things properly. You maintain your assets. But they keep breaking in ways they never used to.

We keep fixing our assets properly, to the right standard, and they keep breaking from weather they were never built for - what are we missing?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The first instinct is to do what has always worked. "Repair it again, back to how it was." But you have done that twice already - you are rebuilding for a world that no longer exists; the old standard was correct for the old climate, but the rain and heat you get now are different, so repairing to the old spec just guarantees the next 'new normal' storm breaks it again. Your site manager says, "it was a freak event, bad luck." But the records show these 'freak' heatwaves and floods are becoming regular - what was once a freak event is now just a bad Tuesday, and relying on luck when the pattern is clear is not a strategy, it is denial. You might even think, "this is bigger than us - the government should fix the climate, the original engineers should have foreseen it." But you cannot wait for a global problem to be solved to stop your own road washing away, and the original engineers were not fools: they designed correctly for the climate they knew, and the world changed after the asset was built. Adapting it to today is now your job.

So before you read on: the real question is not "how do we repair this asset?" but "what has changed in our environment that makes it keep failing?"

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough is realising the problem is not your asset but its environment. The climate your assets were designed for is gone, and the historical record of rainfall, heat and storms is no longer a safe guide to the future. You cannot repair your way back to a climate that no longer exists - so every time an asset fails or comes up for renewal, you have a choice: restore it to the old, obsolete standard, or adapt it to the world you live in now. Five moves:

  • See the change - the environment moved, not just the asset: the breakthrough is realising the problem is not your asset but its environment. The asset is not failing because it was badly built or merely worn - it was built correctly for a climate that has since changed, and you keep diagnosing 'it broke, repair it' when the real diagnosis is 'the world it was built for is gone'. So stop blaming the asset and start studying your own weather: pull the last twenty years of rainfall, heat and storm data for your own place and read the trend. When you see, in your own records, that the summers are hotter and the rainstorms more intense than when the asset was designed, the whole problem reframes - this is not bad luck or bad maintenance, it is a changed duty, and you cannot repair your way back to a climate that no longer exists.
  • Re-base your design assumptions on the climate you now get: the asset was sized against the historical record - the 1-in-20-year storm, the hottest expected week - and that record is no longer a safe guide to the future, because the past has stopped predicting the weather you now actually get. So re-base the assumptions: work out the NEW 1-in-20-year storm from recent data (it may be a third more intense), the new number of days over the heat the shed was built for, the new dry-season the dam must survive. You are not guessing wildly at the far future; you are simply updating the design basis from a vanished climate to the one you measurably live in now. Every plan and renewal that still rests on the old numbers is quietly building for a world that is gone - fix the numbers first, and the right design follows.
  • Build back better at the next repair: here is the leverage: every failure and every renewal is a fork - restore to the old obsolete standard, or adapt to the world you live in now - and the cheapest moment to adapt an asset is when you are already paying to rebuild it. So when the road washes out, do not just replace the culvert like-for-like; put in one sized for the new storm. When the shed roof is replaced, insulate it and paint it to reflect the heat, cutting the cooling bill. The marginal cost of building back BETTER at the moment of repair is small; the cost of building back the SAME is another full failure and another full repair, on a clock that is getting shorter. Spend the repair money once, on the future, instead of again and again on the past.
  • Protect the critical and exposed few first: you cannot upgrade everything at once, and you should not try - so aim the adaptation where a failure is catastrophic or cuts people off, and where the exposure to the changed climate is greatest. Map it: the failure of the main access road and the farm dam strands the harvest and the workers; the back-boundary fence can wait. This is bp-0158's focus-on-the-vital-few married to bp-0147's look-where-the-risk-actually-is - concentrate your limited adaptation budget on the handful of assets whose loss would do the most harm and which the new weather hits hardest, and let the rest follow as they come up for renewal. Adapting the critical few buys the most resilience per rand by far.
  • Add resilience and graceful failure: beyond rebuilding individual assets stronger, design the whole system to BEND rather than break when the extreme finally exceeds even your new design - because it sometimes will. Give the water somewhere to go (deep-rooted planting on the stream banks, an overflow, a swale) so a flood spreads and slows instead of tearing things out; shade and ventilate the cooling units so they cope on the worst days; keep a fallback access route so one washed-out road does not strand everyone. This is bp-0162's bend-not-break applied physically: you are not trying to make the asset invincible (impossible and unaffordable) but to make its failure gentle and survivable rather than sudden and catastrophic, so the worst day costs you a delay, not the whole harvest.

See it transform Nkosenhle's farm. He stops blaming the asset and studies his own last twenty years of weather - the trend is plain: hotter summers, fiercer rainstorms. He works with an engineer to re-base the design: the new 1-in-20-year storm is a third more intense, the summer now has many more days above the heat the shed was built for. So when the road washes out he does not just repair it - he puts in a culvert three times wider; when the shed roof is replaced he insulates and paints it to reflect the sun, cutting the cooling bill sharply. He cannot upgrade everything, so he protects the critical, exposed few first - the access road and the dam, not the back fence - and he adds graceful failure: deep-rooted planting to slow the storm water, shade over the cooling units, a fallback route. The road now holds, the crop is safe, and his repair budget buys genuine improvements instead of repeating last year's fix. Your assets are not failing because they were badly built - they were built correctly for a climate that has since changed: the flood that was 'once in fifty years' when the dam was sized now comes every few years. Repairing them back to the old standard only guarantees the next break, because you are rebuilding for a world that no longer exists. You cannot repair your way back to a climate that is gone - so re-base your assumptions on the weather you now get, build back better at every repair, protect the critical and exposed few first, and design for graceful failure, making each repair and renewal a chance to build for the world you actually live in now.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - think about a house with a garden that floods by the back door whenever it rains hard.

See climate-adaptation at home

For years the owner just lived with the soggy patch - put down a plank to walk on, waited for it to dry, repainted the water-stained wall every couple of years. That is "repairing" back to the old standard, and as the rains grew heavier the problem only got worse.

The new way is to see that the rains are different now. The owner spends a weekend digging a simple gravel drain to channel the excess water away from the house and into the flowerbed, and raises the doorstep by a single brick. They did not try to change the weather - they accepted the new reality of heavier rain and made a small, permanent adaptation to the asset. The problem is solved, for good - because they built for the weather they now get, not the weather the house was built for.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own operation. Where are you fighting the same battle over and over - restoring something to a standard the world has clearly outgrown? It need not be a flood or a heatwave; it could be a process, a system or a physical asset under a pressure that has shifted. You do not need a massive budget, just a new way of seeing.

  1. Re-base your standards. The next time something fails from an outside pressure, stop and ask: "is this a freak event, or the new normal?" Look at the data from the last 5-10 years, not the last 50.
  2. Use repairs to adapt. The next time an asset breaks, see it as the cheapest moment to build back better: before you restore it, ask "what one thing could we change right now, while we are fixing this, to make it fit the world as it is today?"
  3. Find your critical risks. Which one or two assets cause the most disruption when they fail - a server that overheats, a loading bay that floods, a supply line that breaks? Focus there first.
  4. Design for graceful failure. Can you add an overflow, a backup, some shade, a stronger bracket - so the system bends, not breaks, under the next stress?

This was never about finding fault with the past - the people who built your assets did their jobs with the knowledge they had, and the world changed under them; that is not failure, it is just time passing. This is the simple, responsible work of stewardship: making sure the things people depend on - the road to the harvest, the water in the dry season, the cool store that holds the food - are fit for the world as it is today, and as it is likely to be tomorrow.
