Step 1 of 5

Not seven minutes. Seven months.

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, reading a published analysis of an attack on a water utility much like yours. A plant stopped. An operator watching a screen that showed a process which was not happening.

Your first feeling is relief, and it is entirely reasonable. Corathyl Water Treatment Works has a firewall. It has antivirus. The control network is segregated from the office network. The supplier who installed the system assures you, in writing, that it is secure.

Then you reach the timeline, and the relief goes out of you.

The adversary was inside for seven months.

Not seven minutes. Two hundred and fourteen days, during which nothing broke and nothing alarmed and nothing looked wrong - because they were not breaking anything. They were reading. Mapping the network. Finding the drawings. Learning which pump did what, what the setpoints were, and exactly what the operators would see on their screens.

And you understand, with a coldness you did not expect, that you have no idea whether somebody is doing that at Corathyl right now. Not because your defences are weak - but because every one of them was built to stop an entry, and almost all of this attack was a long, quiet education.

Everything I own is designed to stop somebody getting in, and the attack I am reading about was mostly seven months of somebody quietly learning the plant. How do I take apart an attack like this and work out what my own works should actually change?

Before you read on, decide. The published account you have just read describes an attack on a plant much like yours. The intrusion itself took minutes. What followed took seven months — quietly reading engineering documentation, understanding how the process ran, working out what a change would have to look like to matter at that specific plant — and none of it was noticed. Every defence you own at Corathyl is built to stop somebody getting in. You realise, with a coldness you did not expect, that you have no idea whether somebody is doing this here right now. Write down how you would take an attack like that apart and decide what your works should change — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the asymmetry that makes this defensible

• “Strengthen the perimeter — better boundary controls.” More of what addresses the shortest part of the attack. In the account you just read, the entry took minutes; the perimeter was not where the seven months went.

• “Disconnect the control network entirely.” Sometimes possible, often not, and rarely as complete as believed — and it is a single control, so its failure returns you to having no visibility at all inside.

• “Buy a monitoring product.” Possibly right, and bought before you know what you are looking for it becomes an expensive source of alerts nobody can interpret. The question of what would have been visible comes first.

• “It was a sophisticated attacker — we could not have stopped it.” The comfortable answer and the one the seven months refutes. Something that takes seven months offers a great many opportunities to be noticed.

• “Do a full risk assessment of everything.” Unbounded, and it will produce a register rather than a change. You already have a specific, documented attack in front of you — a far better starting point than an abstract survey.

• Stop treating the attack as an event and take it apart as a chain of achievements, each of which had to succeed for the next to be possible. Write each link as what the adversary achieved, never as how — this is a defensive analysis and not a method. Two things then become visible that a perimeter view cannot show you. Most of the links have nothing to do with entry: they are the acquisition of understanding, and each one is a separate opportunity to notice something. And here is what makes operational systems defensible at all — an attack that must produce a physical effect requires the adversary to learn your specific plant, because a generic capability cannot manipulate a process it does not understand. That learning takes time, leaves traces, and is the longest and most fragile part of the chain. Which changes the question entirely: not how do we make the wall higher, but where in this sequence would we have seen something — and that turns an unanswerable worry into a list of specific visibility gaps at your works. A chain fails if any single link breaks.

You deconstruct it into links, and then you count them. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the deconstruction - the chain laid out, measured, and turned into defences for a specific works.

1. Reconstruct the chain in two stages (what was achieved, never how)

An attack on a plant is not one event but a CHAIN, and the ICS CYBER KILL CHAIN splits it into TWO STAGES. STAGE 1 is intrusion preparation and execution - gaining access to information ABOUT the control system, LEARNING THE SYSTEM, and obtaining the means to reach production environments; it looks like an ordinary IT breach. STAGE 2 is ICS attack development and execution, which produces the actual operational impact. Write each link as WHAT THE ADVERSARY ACHIEVED, never as how they did it - this is a defensive analysis, not a method. STAGE 1 (7 links): obtained credentials to a corporate account; established persistent access; located and read engineering documentation on a shared drive; identified the route between corporate and control networks; reached an engineering workstation; read the control logic and operator display configuration; established a way to communicate outward from the control network. STAGE 2 (4 links): developed a change to the control logic matched to THIS plant; tested it against a copy of the configuration; delivered it to the controller; executed it, altering the process while the operator display continued to show normal values.

2. Find where they had to learn YOUR plant

Here is what makes an ICS attack different from an IT attack, and it is the defender's biggest opportunity. A GENERIC ATTACK CANNOT DAMAGE A SPECIFIC PROCESS. To alter a plant without simply breaking it - to change what happens while the operators see something normal - the adversary must know which pump does what, what the setpoints are, how the logic is written, and what the operator display shows. That knowledge is not on the internet. It is in YOUR drawings, YOUR control narratives, YOUR network diagrams, YOUR vendor manuals and YOUR configuration backups. Links 3 and 6 of this chain exist for no other reason. WHICH MEANS YOUR ENGINEERING DOCUMENTATION IS A TARGET, and document control - which every asset manager files under quality or operations - IS ALSO A SECURITY CONTROL. Where those documents live, who can read them, and whether anyone would notice them being read is now a plant-safety question.

3. Count the links, and count the time

Now measure it, because the measurement overturns the instinct. THE CHAIN HAS 11 LINKS. STAGE 1 RAN FOR 214 DAYS - 5,136 hours - from first intrusion to understanding the plant well enough to act. STAGE 2 TOOK 6 HOURS. So the defender's window was 5,136 / 6 = 856 TIMES LONGER than the attacker's execution window, and not one hour of it was used. Read that properly: the failure was never SPEED. Nobody was outrun. For seven months an adversary was quietly reading, and every control the utility owned had been built to stop an ENTRY rather than to notice an EDUCATION. THE PROBLEM WAS VISIBILITY. And the arithmetic cuts the defender's way: THE ATTACKER MUST SUCCEED 11 TIMES; THE DEFENDER NEED ONLY SUCCEED ONCE.

4. Derive a defence for each link - and be honest about which you can do

Go link by link and name the control that would have BROKEN it (stopped it happening) or REVEALED it (made it visible in time). Then classify each honestly, because a defence you cannot fund is not a defence. Reviewing the 11: 7 LINKS could have been broken or revealed by controls the utility ALREADY HELD or could add cheaply - access review, alerting on engineering-document access, monitoring outbound traffic from the control network, controlling removable media, reviewing remote access. 3 LINKS needed REAL INVESTMENT - network monitoring inside the control network, tighter conduit control between zones, logic-change detection. 1 LINK could not realistically have been prevented, but COULD HAVE BEEN DETECTED. Then concentrate where the odds are: STAGE 1 holds seven of the eleven links and 214 of the 214 days. That is where a defender with limited money should spend it.

5. What must survive a successful attack (assume one day they get through)

Assume that one day somebody gets all the way through, and ask what must still hold. THE SAFETY SYSTEM MUST BE INDEPENDENT OF THE CONTROL SYSTEM - genuinely independent, so that reaching the control system does not also reach the last protection layer; separate boxes are not enough if they can be reached from the same place, so check the path, not the label. VERIFIED OFFLINE BACKUPS of control logic and configuration - offline because anything reachable can be altered, and verified because an untested backup is a hope. THE ABILITY TO RUN THE PLANT MANUALLY, and people who have practised it. Then the two duties that decide all of this. FIRST, the independent audit: the owner should audit the system's security independently, ESPECIALLY third-party vendor access points, and CANNOT RELY SOLELY on the vendor or integrator who designed, built or sold it - their assurance is not your audit. SECOND, and the one that belongs to asset management: SECURITY IS DECIDED AT PROCUREMENT AND DESIGN, because ICS components have very long lives and whatever you build will likely stay unchanged for many years.

Box 3 is where the dread turns into something useful. Eleven links, seven months, and an attacker who had to complete every single one of them. Read the last sentence of that box twice. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Defence in operational technology is not about stopping a single fast breach. It is about having the visibility to break any one link in the long, slow chain of an adversary learning your plant. Five questions:

  • What were the links, and in which stage? The ICS Cyber Kill Chain has two: Stage 1, intrusion preparation and execution - gaining access to information about the control system, learning it, and obtaining the means to reach production - which looks like an ordinary IT breach; and Stage 2, attack development and execution, which produces the operational impact. Write each link as what was achieved.
  • Where did they have to learn your plant? A generic attack cannot damage a specific process. The links that exist only to acquire your drawings, logic, setpoints and displays are the ones that make your engineering documentation a target - and document control a security control.
  • How long, and how many? Dwell time against execution time. When the ratio is in the hundreds, the failure was visibility, not speed.
  • What breaks or reveals each link? A control per link, classified honestly by what you hold, what is cheap, and what needs investment - concentrating where the links are many and the time is long.
  • What must survive if prevention fails? Independent safety systems, verified offline backups, and the practised ability to run manually.

The arithmetic that should change how you feel about this. The attacker must succeed at every link. You need to succeed at one. Eleven links, and any single break severs the chain before the plant is touched - which is why an early, cheap break in Stage 1 is worth more than an expensive one at the end. This is not an argument for complacency. It is the reason the work is worth doing at all.

One honest boundary, and it is not negotiable. You are analysing an attack to derive defences for an asset owner. You are not designing the security architecture, not writing the incident response plan, and above all not learning or teaching how to attack anything. Every link in your report says what the adversary achieved and what would have stopped them - never how it was done. No tools, no exploits, no code, no equipment models, no vulnerability identifiers. The test is simple: your report must let a board authorise the right defensive investment, and must not let anyone replicate a single step.

Does it scale? A burglary is not the moment a window breaks. It is weeks of someone learning which house is empty on Thursdays, where the dog sleeps, which gate is never locked - and then four minutes of taking things. Every alarm you own is fitted to the window. Nothing you own notices the watching. And the watching is where all the time is, which means it is also where all your opportunity is.

T4: Your Turn

A published analysis describes an attack on a district heating utility. Summarised, without method:

  • An account belonging to a maintenance contractor was used to reach the corporate network. First activity is dated 8 March.
  • Over the following months the adversary reached a document server and accessed control narratives, network diagrams and a configuration backup.
  • A remote-access route installed for vendor support, and never reviewed after commissioning, was used to reach the control network.
  • Operator display configuration and control logic were read.
  • A modification specific to this plant was prepared and tested against the recovered configuration.
  • On 19 November the modification was delivered and executed over approximately 5 hours, changing pump operation while displays showed normal values.
  • The utility had firewalls, antivirus, and an annual security assurance letter from its control system vendor.
  1. Reconstruct. Lay the chain out as links, and assign each to Stage 1 or Stage 2. State each as an achievement.
  2. Learning step. Identify every link that existed only so the adversary could learn this specific plant, and say what that implies for the utility's document control.
  3. Measure. Compute the dwell time from 8 March to 19 November in days and hours, and the ratio to the 5-hour execution. State what the ratio tells you.
  4. Derive. For each link, give a control that would have broken or revealed it, and classify each as already-held, cheap, or real investment.
  5. Survive. Name what must still hold at this utility if prevention fails, and say what you make of that annual assurance letter.

The test of a real answer: three ways to get this wrong, and the first is the reason this unit exists.

If every defence you propose sits at the perimeter, you have defended the entry and left eight months untouched. Ask of your list: which of these would have noticed somebody reading the control narratives in June?

Second, the vendor's assurance letter is not an audit. The owner should audit the system independently - especially third-party vendor access points - and cannot rely solely on the views and guidance of the party that designed, built or sold the system. Note which link in this chain was a vendor remote-access route nobody reviewed after commissioning, and then consider that security is largely decided at procurement and design, because control system components have very long lives and what is installed tends to stay.

Third, if your derived defences carry no affordability judgement, you have written a wish list. A utility that cannot fund any of your eleven controls is exactly as exposed as it was before you wrote the report.

T5: The Guardrails

Attack analyses go wrong in ways that feel like diligence, and one that feels like relief.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Defending only the perimeter. Every control fitted to the entry, none to the months of quiet reading that follow it.

• Assuming a segregated network is an isolated one. Segregation is a design intention; the honest question is whether any route exists, including the ones installed for support and never reviewed.

• Treating a vendor's assurance as an audit. The owner audits independently, especially third-party access. Assurance from the party that sold you the system is not verification of it.

• Forgetting that engineering documentation is a target. Drawings, narratives, logic and configuration are the source material an adversary needs to damage your process rather than just any process.

• Measuring security by prevention alone. Two hundred and fourteen days were a detection opportunity. Prevention has one chance; detection had thousands of hours.

• Assuming safety systems are safe because they are separate boxes. Check the path, not the label. Independence means it cannot be reached from the same place.

• Backups that are online, or never tested. Anything reachable can be altered, and an untested backup is a hope with a filename.

• No manual fallback, or one nobody has practised. The plan to run manually is worthless if it is first attempted on the worst day.

• Deriving defences with no affordability judgement. An unfundable list changes nothing, and it lets everyone feel the work was done.

• Reading an attack analysis, feeling relief because your plant is different, and changing nothing. This is the failure this technique exists to prevent, and it is the most common one by a distance.

Onalenna's defences were sound and they were all fitted to the door, while the attack she was reading about was mostly seven months of quiet reading. Reconstruct the chain in two stages and state each link as an achievement, never a method; find the links that existed only so somebody could learn your specific plant, and recognise that this makes your engineering documentation a target and your document control a security control; count the links and the dwell time, because a ratio in the hundreds tells you the failure was visibility rather than speed; derive a control per link and classify honestly what you can afford; and decide now what must survive if prevention fails - independent safety systems, verified offline backups, and the practised ability to run by hand. The attacker must succeed at every link. You need only break one. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, find out two things about your own plant: where the control narratives are stored, and whether anyone would know if they were read.
