We inspected that vessel fourteen months ago and passed it, and now it is leaking from a crack that must have been there - so did somebody miss it, or was our procedure never able to find it?

T1: The Ache

The V-201 separator at Adendorff Gas Processing is weeping from a crack in a nozzle weld. It was found this morning by an operator who noticed frost where there should not be frost. Nobody was hurt. The unit is down and the plant is losing about R400,000 a day.

The inspection file is open on the table. Fourteen months ago that weld was scanned and passed. The report is thorough - technique sheet, calibration records, images, signatures.

The metallurgist has worked backwards from the growth rate. Fourteen months ago, that crack was about 2 millimetres tall. It was there, in the weld, on the day of the inspection.

So the room has one question, and it is already being asked in the corridor in a way you do not like: did somebody miss it?

The technician who did that scan is in the building. She has fifteen years of doing this work carefully, and she has read the same growth-rate calculation everybody else has.

And here is what stops you: you have no way to answer. Not "you are not sure" - you have no method that could answer it, even in principle. Nobody has ever measured what size of flaw this procedure can actually find. Everyone has assumed it finds what is there. That assumption has never been a number, it has never been tested, and until it is, "did we miss it?" is not a question anybody in that room can settle - which means it will be settled by whoever speaks with most confidence.

We passed it, and it was there. How do I find out what my inspection procedure can actually detect - so that "did we miss it?" becomes a question with an answer instead of an accusation?

Before you read on, decide what you would do this week. One leaking vessel, one thorough report from fourteen months ago, one technician with fifteen years of careful work, and a crack that was there. Write down what you would actually do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five responses this moment produces, and what each one can establish

• "Review the inspection report in detail." Necessary, and it can only tell you whether the procedure was followed. It was. A perfectly executed procedure that cannot find a 2 mm flaw produces exactly this report.

• "Re-train the technicians." Reaches for the person, because the person is the visible part. It also assumes the answer to the question that has not been asked yet, and it costs a fifteen-year technician her standing on the strength of an assumption.

• "Inspect more often." May well be right, and might equally be pouring money into repeating something that cannot find what you are looking for. Twice as many scans that cannot detect a 2 mm flaw find no more 2 mm flaws.

• "Buy better equipment." The instinct when a method is doubted, and it changes the tool while leaving the question about the whole procedure - access, settings, time, technique sheet, person - exactly where it was.

• "Nothing is perfect; some things get missed." True, and it ends the conversation without producing anything anybody can act on next month.

• What every one of these has in common: each one acts on a guess about how good the procedure is, and none of them measures it. That measurement is possible, it is ordinary, and it produces a single number - the flaw size this procedure reliably finds. With it, "did we miss it?" is arithmetic. Without it, it is whoever is most senior in the room.

T2: The Tool in Action

Take specimens with flaws whose true size you already know. Run the real procedure on them, with the real people, blind. Plot what was found against what was actually there - keeping every miss. Then read off the size the procedure finds nine times in ten, and be honest about the uncertainty in that reading. Watch it run at Adendorff.

Step 1 - Get a set of specimens with flaws whose true size you already know

Lerato Maluleke has thirty-eight specimens made from offcuts of the same 25 mm carbon steel, with fatigue cracks grown into representative welds at heights measured by sectioning: eleven between 0.3 and 1.0 mm, sixteen between 1.0 and 2.5 mm because that is where she expects the answer to live, seven above 2.5 mm, and four with nothing in them at all. The four blanks are not padding - without them she can measure what the procedure finds and never what it wrongly calls a flaw.

Step 2 - Run the real procedure, with the real people, blind

The specimens are numbered randomly and the blanks mixed in. Two technicians who normally do this work scan them with the same equipment and the same procedure sheet used on the plant, at the same gain settings, in a working bay rather than a laboratory. Neither is told anything. Lerato deliberately does not run any of them herself: she wrote the procedure, and a measurement of how well its author performs it would be a measurement of nobody who will ever be on that scaffold.

Step 3 - Plot what was found against what was actually there

Below about 0.8 mm almost nothing is found. Above about 2 mm, everything is. Between them the results scatter - a 1.4 mm crack found by one technician and missed by the other, a 1.1 mm found and a 1.6 mm missed. Lerato keeps every miss on the chart, including two that are uncomfortable. The scatter is not sloppiness; it is what detection actually looks like, and a procedure that showed a clean cut-off would mean the specimens were too easy.

Step 4 - Read the size you can honestly claim, and be honest about the confidence too

The fitted curve reaches nine-in-ten detection at about 1.0 mm. But thirty-eight specimens is thirty-eight, not infinity, so the defensible figure once the uncertainty of that sample is carried through is 1.2 mm. Lerato writes the number that will go on the procedure: this procedure reliably detects flaws of 1.2 mm and above, established on 38 specimens, with two technicians, blind. Not 1.0 mm, which the curve alone would have allowed her to claim.

Step 5 - Put the number where the decisions are made

The leaking vessel's crack was about 2 mm fourteen months ago. The procedure's number is 1.2 mm. So it should have been found, and the honest finding is not that a technician was careless - it is that the access on that nozzle was poor, the scan was done from one side, and the procedure sheet permits that. The sheet is changed. On a second vessel the same study lands at 3.5 mm, and there the finding is the opposite: a 2 mm flaw was never going to be found by anybody, so the inspection interval, not the inspector, is what has to change. Two plants, two answers, and neither could be reached at all without the number.

See what the number did to the corridor conversation. The procedure reliably finds 1.2 mm; the crack was 2 mm; so it should have been found - and the finding is not that anybody was careless, it is that the access on that nozzle allowed a single-sided scan and the procedure sheet permitted it. That sheet is now changed, which no amount of re-training would have achieved. And on the second vessel the same study answered the opposite way: 3.5 mm, so a 2 mm flaw was never going to be found by anyone, and it is the interval rather than the inspector that has to move. Notice what the measurement protects. Before it, a fifteen-year technician's standing rested on which explanation the room found most comfortable. After it, the procedure is accountable and she is not - and that is the same finding that gets the plant safe, not a softer alternative to it.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the second is the one that is quietly hardest to do properly.

  • 1. Specimens with known flaw sizes, same material and geometry, crowded where you expect the limit to be - plus some with nothing in them.
  • 2. Run the real procedure, real people, blind. Not the author, not an expert, not a laboratory.
  • 3. Plot found against actual, keeping every miss on the chart.
  • 4. Read the size found nine times in ten, then carry the uncertainty of a finite sample and read the defensible figure.
  • 5. Put it where the decisions are - what you are guarding against, and how often you must look.

This is a probability of detection study, and now that you have run one its vocabulary is just names for what you did. The curve you plotted is the PoD curve: flaw size along the bottom, the chance of finding it up the side. The size at which it reaches nine-in-ten is a90. The smaller, defensible figure you read after carrying the uncertainty of a finite number of specimens is a90/95 - nine times in ten, with ninety-five per cent confidence given how many specimens you actually tested - and it is the number that goes on the procedure, because it is the only one that survives someone asking how sure you are. The specimens with no flaw in them measure your false-call rate, which matters because a procedure that cries wolf gets ignored on the day it is right. And the region where results scatter has a name too - the transition region - and it is where the study must be crowded, because a study made only of easy flaws produces a beautiful curve about nothing.

It fits any inspection or detection procedure whose findings people act on - weld inspection, medical screening, quality sampling, fraud detection, security screening. Two limits travel with the number. It describes the procedure that was run, on those specimens, by those people - change the access, the equipment or the pressure and you have a different procedure, whose number you do not know. And it says nothing about whether a flaw matters: it tells you what you can find, never what you can tolerate.

T4: Your Turn

Take an inspection or check you rely on - a weld scan, a quality sample, a screening step, an audit, a review. Ask the question that starts everything: what size of problem does this reliably catch, and how does anybody know? Then build the smallest honest version of the study - a set of known cases including some clean ones, run blind by the people who normally do it, plotted with the misses kept in.

Two disciplines decide whether the number means anything. First, the person who wrote the procedure must not be the person who performs it in the study, and the specimens must be blind. It is a small, awkward thing to insist on and it is the whole measurement: an author who knows where the flaws are will find them, and will produce a number that describes a situation nobody will ever be in. Second, keep every miss on the chart, especially the ones that are embarrassing. The temptation to explain away a missed flaw - poor access that day, an unusual geometry - is the temptation to measure an imaginary procedure. Those conditions are part of what you are measuring; if they are common enough to happen in a study, they are common enough to happen on the plant.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your number honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• An unmeasured procedure has no detection capability - it has an assumption. "We inspect it annually" describes effort. It says nothing about what would be found.

• Blind, or it is not a measurement. Anyone who knows which specimens carry flaws will find them. That includes you, and it especially includes whoever wrote the procedure.

• Crowd the specimens where it gets hard. A study made of large, easy flaws produces a confident curve that describes nothing you were worried about.

• Keep the misses, and the blanks. Removing an inconvenient miss measures a procedure that does not exist. Leaving out the flaw-free specimens hides how often the procedure cries wolf.

• Quote the defensible figure, not the flattering one. The curve alone will let you claim a smaller flaw than your specimen count can support. The number that survives being challenged is the one to publish.

• The number belongs to that procedure, not to the technique. Different access, equipment, pressure or people is a different procedure with an unknown number. Do not carry a figure across to a job it was never measured on.

• If the number is worse than what the rules reject, say so - that sentence is the whole reason you measured. A procedure that reliably finds 1.2 mm, working to a code that rejects flaws above 1.0 mm, leaves a band of flaws the code calls unacceptable and the method may not see. There are two honest responses: sharpen the procedure until the gap closes, or declare the residual risk in the open and have it engineered around. There is no third. A signature over an undeclared gap tells everyone working near that vessel that they are protected against something they are not.

• Measure the procedure, not the person. This study exists precisely so that a failure can be traced to access, settings or a technique sheet rather than landing on whoever happened to hold the probe. Used to allocate blame, it will end the honest reporting of misses, and then it measures nothing at all.

You stood over an inspection file that was thorough, correct, and fourteen months old, with a crack that had been there and a question circling the corridor that nobody could answer. What you can put on the table instead is a number: this procedure reliably finds 1.2 mm, on thirty-eight specimens, run blind by two technicians - so a 2 mm crack should have been found, and the reason it was not is a technique sheet that permits single-sided access on that nozzle. This is how "did we miss it?" stops being an accusation looking for somewhere to land, and becomes a question with an answer. The weld gets fixed either way. What the number decides is whether the right thing gets fixed with it. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one procedure you rely on and design its smallest honest study together.)
