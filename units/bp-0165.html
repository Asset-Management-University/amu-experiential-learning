Movement 1 - The Simulation

It is a Tuesday evening, and you are sitting at the back table of the Tank You depot in Athlone, the one near the filling bay. The roller door is half down. You can still smell the faint, sharp tang of LPG that never quite leaves the place. Muhammad is across from you with his laptop open. Between you, printed on a single sheet, is an email from a man called Riaan at a small investment firm in the city. You have read it four times.

Riaan likes the business. He has used your service himself. He wants to put R2 million into Tank You to help you open two new depots and buy a second filling rig. But the email ends with one line that has kept you both quiet for ten minutes: "Send me your valuation and the equity you're offering, and let's talk terms on Thursday."

Thursday is the day after tomorrow.

You and Muhammad started this with your parents' car and a few cylinders. Now you run a small fleet, your own filling plant, and you did R4.8 million in sales last year, with about R720,000 left over as real profit after everything was paid. You are proud of that. But "valuation" is a word you have only heard in podcasts. What is the whole business worth? Not what you feel about it - a number you can say to Riaan's face and defend.

Muhammad turns the laptop toward you. He has typed one figure into a slide, big and bold: R12 million. "I read that tech companies sell for ten times their profit or more," he says. "So twelve feels safe. Maybe we should say fifteen."

You look at the number. Something in your chest tightens. You want to believe it. If the business is worth R12 million, then Riaan's R2 million buys him only a sixth of Tank You, and you keep the rest. That feels good. That feels like winning.

But then you think about Thursday. Riaan has done this many times. You have done it never. If you walk in and say "twelve million" and he asks one simple question - why? - what do you actually have? A number a friend liked. A feeling. A figure copied from a podcast about a software company that has never touched a gas cylinder.

You know how Riaan works, a little. A mutual friend told you he is kind but sharp, and that he respects founders who have done their homework and quietly loses interest in the ones who pluck numbers from the air. If your twelve million falls apart under one question, you do not just lose the argument. You lose his belief that you are serious people. And R2 million is real money - it is the second filling rig, it is the Bellville depot, it is two more drivers with steady work.

The other danger is the opposite one. If you panic and let Riaan name the number, he might say "the business is worth R4 million" and offer R2 million for half of everything you built. Give away half of Tank You on a Tuesday's bad guess, and you can never get it back.

Muhammad is watching your face. "So? Twelve? Fifteen?" The cursor blinks on the slide. Outside, a taxi hoots on Klipfontein Road.

You have R720,000 of yearly profit, R4.8 million of sales, a real fleet, a filling plant, loyal customers, and a name people trust. You have two days. The blank space on the slide is waiting for a number - but more than the number, it is waiting for the reason behind it.

Where do you start? What would you need to work out before you could put any figure on that slide and defend it on Thursday?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Before you read how Ibraaheem and Muhammad worked this out, stop and try it yourself. This is the part that builds the skill - give it a real go first.

Imagine you have those two days. Think it through:

  1. Muhammad's R12 million came from a feeling and a podcast. List Tank You's actual figures, and beside each one write the valuation method it feeds: annual profit R720,000 → an earnings multiple; the value of the fleet and filling plant it owns → asset value; any recent sale of a comparable business → the market. Self-test: a fact only "counts" if you can name the number it would let you compute. If you cannot attach a calculation to it, strike it off. You judge your own list - before any reveal - by whether every item left on it produces a rand figure.
  2. A business earns money every year. If someone hands you a machine that reliably produces R720,000 of profit a year, how might you reason about what that machine is worth paying for today?
  3. Find two reference points you can actually obtain: a recent sale price of a similar small business, and a typical earnings-multiple for the sector. Compute Tank You's value at that multiple (R720,000 x multiple). Self-test: your figure is "grounded" if it lands inside the range your two references imply. You can check this yourself - does your number fall between them? If it sits above both, it is air. That is exactly Muhammad's error.
  4. Actually run two methods on Tank You: (a) the earnings multiple on R720,000 profit, and (b) net asset value (what the fleet and filling plant are worth). Write both numbers down. Self-test built in: a defensible valuation is a range between the two (or a justified weighting), never a single magic figure. You can verify your own answer by checking that you could tell Riaan why the truth lies between the two - using the gap you just computed. You need no solution from us to know whether you produced a range with a reason.

Write down your own rough figure and, more importantly, the reasons you would give Riaan.

Then, before you read on, set your own yardstick. About your own future number, finish these two lines on paper:

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Ibraaheem did not sleep much that night. By morning he had stopped staring at Muhammad's R12 million and started somewhere else: what do we actually know for certain?

He wrote the hard facts on a fresh page. Sales last year: R4.8 million. Real profit after every cost - fuel, salaries, gas, the card-machine fees, his and Muhammad's own pay: R720,000. A fleet that runs. A filling plant they own. A growing customer list. That was the ground. Anything they claimed about Tank You had to grow out of those facts, not float above them.

Then he did the thing that turned the corner. Instead of asking "what do we feel it's worth?", he asked: "If I were Riaan, why would I hand over money at all?" The answer was plain - Riaan is not buying a fleet or a filling plant. He is buying a share of the profit those things produce, year after year. Tank You is, to an investor, a machine that reliably makes R720,000 a year and is growing. The question becomes: what is it worth to own a share of that machine?

This is the heart of it, and it has a name - and only now does the name matter, because now you can see why it exists. The work Ibraaheem was doing is called building a valuation: a defensible estimate of what a whole business is worth, built from evidence rather than feeling. And there is more than one honest road to it. Ibraaheem used two, on purpose.

But before he ran a single sum, Ibraaheem did one more thing - the thing this whole module is about. He wrote down, in advance, what he would have to see for a number to be defensible: "A number is defensible only if two methods built differently land close together, and it does not float above what real, similar businesses actually sold for. If my number sits above every comparison I can find, it is air - and I throw it out, however much it hurts." That was his success line. Now reality could answer.

Road one - value it from its earnings (the multiple method). A profitable business is commonly priced as a multiple of its yearly profit. The multiple says, roughly, "how many years of profit would a buyer pay up front to own this." A risky, unproven business earns a low multiple - maybe 2 or 3. A steady, growing, trusted one earns more. Software companies sometimes reach ten or more because they can grow almost without limit and at tiny extra cost. Tank You is not that - every new delivery needs gas, a van, and a driver. So Ibraaheem was honest. He asked a friend who buys and sells small service businesses what gas-delivery and logistics firms actually change hands for, and the answer was sobering and useful: roughly 4 to 5 times yearly profit for a healthy, growing one.

So: R720,000 profit x 4 = R2.88 million. x 5 = R3.6 million. That is the earnings story.

Road two - sanity-check it against the real market (the comparison method). Ibraaheem did not stop at one road, because a single number can fool you. He looked for what similar businesses had actually sold for - a competitor's route bought out the year before, a courier firm that changed hands. This was the cheapest honest probe he could run: two phone calls and an afternoon, no money risked, nothing he could not undo. Both landed in the same neighbourhood: a little under four times to a little over five times profit. The second road agreed with the first. That agreement is what makes a number defensible - two methods, built differently, pointing to the same place.

Both roads put Tank You at roughly R3 million to R3.6 million today. Not twelve. Not fifteen.

Here is where reality bit, and it is worth being honest about. Ibraaheem had half-hoped the trade would tell him eight times, or that the comparison sales would come in high and rescue some of Muhammad's R12 million. They did not. The surprise was not that the number was a little off - it was that their gut figure was more than three times too high, and the comparison sales, the part he had assumed would be the soft, friendly evidence, were the part that knocked the legs out from under it hardest. His success line had told him exactly what to do with that: the R12 million floated above every real comparison he could find, so by his own rule it was air, and out it went. He did not bend the rule to save the number he wanted. He changed his mind - that was the whole point of writing the line first.

It stung. But look at what it bought them. Riaan offered R2 million. If the business is worth R3.6 million today, before his money goes in, then after he invests, the whole thing is worth R3.6m + R2m = R5.6 million - and his R2 million owns R2m ÷ R5.6m ≈ 36%. (The value before the new money goes in is called the pre-money valuation; after, the post-money. Ibraaheem learned the words once he understood the idea - never the other way round.)

Thirty-six percent is a lot to give away. But here is where the homework paid off twice. Because Ibraaheem walked in with two methods that agreed, real comparison sales, and the honesty to use a logistics multiple instead of a software fantasy, he had something Muhammad's R12 million never had: the high ground. He could argue - truthfully - for the top of the range, R3.6m, even nudge above it, because Tank You is growing faster than the firms he compared it to and customers trust the sealed-cylinder promise. He could push the pre-money toward R4 million, which drops Riaan's slice to R2m ÷ R6m ≈ 33%.

That is the honest trade-off, and Ibraaheem named it to Muhammad plainly. Their gut number (R12m) would have either collapsed under Riaan's first "why?" - losing the deal and Riaan's respect - or, if Riaan had been the one to set the number after they fumbled, it could have been lower than the truth, handing away half the business. The defensible R3.6-4m felt smaller, but it was a number they could hold under pressure. A number you can defend beats a bigger number you cannot, every time.

On Thursday they did not open with a slide. They opened with the two roads, the comparison sales, and the logistics multiple. Riaan asked his "why?" - and for every part of it, they had an answer. They settled the pre-money at R3.9 million, Riaan's R2 million for about 34%, and a handshake. They had given away a third of Tank You. But they had kept two-thirds of a real business, funded the second rig and the Bellville depot, and - quietly, importantly - they had become people Riaan would back again.

Method & Finding

  • The Method (the lesson - reusable): before you commit to a valuation, write down in advance what would make it defensible (two methods agreeing; not floating above real comparison sales), then run the cheapest honest probe - ask the trade for the true multiple and find what genuinely similar things actually sold for - and obey your own rule, even when it kills the number you wanted.
  • The Finding (in this story only): Tank You's gut figure of R12m was more than three times too high; two independent roads both landed near R3-3.6m, and the comparison sales - not the maths - were what exposed the gap.
  • Your Check (how you will know in your world): the real earnings-multiple a trade buyer or broker quotes you for your kind of thing, and at least one actual recent sale price of something genuinely similar. If your number sits above both, it is air.

Do this yourself

The recipe Ibraaheem followed, stripped to the bone, is four moves:

  1. Find the ground. Write the one figure your thing reliably produces in a year - real, not hoped-for.
  2. Road one - earnings. Multiply that figure by an honest multiple for your kind of thing (ask someone in the trade; resist the software fantasy). That gives you a low end and a high end.
  3. Road two - comparison. Find what genuinely similar things have actually sold for. Do the two roads agree?
  4. Do the ownership maths. If new money comes in, the buyer's share is their money ÷ (your value + their money). Decide the number you can defend, then hold it.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You have watched Ibraaheem do it once. Now take one step with a guide beside you, before you walk on your own. This is the same deep problem - but wearing a different coat, on purpose. The disguise is what makes it good practice.

Meet Nosipho. She owns a small flat in Goodwood that she rents out for R7,200 a month - call it R72,000 a year after the months it sits empty and the small repairs. Her cousin Wesley wants to buy the flat from her, and he wants a number by the weekend. Wesley is friendly, but he is also hoping she names something low.

Nosipho opens the property app on her phone. The most eye-catching flat on her street - a renovated corner unit with a balcony and a new kitchen - is listed at R1.4 million. Her chest does the same thing yours did with the R12 million. "Mine's on the same street," she thinks. "So mine's worth about that too. I'll say R1.3 million and let Wesley talk me down a little."

Reveal ▸

Reveal. Road one - earnings. Nosipho's flat reliably produces R72,000 a year. She asks an agent what flats like hers actually sell for relative to their rent, and learns that small unrenovated flats in her area trade at roughly 12 to 15 times their yearly rent. So R72,000 x 12 = R864,000, and x 15 = R1.08 million. Road two - comparison. She ignores the glossy listing and finds what an unrenovated flat like hers, two blocks over, actually sold for last year: R950,000. The two roads agree - somewhere around R900,000 to a million - and they agree a long way below R1.3 million.

Now Nosipho has the high ground, exactly as Ibraaheem did. She can argue honestly for the top of her range, because her tenant is reliable and the building is well kept. And when Wesley asks "why?", she has two roads and a real comparison sale to point at - not a feeling about the corner unit.

The deep-structure lesson. A flat is not a gas business, and the multiples are different - but the problem is the same one underneath. A number built from what your thing reliably earns, and cross-checked against an honest comparison, will defend itself. A number built from the most flattering thing in sight will not. The flattering comparison is the trap in every version of this problem; spotting it is the skill.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The deep shape of this problem is not about gas or about Riaan. It is this: someone is about to put a number on something you built or own, and if you cannot defend a number of your own, theirs wins. That pattern is everywhere. Find it in your world.

  1. Think of something you might one day sell, raise money against, or hand over a share of - a side business, a freelance practice, a plot of land, a stake in a family enterprise, even your own paid time. If someone offered to buy a piece of it tomorrow, what number would you give, and - be honest - would it come from evidence or from a feeling?
  2. Pick that thing and find its "R720,000". What does it reliably produce - money, savings, value - in a year? Write the real figure, not the hoped-for one. That number is the ground everything else grows from.
  3. Now find your "logistics multiple, not software fantasy". What do similar things actually sell for in your world - comparable businesses, similar properties, the going rate for your kind of skill? Resist the most flattering comparison. Hunt for the honest one.
  4. Build two roads, the way Ibraaheem did - one from what it earns, one from what similar things sold for - and see if they agree. If they clash badly, that gap is telling you something. What is it?
  5. Name the "Riaan" in your situation - the buyer, the bank, the investor, the relative with the cheque. What single "why?" would they ask that would expose a number you plucked from the air? Prepare the answer before they ask.

Honest transfer: your world is not a gas-delivery business, and the exact multiples will differ - a flat, a software product, and a hair salon are each valued by their own customs. But the problem-type is identical: build a defensible number from evidence and two agreeing methods, before someone else builds it for you. Take one of the prompts above into the AI facilitator and work your own case through, with your own real numbers, until you have a figure you could defend on a Thursday.

Run the smallest test on your Test Card this week: make the one phone call that gets you a real multiple or a real comparison sale for your thing - and here is how you will know it worked: you will be able to say a number and the evidence behind it in the same breath. Then come back and close the loop out loud: "I predicted ___, reality (the multiple, the comparison sale) did ___, so I now think ___." That is the moment reality, not us, becomes your teacher.
