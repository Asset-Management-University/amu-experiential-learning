Busy all day - on the wrong things?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and Tank You is growing fast - which means every day is a fire to be fought. Your phone never stops. An angry customer, a driver who wants something, a rattling panel, a supplier chasing payment - you race from one to the next, putting out whatever flares up loudest, and you fall into bed each night completely spent, having handled it all.

And still, underneath the exhaustion, there is a cold knot of dread you cannot explain. A feeling that for all your running, something you have not even looked at could bring the whole thing down.

Then it nearly does. Sipho is driving the busiest bakkie when a child darts into the road. He stamps the brakes - and they are soft. No bite, no warning. The bakkie slides, and stops with maybe an arm's length to spare. Sipho phones you, his voice shaking: "Boss... the brakes on that one. There was no warning at all. We nearly had a tragedy today."

The brakes never rattled. Never squealed. They made no noise at all - so they never made your list. And they almost killed a child. It lands on you like cold water:

I work flat out on whatever shouts loudest - but the things that could truly destroy me make no noise. How do I fight the RIGHT fire, not just the loudest one?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Be gentle with yourself for a moment: handling the loudest thing first is what everyone does. The shouting stops when you deal with it, so it feels like progress. It is the most natural habit in the world - and the brakes just showed you its fatal flaw. Loud is not the same as dangerous.

Before you read on, think about that night Muhammad couldn't sleep. He has one list of everything pulling at him. If "loudest first" is the wrong way to sort it, what would be a better way? What questions would have pushed the brakes to the top?

I handle whatever shouts loudest - the angry call, the latest breakdown. It feels productive because the shouting stops. But the brakes never shouted, and they nearly killed someone. Maybe loudness is the wrong way to choose.
What if I judged each thing not by how loud it is, but by two cooler questions: how badly would it hurt me if it went wrong, and how close is it to going wrong? The brakes would have screamed to the top of THAT list.
So I rank everything by those two questions and spend my time and money down that list - loud or quiet. And I go LOOKING for the silent, dangerous things on purpose, because the worst ones never announce themselves.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

That sleepless night, Muhammad stops sorting by loudness and builds a better sorter - two cool questions he can ask of anything competing for his time and money:

  • First, forgive the 'loudest-first' habit: serving whatever shouts loudest is the normal human default, not a flaw - the noise stops when you attend to it, so it FEELS productive. Everyone does it. You don't need more discipline; you need a better sorter.
  • Question 1 - how badly would it hurt us?: if this goes wrong, how bad is the damage? In plain words, not numbers: Annoying / Costly / Business-ending / Life-ending.
  • Question 2 - how close to the edge is it?: how likely is it to fail soon? Whenever / Months away / Right on the edge.
  • Rank by both - then spend down the list: what is BOTH high-damage AND close-to-the-edge wins your scarce time and money FIRST - even if it is silent and nobody is asking about it.
  • Hunt the silent killers on purpose: the most dangerous things usually make no noise. Once a week, ask out loud: what important thing is NOBODY mentioning? (insurance, a contract ending, a worn critical part.)
  • Then find the courage: protecting a quiet, vital thing means telling a loud, lesser one 'later'. Say it calmly and kindly - but say it.

Here is the quiet power of it: loudness measures how much something is bothering you right now. These two questions measure how much actually depends on it. They are not the same - and the gap between them is exactly where disasters hide. The brakes scored near-zero on loudness and near-maximum on these two. That gap nearly cost a life.

The arithmetic is nothing - you can do it in your head with plain words. The hard part is the last two moves: deliberately hunting for the silent, dangerous thing nobody is mentioning, and finding the courage to make a loud, lesser thing wait while you handle it. That is what separates someone who is merely busy from someone who is actually in control.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad takes that night's list and runs it through the two questions instead of by who shouted.

See the list re-sorted - and the silent killer it caught next

Sorted by LOUDNESS (his old way): 1) Angry customer (refund row); 2) Driver wants new seat covers; 3) Rattling side panel; 4) ...the brakes weren't even on the list.

Sorted by the TWO QUESTIONS:

• Brakes on the busy bakkie - hurt? Life-ending. Edge? Right on it. → #1, by a mile.

• Angry customer - hurt? Annoying (a small refund). Edge? Happening now. → handle briefly, later.

• Rattling panel - hurt? Not at all. Edge? Months. → whenever.

• Seat covers - hurt? Not at all. Edge? Whenever. → whenever.

Then he hunts on purpose - "what else is silent?" He asks Ibraaheem: "When does our accident insurance renew?" Ibraaheem checks, goes pale: "Boss... it lapsed last Tuesday." For a week, one crash like Sipho's near-miss would have ended everything. Silent. Top of the list. Renewed that hour.

And the courage: the angry customer phones mid-inspection. The old Muhammad would have dropped everything. The new one says, calmly: "I hear you, and I'll sort it properly this afternoon - right now I'm pulling every bakkie in for a safety check." A loud thing told, kindly, to wait.

For the engineers: this is exactly how a plant decides where its limited inspection budget goes - the same two questions (consequence x likelihood) laid on a grid for hundreds of assets. A bakkie or a refinery: rank by what depends on it, not by what's shouting. (One nuance: used FAST, this is triage - what to handle this week. The very same two questions, applied SLOWLY across an asset's whole life, become its maintenance plan - that is strategy, a tool for another day.)

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You weren't lazy or careless - you were besieged, using the only sorter most people ever learn. The brakes taught you a better one, and it gave you back something precious: control. This works on any crowded, demanding life:

  1. List what's pulling at you - then ignore how loud each one is.
  2. Ask the two questions of each: how badly would it hurt me if it went wrong, and how close is it to going wrong? Rank by both, and spend your time and money down that list.
  3. Hunt the silent ones, and protect them. Once a week ask "what important thing is nobody mentioning?" - and have the courage to make a loud, lesser thing wait.
R

Name your silent, high-stakes thing - and do something about it this week, before it has to shout. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

The loudest wheel gets the grease - but the broken axle is silent. The people who stay safe and get ahead are not the ones who run fastest at the noise. They are the ones who learned to find the quiet thing that matters most, and put it first.
