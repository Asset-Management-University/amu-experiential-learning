Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Kofi, and your printing business is your life's work. A supplier has shown you a new press that could change everything, and they hand you the business case on a single, crisp sheet. The numbers look perfect: it will cost exactly R200,000, be installed and running in 8 weeks, and save you R50,000 a year in ink and time. They are so precise, so confident, that it feels like a fact. You shake hands, sign, and commit your savings and a bank loan to the plan.

Then reality arrives. The R200,000 becomes R300,000 after "unforeseen" wiring and setup. The 8 weeks stretch to 16 while you wait for a specialist part. And when it's finally running, the saving is closer to R30,000 a year, because the new ink costs more than they mentioned. Your careful plan is broken, the loan repayments don't care, and in a quiet, sinking moment you realise it: the person who gave you that confident number got their commission the day you said yes - not the day it came true.

The number was so confident and so precise - so why did reality blow straight through it, and how do I judge the next big number before I bet on it?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You trusted the number because it was precise - and that's easy to do; "R198,550" feels more real than "about R200,000", even though both can be guesses. But precision is not accuracy: a confident guess is still a guess. Maybe next time you'll haggle it down - get R200,000 to R190,000 and feel you've won; but you've only bargained on a single brittle point, and the first surprise still shatters it. Or you swing the other way and decide you'll never trust a forecast again, and freeze - but you can't run a business on a shrug; the answer isn't no numbers, it's numbers you can actually build on.

The single, confident number came from someone rewarded for you believing it. So before you read on: what three things would a number you could actually trust have to show you?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

A single confident number is a guess wearing a suit. So don't ask "what is the number?" - ask three better questions: what's the range, what does it hinge on, and how did the last ones really turn out? Five moves:

  • See the trap - one confident point is brittle: a single cost, a single date, a single benefit looks strong but it's fragile - it has no give. The first ordinary problem (a late delivery, a price rise) breaks it, because a point has no room built in. Life is messy; a plan that's a single number has nowhere to absorb the mess, so the first surprise is also the last straw.
  • Find the incentive that bends it: ask: who is rewarded for this number being BELIEVED, rather than being TRUE? The salesperson on commission, the manager who wants their project approved. This isn't about them being bad people - it's a pressure that bends every forecast the same hopeful way. Once you see the incentive, you know which way the wind is blowing on the number.
  • Demand a range, not a point: ask for three numbers - a worst case, a likely case, a best case - because the single figure you were handed is almost always the optimistic one. The real question becomes: can your plan survive the WORST end? If the press cost R350,000 and saved only R20,000 a year, would you go under? A plan that still works at the bad end of the range is a plan that can actually hold.
  • Test what it hinges on: a few key assumptions hold up the whole forecast - find them, then stress them. Ask 'what does this number depend on to be true?' (the price of a material, a key worker being free, how fast your team learns the new skill), then 'what if that's wrong - what if it takes twice as long?'. The assumptions that swing the answer most are the ones to watch like a hawk, because that's where the plan will actually break.
  • Correct from the track record: ask the killer question: 'for the last three projects like this you did, what was the original forecast for cost and time, and what was the final?' Reality is the best teacher - if such projects always ran 50% over and took twice as long, that's your reality check; adjust the hopeful number by that real history. And start your OWN record: write each forecast down, then what actually happened beside it - your numbers get honest, fast.

See how it would have changed everything for Kofi. Instead of one figure, he asks for a range - and learns the press could cost up to R350,000 and save as little as R20,000 a year; he asks what the quote hinges on, and finds it assumes ink prices hold and his team learns the machine in a fortnight; and he asks the killer question - "how did your last three installations actually compare to their quotes?" - and hears they ran about 50% over. Now he isn't betting on a hope; he's deciding with his eyes open, on numbers he can survive. A single confident number is a guess wearing a suit - so ask who's rewarded for you believing it, demand a range and check you can survive the worst, test the few assumptions it hinges on, and correct it from how the last ones really turned out. A range, its weak points, and a dose of reality turn a brittle bet into a plan built to hold.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a printing press - any confident number for a big commitment hides the same risk. Watch a home renovation.

See the same three questions on a builder's confident quote

A builder gives you a confident quote: "R150,000, and you'll be in by Christmas." You plan your whole life around it. But the real cost is R220,000 and you're still eating takeaways in April. The three questions would have changed the bet:

• Range: "What's the worst this could cost if we hit problems?" - "If we have to rewire the whole house, maybe R250,000." Now you know the real exposure and can decide if you can carry it.
• Hinges: "What does the quote assume?" - "That timber prices hold and my plasterer is free in March." Now you know exactly what to watch.
• Track record: "Could I speak to your last two clients - how did their final bill compare to the first quote?" That answer tells you more than the quote itself.

Same guess in a suit, same three questions - and a decision you can actually survive.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of a big decision you face that rests on a single confident number - a supplier's quote, a projection for a new idea, a budget, a promised timeline. Don't just accept it; treat it as a guess in a suit:

  1. Ask who's rewarded for you believing it. A salesperson on commission? A manager who wants the project? Your own hopeful optimism? Once you see the incentive, you know which way the number is bent.
  2. Demand a range, and survive the worst. Push for worst / likely / best, not one figure - and check your plan still holds at the bad end, because that's the end reality often delivers.
  3. Test the hinges, then check the track record. Find the few assumptions it depends on and ask "what if you're wrong?"; then ask how similar past efforts actually turned out, and adjust by that real history.

Trusting a precise, confident number was never gullibility - we are wired to find precision reassuring, and the person across the table sounded so sure. The only thing missing was a habit: three questions that turn a lone figure into something you can actually weigh. And keep your own record too - write down each forecast, then what really happened beside it, and your own numbers get honest fast. A single number hides the risk; a range, its weak points, and a dose of reality let you see it - so the plan you choose isn't one that merely hopes to work, but one built to hold for the people who are counting on you.
