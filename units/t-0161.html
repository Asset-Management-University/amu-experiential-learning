Step 1 of 5

Two true trends, pulling against each other

T1: The Ache

The town is growing at two per cent a year, and the question on the table is simple: how much treatment capacity will Corathyl Water Treatment Works need in twenty years?

You are Onalenna, and there is an obvious answer. Twenty years of two per cent growth is roughly half as many people again, so build half as much again. Every utility you know would sign that.

But the meter records are open on your desk, and they say something else. Per-capita consumption has been falling for a decade - quietly, steadily - because the town is metered now, because the big leaks were found, because the fittings people buy today use less than the ones they replaced. The town is growing. Each person in it is using less.

Two trends. Both true. Pulling against each other.

And you have to give one number. If you project the total you have watched grow, you may commission a works the town never needs - paid for, through tariffs, by the very people it was built to serve, including the ones who can least afford it. If you lean too hard on the fall, you run short of water in a growing town, and that is not a budget problem, it is families without water.

Nobody has ever asked you to separate the two. They have only ever asked you for the number.

My demand has two true trends running in opposite directions and I have been asked for a single number twenty years out. How do I build a forecast I can actually justify - and hand over something more useful than a guess?

Before you read on, decide. You have been asked for one number: what the town will need in twenty years. Inside that total, two things are true at once and both are well evidenced — the population is growing steadily, and consumption per person is falling as fittings, meters and repairs improve. The total has therefore looked almost flat for years. If you lean on the growth you build capacity the town pays for through tariffs, and the people least able to afford it pay it too. If you lean on the fall you run short of water in a growing town, which is not a budget problem but families without water. Nobody has ever asked you to separate the two. Write down what you would give them — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why a total cannot be extrapolated

• “Extend the historical total — it has been flat, so project flat.” This extrapolates an accident. The total is flat because two fast-moving things happen to be cancelling, and neither has any obligation to keep cancelling for twenty years.

• “Take the growth trend and add a safety margin.” Chooses one of the two true trends and calls the other a rounding error. It is also the expensive direction, and the cost lands on the people you exist to serve.

• “Give a range — best case to worst case.” Honest, and it hands back the problem: nobody can size a treatment works from a range, so somebody will pick a point inside it for reasons you did not supply.

• “Use the regional planning figure.” Borrows somebody else's assumptions without inheriting their reasoning, and you will be unable to defend the number when it is questioned — or to notice when its assumptions stop holding.

• “Nobody can forecast twenty years — give them the number they want.” True about precision and wrong about the task. A twenty-year forecast is not a prediction; it is a structure for a decision that must be made now.

• So stop forecasting the total, because a total is a result, not a cause, and drivers moving in opposite directions hide inside it perfectly. Forecast the drivers separately and recombine at the end — population one way, use per person the other — which makes the opposing movements visible, makes each separately arguable, and makes the whole thing defensible because its assumptions are exposed rather than buried in a trend line. Two disciplines. Things that behave differently must be modelled differently: a large connection arriving as an occasional step must never be smoothed into a trend, since a smoothed step is wrong in both directions at once. And forecast the quantity the decision is actually sized on — capacity is set by the hardest day, not the typical one, so an average-day figure sizes nothing until it is converted, using a factor from your own records rather than a textbook. What you hand over is then not a number but a structure whose assumptions can be revisited as the years resolve them.

You stop forecasting the total. Here is what you do instead.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the twenty-year service demand forecast - the same town, with the drivers pulled apart.

1. What exactly are you forecasting? (and which demand sizes the works)

Name the service, the unit, the horizon and the decision this forecast informs - then name WHICH DEMAND, because the word hides a trap. AVERAGE-DAY DEMAND (ADD) is what the town uses on a typical day. MAXIMUM-DAY DEMAND (MDD) is what it uses on its hardest day, and infrastructure is sized on the PEAK, never the average. The PEAKING FACTOR (MDD/ADD) converts one into the other, and it comes from your own records, not a textbook - at Corathyl it is 1.6. So a forecast expressed in average day sizes nothing at all until it is converted. Here: average-day potable demand for the town Corathyl serves, over 20 years, to decide whether and when to commit a treatment-capacity expansion. Current ADD is 18 ML/d.

2. What is the total actually made of? (decompose, never extrapolate)

Never extrapolate the total, because a total is a RESULT, not a cause - and drivers can move in opposite directions and hide each other inside it. Decompose: RESIDENTIAL DEMAND = POPULATION x PER-CAPITA CONSUMPTION. Here 18 ML/d over 100,000 people is 180 LITRES PER PERSON PER DAY. Then add the NON-RESIDENTIAL blocks - industrial and institutional connections - separately, because they behave differently: they arrive as STEP CHANGES when a plant connects, not as smooth trends, and a step must never be modelled as a trend or a trend as a step. Now you have three things to project on three kinds of evidence, instead of one line to extend with a ruler and a hope.

3. What is the evidence for each driver? (demographic, economic, policy)

Each driver gets its own evidence, its own source and its own stated VINTAGE, because a projection made before the last census carries assumptions you are silently inheriting. DEMOGRAPHIC evidence sets population: 2.0 per cent a year, from the regional projection, whose own assumptions about migration you must read and record. ECONOMIC evidence sets the non-residential blocks: which connections are committed, which are speculative, and when. POLICY evidence sets PER-CAPITA - metering, leak reduction, tariff structure, fittings standards - and THIS IS THE DRIVER EVERYONE GETS WRONG. The documented finding is blunt: suppliers generally projected stable or increasing per-capita demand while actual per-capita demand FELL by about 1.9 per cent a year over two decades, and that under-accounting of efficiency gains explains much of the error in their forecasts. Corathyl's own meter records show the same fall. We will assume 1.0 per cent a year - deliberately CONSERVATIVE against the observed 1.9 - because a forecast should not bet on the best case of the very trend it has just discovered.

4. What happens when the drivers fight? (compute both ways, then convert)

Compute it BOTH ways, because the gap between them is the lesson. Population over 20 years: 100,000 x 1.02^20 = 148,595. THE NAIVE FORECAST holds per-capita flat at 180 L: 148,595 x 180 = 26.7 ML/d, up 49 per cent - build half as much again. THE DECOMPOSED FORECAST lets per-capita fall 1.0 per cent a year to 180 x 0.99^20 = 147.2 L: 148,595 x 147.2 = 21.9 ML/d, up 22 per cent. The drivers partially CANCEL - 1.02 x 0.99 = 1.0098, a net 0.98 per cent a year, not 2 per cent - a difference of close to 5 ML/d on average day. Now convert by the peaking factor of 1.6 to get the capacity actually built: 42.8 ML/d against 35.0 ML/d. THE DIFFERENCE IS 7.8 ML/d OF TREATMENT WORKS - built, staffed, maintained for forty years, and never used.

5. A range with triggers, not a number (make it a tripwire, not a prophecy)

Nobody can know the demand of 2046, and a single number pretends otherwise. Deliver a RANGE - a low case (growth slows, efficiency continues), a central case, a high case (growth holds, efficiency stalls, an industrial connection lands) - and then the two things that make it useful. THE COMMITMENT POINTS: the utilisation level at which expansion must be authorised, given the lead time to design and build, since capacity ordered late is as much a failure as capacity ordered early. And the MONITORING TRIGGERS: the observations that reveal which world you are in soon enough to act - actual connections against projection, measured per-capita against the assumed decline, the peaking factor itself drifting. A twenty-year forecast's job is not to be RIGHT. It is to tell you what to watch, and when you must decide - a tripwire, not a prophecy.

Box 4 is where the money is. Two forecasts of the same town, built on the same population projection, differing only in whether one driver was allowed to move - and between them stands 7.8 ML/d of treatment works. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

The way out of the paralysis has a name: driver decomposition. You stop looking at the aggregated total, separate the underlying forces, project each on its own evidence, and let the arithmetic combine them. Five questions:

  • What exactly are you forecasting? The service, the unit, the horizon, the decision - and which demand. Average-day demand is a typical day; maximum-day demand is the hard day that infrastructure is sized on; the peaking factor (MDD/ADD) converts between them and comes from your own records. A forecast in average day sizes nothing until it is converted.
  • What is the total made of? Never extrapolate a total - a total is a result, not a cause. Residential demand is population x per-capita consumption. Non-residential blocks are projected separately, because they arrive as step changes when a connection lands, not as smooth trends.
  • What is the evidence for each driver? Demographic for population, economic for the non-residential blocks, and policy for per-capita - metering, leak reduction, tariffs, fittings standards. State each source and its vintage, because an old projection carries assumptions you would be inheriting silently.
  • What happens when the drivers fight? Compute it both ways and look at the gap. Then convert by the peaking factor into the capacity that would actually be built, because that is where the error becomes concrete and expensive.
  • How do you deliver it? As a range - low, central, high - with commitment points (the utilisation at which expansion must be authorised, given build lead time) and monitoring triggers (the observations that reveal which world you are in, early enough to act).

The error that dominates all others. It is not bad population data. It is holding per-capita consumption flat. The documented finding is blunt: suppliers generally projected stable or increasing per-capita demand, while actual per-capita demand fell by about 1.9 per cent a year over two decades - and that systematic under-accounting of efficiency gains and policy adoption explains much of the error in their forecasts. If you take one habit from this unit, let it be that you never hold per-capita flat without evidence that it is flat.

And this is not a neutral technical choice. You carry two duties at once. A duty to the future: under-building denies a growing community a fundamental service, and constrains health, dignity and livelihoods. A duty to the present: over-building burdens today's customers - including the most vulnerable - with tariffs paying for stranded assets they do not need, and misallocates public money that had other work to do. Over-building is not the safe option. It is the other failure, and it is quieter, which is precisely what makes it easy.

One honest boundary. You are projecting the demand for service on evidence and expressing it as a range with triggers. You are not forecasting when your existing assets must be renewed - that is the supply side and its own technique - not building an econometric model, and not making the investment decision. You are producing the input that decision cannot be made honestly without.

Does it scale? Forecast the data storage a business will need in ten years. Users and transactions are growing fast - and at the same time compression and more efficient formats mean each transaction takes less space than it did five years ago. Project the total terabytes and you will buy a warehouse of disks. Project user growth and storage efficiency separately, and you will buy what you need, because one driver pushes up while the other pushes down and only their product is real.

T4: Your Turn

You are forecasting electricity distribution demand for a district over 20 years, to decide whether a substation must be upgraded.

  • Today: 40,000 connected households; average-day demand 60 MWh/day; the network's maximum-day to average-day peaking factor is 1.5, from its own records.
  • Demographic evidence: households growing 1.5 per cent a year (regional projection, published three years ago).
  • Policy evidence: per-household consumption has been falling about 1.5 per cent a year - efficient lighting, better appliances, rooftop solar self-generation.
  • Economic evidence: a cold-storage facility is in advanced negotiation and would add a flat 8 MWh/day from year 6. A second, speculative, would add the same from year 14.
  1. Define. State what you are forecasting, and which demand figure will size the substation.
  2. Decompose. Give the current per-household consumption, and identify which drivers are trends and which are steps.
  3. Compute. Project residential demand at year 20 both naively (per-household held flat) and decomposed. Then add the non-residential blocks appropriately.
  4. Convert. Apply the peaking factor to get the capacity each approach would have you build, and state the difference.
  5. Deliver. Give a low, central and high case, one commitment point and two monitoring triggers.

The test of a real answer: three ways to get this wrong, and they are all comfortable.

First, the arithmetic will surprise you. Households rise 1.5 per cent a year and per-household consumption falls 1.5 per cent a year - and those do not cancel to zero, though nearly everyone assumes they do. Work out 1.015 x 0.985 and see what you actually get, then decide whether residential demand rises or falls over twenty years.

Second, the cold-storage facility is a step, not a trend. It does not grow at 1.5 per cent a year and it does not start in year one. Add it as a block, at its year, and do not let it inherit a growth rate it does not have.

Third, the two facilities are not the same kind of evidence. One is in advanced negotiation; one is speculative. If both sit in your central case with equal confidence, you have quietly turned a hope into a commitment - which is exactly how substations get built for customers who never arrive.

T5: The Guardrails

Long-range forecasts fail in patterned ways, and the expensive ones look prudent at the time.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Extrapolating the total. A total is a result, not a cause. Extending it with a ruler hides every driver inside it, including the ones moving the other way.

• Holding per-capita consumption flat. The single documented dominant error. Suppliers projected stable or rising per-person demand while it fell about 1.9 per cent a year. Never assume flat without evidence of flat.

• Forecasting average day and sizing with it. Infrastructure is built for the peak. An unconverted average-day forecast sizes nothing, and quietly under-sizes everything.

• Using a projection without its vintage and assumptions. Someone else's migration assumption becomes yours the moment you use their number without reading it.

• Modelling a step as a trend. A new industrial connection arrives whole, on a date. Growing it at the residential rate invents demand that will never exist.

• Treating speculative and committed demand as equally real. Put them in different cases, or a hope silently becomes a capital commitment.

• Delivering a single number. It tells the decision-maker nothing about what to watch, and it will be wrong in a way nobody detects until the money is spent.

• Believing over-building is the safe option. It is the other failure. Stranded capacity carries sunk cost and decades of maintenance, is paid for through tariffs by the people the works serves, and leaves the system less able to adapt, not more.

• Never revisiting it. A forecast with triggers that nobody checks is a forecast without triggers.

Onalenna was asked for one number and had two true trends. Define what you are forecasting and which demand sizes the asset; decompose the total into drivers instead of extending a line; evidence each driver separately - demographic, economic and policy - and never hold per-capita flat by default, because that is the error that has dominated the record; compute it both ways and convert by the peaking factor so the cost of the method is visible; then deliver a range with commitment points and monitoring triggers. A twenty-year forecast is not a prophecy and does not have to be right. It has to tell you what to watch, and when you must decide.
