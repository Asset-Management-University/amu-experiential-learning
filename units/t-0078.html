A critical screen is down, the plant manager wants it back by midday, and all I have is a smudged pencil note. How do I take this breakdown from panic to a closed, costed, recorded job - properly, in the system?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a cold morning at Letlapeng Manganese, a working mine. You are the new maintenance planner. You stand in front of Screen 3 on the dry-screening plant - a steel deck that shakes ore over big mesh panels to sort it by size. Two fitters are next to you in dusty overalls. The deck is dead still. It should be running.

In your hand is a torn-off page from a school exercise book. On it, in pencil, the night-shift artisan has written: "Screen 3 - bearing on drive end making a grinding noise, getting hot. Stopped it 04:15. Spare bearing? Ask stores." The page is smudged with grease, and a sharp, hot, oily smell comes off the bearing housing.

Your shift supervisor, Kefentse, walks up holding her phone, not smiling. "Plant manager wants Screen 3 back by midday," she says. "Every hour it's down we lose about R48,000 in lost throughput. He's asking who's on it and what parts we're waiting for. I told him I'd find out from you."

You feel the pull to just do something fast - phone the storeman, shout for the bearing, send the two fitters up to start pulling the housing apart right now. It feels like action. But there is a cold thought underneath it: there is no job number, no record, no parts reserved, no history saved. If the plant manager asks "what was wrong with Screen 3 last time, and the time before?" - nobody can answer. And the mine just spent R12 million on a new maintenance system, exactly so this kind of morning would never feel like this again.

A machine is down, the manager is on the phone, and all I have is a smudged pencil note. How do I take this breakdown from panic all the way to a closed, costed, recorded job - run properly in the system, so next time we can answer 'what was it last time?'

Before you read on, decide. Screen 3 is down, the manager is on the phone, and all you have is a smudged pencil note. Two fitters are waiting to be sent. And underneath the urgency: no job number, no parts reserved, nothing that will let anybody answer what was wrong with it last time? — on a mine that just spent R12 million on the system meant to prevent exactly this morning. Write down what you would do. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one costs the next person

• “Send the fitters up now and sort the paperwork later.” It feels like action and it is the one that reliably goes wrong. Nothing is reserving that bearing — another job can draw it while your men are pulling the housing apart, and “later” is a shift that ends with everybody going home.

• “Phone the storeman and get the bearing held.” Better, and it is a promise between two people rather than a record. If he goes off shift, or the part is picked by somebody with a docket, the promise loses to the paperwork every time.

• “Raise the job number afterwards so we capture the cost.” Common, and it captures a number rather than a history. The hours will be estimated, the cause will be remembered, and the parts will be whatever the storeman recalls issuing — which is exactly the record that cannot answer the manager’s question next time.

• “This is an emergency — the system is for planned work.” The most damaging belief in the room, and it sounds like common sense. Breakdowns are precisely the jobs whose history is worth having, because they are the ones that repeat — and a system that only holds planned work can never tell you that Screen 3 has done this three times.

• “Do it properly — but that takes time we don’t have.” The honest objection, and it is worth testing rather than accepting. Ask what it actually costs to raise the job, reserve the part and send a defined instruction — against the cost of the bearing being gone when the fitters reach for it.

Hold the manager’s future question in view while you decide: what was wrong with Screen 3 last time, and the time before? Everything you do in the next ten minutes either answers that or does not.

You do not need to invent a method. The mine already has one - a proper, repeatable way to take this breakdown from a smudged note to a closed, costed, recorded job. You just need to run that cycle, step by step. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is the work-management cycle in a CMMS - a Computerised Maintenance Management System, the single shared notebook for everything to do with looking after equipment, that lives on a computer and never gets lost or smudged. The cycle is the agreed path a job follows from "something needs doing" to "done, paid for, and written down". Think of it as a relay race: each runner hands a baton to the next, and skipping a runner is exactly when parts go missing, costs vanish, and history is never saved. Watch the whole cycle run on the Screen 3 breakdown.

Step 1 - Find the asset (find before you create)

You search the equipment number from the tag riveted to the frame - EQ-100244, 'Vibrating Screen, Double-Deck, Drive Assembly' - at functional location LMN-DSP-SCR3 ('Letlapeng > Dry Screening Plant > Screen 3'). Its last work shows a greasing PM 18 days ago, closed normally. The machine is known; you did not create a new record. Find before you create.

Step 2 - Raise the notification (capture the symptom)

You create a malfunction notification against EQ-100244: 'Drive-end bearing grinding, overheating. Stopped 04:15 night shift.' You tick breakdown = yes (the machine is down, not just faulty), set the malfunction start to 04:15, and the priority to Very High - it is production-critical at R48,000 an hour. You save. The plant manager can already see it; the clock is official.

Step 3 - Create the work order (organise the response)

From the notification you create a corrective work order to the day-shift fitting team, with the operations and planned hours: isolate and lock out (0.5 h), strip the drive-end housing (1.5 h), replace the bearing (1.5 h), refit, align and test-run (1.0 h). Planned labour: 4.5 hours x 2 fitters = 9 man-hours. The job now has a shape.

Step 4 - Reserve the parts, then release

On the order you add the spare - 'Bearing, spherical roller, 130 mm bore', 2 on hand at the Letlapeng store - and reserve 1 against the order (cost loaded: R7,850). You release the order (created -> released); the store issues the bearing against the reservation, and the fitters collect it with their lockout tags. Work starts 07:30 - with the part in hand, not waiting on it.

Step 5 - Confirm and close (turn the job into history)

The fitters find the bearing seized and the seal failed - grit had got in; they fit the bearing and a new seal (R310, added on the spot), refit and test. You confirm: actual labour 5.0 h x 2 = 10 man-hours, parts R7,850 + R310 = R8,160, cause 'seal failure let grit into the bearing'. Machine running 11:20 - downtime 7 h 5 min. The job closes at R4,200 labour + R8,160 parts = R12,360, against R339,840 of lost throughput. Now the next planner sees: two bearings in 14 months, cause a failed seal - the start of a real fix.

See what the cycle did. It took a smudged pencil note and a rising panic and turned them into one closed, costed job - with the part reserved, the hours booked, the cause recorded, and the whole story saved against the machine. And see the mission in it, because the system is not bureaucracy - it is dignity. The panicked version, the one that feels like action, is the one that loses the bearing to another job, hides the true cost, and leaves the next planner as blind as you were this morning. Running the cycle properly serves four people at once: the planner, who gets clarity under pressure instead of a guess; the fitter, who is sent to a defined job with the right part already set aside, not up a ladder to improvise; the machine, whose whole story is kept in one place instead of scattered across pencil notes that get thrown away; and the next person, who will face this same breakdown and deserves to meet knowledge, not a blank. One hard rule sits above all of it: safety comes first, always - in a real emergency you make people safe and stop the danger FIRST, and raise the notification immediately after; the system serves the work, it never delays a life-saving response. The R48,000 an hour is the measure of what is at stake, but the point is reliable production and a machine whose story is known.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the cycle in five moves - the runners in the relay.

  • 1. Find the asset. Confirm the machine exists and where it lives - find before you create.
  • 2. Raise the notification. Capture the symptom while it is fresh; start the system clock.
  • 3. Create the work order. The organised response: who, the steps, the planned hours.
  • 4. Reserve the parts, then release. Secure the spare for this job; give the green light.
  • 5. Confirm and close. Record what really happened; turn the job into searchable history.

The ideas underneath. A functional location is a tidy address for a machine - the place or function that stays ("Dry Screening Plant > Screen 3"); the equipment is the actual machine that sits there and can be swapped out. A PM is preventive maintenance, planned work you choose in advance on a schedule (greasing a bearing monthly), held as a maintenance plan with a task list. A notification is the system's pencil note - the alarm, a record of a problem on a machine at a time, that anyone can raise. A work order is the organised job - the one place where parts are reserved, hours are booked, and cost is gathered; releasing it is the green light to issue the part and start. Confirming records the actual hours, parts and cause; closing turns the finished job into history, and a cause code lets reports read across all that history for trends and repeat offenders. This cycle fits any planned organisation with more equipment than one person can hold in their head - mines, plants, fleets, utilities, hospitals, municipalities; it is overkill for a one-person workshop with three tools, and it must never delay a true emergency.

Two disciplines keep the cycle honest. First, find before you create, every time - the fastest way to ruin a machine's history is to raise a second record because the first took ten seconds longer to find; one machine, one record, or its story splits and every future search comes back half-blind. Second, the job is not done until it is closed - a machine running again with the order left open is the most common lie in maintenance: the parts and hours never settle, the cost never lands, and the cause is never written, so the next failure starts from zero; closing is not the tax on the work, it is the part of the work that makes all the future work easier.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your cycle true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Safety before the system, always. In a real emergency, make people safe and stop the danger first; raise the notification the moment the danger is held. The system serves the work, never the reverse.

• Find before you create. One machine, one record. A duplicate splits the history and blinds every future search. Always search the tag number first.

• Reserve the part, do not just hope for it. Reserving sets the spare aside for this job. The panicked skip - sending fitters up before the part is secured - is how a stripped machine waits days for a bearing.

• Confirm the truth, not the plan. Book the hours and parts that actually happened, and write the cause in plain words. A confirmation that just copies the plan teaches the next planner nothing.

• Closed, or it never happened. An open order is an unfinished job: no settled cost, no saved cause. Close it, and one job becomes knowledge.

You came to the desk with a dead screen, a phone ringing, and a pencil note. You can leave with a closed order: the machine found in the system, the symptom captured, the job defined, the bearing reserved and issued, the work confirmed with its real cause, and the whole thing costed and closed - R12,360 for the job, against R339,840 of lost throughput, and a cause that points at the next fix. One closed order is a fact; a pile of closed orders is knowledge. Run the cycle so the smudged note becomes searchable history that answers 'what was wrong last time?' - that is what the system is for. That is how a plant stops re-learning the same breakdown forever: not by working faster in the panic, but by running the quiet, ordered cycle that turns every job into a lesson the next person can find.
