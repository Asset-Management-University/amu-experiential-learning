The one big thing

T1: The Ache

You have tracked your spending. You know your numbers now - what comes in, where it goes, what you are left with. And the number you are left with is smaller than you want it to be. You are not keeping enough to build the life you are aiming at, and you can feel the distance between where you are and where you want to be.

So you reach for the advice everyone gives: cut the small joys. Stop buying the coffee. Skip the lunch out. Give up the one small thing that makes a hard week bearable. And two things happen at once - it feels miserable, and, if you are honest, you can see that even giving all of it up would barely move the number. The small treats are visible, so they feel like the problem. But they are not the lever, and some part of you already knows it.

I am not saving enough, and the only advice I hear is to punish myself over the small things I can see - which feels bad and would not even be enough. Where is the real lever?

Before you read on, decide. You take home R6,000 a month and you keep about R400 of it. Everyone's advice is the same: give up the Friday takeaway — the one small thing that makes a hard week bearable. And two things are true at once. It would feel miserable. And if you are honest, you can see that giving up all of it would barely move the number. Meanwhile the taxi fare to work, the policy your aunt signed you up for, and the rent on a room you took when the job was further away are simply part of the furniture of your life. Write down where the real lever is — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the visible things are not the lever

• “Give up the takeaway anyway — every bit helps.” It does add up a little, and it costs you the one thing that makes a hard week bearable, and it requires willpower every single week. That is a great deal of effort spent on the smallest number on the page.

• “Try harder to be disciplined.” You are already careful. Discipline aimed at the wrong quantity produces exhaustion and no result, and then feels like a personal failing when it does not work.

• “Earn more.” Genuinely one of the two real levers, and it is usually slower and less in your control than the other one — worth pursuing, and not a plan for this month.

• “Nothing can be done on this income.” Understandable and premature: you have not yet looked at the largest recurring commitments, and one of them may not still be doing the job it was taken on for.

• “I am just bad with money.” Not a finding, and not true here — you are keeping something on a tight income while supporting others. Shame is not a plan, and it points you at the visible small things every time.

• Two moves. First, get an honest number instead of a feeling: what you keep divided by what you earn — R400 of R6,000, so about 6.7 per cent. Hold it correctly: it is a measurement, not a grade and not a confession. A scale is not a verdict. Somebody who knows their real rate has something to work with; somebody who only feels behind has only the feeling, and a feeling cannot be acted on. Second, measure the distance to where it needs to be. If your goal needs nearer 15 per cent — R900 on R6,000 — then the gap is R500 a month, and look what that tells you immediately: the takeaway was never going to close it. That is arithmetic rather than a failure of discipline, and knowing it releases you from a punishment that could not have worked. So the search moves to where the money actually is: the large, recurring commitments — the rent, the transport, the policy — the things that feel fixed and are the only things big enough to matter. They are harder to change, and they change once, permanently, which is exactly why they are the sustainable lever while the small denials demand willpower every week and deliver almost nothing.

There are two moves: find the true size of the gap, and find the one big thing that closes it. Let us learn them.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you find the gap between where your savings are and where they need to be, and then find the single highest-impact change to close it - without shame, and without wasting your effort on the wrong thing.

1. Your savings rate - a simple, honest number

START WITH ONE SIMPLE, HONEST NUMBER: YOUR SAVINGS RATE. You already know your income and your spending from tracking them - this turns them into a rate. Take WHAT YOU KEEP in a month, divide it by WHAT YOU EARN that month, and that is your savings rate. If you take home R6000 and you keep R400 of it, your savings rate is R400 divided by R6000 - ABOUT 6.7 PERCENT. Here is how to hold that number: IT IS NOT A GRADE AND IT IS NOT A CONFESSION. It is a MEASUREMENT, the way a scale is a measurement - and you cannot change what you refuse to look at. A person who knows their real rate has something to work with; a person who only feels 'behind' has only the feeling. GETTING AN HONEST NUMBER IS THE WHOLE FIRST STEP, and it is done without judgement - you are reading an instrument, not passing a sentence on yourself.

2. The gap - the distance to a sustainable rate, a target not a verdict

NOW THE SECOND NUMBER, AND THE MOST USEFUL ONE YOU WILL EVER HAVE: THE GAP. Somewhere ahead of you is the rate you would need to reach your goal in a reasonable time - you worked toward that when you set your wealth target. Say that goal needs closer to 15 PERCENT, which on R6000 is R900 a month. You are keeping R400. So your gap is R900 minus R400 - ABOUT R500 A MONTH. And here is how to hold it, because everything depends on it: THE GAP IS A TARGET TO AIM AT, NOT A STICK TO BEAT YOURSELF WITH. IT IS A COMPASS BEARING, NOT A REPORT CARD. A person who knows their gap is R500 has something specific they can aim at; a person who only feels 'behind' has nothing but the weight of the feeling. NAMING THE GAP TURNS A VAGUE WEIGHT INTO A SPECIFIC TARGET - and a specific target is something you can actually hit. It measures a distance to travel, never the worth of the traveller.

3. Sustainable beats heroic - aim for a rate you can hold

BEFORE YOU PICK A CHANGE, PICK THE RIGHT KIND OF RATE TO AIM FOR, BECAUSE THIS IS WHERE MOST PLANS DIE: SUSTAINABLE BEATS HEROIC. It is tempting to swing hard - to declare you will save 40 percent starting Monday, live on nothing, and close the whole gap at once. THAT PLAN ALMOST ALWAYS BREAKS, and a rate you keep for three weeks and then abandon in exhaustion builds nothing. A MODEST RATE YOU CAN ACTUALLY HOLD - month after month, through the ordinary bumps of a real life - BUILDS REAL WEALTH, because CONSISTENCY IS WHAT COMPOUNDS, NOT INTENSITY. So aim for a rate that fits your real life, not a heroic one that fits a fantasy of it. And know this clearly: A SMALL RATE YOU CAN SUSTAIN IS A GENUINE VICTORY, NOT A FAILURE TO REACH A BIGGER ONE. SMALL AND SUSTAINED IS REAL. Five percent held for ten years beats twenty percent held for one month, every single time - which is exactly why a lean budget is not shut out of wealth-building; it is invited in on its own honest terms.

4. The highest-impact change - the one big thing, not the many small ones

NOW YOU CLOSE THE GAP - AND HERE IS THE MISTAKE ALMOST EVERYONE MAKES, SO WATCH FOR IT. When people look for money to save, their eyes go straight to the small, VISIBLE treats: the coffee, the takeaway, the little pleasure they feel slightly guilty about. But those are visible precisely because they are small and frequent, and THEY MOVE THE NEEDLE VERY LITTLE. Meanwhile the costs that actually dominate a budget are big, INVISIBLE and recurring - housing, transport, the interest bleeding out of a debt, and the quiet pile of small monthly subscriptions and shared costs you set up once and stopped noticing. This is a trick your own mind plays, and it has a name: VISIBILITY BIAS - you focus on the small cost you can SEE and feel guilty about, and you miss the large recurring cost doing the real damage. So finding your highest-impact change means deliberately looking AWAY from the guilt and toward the BIG MONEY: go to your three largest recurring categories and ask, is there ONE change here - a transport arrangement shared or altered, a debt consolidated so the interest stops bleeding, a bundle of forgotten subscriptions cancelled - that closes most of the gap in a single move? THIS IS LEVERAGE, NOT GUILT - strategic, not moral. The small treat is often the very thing that makes a lean week bearable, and giving it up would cost your morale far more than it saves your budget.

5. When there is nothing to cut, the lever is income - and that is honourable

ONE HONEST BRANCH, AND IT IS THE MOST IMPORTANT IN THE UNIT. Sometimes you look at the big recurring costs honestly and find there is GENUINELY ALMOST NOTHING TO CUT - rent, food and transport take nearly all of it, and what is left is already the bare bones of a life. If that is you, hear this plainly: THE GAP IS NOT A SIGN THAT YOU ARE CARELESS, AND IT IS NOT YOURS TO FIX BY LIVING SMALLER. When a budget has no slack, spending is not the lever - INCOME IS. The honest, dignified and highest-impact move is to PROTECT YOUR NECESSITIES and turn your attention to growing what comes in: an extra shift or a side income, a skill that raises your earning, or a grant or support you are genuinely entitled to and may not be claiming. THE INCOME LEVER IS HONOURABLE - it is a real and powerful strategic choice, NOT a last resort or an admission of failure. A person on a lean income who finds R500 of new income has done something far harder and far more valuable than a person with slack who cancelled a subscription, and this technique respects that completely. Accept where you stand, find the lever that actually moves, and never live smaller out of shame.

T3: The Blueprint

  • What is the gap? The distance to a sustainable rate. The difference between the rate you are at and the rate your goal needs. It is a target, not a verdict.
  • How high should I aim? Sustainable beats heroic. A modest rate you hold for years builds more than a heroic one you abandon in a month. Pick a rate that fits your real life.
  • What change should I make? The highest-impact one. Look past the small, visible treats to the big recurring costs that quietly do the real damage. One change, most gap closed, least sustainable pain.
  • What if there is nothing to cut? Then the lever is income. For a lean budget, the honest and powerful move is to protect your necessities and grow your income or claim the support you are owed.

T4: Your Turn

Use your own numbers if you have them, or work with this example: a take-home income of R6000 a month, currently keeping R400, with big recurring costs of rent R2600, transport R1400, debt repayments R700 (of which a chunk is interest), and a scatter of small monthly subscriptions totalling R300.

  1. Calculate your current savings rate. What you keep divided by what you earn.
  2. Define your gap. Choose a sustainable target rate that fits the goal, and work out the gap in RAND per month between where you are and where you want to be.
  3. Find your highest-impact change. Look at the three biggest recurring categories. Name one single change in one of them that could close most or all of the gap - and explain why it has more leverage than cutting three or four small, visible treats.
  4. Consider the income lever. If an honest look showed your budget had no real slack, say so - the correct answer would then be that your highest-impact lever is income. Name one concrete step to grow your income or claim support you are entitled to.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the rate: R400 divided by R6000 is about 6.7 percent. An honest number, not a judgement.

Second, the gap: if the goal needs about 15 percent, that is R900 a month; the gap is R900 minus R400 = R500 a month. A specific target you can aim at.

Third, the highest-impact change: it lives in the big recurring costs, not the R300 of subscriptions and certainly not a daily treat. The strongest single moves here are on transport (a shared or changed arrangement could free a large part of R1400) or on the debt (consolidating or accelerating it to stop the interest bleeding). The mark of a real answer is that it names one change in a big category that closes most of the R500 in a single, sustainable move - and explains that cutting small visible treats would take dozens of painful sacrifices to reach the same figure, if it reached it at all. That is visibility bias defeated.

Fourth, the income branch: if rent, food and transport genuinely took it all, the honest answer is that there is little to cut and the lever is income - a named step such as taking on a specific extra shift, turning a skill into a small side income, or checking a support or grant you qualify for and are not claiming. The mark of a real answer here is that it protects the necessities and treats growing income as a strong, dignified strategy, not a fallback.

T5: The Guardrails

The gap is a target to aim at, not a stick to beat yourself with - and you close it with the one big thing you can sustain, not the hundred small things you feel guilty about.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Chasing the small visible treats. They call loudest for your guilt and move the needle least. Look away from them and toward the big recurring costs where the real money lives.

• Falling for visibility bias. The cost you see and feel bad about is rarely the one doing the damage. The damage is usually large, recurring and quiet. Hunt the quiet costs.

• Aiming heroic instead of sustainable. A huge saving you abandon in a month builds nothing. A modest one you hold for years builds wealth. Pick a rate you can keep.

• Treating the gap as a verdict. It is a compass bearing, not a report card. It tells you which way to aim, not what kind of person you are.

• Assuming there is always something to cut. Many lean budgets have no slack, and that is a fact about circumstances, not character. When there is nothing to cut, the lever is income.

• Treating the income lever as a last resort. Growing what comes in is a strong, honourable strategy - often harder and more valuable than trimming a cost. Give it equal weight.

• Shaming a small comfort. The small treat is often what makes a hard week bearable; cutting it can cost your morale far more than it saves your budget. Leverage, not guilt.

• Cutting so hard the plan breaks. A change you cannot live with is a change you will not keep. Choose the one big thing you can actually sustain, and let consistency do the rest.

You knew you were not saving enough; now you know the two moves that turn that feeling into a plan. Find your savings rate honestly - what you keep over what you earn. Name the gap to a sustainable target, and hold it as a bearing to aim at rather than a stick to beat yourself with. Then close it with the one big thing you can sustain: the large recurring cost that quietly does the real damage, or - when a budget is already lean - your income, which is the most honourable lever of all. Your financial power comes from changing the one big thing you can sustain, not from feeling guilty about the small things you can see. Look past the noise, find the lever, change one thing you can keep - and let consistency do the rest.
