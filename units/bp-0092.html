I know we run a good shop - so why can't I prove it, and is the answer to just put on a show for the audit, or something deeper?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Naadira, and you run a brilliant little workshop making specialist parts for bigger machines. You are good - really good - and your biggest customer has trusted you for years because your work is excellent and you always deliver. But now they've grown, and their new rules mean they must audit their suppliers. An audit team arrives with a checklist, and the contract - worth 70% of your business - is on the line.

They ask four simple-sounding things. "Show us your true cost for each part." You guess it's about R12.50 - but the notebook in your drawer says R11.80 and your senior person Lebo's spreadsheet says R13.10; they don't match. "Show us who is allowed to approve a change or a discount." The answer is "me, and Lebo when I'm out" - but it's written nowhere. "Show us your plan to renew the big cutting machine everything depends on." The plan is in your head: keep it serviced, save up. "Show us proof the parts a subcontractor makes for you meet your standard." You know they do, because you check them yourself - but there's no record. A knot tightens in your stomach: you ARE in control, the shop runs well - but all the proof lives in your head, in Lebo's head, in a phone group-chat, and in three records that disagree.

I know we run a good shop - so why can't I prove it, and is the only answer to put on a show for the auditors, or is there a deeper problem I need to fix for myself?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to panic and perform readiness instead of creating it. You could pull an all-nighter and build a big, shiny folder of fresh spreadsheets and signed pages - impressive, but not how you actually work; auditors are paid to spot the difference between a real system and a show, and even if you pass, the folder is useless to you the next day. Or you get defensive - "just trust us, our track record speaks for itself" - but a party with power over your future needs proof, not promises; an audit exists precisely because "trust me" stops being enough. Or you do the bare minimum, answering only the four boxes on their list - you scrape a pass, but the knowledge still lives in heads and scattered notes, so the next audit, or the day Lebo's on leave, or the loan application, springs the very same trap.

So before you read on: these four questions feel like four separate, annoying chores - but what if they're not four problems at all, but four windows looking into the same room?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The answer is not to perform readiness but to create it - and the four demands are one demand seen from several sides: is this operation actually under control? Answering it needs not a mountain of paperwork but a single, honest, connected story. Five moves:

  • See the trap - it all lives in heads: your business runs on knowledge inside a few capable people and records kept in several places that don't match - a strength that got you started, but now a risk. If you or your senior person aren't in the room, the full picture isn't in the room either; an operation that can't be SEEN by others can't be proven to anyone - not the auditor, and not fully even to you.
  • See the four demands as one: true cost, who-may-decide, the renewal of what you depend on, and standing behind work done in your name are not four separate chores - they are four windows onto one question: is this operation under control? Your true cost includes setting aside for the machine's renewal; who can approve that budget is a decision right; the part's quality is the outcome. Answer the one question and you answer all four.
  • Clarity before defence - see it yourself first: don't build the system for the auditor - build it for YOU, because you cannot defend what you cannot yet see clearly. Start by getting your own records to agree. A small, honest picture you understand and use every day is worth a thousand times more than a thick folder faked for a visitor - and unlike the folder, it's still true the day after they leave.
  • Build one connected line of sight: write a single, joined-up thread anyone can follow end to end: from the resources you depend on (the cutting machine), through your true costs (a few cents of every part set aside for its renewal) and who is allowed to decide what (only the owner approves a cost change over a set size), to the outcome the customer pays for (the part, checked against a standard, the check recorded). One story, written down - that is the proof, because it IS the control.
  • Fix the trap, not this audit: build it so the operation runs correctly whether or not you are in the room - the knowledge living in a simple shared system, not only in heads. Then this audit passes as a side effect, and so does the next one, and the bank's, and the day you hand over - because you fixed the trap itself, not just the boxes on one checklist. (This is the operations-mechanics cousin of making your unwritten culture explicit.)

See it change everything for Naadira. She stops building a folder for the auditors and builds the truth for herself: she reconciles the three cost records into one real figure, writes the one line of sight - the cutting machine, a few cents of every part set aside to renew it, who may approve a change, the standard each part is checked against and the check recorded - and suddenly she can not only pass the audit but run a tighter, calmer shop. The knowledge now lives in a simple shared system, not only in her and Lebo's heads. The answer is not to perform readiness but to create it; the four demands are one demand seen from several sides; so see it yourself first (a small honest system beats a large fake one), build one connected line of sight, and fix the trap, not the audit - make it run right whether or not you're in the room.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a workshop - the same trap catches any good operation that lives in its people's heads. Watch a freelancer collective.

See the same line of sight in a freelancer collective

A small collective of freelancers - designers, writers, coders - teams up for big projects. A huge company wants to hire them for a year, but first its procurement team must vet them: proof they're a reliable business, not a loose group. The same four questions arrive:

• True cost? They agree one clear price list (a writer's day rate, a coder's), not five different rates floating in emails.
• Who decides? They write down who the project lead is and what they may promise a client without a full group meeting.
• Renewal? A plan for finding and bringing on a new member who meets the standard if their best coder leaves mid-project.
• Quality? A simple written check so a new designer's work matches the collective's standard.

Same answer: one simple "operating manual" - a written thread proving they can deliver even if one member is away. They create real control, and win the contract as a result.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of something you run well, but where the knowledge lives mostly in your head and scattered notes - a small business, a community project, even your family's finances. One day a party with power over its future will need to understand it: a bank weighing a loan, a big client weighing a contract, a funder reviewing a grant, the person who'll take over from you. They'll ask the same four things in different words - what does it really cost? who gets to decide? how do you keep it going? how do you guarantee quality?

  1. See the four as one. They're all asking "are you really in control?" - don't answer four chores, answer the one question.
  2. Build it for yourself first, small and honest. Get your own view clear and your records agreeing before you ever defend it to anyone.
  3. Write the single connected thread, and make it run without you. From the resources you depend on, through the costs and decisions, to the outcome people rely on - built so it runs right whether or not you're in the room.

Running on capable people and know-how carried in your head is not a failing - it is how almost every good operation begins, and it is a real strength. The audit is not an accusation; it's a growing-up moment, the point where what you carry must become something others can see and trust. So don't fake a folder and don't take offence - give yourself the quiet gift of clarity: build the small, honest, connected picture for your own eyes first, and the proof for everyone else follows for free. Provable control keeps the trust of the people who rely on you - and, more deeply, it makes your work stronger, calmer, and easier to carry. Real control serves people; the audit is only the test that shows you already have it.
