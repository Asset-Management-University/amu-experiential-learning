Until the fire chief arrives

T1: The Ache

It is 14:41 and the world has gone very small.

Something is wrong on the east side. You have three facts and two of them may not be facts. Four people are talking to you, one of them on a radio you can barely hear, and every one of them is waiting for you to say something. The plant is still running. You do not know whether it should be.

You are aware, distantly, that you know a great deal about this plant, and that none of it is arriving in a useful order.

And underneath all of it is the thing nobody says out loud: you are about to make a decision that will be looked at afterwards by people who will have all the information you do not have now.

Everything I know is true and none of it is arriving in an order I can use, and somebody has to decide in the next few minutes. How do I command an incident instead of being carried by one?

Before you read on, decide. Everything arriving is true. None of it is arriving in an order you can use. The plant is still running and you do not know whether it should be, people are asking you things, and underneath it sits the part nobody says out loud: this will be looked at afterwards by people who will have all the information you do not have now. Write down what you would actually do in the next few minutes, and in what order — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two that are sometimes right

• “Decide now — hesitation is how people get hurt.” Under pressure this feels like the only responsible option, and it is right in exactly two situations: harm happening now, and a situation growing now. Outside those two, a short deliberate period spent assembling fragments into a picture buys a better decision than the seconds it cost. Naming the two exceptions is what stops the discipline becoming paralysis — and what stops you using “I am collecting” as a place to hide.

• “Take charge of the whole scene — somebody has to.” Somebody does, and it may not be you. There is a real command here and it is yours: the asset, stabilising it, isolating what must be isolated, deciding what the system does next. And an emergency scene is commanded by whoever holds statutory authority for it. The worst version of this evening is you presiding while somebody qualified waits to be told what is in the tank.

• “Stand back and wait for the emergency services.” The opposite error, and just as costly. On their arrival command of the scene passes to them and your role becomes technical adviser to that command — which is frequently the more consequential of the two. Between now and then, the isolations and the state of the plant are nobody else’s to decide.

• “Get to the plant and see it for myself.” Powerful, and it trades your reach for one pair of eyes. The people already on the scene are the only real instrument you have — they can see what no indication shows — and what comes back from them depends almost entirely on what you ask and how you ask it. Standing next to one of them, you have lost the other five.

• “Write it all up properly once this is over.” It will not survive. A log written as it happens and one reconstructed afterwards are not the same document, and the difference lands on two people: whoever takes the handover from you tonight, and whoever examines this later holding everything you did not have.

Notice what you actually said at the start: you know a great deal about this plant, and none of it is arriving in a useful order. That is a problem about order, not about knowledge — and order is something you can impose.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the command - knowing your boundary before you need it, collecting before deciding, treating the people on the scene as your only real instrument, a tempo with a log, and a handover that keeps what the first hours bought.

1. Before anything else, know what you command and what you do not

<strong>Settle this first, on an ordinary afternoon, because you will not settle it well at three in the morning.</strong> <strong>What you command is the asset and the continuity of what it does.</strong> Stabilising the plant, isolating what must be isolated, directing technical resources, protecting people in relation to the asset, and deciding what the system does next. <strong>That is a real command and it is yours.</strong> <strong>What you do not command is an emergency scene.</strong> You are not a statutory incident controller. You do not direct fire, medical or police resources, you do not hold legal authority over the site during a declared emergency, and you must not be the person who believes otherwise while somebody qualified is waiting to be told what is in the tank. <strong>Write down the transition and write it down now:</strong> 'On arrival of the emergency services, command of the scene passes to them. My role becomes technical adviser to that command.' <strong>And understand that the second role is not a demotion - it is often the more consequential one.</strong> When the fire chief asks what happens if they put water on the east side, you are the only person on that site who can answer, and the speed and certainty of your answer is worth more than any instruction you would have given. <strong>Professional humility here is a competence, not a courtesy.</strong> Somebody who cannot hand over command is a hazard in the same way a wrong drawing is.

2. In the first twenty minutes, collect information rather than decisions - and log the time on every fact

<strong>The world shrinks to a tunnel and time stops behaving. That is not a failure of nerve - it is what an incident does to everybody, and the only defence is a structure you decided on beforehand.</strong> <strong>In the first twenty minutes your job is to build a picture, not to look decisive.</strong> <strong>Log every fact with the time it arrived and who gave it.</strong> Mine: <strong>nineteen reports in twenty minutes, of which six conflicted with another report.</strong> <strong>Now look at what the six actually were.</strong> <strong>Four were the same underlying fact described from different vantages</strong> - the control room saying pressure is falling and the field saying the line is holding, which are both true about different sides of a closed valve nobody had mentioned. <strong>Two were genuine disagreement</strong>, and those two are where your attention belongs. <strong>Resolving four apparent conflicts into one fact is the single most valuable thing you do in the first half hour</strong>, and it is done by asking what each person actually saw rather than which of them is right. <strong>Two exceptions to collecting before deciding, and only two: anything that stops somebody being hurt, and anything that stops the situation growing.</strong> Those you decide immediately and log afterwards. <strong>Everything else waits twenty minutes, and the waiting is a decision you are making on purpose.</strong>

3. Treat the people on the scene as the only source of what is actually happening - not as resources to be moved

<strong>You are in a room with a plan of the plant. They are standing next to it.</strong> <strong>Everything you will ever know about this incident comes through them, which makes how you talk to them the whole of your effectiveness.</strong> <strong>Ask what they saw, not what they think.</strong> 'What does it sound like' gets you a fact. 'Do you think it is the bearing' gets you their guess dressed as a report, and you will treat it as a fact an hour later. <strong>Ask the person who has been there longest what is unusual</strong>, because an operator who has heard that plant every day for nine years will notice a change no instrument is watching for. <strong>Mine came from exactly there.</strong> The operator said at three minutes past that the discharge note had changed. <strong>It was not acted on until fifty-two minutes past - forty-nine minutes - because it arrived in a sentence rather than on an instrument, and I was looking at instruments.</strong> <strong>Write that number down afterwards. It is the sharpest measure of your command that exists.</strong> <strong>And be careful with the person closest to the failure.</strong> They are standing in the room, they are frightened, and they are the first casualty of whatever went wrong rather than its cause. <strong>Your first act of leadership is to protect them from the question of who did this, and to keep asking what the system allowed.</strong> You will need what they know, and a frightened person protecting themselves is a person who has stopped telling you things.

4. Decide on a tempo, and log each decision with what you knew at the time

<strong>Set a tempo and hold it, because an incident with no rhythm becomes a series of interruptions and the person in charge becomes the busiest person rather than the one thinking.</strong> <strong>Every fifteen minutes: what is the situation now, what has changed since the last cycle, and what is the next decision.</strong> Say it out loud to the same group each time, so that everybody is working from one picture rather than several. <strong>Then log each decision with what was known WHEN IT WAS TAKEN.</strong> Not what you learned afterwards - what was on the table at that moment. <strong>This is the single most valuable record the incident will produce, and it takes about twenty seconds a decision.</strong> <strong>Two reasons, and the second matters more.</strong> <strong>The first is that it protects you</strong> - a decision that looks poor with hindsight often looks entirely reasonable against what was actually known at half past two, and without the log nobody can tell the difference, including you. <strong>The second is that it is the only way this becomes teachable.</strong> An incident that went well and cannot be reconstructed teaches the organisation nothing and leaves it depending on you being there next time. <strong>The goal is not a heroic performance. It is a boring, repeatable, auditable one that somebody else could run.</strong>

5. Hand over cleanly - and set up the enquiry before you are tired

<strong>You will hand over, either to the emergency services or to the next shift, and a bad handover loses everything the first hours bought.</strong> <strong>Four things, in this order, in under three minutes:</strong> <strong>what is happening now</strong>, <strong>what has been done and what is in progress</strong>, <strong>what is not yet known</strong> - which people skip and which is the most useful of the four - and <strong>what I am worried about</strong>, said plainly, because a concern that lives only in your head leaves the site when you do. <strong>Then, before you go home, do the two things that decide what anybody learns from this.</strong> <strong>Preserve the evidence.</strong> Say what must not be moved, cleaned, reset or overwritten, and say it to somebody who will still be here in the morning - because the trend data with a seven-day buffer will be gone by Tuesday and the failed component will be in a skip. <strong>And write down the question the enquiry will ask, now, while you are still angry and frightened and honest.</strong> <strong>Make it 'what in the system allowed this' and put it in writing before anybody else frames it as 'who did this'</strong>, because the second question will be asked by somebody in the next two days and it is much harder to redirect an enquiry than to aim one. <strong>You are the only person who can do this, and the window is about four hours wide.</strong>

Box 1 is settled on an ordinary afternoon, and box 3 is where the whole of your effectiveness actually lives. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Incident command is not deciding faster than other people. It is building, under pressure and out of fragments, a picture that is good enough to act on - and creating the calm in which the people who actually know things can tell you. Five moves:

  • Know your boundary. The asset is what you command. Settled beforehand, because you will not settle it well at 03:00.
  • Collect before you decide. Log every fact with its time and its source. Two exceptions only: harm now, and growth now.
  • The people on the scene are your instrument. Ask what they saw, not what they think.
  • A tempo, and a decision log. What was known WHEN it was taken.
  • Hand over cleanly, and aim the enquiry before you are tired.

The arithmetic of the first twenty minutes. Nineteen reports. Six conflicts. Of those six, four were the same fact from different vantages - the control room reporting falling pressure and the field reporting a line holding, both true about opposite sides of a closed valve nobody had mentioned - and two were genuine disagreement. Resolving four into one is the most valuable half hour's work available to you, and it is done by asking what each person saw rather than which of them is right.

The number that measures your command. The operator said at T+3 that the discharge note had changed. It was acted on at T+52. Forty-nine minutes, because it arrived in a sentence rather than on an instrument and the instruments were where everybody was looking. Write that number down afterwards. There is no better measure of whether the people on the scene were actually your instrument or merely present.

The boundary, and why it is a competence. You command the asset. You do not command an emergency scene, you are not a statutory incident controller, and when the emergency services arrive they are in command. Your role becomes technical adviser - and that is frequently the more consequential role, because when the fire chief asks what happens if water goes on the east side, you are the only person on that site who can answer. Somebody who cannot hand over command is a hazard in the same way a wrong drawing is.

On the person closest to the failure. They are in the room, they are frightened, and they are the first casualty of whatever went wrong rather than its cause. Protecting them from the question of who did this is not kindness at the expense of rigour - it is rigour. A frightened person protecting themselves stops telling you things, and what they know is what you need.

An honest boundary on this unit. This teaches command of the asset in an incident. It is not emergency response training, it does not qualify anybody as a statutory incident controller, and it does not cover directing fire, medical or police resources - those are separate disciplines with their own training, their own authority and their own law. Where your organisation has a designated emergency structure, it takes precedence over anything here. And no unit prepares you for the feeling; it only gives you something to hold when it arrives.

T4: Your Turn

You are the senior asset person on site. It is 02:10.

TimeWhat arrives
02:10High-level alarm, effluent balance tank. Operator acknowledges
02:14Operator radios: "there is a smell outside the pump house, sharp, not the usual"
02:16Control room: transfer pump running, discharge pressure normal

02:26Operator at pump house: "I cannot see the bund, it is dark, I think there is liquid"

  1. Two of these require a decision now, before any picture is complete. Identify them and say why each qualifies.
  2. Sort the rest. Which are facts, which are interpretations, and which pairs might be the same fact from different vantages?
  3. 02:14 and 02:26. These came from operators, in sentences, about things no instrument is watching. Say what you do with them and why they are the most valuable items in the list.
  4. The works manager at 02:27. What do you say, and how long do you spend? Be specific - this is a real trap.
  5. Your boundary. At what point in this sequence, if any, does your command end? Say what triggers it and what you become.
  6. It is now 06:00 and it is over. Name the three things you do before you go home, and say which of them has the narrowest window.

Work it before you turn over.

T5: One Way Through

1. The two that cannot wait. 02:22, the contractors. There is an unidentified sharp smell 40 m from people working at night in a confined gallery - that is potential harm now, and the answer is that they stop and withdraw to a known-safe point until you know what the smell is. You can be wrong about this at almost no cost and right at very large cost. 02:19 plus 02:26, the rising level with possible liquid in the bund. That is the situation growing, and it warrants an immediate move to stop the transfer or contain the escape, decided before you understand the cause.

2. Facts, interpretations, and the same fact twice. Facts: the alarm at 02:10, the level still rising at 02:19, the smell at 02:14, the darkness and possible liquid at 02:26. Interpretations: "instrument fault suspected" at 02:24 - that is a hypothesis wearing the clothes of a report, and if you write it in your log as a fault you will stop investigating it. The likely same fact: 02:16 normal pressure and 02:24 zero pressure are not necessarily a failing instrument; a pump that has lost suction, or a line that has opened to atmosphere, produces exactly that sequence - and 02:26's possible liquid is consistent with the second. Three reports, one story, and the story is not an instrument fault.

3. The two sentences. They are the most valuable items in the list precisely because no instrument is watching for them. A sharp unusual smell and liquid in a bund are both direct observations by people standing in the place. What you do with them: treat each as a fact, log it with the time and the person, and act on it - send somebody with a light to the bund, and treat the smell as unidentified until it is identified rather than until it is explained away. The failure mode this unit exists to prevent is exactly this: information arriving in a sentence while attention is on a screen.

4. The works manager. The trap is that this call will take twenty minutes if you let it, at the worst possible moment. What you say: "Level rising on the effluent tank, unidentified smell, possible spill to bund, contractors withdrawn, I am dealing with it. I will call you at three." Then you end the call. Twenty seconds, four facts, one commitment, and the commitment is what buys you the silence. A manager who has been told what is happening and when they will next hear will usually wait. A manager who has been told nothing will call every ten minutes, and will be right to.

5. Your boundary. It ends the moment this becomes an emergency requiring the emergency services - most likely at 02:14, on an unidentified sharp smell, which is a decision to call rather than a decision to investigate first. What triggers it: anything you cannot identify that could harm somebody, or any escape you cannot contain. What you become: technical adviser to whoever takes command, and the most useful person available to them - you know what is in the tank, what it does when it meets water, where the isolation points are, and what is downstream of the bund. That is not a smaller job.

6. Before you go home. Preserve the evidence - say what must not be reset, cleaned or overwritten, to somebody who will still be here at nine. Write the decision log up while you still remember what you knew at each point. Aim the enquiry - put in writing, tonight, that the question is what in the system allowed this. The narrowest window is the evidence. The trend data may roll off within days, the bund will be cleaned because cleaning it is the obviously responsible thing to do, and the operator's recollection of what they smelt at 02:14 will be reshaped by everything they hear in the next week. Two of the three can be done on Monday. That one cannot.

What you can do now that you could not before. Settle your boundary before you need it. Collect for twenty minutes while deciding only what genuinely cannot wait. Ask people what they saw. Hold a tempo and log what was known when. Hand over in four parts including what you do not know. And aim the enquiry while you are still honest.

The picture will not arrive complete. It never does, and the people who look at your decisions afterwards will have information you could not have had.

Command is not knowing what is happening. It is building a picture good enough to act on, out of fragments, from people who are frightened - and being the reason they can still tell you things.
