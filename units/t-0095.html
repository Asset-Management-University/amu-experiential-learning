The information your decisions can trust

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. You have your Asset Management Plan for the pumps - the "how" - and it feels good. Your first real action is to schedule this year's pump overhauls. It is a big call: thousands of rand, and a critical pump offline for days. You want to get it right.

So you open the asset register on the office computer. To decide, you need three things for each pump: when it was last overhauled, its total running hours, and its recent condition reports. The register says Pump 2 was overhauled eleven months ago. Good - it can wait.

Except. You remember the grease-caked logbook under the workbench. You dig it out. The last overhaul entry is from three years ago. The computer is lying. Uneasy now, you check the running-hours field: blank, for two of the three pumps. The condition reports? Not linked to anything - in a folder somewhere, maybe, if they exist at all.

You are stuck. You cannot make a defensible R80,000 decision, because the information you were told to trust is wrong, missing, or scattered. And here is the strange part: the problem is not that there is no data. Data is everywhere. It is just useless - you cannot tell which of it is true.

I am drowning in asset data and yet I cannot make one trustworthy decision, because I have no way to know which of it is true. How do I get information I can actually rely on, when it matters most?

Before you read on, decide. You have to call it: overhaul Pump 2 now, or in six months. It is an R80,000 decision that takes a critical pump offline, and you will have to defend it. The maintenance system has running hours. The condition record has vibration readings, though nobody is sure how complete they are. There is an overhaul date on a spreadsheet and a different one in the register. Somebody quotes a cost from memory. There is a great deal of data and you cannot tell which of it is true. Write down how you would get to a decision you could defend — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question none of them asks first

• “Clean up the data — get all the systems agreeing.” Months of work across everything you own, most of which no decision depends on, and the pump call is due now. It also has no natural end: cleaned against what standard, to what accuracy, maintained by whom?

• “Fit better instrumentation and start collecting properly.” That produces trustworthy data starting today, which is no help at all for a decision that turns on the last six months, and adds to the pile you already cannot navigate.

• “Go and inspect the pump myself.” Genuinely useful, and it answers one of the four things this call needs while telling you nothing about the other three, which are historical.

• “Ask the people who work on it — they know the truth.” They do know a great deal, and it is memory: unrecorded, unverifiable and not defensible six months from now when somebody asks why you spent R80,000.

• “Wait until the data is better.” The decision does not wait, and deferring it is a decision — the six-month option, chosen by default rather than on evidence.

• Notice that every one of them starts at the data. Start at the other end instead. Name the decision, and then ask what few facts it actually turns on — here, when it was last overhauled, how many hours it has run, what the vibration has been doing, and what an overhaul would cost. Four things, not four hundred. Then ask of each, separately, how good does this need to be for THIS decision — the overhaul date exact, the hours within about twenty, the vibration record complete enough to show a trend, the cost within about ten per cent — because that is what makes it affordable: a fact that must be perfect is expensive, and one that need only be roughly right may already exist. Then give each fact one home and one person answerable for it being true, which is what ends the arbitration between copies. You do not have a data problem. You have four facts to establish and no agreement about where they live.

What you need is not more data. It is a plan for your information - a deliberate design for what to trust. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is what a deliberate plan for information looks like. It is not the register itself - it is the blueprint for what the register must hold. And notice where it starts: not with the data, but with the decision. Here it is worked through for that R80,000 overhaul call.

1. The Decision (start here, always)

When should we overhaul Pump 2 - now, or in six months? It is an R80,000 call that takes a critical pump offline, so it has to be defensible.

2. The Information It Needs

To decide well we need four facts: the date of its last overhaul; its total running hours; the recent vibration trend; and a current overhaul cost estimate.

3. The Quality It Must Be (accuracy, completeness, timeliness)

Each fact only has to be as good as the decision needs. Last overhaul date: exact. Running hours: within about 20 hours, updated weekly. Vibration trend: at least 95% complete over the last six months. Cost estimate: within about 10%.

4. Where It Lives, and Who Keeps It True

Each fact has one agreed home and one owner who keeps it true: running hours in the maintenance system, logged by the shift operator; the vibration trend in the condition record, owned by the reliability engineer; the cost estimate from the finance system. One source of truth per fact - no more guessing which copy is right.

5. How We Check It Stays True

We earn trust by checking, not by assuming the screen is right. Each month the supervisor spot-checks the logged running hours against the pump's own meter; each year the overhaul dates are audited against the work orders. What is never checked cannot be trusted.

See the move? It starts from the decision and works back to the few facts that decision needs, says exactly how good each must be, gives each a single home and a named owner, and a way to check it stays true. That is the difference between drowning in data and trusting your information. Now let us walk through it, so you can build one yourself.

T3: The Blueprint

This is an Asset Information Strategy. It comes from ISO 55001, clause 7.6 ("data and information"). It exists to make sure the information you gather actually serves the decisions in your plan - instead of piling up in a data graveyard that no one trusts.

The whole secret is one move: start with the decision, then work back to the information. Think of cooking. You do not walk into the shop and buy one of everything; you decide what meal you are making, then write the list of ingredients it needs. Information is an ingredient for a decision - so you decide what you are making first. That single habit is the antidote to both poisons: the data graveyard (mountains of data collected and forgotten) and the data desert (nothing you can trust when you need it).

Now each box, as the question it answers:

  • 1. The Decision - What must we actually decide? This comes straight from your Asset Management Plan. No decision, no need for the information.
  • 2. The Information It Needs - What few facts would let us decide well? Both kinds count: technical and condition facts (running hours, vibration) and financial ones (the cost estimate).
  • 3. The Quality It Must Be - How good is good enough? This is the engineer's specification for data, and it has three sharp edges: accuracy (how correct - within 20 hours?), completeness (how much is there - 95% of readings?), and timeliness (how current - updated weekly?). Fit for the decision, not perfect for its own sake.
  • 4. Where It Lives, and Who Keeps It True - One home, one owner. Each fact gets a single source of truth and a named person responsible for it. Information that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, and quietly rots.
  • 5. How We Check It Stays True - How do we know it is still right? Trust is earned by verification, not assumed because it is on a screen. This is the audit, and it is what would have caught the lie about Pump 2.

(All of this is "documented information", which ISO 55001 clause 7.5 says must be properly controlled - stored, protected, updated, and kept.)

Does it scale? Just as Onalenna's strategy defines what information she needs to plan an R80,000 pump overhaul, a major airline's information strategy defines what data it needs - to a precise, audited quality - to decide when to service a R500-million jet engine. The logic is identical; only the scale differs.

T4: Your Turn

Now you design information you can trust. Take an asset you know well - a car, a laptop, a piece of lab or sports equipment. Name two real decisions you have to make about it (when to replace the tyres? when to back up or upgrade?). Then, for each, work across the five boxes: start from the decision, name the facts it needs, set how good they must be, decide where each lives and who keeps it true, and how you would check it.

Two decisions feels like a lot? Do just one, all the way across. The habit is the prize: decision first, information second.

T5: The Guardrails

A plan for information is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then strengthen any box that needs it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The data hoarder. Believing more data is always better. It is not - it builds expensive graveyards no one trusts. If a fact is not tied to a decision, do not collect it.

• Gold-plating. Demanding perfect accuracy for everything. The quality you need to track spare bolts is not the quality you need for a safety valve. Set quality that is fit for the decision, not flawless.

• The orphaned fact. Information with no named owner. If no one is responsible for keeping it accurate and current, it will decay - guaranteed. Every fact needs one home and one keeper.

• System before strategy. Believing a shiny new maintenance system will fix your information. A system is a tool, not a strategy. Put bad data into a fancy system and you just get faster, costlier bad data. Decide the rules first.

• The trust assumption. Believing a fact is right just because it is on a computer. That assumption is exactly what nearly cost Onalenna R80,000. Trust is earned by checking (box 5), never assumed.

Onalenna was not short of data that morning - she was drowning in it, and could not trust a drop. The cure was not more; it was a deliberate design for what to trust, built backwards from the decisions that matter. Now you hold that tool. Master it, and you will never again be paralysed by phantom data - you will know exactly what you need to know, and that it is true.
