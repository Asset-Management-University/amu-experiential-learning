Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Phila, the engineering manager at a plastics factory. The air hums with the rhythmic clatter of machinery spitting out thousands of bottle caps an hour. Your eyes are fixed on the newest machine on the line - a gleaming R2 million injection moulder, installed just six weeks ago. It should be the pride of the floor. Instead, it is your shame.

Every eight hours a small hydraulic filter clogs and the machine stops. To get to it, your technician has to remove three safety guards, lie on the greasy floor, and use a special wrench to reach past a hot pipe - a 30-minute job that halts the entire line. The supplier's manual says the filter should last 1,000 hours; it is failing after eight, because the machine cannot handle the production rate it was bought for without overheating the hydraulics. Your team is tired and getting cynical. "It's brand new, boss," your lead technician said this morning, wiping oil from his hands. "It feels like it was designed by someone who has never had to fix anything."

You look at the shiny machine, a monument to a bad decision.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fix the symptoms - to maintain your way out of it. You rewrite the schedule to replace the filter every shift, before it fails. But this just plans for the failure: it adds cost and downtime, and your technicians are still wrestling with the terrible access. Next you wonder if the operators are to blame, so you run a refresher training session - but the data shows they are running it exactly as specified; the machine simply cannot do the duty it was bought for without cooking its own hydraulics. They are doing their best with a tool that is not right for the job. Finally you call the supplier in a rage. The project manager is calm. He pulls out the purchase specification you signed and points to the technical data sheet: "we have delivered the machine you ordered. It meets every clause in the contract." He is right. The problem was not in what they delivered. The problem was in what you asked for.

So before you read on: if you cannot maintain it, train it, or shout it better - where was this misery actually decided, and how do you stop buying it next time?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you realise the machine's entire life of misery was decided the day that specification was written. Its reliability, its running costs, and how hard it would be to fix were already locked in. You cannot maintain your way out of a bad design - the real leverage was never on the factory floor, it was in the office, in what you asked for before you bought. Five moves change how you buy the next asset:

  • See it - reliability is designed in, not maintained in: the breakthrough is realising the machine's whole life of misery was decided the day the specification was written. By the time a new asset arrives, most of its reliability, its running cost and how hard it will be to maintain are already locked in - you can no more maintain them in afterwards than you can diet your way out of a meal already eaten. So stop judging a purchase by output and sticker price alone, and stop looking for the answer on the factory floor: the real leverage is upstream, in the office, in what you ask for before anyone builds anything. You cannot maintain your way out of a bad design - which means the cure for next time is not better heroics after delivery, it is a better specification before purchase.
  • Specify for the whole life and the real duty: a spec written for the lowest purchase price buys exactly that - the lowest purchase price, and a lifetime of the costs it hid. So specify for the WHOLE LIFE and the REAL duty: the conditions the asset must actually survive (your true production rate, your dust, your heat, your hours) and the total cost to own it, not just to buy it. Build a simple total-cost-of-ownership view and the picture flips - the R2 million machine that is cheap to buy but guzzles energy and stops the line becomes the most expensive on the floor, and the R2.4 million alternative suddenly looks like the bargain it is. This is bp-0086's whole-life-cost discipline and bp-0076's decision-criterion, applied at the moment you write the spec: the cheap-to-buy lemon can only win if the spec lets it.
  • Put maintainability in the spec - and bring in the people who fix it: operability and maintainability do not happen by accident; they happen because someone demanded them in writing. Put them in the spec - every routine service point reachable without removing major components or special gymnastics, safe isolation, the right access and clearances - and, crucially, bring in the people who will actually run and fix the thing. Send your lead technician to the supplier and have him ask 'show me how you change this part; now show me how you calibrate that sensor', and make his verdict a condition of the sale. The people who will live with the asset for twenty years know what makes it maintainable far better than the brochure does; a spec written without them is a spec written by people who will never have to fix it.
  • Demand evidence, not brochures: a brochure is a promise the supplier writes; evidence is a promise they have to keep. So before you accept delivery, make the supplier PROVE the asset meets your duty rather than letting you discover later that it does not. Ask for real references from sites running the same machine under conditions like yours, and write a site-acceptance test into the contract - it must run, say, 72 hours straight at your full production rate before you sign it off and pay. That single clause shifts the risk back to where it belongs: the supplier proves the reliability up front, on your floor, instead of you finding the truth six weeks in with a clogging filter and a stopped line. Trust the demonstrated performance, never the glossy claim (the same trust-but-verify discipline as bp-0100 and bp-0123).
  • Close the loop from service back into the next spec: the failures you suffer are paid for; the least you can do is learn from them. Capture what actually fails in service - which parts, how often, why (this is what bp-0154's work history and bp-0134's root-cause work are FOR) - and feed it straight into the specification for the next purchase: 'never again do we buy a pump with a sealed bearing from that supplier; every moulder must hold its filter for 500 hours at our rate.' Without this loop you buy the same lemon again and again, each time surprised; with it, every painful lesson becomes a clause that protects the next purchase. The spec is where the organisation's hard-won experience either lives or is thrown away.

See it change Phila's next purchase. He stops judging on output and price alone and builds a total-cost-of-ownership view: the R2 million machine, cheap to buy, is the most expensive on the floor once its energy and downtime are counted, and the R2.4 million alternative is suddenly the bargain. He makes his lead technician part of the buying team, sending him to the supplier's factory to ask "show me how you change this part, now show me how you calibrate that sensor" - and his verdict becomes a condition of sale. He demands references from sites running the machine at his real duty, and writes a site-acceptance test into the contract: 72 hours straight at full rate before he signs. And he starts a simple log of what fails on the old machines and hands it to procurement: "never again do we buy that part from that supplier." An asset's whole life is mostly decided before it ever arrives. Its reliability, running cost and maintainability are not things you add later with good maintenance - they are things you demand at the very beginning: specify for the whole life and the real duty, put operability and maintainability in the spec, bring in the people who will run and fix it, demand evidence not brochures, and feed every failure back into the next specification. You cannot maintain your way out of a bad design, so specify and buy for the life the asset must actually live, not just for the day it is delivered.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - think about buying a cheap, unbranded kettle.

See the whole-life choice in a kettle

It costs R150, half the price of a known brand, so you buy it. A month later the automatic shut-off fails - a fire risk. You cannot find spares; the screws to open it are a strange shape; and even if you got inside, you would not know what to do. It was cheap to buy, but its short life and the risk it creates make it incredibly expensive.

A smart buyer does not just look at the price tag. They ask: what is the warranty? Are spares available? Do the reviews say it lasts? They are specifying for its whole life - a life of safely boiling water, day after day - and they pay R300 for the kettle that lasts five years rather than R150 for the one that lasts five weeks. They buy for reliability, not just for price. Your machines, your vehicles, your systems are the same, only with far more zeros.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at how your organisation buys new things - not just huge machines, but vehicles, IT equipment, software, even office furniture. Where do you focus only on the upfront price, and then suffer the consequences for years? Where do your new assets arrive with their problems already built in? You can stop the cycle.

  1. Specify for the whole life. Next time you buy, ask about the cost of energy, spares and common failures, and add them to the purchase price to see the true cost.
  2. Put maintainability in the spec. Add a simple clause: "all routine service points must be accessible without special tools or the removal of major components."
  3. Involve the end-user. Before you buy the software, let the team who will use it test it; before the vehicle, let the driver and the mechanic look it over.
  4. Demand proof. Ask for real-world evidence the asset can do your job, and feed the failures of your old assets back into the specification for the new ones.

This was never about a careless buyer or engineer - they were following a system that rewarded the lowest purchase price and judged procurement on the day of delivery, not the decade after it. The fix is to change what the system asks for. You have the power to stop buying new problems: by specifying for the life you need, you make the lives of your people easier from the very first day, and you build a more reliable, less frustrating world to work in.
