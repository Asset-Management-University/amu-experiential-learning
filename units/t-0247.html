A promise cast in concrete

T1: The Ache

The design was cheap to build. It looked right on the drawing, it came in on budget, and it was signed off. And now it will be expensive to live with for twenty years.

Every repair on the main pump means a permit and a broken-out wall. Every routine sample means an operator leaning over an open channel. The strainer that needs cleaning every day sits behind a fixed guard that takes two people and a permit to open - so the cleaning quietly stops happening. None of this was a construction fault. It was designed in, and no one caught it, because no one walked the drawing as the person who would have to live with it. The fault was a line on a page that became a two-decade burden.

The design looked fine on the drawing and came in cheap - so why is it a costly, dangerous misery to operate and maintain? How do I appraise a design before it is built, when a fault is still just a line on a page?

Before you read on, decide. It came in cheap and it looked clean on the drawing. Two years on: the pump cannot be withdrawn without dismantling pipework, so a bearing change is a permit job with scaffolding and two people instead of an hour with one. The strainer must be opened to reach the instrument behind it. And the monthly inspection on the upper walkway has quietly stopped happening — nobody decided that, it just became something people did not get to, because getting there is unpleasant and slightly frightening. None of it is a construction fault; it was all built exactly as drawn. Nobody ever walked that drawing as the fitter who would live with it. Write down how you would appraise a design before it is built — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what is actually being appraised

• “Have the engineers check it more carefully.” They did check it, and it is technically correct — it does what it was designed to do. Technical correctness and operability are different questions, and only one of them was asked.

• “Specify better in the tender.” Necessary and insufficient — a specification lists requirements, and these faults live in the arrangement: what sits in front of what, and how much room is left. That is visible on a drawing and invisible in a clause.

• “Fix it now — modify the pipework.” You will have to, at many times what it would have cost as a line on a page. That ratio is the entire argument for appraising earlier.

• “Award on whole-life cost rather than capital.” Right in principle and it needs somebody able to see, in a drawing, that this arrangement costs two people and a permit every time. Without that reading, whole-life cost is a formula fed by guesses.

• “Tell the crew the inspection is mandatory.” The inspection stopped because reaching it is unpleasant and unsafe-feeling. Insisting harder produces either a resented task or a signed sheet, and one of those is worse than the gap.

• The great error is appraising a design on how it looks and what it costs to build — when the build cost is the small part, and the great majority of what this asset will cost across its life was settled while it was still on paper. And it is not only money that was fixed at design. What was also decided is the daily working life of everybody who operates and maintains it: whether they work in safety or in danger, with reasonable effort or with heroics, with dignity or with a permit and a crowbar. A design is a promise cast in steel and concrete about the next twenty years of other people's work, and that promise is what you are appraising — not a drawing. So judge it against what it will be, on criteria that are human as much as technical: can it be operated and maintained without putting people in harm; can an ordinary person run it without awkward or dangerous reaches; and can the things that must be maintained actually be reached, isolated, removed and replaced — because maintainability is a design property, not a matter of later effort. And you find these faults by walking the drawing in the shoes of the people who will live with it, which is the thing nobody did. On paper every one of them was free.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you appraise an asset design - seeing that a design is a promise about decades, judging it against the whole-life human criteria, walking it through the eyes of the people who will run and maintain and live beside it, and catching the faults the drawing hides while they are still cheap to fix.

1. A design is a promise about decades

The great error is to appraise a design on how it LOOKS on the drawing and what it costs to BUILD. But the build cost is the small part. A COMMON RULE OF THUMB IN ASSET MANAGEMENT HOLDS THAT 80 PER CENT OR MORE OF AN ASSET'S WHOLE-LIFE COST IS FIXED DURING ITS DESIGN. THE DECISIONS YOU MAKE ON PAPER DETERMINE THE COST IN REALITY FOR DECADES. And it is not only money that is fixed at design - it is the daily working life of every person who will operate and maintain the thing: whether they work in safety or in danger, with reasonable effort or with heroics, with dignity or with a permit and a crowbar. A DESIGN IS A PROMISE CAST IN STEEL AND CONCRETE about the next twenty years of other people's work. You are not appraising a drawing; you are appraising that promise.

2. The whole-life human criteria

You judge a design against what it WILL BE, not how it looks. SAFETY: can it be operated and maintained without putting people in harm? OPERABILITY: can an ordinary operator run it without heroics or awkward, dangerous reaches? MAINTAINABILITY - this is a design property, appraised against clear criteria: CAN IT BE ACCESSED, ISOLATED, CLEANED, TESTED, AND REPAIRED WITH REASONABLE EFFORT? WHOLE-LIFE COST: the cost to operate and maintain it over its whole life, which DWARFS the capital cost and is mostly locked in here. And ENVIRONMENTAL AND SOCIAL FIT: its footprint, and its effect on the community that lives alongside it and depends on what it produces. A design that scores beautifully on capital cost and elegance and badly on these has FAILED the appraisal, not passed it.

3. Walk the design, human-centred

Here is the move that separates a real appraisal from a paper one, and it is the HUMAN-CENTRED, design-thinking contribution: before you sign off, you WALK THE DESIGN THROUGH THE EYES OF THE PEOPLE WHO WILL LIVE WITH IT. You EMPATHISE - you stand where the maintainer will stand ('to change this pump I would have to remove that wall'), where the operator will stand ('the only sample point is at head height over an open channel'), where the neighbour will stand ('the discharge runs past the allotments'). You TEST THE HARD TASKS - you walk through the pump replacement, the daily clean, the annual inspection with the people who will actually do them, and you watch where the design fights them. A PURELY TECHNICAL REVIEW CHECKS THAT THE NUMBERS ARE RIGHT; A HUMAN-CENTRED APPRAISAL CHECKS THAT A HUMAN BEING CAN ACTUALLY LIVE WITH THE RESULT.

4. Surface what the drawing hides

The costliest and cruellest design faults are precisely the ones a technical review CANNOT see, because they are not errors in the calculation - they are the lived consequences of a correct calculation laid out without regard for the person. A DESIGN THAT IS TECHNICALLY ELEGANT BUT DIMINISHES THE PERSON WHO MUST OPERATE OR MAINTAIN IT IS A FAILED DESIGN. A PUMP THAT REQUIRES A MAINTAINER TO CRAWL INTO A CONFINED, HOT SPACE IS A FAILURE. A SAMPLE POINT THAT FORCES AN OPERATOR TO LEAN OVER AN UNGUARDED CHANNEL IS A FAILURE. THE APPRAISAL IS NOT COMPLETE UNTIL THE DESIGN PASSES THE DIGNITY TEST. None of these is visible as a fault on the drawing; all are obvious the moment you stand where the person will stand.

5. The verdict - honour the people

So the verdict is not 'is this design technically elegant?' but the one question that governs the whole appraisal: DOES THIS DESIGN HONOUR THE PEOPLE WHO WILL LIVE WITH IT? And the mercy of appraising early is this: WHEN YOU CATCH A FAULT ON PAPER, THE FIX IS A LINE ON A DRAWING; catch it after the concrete is poured, and the fix is a DEMOLITION CREW, or twenty years of people paying for it with their effort and their safety. A design either honours the people who will live with it, or it burdens them - and your appraisal decides which. Fix it on paper, not in concrete, and never sign off a design that diminishes the people who must run and maintain it.

T4: Your Turn

Onalenna hands you a proposed design for a small chemical-dosing station at Corathyl. The drawing shows the layout, the key equipment, and the access points, and it is on budget. Your job is to appraise the promise, not the drawing.

Two facts about the life of this asset: an operator takes a chemical sample here every day; and the primary dosing pump fails and must be replaced roughly every eighteen months.

On the drawing you notice: the sample tap is mounted high on the wall, directly above the open dosing tank; and the dosing pump is installed in a recess boxed in by pipework on three sides, with a fixed guard across the front.

  1. Walk the design. Appraise it as (a) the operator taking the daily sample and (b) the maintainer replacing that pump - stand where each of them stands.
  2. Surface the faults. Name at least two faults not obvious from a purely technical review - one operability/safety, one maintainability.
  3. Name the cost. For each fault, describe the whole-life cost in money (downtime, labour), risk (safety), and human experience (difficulty, indignity).
  4. Give the verdict. State your verdict against the one question - does this design honour the people who will work here - and recommend the specific changes required.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the walk: as the operator, taking a daily sample from a tap mounted high over an open tank means reaching and leaning over an open chemical surface every single day. As the maintainer, replacing a pump boxed in by pipework on three sides behind a fixed guard means dismantling surrounding pipework and removing the guard every eighteen months just to reach it.

Second, the faults: an operability/safety fault - the sample point forces a daily reach over an open chemical tank, a splash-and-fall hazard designed into a routine task; and a maintainability fault - the pump has no access clearance and no removal path, so a routine, predictable replacement becomes a major strip-down every time.

Third, the cost: the sample point costs little money but carries a daily safety risk and a quiet indignity (an operator made to do something unsafe to do their job), and safety risk repeated daily for years is how incidents eventually happen. The pump access costs real money and time - hours of extra labour and days of extra downtime at every failure, multiplied across a twenty-year life - plus the frustration and rushed corner-cutting that cramped, awkward maintenance breeds.

Fourth, the verdict: this design does not yet honour the people who will work here, so it is not approved as drawn - but both faults are cheap to fix on paper. Move the sample point to a safe, reachable location away from the open tank (or enclose the tank and pipe the sample to an accessible tap), and give the pump a clear access route and removal clearance with the guard designed for tool-free, one-person access. Fixed on the drawing, this is a good design; built as drawn, it is a twenty-year burden. Fix it now, while the fix is an eraser.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Appraising the drawing, not the promise. Elegance and capital cost are the small part. Most of the whole-life cost and all of the daily human experience are fixed at design - appraise for the twenty years, not the picture.

• Judging by build cost. The cheapest to build is often the costliest to own. Weigh the operate-and-maintain cost over the whole life, which dwarfs the capital cost.

• Skipping the people. A design review without the operators and maintainers in the room misses the faults only they can see. Walk it human-centred, or you appraise blind.

• A technical-only review. Correct numbers can still lay out a safety trap or a maintenance nightmare. The calculation being right is not the design being livable.

• Designing in a dignity trap. A pump that needs a maintainer to crawl into a hot, cramped space, or a control that makes an operator lean over moving machinery, is a failed design however elegant. The dignity test is not optional.

• Deferring the fix to after build. On paper the fix is a line; in concrete it is a demolition crew, or people paying for it for two decades. Catch it while it is still cheap.

• Treating maintainability as an afterthought. Access, isolation, cleaning, testing and repair are design properties, decided now. Leaving them for the people on the ground to solve later is leaving them an impossible job.
