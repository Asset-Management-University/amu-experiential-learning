A system running on one person's conscience

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and by every ordinary measure you have done this job right. There is a policy. There is a SAMP you built and the board celebrated. There are costed plans, and the work gets done. You are good at this.

Two years later, a pump station is overwhelmed - the region has outgrown its capacity, badly and suddenly. A board member asks the obvious question: how did we not see this coming? And the answer, when you trace it, is brutal. The region grew faster than the SAMP assumed. But the SAMP was approved once and never looked at again. Decisions quietly drifted away from it, month by month, and nobody caught the drift - because there was no review, no oversight, and no one at board level actually tasked with watching the asset-management system as a system.

It all rested on you paying attention. And the one thing you could never do, however conscientious you were, was oversee your own work. Good management. Zero governance. And the gap was invisible right up until the moment it wasn't.

Everything I built works only as long as I personally keep watch - and I cannot check my own work. How do I design the structure that directs this system, oversees it independently, and holds someone to account, so it does not depend on one person's conscience?

Before you read on, decide. The system is good. You built it, it works, and nobody can fault the management of it. You were away for two weeks and something consequential went through unchecked — nothing dramatic, nothing anybody was careless about; it simply passed, because the person who would have caught it was you. You write the plans, you do the work, and you are the only one who checks any of it. Someone senior said last month that the assets are in good hands, and took the fact that it all runs smoothly as evidence that it is being overseen. Write down what is actually missing — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the one thing you can never do

• “Be more thorough — build better checks into my routine.” More of the thing that already works, added to the same pair of hands. Every check you design is a check you are also performing, so the dependency is untouched and the exposure is identical.

• “Document everything so somebody could pick it up.” Genuinely worth doing, and it addresses continuity rather than oversight. Perfect documentation of a system nobody independent is looking at is a well-described blind spot.

• “Hire an assistant to help carry the load.” More capacity in the same function. Somebody who reports to you and works to your judgement is not in a position to find your errors, however capable.

• “Bring in an external audit each year.” Closer, and as a stand-alone it is a snapshot from outside with no standing between visits, no authority to decide anything, and nobody accountable for acting on what it finds.

• “Nothing is wrong — the system works.” It does, and that is precisely why this is invisible. A well-run system looks exactly like a well-governed one from the inside, which is what your senior colleague was reading when he said the assets were in good hands.

• The flaw is structural rather than personal, and it has one sharp form: however conscientious anybody is, nobody can oversee their own work. One pair of hands doing the work and checking it gives you excellent management and zero governance, and the two get confused because they look identical from within. So separate running the system from directing and overseeing it, and put them in different hands — the second is a different job, not a more senior version of the first: it sets the direction, approves the plan, and holds the running of it to account. Three things make that real rather than nominal. Decision rights must be explicit — who recommends, who approves, who is accountable — because unstated authority collapses straight back onto whoever is most conscientious, which is how you got here. One named person must be accountable for the system working, since a responsibility shared by a committee is held by nobody. And review happens on fixed dates, not when something goes wrong — a review triggered by trouble is not oversight, it is a reaction, and it arrives after the thing it was supposed to prevent.

That structure has a name - governance - and it fits on one page. Here it is, for the water board.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the governance framework and cycle Onalenna should have designed the day the SAMP was approved.

1. Governance or management? (draw the line, and keep the hats separate)

Two different jobs, and they must not be the same hands. MANAGEMENT runs the asset-management system day to day - Onalenna and her team writing plans, doing the work, keeping the plant reliable. GOVERNANCE sits above it: the board sets the direction (the policy), approves the SAMP, and holds management to account for delivering the organisational objectives. The failure at the root of the ache was that these were fused - the person doing the work was the only one watching it. You cannot oversee your own work; nobody can. Separate the hats first, or nothing below matters.

2. Who is accountable, and who decides what? (roles, decision rights, the AM Council charter)

THE GOVERNING BODY (the regional water board): sets policy, approves the SAMP, holds management to account. THE AM COUNCIL: a cross-functional steering group - operations, finance, engineering - that coordinates asset direction; its CHARTER, one line: 'purpose - align asset decisions to the SAMP; members - heads of ops/finance/engineering; authority - recommends the SAMP and prioritises the capital programme; meets quarterly.' THE AM SYSTEM OWNER: one named senior person accountable for the system working. DECISION RIGHTS made explicit: the Council RECOMMENDS the SAMP; the board APPROVES it; the system owner is CONSULTED; the plant is INFORMED. No one should have to guess who decides.

3. How does the board KNOW it is working - independently? (the three lines)

The three lines model. FIRST LINE: management runs the assets and owns the risks (Onalenna's team). SECOND LINE: oversight functions - risk, compliance, safety - that monitor and advise. THIRD LINE: an INDEPENDENT internal audit that reports straight to the board, not through management. The point of the third line is brutal and necessary: if every assurance the board receives comes through the people being assured about, the board is blind. Independent audit is how a mistake at the wheel gets caught by someone who is not steering.

4. On what schedule is it reviewed? (the governance cycle - with dates, not good intentions)

The rhythm that would have prevented the ache. QUARTERLY: a performance report to the board against the SAMP objectives. ANNUALLY: a formal management review of the whole asset-management system - is it delivering, what has changed, what must adjust. EVERY ~5 YEARS: a strategic refresh of the SAMP itself, because a 20-year plan cannot be a set-and-forget document. These are SCHEDULED DATES in the calendar, not 'when someone remembers'. A governance cycle with no dates is not a cycle - it is a hope, and the SAMP went stale on exactly that hope.

5. When it misses, who acts - and does it close? (the accountability loop)

A finding with no owner is a finding that recurs. When the quarterly report shows an objective slipping, or the third line raises a gap, it becomes a corrective action with a NAMED owner, a defined action, and a DEADLINE - logged, tracked, and reported back to the board as closed or not. The stale SAMP would have been flagged at the first annual review, given an owner, and refreshed - the loop would have caught it a year before the pump station was overwhelmed. Governance is not the paperwork; it is the guarantee that a known problem cannot quietly stay open.

Notice that Onalenna's name barely appears - and that is the whole point. Every box builds a structure that works whether or not any one person is paying attention: someone other than the doer checks the work, someone independent tells the board the truth, the reviews happen on fixed dates, and a known problem cannot quietly stay open. This is a system given a conscience of its own.

T3: The Blueprint

Everything here rests on one distinction, and if you get it wrong nothing else works. Management runs the system - does the work, keeps the plant reliable. Governance directs and controls it - sets the direction, provides oversight, and holds management to account for delivering the organisation's objectives. Governance is the mind; management is the hands; and the failure in the ache was that they were the same person. Five questions build it:

  • Who directs and oversees this, versus who runs it? Draw the line between governance and management and keep the hats separate. The people who do the work cannot be the only ones who check it - not because they are dishonest, but because no one can see their own blind spot. This separation is the foundation; the other four boxes are built on it.
  • Who is accountable, and who decides what? Name the governing body (the board - approves the policy and the SAMP, holds management to account), the AM Council (a cross-functional steering group that coordinates asset direction) with a charter that states its purpose, members, authority and cadence, and a single named system owner. Then make the decision rights explicit - who recommends the SAMP, who approves it, who is consulted, who is informed - so no one ever has to guess who decides.
  • How does the board KNOW it is working - independently? Use the three lines model. The first line runs the assets and owns the risk (management). The second line is oversight - risk, compliance, safety - monitoring and advising. The third line is an independent audit that reports straight to the board, not through management. That independence is the crux: if every assurance the board hears comes through the very people it is being assured about, the board is blind. The third line is how a mistake at the wheel gets caught by someone who is not steering.
  • On what schedule is it reviewed? The governance cycle is a rhythm with real dates: routine performance reporting, an annual management review of the whole system, and a periodic (commonly around five-yearly) strategic refresh of the SAMP - because a multi-decade plan cannot be set-and-forget. A cycle with no dates in the calendar is not a cycle; it is the exact hope on which the SAMP went stale.
  • When it misses, who acts, and does it close? The accountability loop: every performance miss or audit finding becomes a corrective action with a named owner, a defined action, and a deadline, tracked to closure and reported back up. A finding with no owner recurs. This loop is the guarantee that a known problem cannot quietly stay open - which is the one guarantee the ache was missing.

One honest boundary. You are the architect, not the king. Your job is to design the council chamber and the rules of court - the structure by which the board governs - not to sit on the throne yourself. You do not govern (that is the board), you are not re-writing the SAMP or the policy, and governance is not management. Governance directs and checks; management does; and they cannot be the same hands.

Does it scale? A ship's navigator sets the course and the captain oversees the voyage while the helmsman steers - three roles on purpose, so that an error at the wheel is caught by someone whose job is to be watching, not steering.

T4: Your Turn

A different organisation: a hospital that runs its own estate - buildings, backup power, medical-gas systems, water. It has good facilities managers and no asset-management governance. Design it.

  1. The AM Council charter, in one line. Purpose, who sits on it, what authority it has, how often it meets.
  2. The three lines. Assign each line to a real role in a hospital estate - who is first line, who second, who is the independent third line reporting to the board?
  3. The cycle. Set the governance-cycle dates: what is reported, how often, and when is the SAMP refreshed?
  4. One decision right. State clearly who approves the estate's SAMP versus who recommends it - and why those must be different.

The test of a real answer: look at your third line. If the person auditing the estate reports to the person running the estate, you have not built a third line - you have built management checking itself. The independence is the whole point; if it is not there, redraw it.

T5: The Guardrails

Governance goes wrong in a handful of ways, and most of them are governance in name only.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Management with a grander name. If the people who do the work are also the only ones who review it, you have not added governance - you have relabelled management. The separation of hats is the non-negotiable.

• No independent third line. Assurance that flows entirely through the people being assured about tells the board nothing. Independent audit reporting to the board is what makes the assurance worth having.

• An AM Council with no charter. Without a stated purpose, membership and authority, a council has no teeth and drifts into a monthly talking shop. The charter is what gives it the right to decide.

• A cycle with no dates. "We review it regularly" is not a cycle. Fixed dates - this quarter's report, this year's management review, this decade's SAMP refresh - are what make oversight happen instead of intending to.

• Approving the SAMP once and never refreshing it. The exact failure that started the ache. A strategy is a living document; a governance cycle that never revisits it guarantees it goes stale where nobody is looking.

• A corrective-action loop that never closes. Logging findings and never resolving them is worse than not logging them, because it looks like control while being none. Every finding needs an owner and a deadline, tracked to closed.

• Mistaking governance for bureaucracy. Governance is oversight proportionate to consequence, not paperwork for its own sake. If a control does not change a decision or catch a risk, it is not governance - it is friction, and it should go.

Onalenna built a system that worked beautifully and depended entirely on her never looking away - and one year of not looking was enough to overwhelm a pump station. Now you can build the other thing: a structure that directs the system, oversees it with eyes that are not management's own, reviews it on fixed dates, and closes every gap it finds. That is governance - and it is the difference between a system that has one person's conscience and a system that has a conscience of its own.
