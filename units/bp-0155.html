Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Anathi, a works coordinator at a large processing plant. It is 9am on Monday and you are staring at the screen of new work requests. It feels less like a to-do list and more like a pile of riddles.

  • Request 7781: "Pump in Area 4 is broken." There are six pumps in Area 4. Which one? And does "broken" mean leaking, noisy, or not running at all? You cannot plan the job.
  • Request 7782: "Fix the thing on the packing line." The packing line is fifty metres long. What thing?
  • Requests 7783, 7784 and 7785: three different shift supervisors, all for the same fault - "Conveyor B making a loud squealing noise." Now you have three jobs in the system for one fault, and if you are not careful you will send three people, or order the same parts twice.

You spend your morning phoning people and walking the plant floor, trying to translate vague clues into actual work. You are a planner, but you have become a full-time detective. The technicians are frustrated, the operators are not getting their problems fixed, and the system is choking.

I cannot even tell what actually needs doing from these requests - the whole system is choking on bad information at the front door; how do I fix this?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fix the symptoms - to force the system to work. "I will chase down every requester for the real story." This works for a day or two, then you get nothing else done: you become a human translation service, it does not scale, the next batch of fifty requests is just as bad, and you are still the bottleneck. "I will get tough - a new form with twenty mandatory fields so nothing is missed." Asset number, sub-location, fault code, consequence score, suspected part... and the result? Nobody uses it. The form is so difficult that people stop raising jobs in the system altogether, catching technicians in the corridor or leaving sticky notes on your desk; the front door is now completely bypassed, and the chaos is worse. "Fine - I will just approve the mess and let the planners sort it downstream." But they cannot. Garbage in, garbage out: technicians are sent to Area 4 and have to call back "which pump?", the wrong work gets scheduled on a requester's bad guess, and the whole system is built on sand.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop trying to fix individual bad requests and focus on the source. You realise the entire work-management system can only ever be as good as the requests that enter it - no amount of clever planning or prioritising downstream can rescue a job that was vague, duplicated or wrong from the start. Your mission is to guard the front door. Five moves:

  • The front door sets the ceiling: the breakthrough is to stop firefighting individual bad requests and see the system whole: a work-management system can only ever be as good as the requests that enter it, because every step that follows - planning, prioritising, scheduling, doing the work, recording the history - inherits the quality of that first request. A brilliant planning team fed by 'fix the thing in area 3' still produces chaos; the cleverness downstream cannot rescue what was broken at the source. This is just the oldest rule in information: garbage in, garbage out. So the highest-leverage place to act is not the planners' desks or the technicians' vans - it is the FRONT DOOR, the moment a request is raised. Fix the quality of what comes in, and a hundred downstream problems you have been firefighting one by one simply stop being created. Guard the door, and you stop mopping the floor.
  • Capture what, where and why: a request you can actually act on answers three plain questions, every time: WHAT is wrong (the symptom or condition - 'leaking', 'squealing', 'will not start', not just 'broken'), WHERE exactly (a specific asset and its ID or tag, not 'a pump in Area 4' when there are six), and WHY it matters (the consequence or risk if it is not done - a safety hazard, a stopped line, a slow leak). Those three - what, where, why - are the 'minimum viable request', the least information that lets a planner understand and judge the job without launching a detective hunt down the plant floor. Most of the morning you lost to phone calls was spent extracting exactly these three facts that should have arrived with the request. So name them as the standard, make them the required heart of every request, and the translation work that was eating your day disappears - because the information arrives complete.
  • Describe the need, not a guessed solution: this is the subtle one that quietly causes the most wrong work: requesters naturally jump straight to a solution - 'replace the pump seal', 'order a new motor' - when what they have actually observed is a symptom - 'the floor is wet under the pump'. The trouble is that the guessed solution is often wrong (the water may be a cracked pipe, not the seal), but once it is written as the request, the system dutifully does it, the real fault remains, and the job comes back. So coach people to describe the NEED - what they see, hear, smell, what is or is not happening - and leave the diagnosis to the planner and technician who can find the right fix. Ask 'what is it doing?' rather than 'what part do you need?'. Capturing the honest symptom instead of a confident guess is the difference between fixing the problem and merely doing a task - and it is how you stop paying to solve the wrong thing.
  • Screen and de-duplicate at intake: a clean system needs a single point of entry with a quick, light screen - at first it can simply be you, spending thirty seconds on each new request before it joins the queue. That tiny check does three powerful things. It DE-DUPLICATES: the three 'squealing Conveyor B' jobs from three shifts become one, so you do not dispatch three crews or order parts twice. It spots REPEATS: a fault that keeps coming back is not just another work order to raise - it is a signal to investigate the root cause (the realm of root-cause analysis), so you stop patching the same failure forever. And it FILTERS: the occasional request that is not actually needed, or belongs elsewhere, is caught before it clogs the works. A few seconds of screening at the door saves hours of duplicated, wasted and wrong work behind it - it is the cheapest, highest-return habit in the whole system.
  • Make the good request the easy request: here is the move that makes all the others stick, and the one the 20-field form got exactly backwards: people will give you a good request only if a good request is the EASIEST thing to raise. If the proper channel is a slow, punishing form, they route around it - corridor conversations, sticky notes, shouting - and the front door is bypassed, which is worse than a bad request, because now the work is invisible. So design the path of least resistance to ALSO be the path of best information: a simple, clean form on a tablet on the floor with just the essentials - WHERE is it (a pick-list of assets, so the ID is captured without anyone typing it), WHAT is the problem (a free box for the symptom), and a button to add a PHOTO (a picture often says more than a paragraph). When raising a clear, complete request is faster than catching a technician in the corridor, people do it - and the front door fixes itself. Make the right way the easy way, and good information flows in on its own.

See the chaos drain away for Anathi. Clear, actionable requests start arriving: a specific asset ID, a real symptom, a photo. Planners can plan. Technicians are sent to the right place with the right parts to fix the right problem, first time. The backlog, now clean, can be managed properly, and the most important jobs prioritised effectively. She is no longer a detective translating riddles - she is running a system that works. A work-management system is only as good as the requests that feed it - garbage in, garbage out. To stop the chaos downstream, guard the front door upstream: make a good request - one that is clear on the what, where and why, describes the need rather than a guessed solution, and is checked for duplicates - the easiest and the normal way for work to enter. Fix the source, and a hundred downstream problems you were firefighting one by one simply stop being created.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - picture a busy restaurant kitchen.

See the scribbled order that wrecks the service

A waiter scribbles an order saying "Table 9, the steak," and hands it to the chef. Chaos follows. Is that a fillet or a sirloin? Rare or well-done? With chips or salad? The chef has to stop, shout back at the waiter, or guess - and if they guess wrong, the food is sent back, the customer is unhappy, and the kitchen has wasted time and ingredients.

The fix is not a more talented chef. The fix is a better front door: a simple, clear order pad. When the waiter writes the table number, the exact dish, how it should be cooked, and the sides, the request is complete, and the chef can cook the right meal, efficiently, the first time. The quality of the entire service depended on the quality of that first request - and your work orders, your tickets, your briefs are exactly that order pad, only with more zeros at stake.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at the "front door" of your own work system - maintenance work orders, IT helpdesk tickets, project briefs, customer support requests. How much of your team's time is spent just trying to figure out what the request actually means? You can start to guard it today:

  1. Define your "good request". What are the absolute minimum pieces of information needed to act? For most, it is simply WHAT is the problem, WHERE is it, and WHY does it matter. Write that standard down.
  2. Coach the need, not the solution. Gently guide people to describe the symptom they see - ask "what is it doing?" rather than "what part do you need?" - so the right problem gets solved.
  3. Start a screening habit. Before a request enters the main queue, have one person give it a quick review: does it make sense, and is it a duplicate of another? This simple check prevents enormous waste.
  4. Make the right way the easy way. Look at how people submit requests; if the form is complicated or hard to find, design the simplest possible path to give you the clear information you need - a simple form people use beats a complex one they bypass.

Name the front door you will guard, the three things your "good request" must always contain, and the one change that would make raising a good request the easiest way in. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Design your minimum-viable-request and a simple intake form together.)

By making every request clear and actionable from the start, you are not creating bureaucracy - you are eliminating frustration and waste for everyone: the people raising the work, the people planning it, and the people doing it. Guard the front door, and the right work gets done, once - which is how the person waiting on the other end of that work is actually served.
