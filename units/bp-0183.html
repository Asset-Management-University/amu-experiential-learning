Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Nondumiso, three weeks into the job of finance officer for a small town's water service. It is a Thursday morning in the cramped municipal office; the window behind you is open because the air-conditioner gave up two summers ago, and you can hear the slow knocking of the old pump at the treatment works two streets away - a sound everyone here stopped noticing years ago, but you haven't yet.

On the desk is a single sheet: this year's water account summary. You hold it with both hands because the numbers don't sit still in your head. The town billed R18 million for water last year. It cost R20 million just to run the service - to buy chemicals, pay the operators, pump the water, fix the leaks. So the service lost R2 million before you even ask the harder question. And there is a harder question.

Across the desk, Mthobisi, the operations manager, stands with his hard hat in his hand. He has worked here for thirty years and he is the one who keeps the town in water. He rubs his thumb along the brim of the hat, over and over, while he waits for you to say something. "The reservoir lining is going," he says quietly. "The big pump is on its last bearings. The eastern pipes are older than I am. I've been asking for renewal money for nine years." Then he says the thing that makes your stomach drop: "There has never been renewal money. The tariff was never built to put any aside." You look at the sheet again. Nowhere - not one line - sets aside a single rand for the day the pump dies or the reservoir must be relined. Those assets are worth tens of millions and they are wearing out in real time, and the price the town charges was never designed to replace them. The water is being sold for less than it truly costs to keep the water coming.

You can already feel where the cheap, obvious answer is pulling you: the service is losing money, so cut spending. Defer the reservoir lining another year. Stretch the pump. Hold the tariff, because the councillors will never agree to raise it in a hard year and the residents already struggle to pay. Just get through this budget and survive. But Mthobisi is still rubbing that hat brim, and the old pump is still knocking two streets away, and you have a cold feeling that "survive this year" is exactly the choice that has been made nine years running - and that each time it was made, the hole got deeper, not shallower.

We billed R18m and it cost R20m just to RUN, the pump and pipes and reservoir are all wearing out with nothing set aside to replace them, and the easy answer is to defer and stretch and hold the tariff again - but that's the exact choice made for nine years; where do I start?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The service loses money, so the instinct is to spend less. "Defer the reservoir lining another year." But a deferred lining doesn't stay still - it leaks more, so running costs rise and next year's gap is wider; you are not saving, you are borrowing from the asset at a brutal rate. "Hold the tariff - residents can't take a rise." But the price is already below what the water truly costs, so holding it just deepens the hole the assets are paying for; the bill doesn't vanish, it lands later and larger on next year's residents. "Cut spending until the books balance." But the only flexible thing left to cut IS renewal - and cutting renewal is the very thing feeding the spiral, so balancing this year's book guarantees a worse one next year. So before you read on: name the cost that is nowhere on that R20 million sheet, and the single thing that has been true every year that makes the hole deepen instead of close.

I am Nondumiso, three weeks into the job of finance officer for a small town's water service. On my desk is this year's water account summary, and the numbers won't sit still. The town billed R18 million for water last year. It cost R20 million just to RUN the service - chemicals, power, wages, fixing leaks. So we lost R2 million before the harder question. And there is a harder question. Mthobisi, the operations manager (thirty years here, the one who keeps the town in water), says quietly: 'the reservoir lining is going, the big pump is on its last bearings, the eastern pipes are older than I am - I've been asking for renewal money for nine years.' Then the gut-punch: 'there has never BEEN renewal money - the tariff was never built to put any aside.' I look at the sheet: not one line sets aside a single rand for the day the pump dies or the reservoir must be relined. Assets worth tens of millions are wearing out in real time, and the price we charge was never designed to replace them. The water is being sold for less than it truly costs to keep the water coming. The easy answer pulls hard: we're losing money, so cut - defer the lining another year, stretch the pump, hold the tariff (the councillors won't raise it in a hard year, residents already struggle to pay), just survive this budget. But 'survive this year' is exactly the choice made nine years running, and each time the hole got DEEPER. Where do I start?
Don't reach for the cut. The service is not simply 'losing money' - it is priced below its TRUE WHOLE-LIFE cost, and the difference is being paid by the assets themselves, quietly wearing out. The R20 million is only the running cost; nowhere on the sheet is the cost of the pump, the pipes and the reservoir WEARING OUT each year and one day needing to be replaced. Put a number on that: the pump cost R4 million and lasts ~20 years, so it uses up ~R200,000 of itself a year; the relining is R6 million every ~30 years (R200,000 a year); the pipes are worth ~R24 million over 50 years (~R480,000 a year) - about R900,000 of asset consumed every year whether or not anyone sets it aside. So the TRUE cost of the water is not R20 million, it is about R20.9 million - and the town billed R18 million. Now see the engine: a price below true cost means a gap every year, and the gap gets filled the only way it can - by NOT renewing (skip the lining, stretch the pump), which makes the assets degrade, which raises running costs, which WIDENS next year's gap - a compounding spiral, running nine years. 'Defer and stretch and hold' does not save money; it BORROWS from the assets at a brutal rate (a R6 million relining deferred becomes a R10 million emergency rebuild plus days with no water). The real question is not 'how do I cut my way out?' but 'what is the whole cost of keeping the water coming, and how do I close the gap on purpose before it closes itself the cruel way?'
Five moves stop the asset base eating itself. (1) MEASURE THE TRUE COST OF SERVICE - the whole cost, not the bill: running costs (R20m) PLUS each asset's yearly wear-out (pump ~R200k + relining ~R200k + pipes ~R480k = ~R900k), so the true cost is ~R20.9m against R18m billed. (2) DIAGNOSE THE SPIRAL - a price below true cost forces a gap, filled by skipping renewal, which degrades the assets, which raises running costs, which widens next year's gap: a self-feeding loop, not a one-off shortfall. (3) EXPOSE DEFERRAL AS HIGH-INTEREST DEBT - 'defer, stretch, hold the tariff' does not save money, it borrows from the assets at a punitive rate (a R6m relining deferred becomes a R10m emergency rebuild plus days with no water); the bill always arrives, deferral just picks a worse day and a worse payer (next year's residents). (4) BUILD A STAGED PATH TO SUSTAINABILITY - put the ~R900,000 renewal on the books as a named line (you can't close a gap the books pretend isn't there); close the ~R2.9m gap deliberately, not in one shock - a fair tariff rise phased over three years, paired with cutting real waste (the leaks lose treated water already paid to clean) and a stronger indigent rebate so the poorest are protected while the price moves toward the truth. (5) TRIAGE RENEWAL TO STOP THE BLEEDING - spend the first renewal rands where a failure would be most catastrophic and expensive (the big pump's bearings, the worst leaks), so the spiral stops accelerating even before the tariff fully catches up. The point is not money for its own sake; it is to KEEP the water coming - a service priced below the cost of renewing its own assets is a promise it cannot keep.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Mthobisi said it first, the next morning, standing at the treatment works while the old pump knocked beside them: "We've been selling water for less than it costs to keep selling water. And every year we do it, there's less works left to sell it from." Here is how the two of you worked it through, in five moves:

  • Measure the true cost of service - the whole cost, not the bill: Nondumiso makes three columns on a page. The running cost the town already knew: R20 million a year - chemicals, power, wages, repairs. Then a column nobody had ever filled: what it costs for the assets to wear out. The big pump cost R4 million and lasts about 20 years, so it uses up roughly R200,000 of itself every year. The reservoir relining will cost R6 million and is due about every 30 years - R200,000 a year. The eastern pipe network is worth perhaps R24 million and lasts 50 years - close to R480,000 a year wearing away. Added up, the assets consume around R900,000 of themselves every single year, whether or not anyone sets the money aside. So the true cost of keeping the water coming was not R20 million; it was about R20.9 million - and the town billed R18 million. This is the move everything else depends on: the bill tells you this year's cash, but the WHOLE cost of a service includes the slow using-up of the very assets that deliver it. A service whose price covers only the cash and ignores the wearing-out is not actually paying for itself - it only looks as if it is, because the bill for the assets has not arrived yet.
  • Diagnose the spiral, and which way it turns: with the true cost on the page, the engine becomes visible. The price was below the true cost, so every year there was a gap - and the gap was filled the only way it could be: by NOT renewing the assets. Skip the lining. Stretch the pump. Defer the pipes. But a deferred reservoir doesn't stay still - it leaks more, so running costs rise, so next year's gap is wider, so even more renewal gets skipped, so the works degrade faster, so the service gets worse, so fewer residents are willing to pay, so recovery falls further. Drawn as a loop on the page, it bites its own tail: each year's 'survive' made the following year harder. The hole was never a one-off shortfall - it was a spiral, and it had been running for nine years. This is why cutting harder cannot be the answer: the thing being cut IS the renewal, and cutting renewal is exactly what feeds the spiral. You cannot save your way out of a hole you are digging with the saving.
  • Expose deferral as high-interest debt: you name the cheap answer honestly for what it is. 'Defer, stretch, hold the tariff' does not save money - it BORROWS money, from the assets, at a brutal interest rate. A R6 million relining deferred becomes a R10 million emergency rebuild after a failure, plus the days the town has no water at all. The bill always arrives; deferral only chooses a worse day for it to arrive, and a worse person to hand it to - usually next year's residents, who pay for water they barely received. This reframing is what breaks the nine-year habit, because 'defer' had always been spoken as the responsible, frugal choice - it spends less THIS year - when in truth it is the most expensive choice available, just with the cost hidden in the future and in the silent degradation of the works. Once everyone in the room sees deferral as borrowing at a punitive rate against the town's own pump and pipes, 'just survive this year' stops sounding careful and starts sounding like what it is.
  • Build a staged path to sustainability: the decision is not 'raise the tariff and hope'. It is: first measure the true cost including renewal, then close the gap deliberately and visibly - before the spiral runs further - in three honest moves, in order of reach. MAKE THE TRUE COST VISIBLE: put the R900,000 renewal figure on the books as a real, named line - a renewal provision - so every budget after this one shows the whole cost, not the flattering half of it; you cannot close a gap the books pretend isn't there, and putting renewal on the books makes the deficit look BIGGER in the first year precisely because, for the first time, it tells the truth. CLOSE THE GAP IN STEPS, NOT A SHOCK: the true gap was about R2.9 million (R20.9m true cost minus R18m billed), and jumping the tariff that far in one year would break households that already struggle - so a fair tariff rise phased over three years, paired with cutting real running waste (the leaks were losing treated water the town had already paid to clean), and paired with a stronger indigent rebate so the poorest are protected while the price moves toward the truth. Closing the gap costs real people real money now, in a town where money is tight - which is exactly why it must be staged and protected, never a single cruel jump.
  • Triage renewal to stop the bleeding: you do not wait for the tariff to fully catch up before you defend the asset base - because the spiral is accelerating now. So you protect the asset base while the gap closes: spend the first renewal rands where a failure would be most catastrophic and most expensive - the bearings on the big pump, the worst eastern leaks - so the spiral stops accelerating even before the price reaches the truth. What Nondumiso and Mthobisi had really discovered is a single idea: the service had been priced below its true whole-life cost - the cost including the renewal the assets will need - so the asset base was quietly consuming itself, and each year's deficit compounded into the next. You do not fix this by cutting harder, because cutting renewal is the very thing that feeds the spiral. You fix it by measuring the whole cost of service, putting renewal on the books as a real number, and closing the gap on purpose before it closes itself the cruel way - through a pump that dies with no money behind it and a town that wakes up with no water. Telling that truth is politically harder than deferring, every single year - which is precisely why deferral wins for nine years and the assets pay the price in silence. The whole point is not the money; it is to keep the water coming. (For the one-off cost of an asset's final decommissioning, see bp-0166; for proving afterwards what the spend delivered, bp-0142.)

See where it lands. The renewal is on the books as a real R900,000 line; the ~R2.9 million gap has a staged, fair path toward closing, with the poorest protected; and the first renewal rands are already defending the pump and the worst leaks, so the spiral has stopped accelerating. The water keeps coming - which was always the whole point. A service is not sustainable if its price covers only this year's cash costs; the true price must also cover the cost of renewing the assets that will deliver the service next year, or the asset base quietly consumes itself and each year's deficit compounds into the next. You do not fix this by cutting harder - cutting renewal is the very thing that feeds the spiral - but by measuring the whole cost, putting renewal on the books as a real named line, and closing the gap on purpose, staged and with the poorest protected, before it closes itself the cruel way through a pump that dies with no money behind it. A book that shows profit can still be running the spiral, because cash costs are not whole costs.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own world, try one in-between case - a little harder than the town's, because here the spiral is wearing a friendly disguise, and at a completely different scale.

See the laundry whose books say "profit" and still runs the spiral

Sibongiseni runs a busy laundry in a shipping container in a busy township - and her books say she's profitable. She has one big industrial washer-dryer that cost her R60,000, bought on a loan she's nearly finished paying. She charges R40 a load. Last month she did 600 loads - R24,000 in. Her cash costs were water, electricity, soap, and a part-time helper, about R16,000. So her books showed R8,000 profit, and she felt good: the service is delivered, the customers are happy, the money came in.

What is missing from her R16,000 of costs? Everything she paid cash for is in there - but the machine itself is wearing out with every load: the drum, the motor, the bearings. That R60,000 washer lasts about 5 years of this volume before it must be replaced, so R60,000 over 5 years is R12,000 a year of machine wearing away - about R1,000 a month she is not charging for. Her real monthly cost isn't R16,000, it's R17,000. Her real profit isn't R8,000 - and here's the trap: when the machine dies in year five, she'll need R60,000 she never set aside, so she'll borrow again, at interest, and her R40 price will look even further below true cost. She is the town in miniature: charging below whole-life cost, so the asset quietly eats itself, so the next machine costs more than the last. The disguise is that her books say "profit" - because, just like the municipality, her price-setting never put the wearing-out on the page. So she does the town's worksheet: her real true cost per load includes that R1,000-a-month machine wear, and her R40 price needs to drift, over time and in small steps so she doesn't lose customers in one jump, toward a price that keeps a machine fund alive. The lesson of this step: a book that shows profit can still be running the spiral - because cash costs are not whole costs. Counting this month's cash is not the test; counting the whole cost, renewal included, is.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The town's problem was not "a service that loses money". It was charging less than the true whole cost - running costs plus the renewal the assets will need - so the asset base quietly pays the difference and wears itself out, and the gap compounds every year. That same shape hides far beyond a water account. Find it where you are:

  1. Write the whole cost. Pick one thing you charge for and write everything it costs you in cash this year. Then ask the question almost nobody asks: what does the equipment, vehicle, building or tool behind it cost as it wears out each year (value divided by years of life)? Add that in. Is your price above or below that true total?
  2. Find who pays the difference, and check for the spiral. If your price is below the true cost, the difference is coming from somewhere - usually an asset you are quietly running into the ground; name it. And over the last few years, has the gap widened while "deferring" or "holding the price" was how you survived each year? That is the spiral, not just a bad year.
  3. Put renewal on your own books as a named line - a renewal or replacement fund - even if it's small. It will look worse before it looks better. That's the truth doing its job.
  4. Plan the close, staged not sudden, and notice where else it hides. What is a fair step toward a true-cost price over two or three years, and how would you protect whoever genuinely can't absorb it? Then look around: a rent that never covered the building's upkeep, a subscription below what serving each customer truly costs, a family car run with nothing set aside for the day it dies, a skill or relationship you keep drawing on without ever renewing.

If it's below, the asset is paying the difference - so put the wear-out on the page as a named line and plan a fair, staged step toward the truth. (Working with an AMU facilitator? This is the same problem-type - a price set below the whole, renewal-included cost, so the asset base eats itself while the gap compounds - in your own world. The water keeps coming only if the price is built to keep the works alive.
