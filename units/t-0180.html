Ninety sensors and nine thousand nodes

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and you have built a digital twin before. It was for a single pump set, and it worked beautifully - every bearing temperature, every flow, every vibration that mattered had an instrument on it, and the twin simply showed you what was true.

Now the municipality has asked you to specify a digital twin for the town's water distribution network. 850 km of mains. 62,000 connections. 18 pressure zones. A hydraulic model with 9,500 nodes.

So you start costing what you know how to do. An installed pressure logger is about R18,500. Instrumenting every node comes to R175,750,000, against a capital allocation of roughly R4 million - forty-five times the money. You stop before you finish the spreadsheet.

Your instinct is that the project is impossible at this scale.

It is not. You are being asked for a different kind of thing, and you have not noticed yet.

I can afford ninety sensors for nine thousand five hundred nodes. What am I actually designing, and how do I specify it so that the people who build it build the right thing?

Before you read on, decide. The network has about 9,500 nodes. You started costing instrumentation at roughly R18,500 a node and stopped partway through the spreadsheet, because the total was heading past R175 million against a capital allocation of about R4 million — some forty-odd times the money. What you can afford is around 90 pressure loggers. Meanwhile three people want three different things from this: one wants to locate leaks within minutes, one wants to manage pressure by zone, one wants a renewal list once a year. Your instinct is that the project is impossible at this scale. Write down what you would actually specify — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the sentence the whole specification turns on

• “Tell them it cannot be done for the money.” True of the thing you were costing and false of the thing being asked for. You would be declining a project on the basis of a specification nobody wrote.

• “Do one zone properly as a pilot.” Sensible-sounding, and it answers none of the three questions — a well-instrumented one per cent tells you about that one per cent, and the decisions are all network-wide.

• “Ask for more money.” Forty-fold. That is not a budget you argue for; it is a budget nobody would ever approve, and asking spends credibility you will need for the thing that is affordable.

• “Put the ninety loggers where access is easiest and start collecting.” Guarantees the sensors answer whichever question their locations happen to suit — which is to say, none of the three deliberately.

• “Buy the platform and let the vendor work it out.” Hands over the one judgement that is genuinely yours. Untold, a builder will build the kind that reflects — because that is the normal kind — and you will get a confident-looking picture of 9,500 nodes based on 90.

• Here is what you have not noticed: you are being asked for a different kind of thing. Fed by dense measurement, such a model reflects a system; fed by ninety sensors across 9,500 nodes it must infer it — and inference is a different object with different obligations. So write that down and put the number on it: 90 of 9,500 is under one per cent observed, and everything downstream depends on that sentence, including whether the builder builds the right thing. Then design from the decisions rather than from completeness, and notice that your three are genuinely incompatible — locating a leak wants fine resolution and minutes; managing pressure wants zones and hours; prioritising renewal wants attributes and a year. Those are three instruments wearing one name, so rank them, and specify the fidelity, resolution and update rate for each — including where the ninety go, which follows from the ranking and not from where access is easy. And last, the thing that makes an inferring model safe to act on: require it to show where it is confident and where it is guessing, or a plausible inference will be read as a measurement.

Here is the specification that answers it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the portfolio twin specification - what it is for, what it actually is, what feeds it, where the few sensors go, and how honest it is required to be.

1. Start from the decisions - and accept that they need different fidelity

NAME THE DECISIONS FIRST, AND RANK THEM. A portfolio twin that tries to answer every question at once answers none of them well, because the questions want different things from it. LEAK LOCALISATION needs high spatial resolution and updates every few minutes. PRESSURE MANAGEMENT needs zone-level resolution and updates hourly. RENEWAL PRIORITISATION needs pipe-level attributes and updates once a year. CAPACITY PLANNING needs demand scenarios, not live state at all. Those are four different instruments wearing one name. So rank them - here: 1. leak localisation, 2. pressure management, 3. renewal prioritisation - and SPECIFY THE FIDELITY, SPATIAL RESOLUTION AND UPDATE RATE FOR EACH. Everything that follows in this specification is downstream of this ranking, including where the sensors go. A twin designed without a ranked decision list is designed to somebody's idea of completeness, and completeness is not affordable.

2. State what this twin actually is - a model that INFERS, not a mirror that reflects

THIS IS THE SENTENCE THE WHOLE SPECIFICATION TURNS ON, SO WRITE IT DOWN AND PUT A NUMBER ON IT. The network has 9,500 MODEL NODES. The affordable instrumentation is 90 PRESSURE LOGGERS. That is 0.95 PER CENT OF NODES - ONE MEASUREMENT PER 106 NODES, or 5 loggers per pressure zone across 18 zones. SO 99 PER CENT OF WHAT THIS TWIN TELLS ANYONE IS COMPUTED, NOT OBSERVED. A single-asset twin can be instrumented until it largely REFLECTS measurement, and its model refines what the sensors say. A portfolio twin is the other way round: THE MEASUREMENTS CONSTRAIN THE MODEL, and the model estimates pressures at the nodes that have no sensor on them. That is not a compromise, it is the design - inferring the unmeasured from the measured is the entire job. Specify the class of model that does the inferring - here, A CALIBRATED HYDRAULIC NETWORK MODEL - and stop pretending the sensor count is a budget problem. It is a fact about what kind of thing you are building.

3. Specify the data sources - and the rule for when they disagree

A PORTFOLIO TWIN IS FED BY SOURCES THAT NOBODY DESIGNED TO WORK TOGETHER, so the specification must say how they are joined. For each source: OWNER, FORMAT, UPDATE RATE, SPATIAL RESOLUTION, KNOWN RELIABILITY. Telemetry from the loggers and flow meters (operations, minutes, point). The ASSET REGISTER - diameter, material, age (asset management, on change, pipe). The NETWORK MODEL geometry (planning, on revision, node). CUSTOMER METER readings (revenue, monthly, connection). WORK ORDERS - repairs, valve operations (maintenance, daily). Then THE PART THAT IS ALWAYS LEFT OUT: A MASTERSHIP RULE PER FACT, and a resolution process. THE CONFLICT WILL HAPPEN. The asset register says a trunk main is 300 mm ductile iron. The model will only calibrate if that same pipe behaves like a 250 mm pipe of much higher roughness. Customer complaints in that zone report low pressure. WHICH ONE IS MASTER - the as-built record, the calibrated model, or the reported reality? Write the rule: the CALIBRATED MODEL is master for hydraulic behaviour; the ASSET REGISTER is master for physical attributes; AND A DISAGREEMENT RAISES A WORK ORDER TO FIELD-VERIFY THE PIPE - because a model that fits by pretending a pipe is something it is not has hidden a real defect, probably a partially closed valve or a collapsed lining. A twin with no mastership rule does not have a data problem. It has an argument, every week, that nobody is empowered to settle.

4. Place the few sensors where they most constrain the model

THIS IS THE REAL DESIGN ACT, AND IT IS THE ONE MOST OFTEN DELEGATED TO WHOEVER INSTALLS THE KIT. With one measurement per 106 nodes, WHERE those 90 loggers sit determines how much of the network the model can actually see. Place them WHERE THEY MOST REDUCE UNCERTAINTY IN THE DECISIONS FROM BOX 1: at ZONE BOUNDARIES and inlets, so each zone's inflow and head are constrained; at HYDRAULIC EXTREMITIES - the highest and furthest points, where pressure is lowest and where the model's error is largest; at points of HIGH SENSITIVITY, where a small modelling error produces a large pressure difference; and at HIGH-CONSEQUENCE nodes such as the clinic, the school and the reservoir feed. The 24 FLOW METERS go on the zone inlets, because a zone whose inflow is unmeasured cannot be mass-balanced and leakage in it cannot be estimated at all. AND WRITE DOWN WHAT YOU ARE REFUSING: not on the chamber beside the depot because the access is good; not clustered where the network is already well understood. LEFT UNSPECIFIED, SENSORS MIGRATE TO CONVENIENCE, and you end up with 90 measurements of the part of the network you never had to ask about.

5. Calibrate across varied conditions, hold data back to validate, and declare the uncertainty

CALIBRATE ON TIME SERIES ACROSS MULTIPLE DAYS OF DIFFERENT HYDRAULIC FUNCTIONING - inflow, pressure and tank level over high and low demand, weekday and weekend, one pump set running and two - NOT on a single snapshot, because a model tuned to one hour of one day is tuned to one hour of one day. THEN HOLD MEASUREMENTS BACK. Reserve about 15 PER CENT of the loggers - 14 of the 90 - as a VALIDATION SET the calibration never touches, so 76 loggers fit the model and 14 test it against readings it has never seen. Without a hold-out, the only evidence the twin works is that it reproduces the data it was fitted to, which is not evidence. AND THEN DECLARE THE UNCERTAINTY, WHICH IS A SAFETY REQUIREMENT AND NOT A REFINEMENT. Operational decisions taken on this twin change what comes out of people's taps. So the specification must state: ALL DATA PRESENTED TO USERS MUST BE CLEARLY LABELLED. DIRECT SENSOR READINGS SHALL BE LABELLED 'MEASURED'. ALL VALUES DERIVED FROM MODEL CALCULATION SHALL BE LABELLED 'INFERRED' AND DISPLAYED WITH A QUANTIFIED UNCERTAINTY BOUND - for example 'Pressure: 312 kPa (INFERRED, +/- 21 kPa)'. A twin that shows an inferred value in the same typeface and with the same confidence as a measured one will be trusted hardest exactly where it deserves it least.

Box 2 is the one that changes everything, and boxes 3 to 5 are what follow from taking it seriously. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A single-asset twin and a portfolio twin differ in kind, not in scale. One reflects measurement; the other infers an entire system from a handful of it. Five questions:

  • What decisions must it serve, and in what order? Decision-led fidelity. Leak localisation, pressure management and renewal planning want different resolutions and different update rates. Rank them, or you will be designing for completeness, and completeness is what costs R175 million.
  • How much of this is computed rather than observed? Inference against measurement. 90 loggers against 9,500 nodes is 0.95 per cent - one measurement per 106 nodes, 5 per zone - so 99 per cent of the twin's output is inferred. The measurements constrain the model; the model estimates the pressure at every node that has no sensor on it.
  • What feeds it, and who wins when two sources disagree? Federation and mastership. Telemetry, the asset register, the model geometry, customer meters, work orders - five owners, five update rates, five ideas of the truth. Name a master for each fact and a process for the conflict, because the conflict is certain.
  • Where do the few sensors go? Placement for observability. Zone boundaries, hydraulic extremities, points of high sensitivity, high-consequence nodes. Not where the access is easy - which is where they go if this box is left blank.
  • How do you know it works, and does it say how sure it is? Calibration, validation and declared uncertainty. Calibrate across varied operating conditions, hold 15 per cent of the loggers back to test against readings the model has never seen, and label every value MEASURED or INFERRED with a bound.

The arithmetic that makes the point. 90 loggers over 9,500 nodes is 0.95 per cent, or one measurement per 106 nodes. Hold 14 of them back for validation and you are calibrating a 9,500-node model on 76 measurements - 0.8 per cent of it. That sounds impossible until you see what the model contributes: it already knows the pipe network, the elevations, the mass balance and the physics of flow, so the sensors are not describing the network, they are pinning down the few things the physics cannot tell you - roughness, real demand distribution, which valves are actually open. That is why placement matters more than count, and it is why the same 90 loggers can produce a good twin or a useless one.

And the requirement that is not negotiable. This twin will be used to make decisions about supply to real households - which zone to throttle overnight, where to dose, which main to shut. A user who reads an inferred pressure as a measured one is being misled by the instrument. So the specification mandates that direct sensor readings are labelled MEASURED, and every model-derived value is labelled INFERRED and displayed with a quantified bound, for example "Pressure: 312 kPa (INFERRED, +/- 21 kPa)". A twin that lies about its own certainty is not a tool, it is a trap - and it will be trusted hardest exactly where it deserves it least.

One honest boundary. You are specifying the portfolio twin - the decisions it serves, the data it needs, how those sources federate, where the sensors go, and how it is calibrated, validated and declared. You are not building the hydraulic model, not writing the calibration routines, and not selecting the platform. Your document is what the modellers, integrators and operators build from.

Does it scale? A weather service does not put a thermometer in every street. It puts a few hundred well-sited stations into a physical model of the atmosphere, and the model tells you the temperature at your house - which nobody measured. You accept that forecast because it comes with a confidence attached to it. Your network twin is the same instrument, and it owes its users the same honesty.

T4: Your Turn

A district municipality asks you to specify a digital twin for its electricity distribution network. What you are given:

ItemFigure
Medium-voltage feeder network320 km, 41,000 connections
Feeder groups12
Nodes in the network model4,600
Affordable instrumentation48 voltage loggers, 16 feeder current meters
Existing sourcesAsset register (assets dept, on change); network model (planning, on revision); prepaid meter sales (revenue, daily); outage call log (call centre, live); protection relay events (operations, live)
Standing complaintLow voltage at the ends of two rural feeders, reported for years, never quantified
  1. Decisions. Rank the three decisions this twin must serve, and give each one a required resolution and update rate.
  2. Inference ratio. Compute it - percentage of nodes measured, nodes per measurement, loggers per feeder group - and write the sentence that goes at the top of the specification.
  3. Federation. Tabulate the sources with owner, update rate and resolution, then state a mastership rule for at least one fact that two sources will disagree about.
  4. Placement. Allocate the 48 voltage loggers and 16 current meters against your ranked decisions, and say explicitly what you are refusing to instrument.
  5. Validation and honesty. Define the calibration conditions, the hold-out set, and the labelling requirement for inferred values.

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, your step 2 arithmetic should come out at 1.04 per cent of nodes measured, one measurement per 96 nodes, 4 loggers per feeder group - and a 15 per cent hold-out is 7 loggers, leaving 41 to calibrate on. If your inference ratio is not written into the specification in plain language, the specification does not yet say what the thing is.

Second, the standing complaint is the trap and the gift together. The rural feeder ends are exactly where the model is least constrained and the consequence is highest, so placement should send loggers there - but placing loggers to confirm a complaint is different from placing them to constrain the model, and a good answer knows which argument it is making.

Third, in step 3, notice how quickly you need a rule. The prepaid sales say a feeder serves 3,100 paying connections; the asset register lists 3,850 connections on it; the model was built from the register. Which is master for load - and what does the twin do with the difference, given that it is either unmetered supply, a stale register, or both?

T5: The Guardrails

Portfolio twins fail in ways that make them look more impressive and less true.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Designing a mirror instead of a model. The instinct from single-asset work. It ends either in an unaffordable sensor bill or in a twin that only covers the small part you could instrument.

• Building for every decision at once. Leak localisation and renewal planning want different resolutions and different update rates. Unranked, the twin is designed to somebody's idea of completeness.

• Leaving the inference ratio out of the specification. If users are not told that 99 per cent of what they see is computed, they will assume it is observed. That assumption is on you, not on them.

• Sensors placed where access is easy. The default outcome when placement is unspecified. You get 90 measurements of the part of the network you already understood.

• Calibrating on a single snapshot. A model tuned to one hour of one day is tuned to one hour of one day. Varied operating conditions or it is not calibrated.

• No hold-out set. Without measurements the calibration never saw, the only evidence the twin works is that it reproduces what it was fitted to. That is not evidence.

• Inferred values shown like measured ones. The safety failure. Label them, bound them, and make the difference visible in the interface rather than in an appendix.

• No mastership rule. Two sources disagree, nobody is empowered to settle it, and the argument recurs every week until people stop using the twin.

• Calibrating away a real defect. If the model only fits when a 300 mm main behaves like a 250 mm one, you may have found a closed valve or a collapsed lining. Raise a work order rather than a fudge factor.

• Forgetting the network changes. Mains are replaced, zones are re-valved, demand moves. A twin nobody maintains does not become obviously wrong. It becomes confidently wrong.

Onalenna could not afford to measure the network, so she specified something that infers it. Rank the decisions the twin must serve and give each the fidelity and update rate it needs; state plainly that 90 measurements against 9,500 nodes means 99 per cent of the output is computed, not observed; list every data source with its owner, rate and resolution, and name a master for each fact together with the process for when they disagree; place the few sensors where they most constrain the model rather than where access is easy; calibrate across genuinely varied conditions, hold data back to validate against readings the model has never seen, and label every inferred value with its uncertainty. A portfolio twin is not a mirror of the assets. It is a model of them, constrained by a few well-chosen truths - and it owes its users an honest account of which is which.
