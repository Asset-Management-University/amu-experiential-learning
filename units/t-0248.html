Earned, not spent

T1: The Ache

The project reported green all the way. The budget burn looked normal - money going out at about the rate you expected. The calendar looked fine. And then, a month from the deadline, it all came apart at once: a massive overrun, a schedule slip you could not recover, and a client who trusted you.

Here is what stings: the failure was not in the last month. It was in the first. The truth was knowable when you were a third of the way in - it was sitting right there in the numbers. You just never saw it, because you were measuring the money you had spent instead of the value you had earned, and you told yourself the comforting lie that spending was progress.

My project reported on track the whole way and then blew up at the end - how do I know honestly, and early, whether a project is really on cost and on schedule, and where it will actually finish?

Before you read on, decide. Twelve months, R14 million, reported on track at every monthly review. It finished five months late and R4.1m over. Looking back now at month four: you had spent about a third of the budget, the report said thirty-five per cent complete — a figure the site engineer gave you off the top of his head — and the two commissioning packages that were supposed to be finished by then had not been started. Nobody hid anything. Everybody was reassured, including you. Write down how you would know honestly and early where a project will actually finish — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the comforting lie

• “Review more often — go weekly.” More frequent readings of the same measure. Weekly reports of money spent and a percentage somebody estimates by feel would have reassured you fifty times instead of twelve.

• “Add contingency.” Absorbs the overrun and hides it for longer. It also does nothing about the schedule, which is where this started.

• “The estimate was optimistic — hold people to tighter reporting.” Closer, and notice what you would be tightening: an opinion. “Thirty-five per cent” was never checked against anything you could point at, and asking for it more sternly produces a more confident opinion.

• “Watch the spend against the plan.” That is what you did. Spend tracked the plan almost exactly — which is precisely why everything looked fine while two packages had not begun.

• “It only became clear at the end — nobody could have known.” It was in the numbers at month four. What was missing was not information but a measure that would have shown it.

• The great lie of project control is reading progress off the budget burned: we have spent half the money, so we must be about half done. It is almost always false and it is always comforting, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. Spending money is not the same as completing work — you can spend a fortune with little to show. So measure the work actually done, valued in the same currency as the plan, and you can compare it honestly against both the plan and the cost. That needs three figures, all in money: what you planned to have finished by now, what you have genuinely finished, and what you have spent. The middle one is the whole discipline, because it must come from real, verifiable deliverables rather than somebody's sense of how far along things feel — and your thirty-five per cent is exactly the failure it guards against. From those three, two different truths fall out that no single number can carry: whether you are ahead or behind, and whether it is costing more than it should. They are independent, so a project can be on cost and badly behind — which is what yours was. And the reason to do this early is that the trend forecasts the finish while you can still act, rather than confirming afterwards what everybody eventually found out.

Progress is the value you have earned, not the money you have spent - and the trend tells you the future while you can still change it. Let us learn to read it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you control a project on cost and schedule - measuring what you have genuinely earned rather than what you have spent, reading the two truths that fall out of three honest numbers, and forecasting the finish from the trend while there is still time to act.

1. Earned, not spent - progress is work done, not money gone

THE GREAT LIE OF PROJECT CONTROL is reading progress off the budget burned or the calendar: 'we have spent half the money, so we must be about half done.' It is almost always FALSE, and it is always COMFORTING, which is exactly why it is dangerous. SPENDING MONEY IS NOT THE SAME AS COMPLETING WORK - you can spend a fortune and have little to show, or spend carefully and be well ahead. To tell the truth about a project you have to measure the WORK ACTUALLY DONE, valued in the same currency as the plan, and compare it honestly against both the plan and the cost. That measured value of completed work is the one honest anchor, and everything else hangs off it. Half the budget spent is NOT half done.

2. The three numbers - planned, earned, spent

At any point in the project you need THREE figures, all in money so they can be compared. PLANNED VALUE - the budgeted value of the work you PLANNED to have finished by now (read straight off the schedule and budget). EARNED VALUE - the budgeted value of the work you have ACTUALLY finished, measured by GENUINE, VERIFIABLE DELIVERABLES and not by an optimistic guess of 'percent done' (the single most abused number in project management). And ACTUAL COST - what that completed work has actually cost you. PLANNED, EARNED, SPENT. Get those three honestly and the project can no longer hide from you.

3. The two truths - schedule and cost, separated

From the three numbers, TWO PLAIN TRUTHS fall out. ARE YOU AHEAD OR BEHIND ON SCHEDULE? Compare what you have earned with what you planned to have earned: EARNED MINUS PLANNED. If you have earned less than you planned, you are behind. ARE YOU OVER OR UNDER ON COST? Compare what you have earned with what it cost: EARNED MINUS SPENT. If the work you have done cost more than it is worth, you are over. Two subtractions, two honest answers - SCHEDULE AND COST, SEPARATED, so a project that is on budget but slipping, or on time but bleeding money, cannot disguise one behind the other. Worked: earned R240k, planned R300k -> schedule minus R60k (behind); earned R240k, spent R320k -> cost minus R80k (over).

4. Forecast the finish from the trend

A single bad reading might be a blip; A TREND IS A FORECAST. If, a third of the way in, you have consistently earned less than you planned and spent more than you earned, the project is telling you what it will keep doing - and honest control uses that to forecast where it will actually end: THE ESTIMATE AT COMPLETION. A common and sober forecast is to assume your current cost performance continues - if you have been getting, say, 75c of value for every rand (R240k earned / R320k spent = 0.75), you divide the total budget by that rate: R900,000 / 0.75 = R1,200,000, a R300,000 overrun visible at one-third done. This is a COMMON forecast that assumes the current trend holds, NOT a universal law; other forecasts apply when you have good reason to believe the rest will go differently. Either way: forecast from the performance you are ACTUALLY achieving, not from the hope that it will improve.

5. Act while the gap is small - a compass, not a hammer

The whole point of control is not to produce a report; it is to SEE THE TRUTH EARLY ENOUGH TO DO SOMETHING ABOUT IT. So measure by real deliverables, look early and often, and act on the forecast while the gap is still small - re-scope, re-plan, add capacity, or have the honest conversation with the client now rather than at the end. And how you use these numbers matters as much as the numbers themselves. THESE NUMBERS ARE A COMPASS, NOT A HAMMER. THEY EXIST TO HELP YOU AND YOUR TEAM STEER THE PROJECT TOGETHER, NOT TO FIND SOMEONE TO BLAME. A VARIANCE IS A SIGNAL TO ASK 'WHAT IS THE STORY HERE?' AND 'HOW CAN WE HELP?', NOT A REASON TO PUNISH. Because A PROJECT THAT REPORTS GREEN WHILE IT IS SECRETLY FAILING IS A DEBT THAT THE CREW WILL PAY FOR LATER IN BURNOUT, AND THE COMMUNITY WILL PAY FOR IN DELAYED SERVICES. Honest, early control is a form of respect: it honours the truth that your work affects other people.

T3: The Blueprint

Here is the truth in numbers. Onalenna is a third of the way into a refurbishment budgeted at R900 000. By the plan, she should have completed R300 000 of work by now (planned value). But checking real deliverables, she has genuinely completed only R240 000 of work (earned value) - and it has cost R320 000 to do it (actual cost). She reads the two truths. Schedule: earned minus planned, R240 000 - R300 000 = minus R60 000 - she is behind, having earned R60 000 less than planned. Cost: earned minus spent, R240 000 - R320 000 = minus R80 000 - she is over, because work worth R240 000 cost R320 000. Her cost performance is R240 000 / R320 000 = 0.75 - she is getting 75c of value per rand. She forecasts the finish: R900 000 / 0.75 = R1 200 000 - a R300 000 overrun, and she can see it at one-third done, while there is still time to act. The manager next door reads "R320 000 spent of R900 000, plenty left" off the budget line, reports on track, and discovers the disaster when the money runs out with the job half finished.

Does it scale? This is how you keep an honest eye on anything with a budget and a deadline - a build, a study, a personal savings goal. The comforting story is the expensive one; the honest number, read early, is a gift.

T4: Your Turn

A refurbishment at Corathyl has a total budget of R500 000. Three months in, the plan said R200 000 of work should be complete. A check of real, verifiable deliverables shows only R150 000 of work is genuinely done. The accounting team confirms R180 000 has been spent to date.

So: planned value R200 000; earned value R150 000; actual cost R180 000; budget at completion R500 000.

  1. Schedule. Is the project ahead or behind schedule, and by how much in rand?
  2. Cost. Is it over or under budget, and by how much in rand?
  3. Forecast. Based on this trend, what is the new forecast for the final cost?
  4. Tell the truth. Your sponsor, seeing only that R180 000 of a R500 000 budget has been spent, believes the project is well under budget. Write the one sentence you would use to explain the real situation - honouring their intelligence, and stating the truth clearly.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, schedule: earned minus planned, R150 000 - R200 000 = minus R50 000. The project is behind schedule - R50 000 of planned work has not been done.

Second, cost: earned minus spent, R150 000 - R180 000 = minus R30 000. The project is over budget - work worth R150 000 has cost R180 000.

Third, the forecast: our cost performance is R150 000 / R180 000, a rate of 5/6 (0.833 rounded). If this trend continues, the final cost is forecast to be the total budget divided by this rate: R500 000 / (5/6) = R600 000. Using the rounded 0.833 instead of the exact fraction gives a very close but not identical R600 240 - always carry the exact fraction through a forecast division, not a rounded display figure. This is a forecast overrun of R100 000, visible now while there is still time to act. (This assumes the current trend holds; if there is a solid reason the rest will run differently, say so and forecast accordingly.)

Fourth, the honest sentence to the sponsor - something like: "We have spent R180 000, but we have only completed R150 000 worth of the work, so at the rate we are actually going the R500 000 job is on track to cost about R600 000 - which is why I want to act now, while we still can." It grants the sponsor the real numbers, names the trap in the money-spent read, and turns the truth into a call to act rather than a confession.

T5: The Guardrails

Measure what you have earned, not what you have spent - and act on the trend while the gap is still small, using the numbers as a compass to steer by, never a hammer to blame with.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Measuring progress by money spent or time elapsed. Spending is not doing. Half the budget gone is not half the job done. Measure the work actually completed, valued against the plan.

• Trusting optimistic "percent done" guesses. The most abused number in project management. Measure earned value by genuine, verifiable deliverables, not by how finished a task feels.

• Watching only cost, or only schedule. A project can be on budget and slipping, or on time and bleeding money. Read both truths separately, from the same three numbers.

• Assuming an overrun will stop on its own. A trend is a forecast, not a blip. If you are behind and over at a third of the way, project that performance across the rest and believe the number.

• Reporting green to avoid a hard conversation. A project that reports green while failing is a debt the crew and the community pay later. Honest, early control is respect, not bureaucracy.

• Using the numbers to punish. A variance is a compass, not a hammer - a signal to ask what the story is and how to help, not a reason to blame. Steer together.

• Leaving control until the end. Control that arrives too late to act is just an autopsy. Look early and often, while the gap is still small enough to close.
