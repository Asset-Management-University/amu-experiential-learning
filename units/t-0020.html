Step 1 of 5

I have R180 million and four projects worth R385 million - how do I choose the best SET of them, instead of ranking them and funding down the list until the money runs out?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning in the boardroom of the Thuso Regional Water Board, in an inland region. You are the new capital planner. The board serves six towns and about 240,000 people. On the table is a single sheet - the draft capital budget. You have R180 million to commit this year, and four projects competing for it:

  • A. Replace the 30-year-old high-lift pumps - R120m, saves R14m a year in energy and breakdowns.
  • B. A new reservoir for the eastern towns - R95m, but only needed if a planned mine expansion goes ahead.
  • C. A telemetry and leak-detection system - R30m, cuts water losses worth R6m a year.
  • D. A second raw-water intake from the river - R140m, a safeguard against drought.

Added up they cost R385 million. You have R180 million. You cannot do them all.

So you do the obvious thing and rank them. A returns R14 million a year on R120 million; D returns R11 million on R140 million; C returns R6 million on R30 million, which is the best rate of the four but the smallest sum. You line them up, draw a line where the money runs out, and it comes to A and D - R260 million, which is R80 million more than you have, so it comes to A and C. On paper that is a defensible plan.

Then the works manager mentions, in passing, that A and D both need the crane crew and the river-shutdown window, and there is one window a year. Your ranking had them first and second.

The board chair, Mrs Diallo, taps the sheet: "Last year's planner ranked them by payback and bought down the list. Then the mine froze its expansion, and we had a R95 million reservoir serving fields of nothing. I do not want that again." She will ask you why you have not funded the two biggest projects, and "because they cannot both happen" is a true answer that a ranked list gave you no way of noticing.

I have R180 million and four projects worth R385 million, and two of my top choices cannot both happen this year. How do I choose the best SET, instead of ranking them and funding down the list?

Before you read on, decide. Four projects, R180 million, one crane window, and a chair who has already watched this go wrong once. Write down what you would actually put in front of her - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does with that crane window

• "Rank by return per rand and fund down the list." The standard move, and it is why you are stuck: the list has a column for return and no column for the fact that two of its entries need the same crane. Applied faithfully it produces a set that cannot be built.

• "Fund A and D anyway and sort the crane out later." A common answer once the numbers look right, and the shutdown window is a physical fact about a river, not a scheduling preference. Later is where this becomes a half-finished intake and an overrun.

• "Ask for more budget." Sometimes correct and worth doing, and it does not touch the problem - with R260 million you still cannot use one crane window twice, so the conflict survives every budget you are given.

• "Weight and score all four against criteria." More rigorous-looking, and it is still ranking. A score per project cannot express "these two are fine apart and impossible together", because that fact does not belong to either project - it belongs to the pair.

• "Take them one at a time - fund A now, decide the rest later." Safe, and it hands the leftover R60 million to whoever asks first, which is how last year's reservoir was bought.

• What the first five share is that they all rank projects. But you are not buying a project - you are buying a combination, and the properties that matter here belong to combinations rather than to their members: which pairs are impossible, which pairs are worth more together, what the whole set costs and how exposed the whole set leaves you. Rank projects and you will find the best projects. You need the best set, and the best set is often not made of the best projects.

T2: The Tool in Action

Put a benefit and a risk against each candidate. Write down which projects cannot go together and which need each other. List the affordable, allowed combinations. Throw out every combination another one beats outright. Then name what the survivor gives up. Watch it run on the four projects.

Step 1 - Put a benefit and a risk against each candidate, in units you can add up

Thabo Nkosi writes four rows. A, the high-lift pumps: R120 million, saves R14 million a year, low risk - a like-for-like replacement with two priced tenders. C, telemetry and leak detection: R30 million, R6 million a year, low risk. D, the second raw-water intake: R140 million, R11 million a year of avoided drought cost, medium risk - the benefit depends on how often the river runs low. B, the eastern reservoir: R95 million, and its benefit depends entirely on whether the mine expands, so he does not put a number in that row at all - he flags it, because a figure he cannot defend would be worse than a blank.

Step 2 - Write down which projects cannot go together, and which need each other

Thabo finds three. A and D both need the same crane crew and the same river-shutdown window, and there is one window a year - so they cannot both happen this year, whatever the budget says. C is worth about a third less without A, because leak detection on pumps about to be replaced measures a system that is about to change. And B is on hold pending the mine's decision. He writes these three lines at the top of the page, because every one of them is invisible to a ranking and each one has sunk a capital plan somewhere.

Step 3 - List the affordable, allowed combinations - the sets, not the projects

Against R180 million and the three rules, Thabo gets four allowed sets. A + C: R150 million, R20 million a year, low risk, R30 million unspent. D + C: R170 million, R17 million a year, medium risk. A alone: R120 million, R14 million, low. D alone: R140 million, R11 million, medium. A + D is not on the list at all - it breaks the crew rule, and it is exactly the set a payback ranking would have chosen, because A and D would have come first and second.

Step 4 - Throw out every set that another set beats on both counts

D + C costs R20 million more than A + C, returns R3 million a year less, and carries more risk - beaten on every count, so it goes. A alone is beaten by A + C, which costs R30 million more and returns R6 million a year more at the same risk, and both fit. D alone is beaten by D + C on the same reasoning. Four sets become one: A + C. Thabo notes what that means and does not hide it - the drought intake is not funded this year, and that is a decision about risk, not an oversight.

Step 5 - Say what the chosen set gives up, and put the unspent money somewhere

Thabo recommends A + C at R150 million: R20 million a year at low risk, and the honest sentence beside it - the drought intake is not funded, so a one-in-twenty-year dry season would cost the board about R11 million and it returns to the table next year first in line. The R30 million left over is not left loose either: the board resolution ring-fences it against the intake, so next year that project needs R110 million rather than R140 million and arrives with a third of its cost already found. Mrs Diallo's question at the meeting is the right one - why not the two biggest projects - and the answer is one line: they cannot both use the crane window, so that set was never available to us.

See what listing sets instead of projects did. A + D - the ranking's own top two - never appears on the list at all, because it breaks the crane rule; and of the four sets that are genuinely available, three are beaten outright by A + C, which returns R20 million a year at low risk for R150 million and leaves R30 million over. Nothing was scored, weighted or argued about to get there. The comparison did it, once the things being compared were combinations that could actually be built. And notice the two sentences Thabo puts beside the recommendation, because they are what makes it survive the meeting rather than merely be correct: the drought intake is not funded, which leaves the board about R11 million exposed in a one-in-twenty-year dry season and it returns first in line next year; and the R30 million left over is allocated on purpose rather than left loose, because unspent money that nobody claimed is how last year's reservoir got bought. For 240,000 people's water money, "we chose the best set we could build" is a defensible sentence in a way that "we funded down the list" has never been.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the second one is the one a ranking cannot do at all.

  • 1. Put a benefit and a risk against each candidate, in units that add up, on one scale you wrote down.
  • 2. Write down the conflicts and the dependencies - which pairs are impossible, which pairs need each other.
  • 3. List the affordable, allowed combinations, and total each one's cost, benefit and risk.
  • 4. Discard every set another set beats on all counts - more benefit, no more risk, no more money.
  • 5. Name what the survivor gives up, and allocate whatever it does not spend.

What you have done is portfolio optimisation, and the whole of it rests on one shift: the thing being chosen is a set, so the properties that decide it - conflicts, dependencies, total exposure - are properties of sets and simply have nowhere to live in a ranked list of members. Move 4 has a name worth knowing because it is the part that needs no judgement: a set is dominated when another available set gives at least as much benefit at no more cost and no more risk, and a dominated set is waste rather than a hard call. What survives after you remove the dominated sets is the efficient frontier - the short list of real trade-offs, where you cannot gain more benefit without accepting more risk or spending more money. Everything below the frontier can be discarded by arithmetic; choosing along it is the judgement you are paid for, and move 5 is where you show it.

It fits any fixed pot with candidates that interact - capital budgets, maintenance shutdowns, hiring plans, research portfolios. Three limits. It needs a benefit unit that genuinely adds, so where the candidates' benefits are incommensurable (a clinic and a road) it will produce arithmetic that hides the real argument. It is only as honest as the risk scale, which is one scale applied the same way to everything or it is a thumb on the scale. And it says nothing about whether any candidate is worth doing at all - a portfolio of bad projects has a frontier too, and refusing the lot is still an available answer.

Three neighbouring questions each have a unit of their own, because each is a different decision and none of them is this one. Whether a request is defined well enough to compete at all - the reason last year's reservoir carried a figure nobody could stand behind - is answered in t-0289, which scores how far each request has been worked out before anybody sees a cost. What the freedom to wait on the mine is actually worth, as a number rather than a hedge, is answered in t-0290, which prices waiting against committing today. And turning a chain of decisions across future years into a standing rule - if the mine expands, build B; if the drought index rises, build D - is t-0087. This unit assumes those three have been answered and asks only which affordable set you buy this year.

Then write the conflicts and dependencies before you combine anything, list the sets you could actually build, and see how many of them one set beats outright.

Two disciplines decide whether this produces a defensible answer or a confident wrong one. First, hunt the conflicts before you total anything, and ask the people who do the work. Shared crews, single shutdown windows, one server migration weekend, the same two experienced fitters - these never appear in a business case, because a business case is written per project and a conflict belongs to a pair. Ask the works manager, the ward sister, the shift supervisor: "if we did both of these this year, what breaks?" A set assembled without that question is an arithmetic exercise about combinations that cannot be built. Second, write your risk scale down before you score anything, and apply it to every candidate the same way. Risk is where a preference hides most comfortably: score the project you favour as low risk and everything else as medium, and the frontier will hand your preference back to you looking like a result. If you cannot say what makes something medium rather than low, you do not have a scale yet - and the honest move is to leave the risk column blank and say so, as Thabo did with the reservoir's benefit, rather than fill it with a number you could not defend to Mrs Diallo.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the choice of set honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Ranking projects and funding down the list. This is the trap the whole method exists to answer. A list of members cannot hold a fact about a pair, so the conflict that makes your top two impossible will not appear until somebody mentions the crane.

• Conflicts and dependencies never written down. They live with the people who do the work, not in the business cases. Ask what breaks if two things happen in the same year, before you total a single set.

• A risk scale invented as you go. One scale, written first, applied identically to every candidate. Scored project by project, risk becomes the column where a preference hides and comes back out looking like arithmetic.

• Benefits that do not add up. Rands a year add; "strategic importance" does not. If the candidates' benefits are genuinely incommensurable, say so - the arithmetic will otherwise bury the real argument rather than settle it.

• A dominated set kept because one of its projects is a favourite. If another available set gives more benefit at no more cost and no more risk, the first one is waste, and no amount of enthusiasm for one of its members changes that.

• The trade-off not stated. Every chosen set leaves something unfunded and somebody exposed. Say what, say how much, and say when it comes back - or the exposure becomes a surprise that lands on the next planner.

• Unspent money left loose. Money the set does not spend gets absorbed or claimed by whoever asks first. Allocate it on purpose, in the same paper.

• The frontier mistaken for a decision. It removes the waste and hands you the genuine trade-offs. Choosing among them is judgement, and it is yours - the method only guarantees you are choosing between sets that are all worth choosing between.

You walked in with R180 million, four projects worth more than twice that, a crane window your ranking could not see, and a chair who had watched good money build a reservoir for fields of nothing. You can put something else in front of her: four candidates with a benefit and a risk on one scale, three conflicts written at the top of the page, the four sets that could actually be built, three of them beaten outright by A + C - and the honest sentence beside it, that the drought intake is not funded, what that exposes, and when it returns. A ranking finds the best projects. You are buying a set, and the best set is often not made of the best projects - which is exactly the fact a list has nowhere to record.
