T1: The Ache

You are Adaeze, and you now have a question and a literature review that holds together. The hard part is supposed to be behind you.

What is in front of you is a year of your life and a form.

The year is the part that frightens you. You will spend it collecting data, and you will not know whether it was worth collecting until the end, and by then the year will be spent. Somebody in your cohort last year got to month ten and found that his measurements could not answer his question. He did not fail. He just started again, with eight months left, and he looks tired now.

The form is the part that irritates you. Eleven pages of ethics application, and you are studying rollers. It reads like it was written to catch you out.

Both of these are the same fear wearing different clothes: that there is something wrong with this and I will not find out until it is too late to fix.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the design - method matched to question, everything else that could produce the result, the denominator, the deliberate pilot, and the ethics application written to what the committee is actually asking.

1. Match the method to the question - and write down why you rejected the others

<strong>A method is chosen for a question, not for a discipline.</strong> My question asks whether idler-roller failure rate differs by season and whether carry-back is associated with it. <strong>List what could answer it, and what each would cost me.</strong> A <strong>controlled experiment</strong> - vary carry-back deliberately and observe failures - would give the strongest causal claim and is unavailable, because deliberately running a conveyor dirtier is a safety and production decision no plant will grant a part-time student. A <strong>prospective study</strong> - instrument now, measure forward - is clean but needs three years of seasons and I have fourteen months. A <strong>retrospective observational study</strong> using three years of existing maintenance records, with a forward inspection component for the residue measurement, fits the time and the access and yields association rather than cause. <strong>Choose it, and then write the sentence that most proposals leave out:</strong> 'A controlled trial was rejected because deliberate degradation of belt cleaning cannot be justified on an operating plant. A prospective design was rejected because the required observation period exceeds the study period.' <strong>Naming what you rejected and why is not padding.</strong> It is the difference between a design and a default, and an examiner can tell which one they are reading within a paragraph.

2. Write down everything else that could produce your result - then design each one out or measure it

<strong>Before you collect anything, write down every other thing that could produce the result you expect.</strong> This list is the single cheapest hour in the whole study. For me: <strong>the commodity changes.</strong> The terminal handles a wetter, finer ore through the same months as the rainy season, so commodity and season move together and either could be doing the work. <strong>The maintenance regime changes.</strong> More inspections happen in the wet season, so more failures may be found rather than occurring. <strong>The throughput changes.</strong> Higher tonnage in one season means more operating hours, and more hours means more failures for no interesting reason at all. <strong>Now deal with each, honestly.</strong> Commodity: I can stratify - there are dry-season months when the same commodity runs, so I compare within commodity as well as across seasons. Inspection frequency: I can obtain inspection counts per month and check whether detection opportunity tracks the pattern. Operating hours: <strong>this one is not a stratification problem, it is a denominator problem, and it is dealt with in box 3.</strong> <strong>And write down the one you cannot fix</strong> - ambient temperature also varies with season, and I have no way to separate its effect from moisture with the data available. <strong>That sentence belongs in the limitations, written by me, now, rather than found by an examiner later.</strong>

3. Work out what you are dividing by - and how much data you actually need - before you collect any

<strong>The denominator is the study. Almost every wrong conclusion in maintenance data is a count that should have been a rate.</strong> Three years of records for the incline sections give <strong>412 roller failures</strong>: <strong>214 in wet-season months and 198 in dry-season months</strong>. Stop there and the answer is that seasons barely differ - 214 against 198, about eight per cent. <strong>Now put in the denominator.</strong> The wet season is roughly five months a year, so across three years it is <strong>15 months of exposure against 21</strong>. The rates are <strong>14.3 failures per month in the wet season against 9.4 in the dry</strong> - a ratio of <strong>1.51</strong>. <strong>The same 412 failures say 'no real difference' and 'half as many again' depending only on what you divided by</strong>, and nothing about the data changed. <strong>Then ask whether you have enough.</strong> 412 events across two groups is a workable volume for the comparison I intend; 41 would not have been, and I would rather know that now than in month eleven. <strong>Where operating hours are available, use them rather than months</strong> - hours are the honest exposure and months are a stand-in for them. <strong>Write down which one you used and why, because the reader cannot tell from the result.</strong>

4. Pilot it small and deliberately - the point is to find the flaw, not to succeed

<strong>Run the entire method small, on purpose, before you run it properly - and go looking for the thing that breaks it.</strong> Mine is <strong>two weeks, about 24 hours</strong>, which is <strong>12 per cent of my 200-hour collection budget</strong>. In it I do every step once: extract three months of records rather than thirty-six, take residue measurements at four locations rather than the full set, and carry both all the way through to a rate. <strong>What am I looking for?</strong> Whether the work orders actually distinguish an idler failure from a scheduled replacement - because if they do not, my entire outcome variable is unusable and no analysis can rescue it. Whether residue depth can be measured repeatably by one person with the access available while the belt is running. Whether the incline sections are identifiable in the asset register at all. <strong>A pilot that goes badly has done its job.</strong> Twenty-four hours spent discovering that failures and replacements are indistinguishable is twenty-four hours; discovering it in month eleven is the year. <strong>Write down in advance what you will do if it fails</strong>, because deciding that in the moment, having just lost two weeks, is the worst condition anybody ever makes that decision in.

5. Write the ethics application to what the committee is actually asking - who could be harmed, and what you have done about it

<strong>The form looks like a legal document and it is not one. It is a committee of people asking a single question in many ways: could this study harm somebody, and has the researcher thought about it.</strong> Answer that question and the form becomes straightforward. <strong>Start by listing everyone the study touches</strong>, which for a records-based engineering study is more people than it first appears. <strong>The people whose work the records describe.</strong> Every work order carries a name. My question is about rollers and seasons, not about who did what - so I have no reason to hold names and I remove them at extraction, which I say in the application. <strong>The people I inspect alongside.</strong> A researcher on a walkway during operations is a person in a live plant, and my presence must not change what anybody does or put them at risk; the site's permit and induction requirements apply to me exactly as to a contractor. <strong>The organisation granting access.</strong> They are exposed if the study is read as an audit of their maintenance, so what will and will not be published, and in what form, is agreed in writing before anything is collected. <strong>Now the sentence that most engineering applications omit and every committee is waiting for:</strong> what happens if I find something unsafe. <strong>Write it down in advance:</strong> 'If inspection reveals a condition presenting immediate risk, it will be reported to the site's responsible engineer that day, before any consideration of the study.' <strong>That is not a concession to bureaucracy. It is the thing an engineer would do anyway, said out loud, in writing, before it is needed.</strong>

T3: The Blueprint

Research design is the practice of finding the flaw that would ruin the study while finding it is still cheap. Five moves:

  • Match the method to the question, and record the rejections. Design, not default. A controlled trial rejected because deliberate degradation cannot be justified on an operating plant; a prospective design rejected because it needs three years and you have fourteen months.
  • List everything else that could produce your result. The alternative explanations. Commodity, inspection frequency, operating hours - each stratified out, measured, or honestly declared as unfixable.
  • Find the denominator before you collect. Rates, not counts. 214 against 198 says "no real difference". 14.3 per month against 9.4 says "half as many again". Same 412 failures.
  • Pilot the whole method small, hunting for the break. The deliberate pilot. 24 hours, 12 per cent of the collection budget, doing every step once.
  • Write the ethics application to the committee's real question. Who could be harmed, and what protects them. Including the sentence engineering applications usually omit.

The arithmetic that carries the unit. Three years of incline-section records: 412 roller failures, 214 in wet-season months and 198 in dry. As raw counts that is a difference of about eight per cent - nothing. But the wet season is roughly five months a year, so over three years the exposure is 15 months against 21. As rates: 14.3 failures per month against 9.4, a ratio of 1.51. The same data, unchanged, says "no effect" or "half as many again" depending entirely on what you divided by. This is not a subtle statistical point. It is the commonest way a maintenance dataset produces a confident wrong answer, and where operating hours are available they are the honest exposure - months are a stand-in.

Why the confounder list is the cheapest hour in the study. Writing down every other thing that could produce your expected result takes an afternoon and changes the design. Adaeze's terminal runs a wetter, finer ore through the same months as the rain, so commodity and season move together - and if she had not noticed, she could have run a flawless year-long study and attributed to weather an effect that belonged to material. The fix is not clever; it is comparing within commodity as well as across seasons, and it costs nothing if you think of it first. Afterwards it costs the study.

On the pilot, and why it is designed to fail. A pilot is not a rehearsal you hope goes well. It is a search for the thing that breaks the method, and the best possible outcome is finding one. The specific fear worth naming: if the work orders do not distinguish a genuine idler failure from a scheduled replacement, the entire outcome variable is unusable and no amount of analysis will rescue it. Twenty-four hours to discover that is twenty-four hours. Month eleven to discover it is the year - and it is what happened to that researcher in the cohort above, who did nothing wrong except find out late.

On the ethics form, and what it actually is. It is not a legal trap and it was not written to catch you. It is a committee of people asking one question in eleven pages of ways: could this harm somebody, and has the researcher thought about it. Once you read it that way you can answer it, because you already know how to think about who a piece of work touches. And engineering applications routinely omit the one thing a committee most wants to see - what you will do if you find something unsafe. Writing that down in advance is not bureaucracy. It is what you would do anyway, said out loud, before you need it.

An honest boundary. This gives you a design you can defend and an application that answers the real question. It does not guarantee approval - the committee holds that, and they may know a consideration you do not. It does not guarantee your study works; some flaws are not visible until you are inside. And it does not replace your supervisor, who has watched this go wrong before in ways neither of you can predict from a canvas.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. What he has right. A specific, answerable question aimed at a real safety concern. Rates rather than counts, unprompted. Three plausible confounders identified and all of them stratifiable. This is careful work, and the problem that follows is not a failure of care - it is a category of risk his engineering training never had reason to cover.

2. The fatal flaw. His study measures the behaviour of identifiable individuals, in a workplace, with findings that reach their employer. Fourteen people on nights, four permit issuers. Any result about night shift is a result about those four.

3. Why anonymisation does not save it. Removing names does not remove identifiability when the population is four. "Non-compliance was higher among night-shift issuers" is, in a plant that size, a sentence with faces attached, and everyone in the building can attach them. The information is in the structure of the finding, not in the name fields - and CCTV compounds it, because footage of a person performing an isolation cannot meaningfully be anonymised at all.

4. The enthusiastic manager. Because he can grant access to data about people who have not been asked, and because his enthusiasm creates the exact pressure the participants cannot resist. If your manager wants you in the study, you are not consenting freely - and Musa, who reports to him too, is not neutral either. "Whatever you need" is an offer of something that is not his to give.

5. A redesign. Keep the safety question; move it off the individuals. Ask instead: "Do the conditions under which isolation permits are issued differ by shift - in supervision availability, permit complexity, concurrent work, and time pressure - and do those conditions differ in ways known to increase procedural error?" That is answerable from rosters, permit records and workload data with no individual behaviour measured, no CCTV, and no consent problem. What is lost: he can no longer say non-compliance is higher at night, which was the original claim. What is gained: he can say night shift is being asked to do the same safety-critical task with less supervision available - which is a finding the organisation can act on, and one that does not require four people to be identified as the problem. It is also, on the evidence, the more likely explanation.

6. His unsafe-finding clause, and why it is harder. "If observation identifies a practice presenting immediate risk to a person, it will be reported to the responsible engineer that day." Adaeze's version costs her nothing - a corroded roller has no interests. Musa's may identify a person and reach their employer, and he has to write it down anyway, in advance, knowing that. That is precisely why it must be written before it happens rather than decided in the moment: safety outranks the study, and the time to commit to that is when it is still hypothetical.

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Treating anonymisation as sufficient when the population is small. Removing names does not remove identifiability when only a handful of people could be the subject - the information is in the structure of the finding, not the name field.

• Skipping the confounder list because the design already feels sound. An afternoon spent listing what else could produce your result is the cheapest hour in the study. Skipped, it becomes the whole year.

• Mistaking enthusiastic access for a green light. An offer of "whatever you need" from someone with authority over your participants is pressure they cannot resist, not a gift you should accept unexamined.

• Running the full method before piloting it small. A pilot that finds nothing wrong has not proven the method works - it may simply be too short to find the break.

What you can do now that you could not before. Choose a method for a question and record what you rejected. List what else could produce your result and design each one out. Find the denominator before you collect a single record. Pilot the whole method deliberately, hunting for what breaks it. And write an ethics application that answers what the committee is really asking.

That researcher from the cohort above did nothing wrong. He found out late, and late is the entire difference. Everything in this unit is machinery for moving the discovery of a flaw from month eleven to week two.

You cannot make a study that cannot fail. You can make one that fails early, while failing is still affordable.
