Eleven risks and the draft contract puts almost all of them on us - how do I decide where each one actually belongs?

T1: The Ache

It is 09:40 on a Tuesday in the council chamber at Mthonjeni District Municipality. You are the lead project analyst. On the table in front of you is a single sheet: a one-page summary of a proposed deal to build and run a new bulk water-treatment works that will serve 180,000 people who currently queue at tankers. A private builder will fund and run it for 25 years; the municipality will pay them a monthly charge.

Across the table, the Chief Financial Officer taps the page. "The builder's lawyers sent their draft last night," she says. "They want us to carry the risk if the river runs low, if the community protests, if the rand falls. We'd carry almost everything. And council votes on Thursday." Her voice is flat and tired. You can smell the burnt coffee from the urn at the back of the room.

Here is what is stuck to you. Your name is on the recommendation memo. If you wave this through and a drought stops the plant, the municipality pays the builder anyway and the taps still run dry - and that will be your signature. If you reject it out of fear, 180,000 people keep queuing at tankers for another five years.

The number that worries you most: the builder's monthly charge is R14.2 million. Over 25 years that is R4.26 billion of public money. The page lists eleven risks and says nothing at all about who should carry each one. Every row is simply written as the municipality's.

You feel the pull to do the easy thing - copy the builder's draft into your memo, trust that their lawyers got it right, and move on. But "their lawyers wrote it" is not the same as "it is fair to the public."

Eleven risks and the draft puts almost all of them on us. How do I decide where each one actually belongs - in a way I can defend on Thursday and reuse on the next deal?

Before you read on, take a position. R4.26 billion of public money over 25 years, eleven risks, a draft written by the other side's lawyers, and council votes on Thursday. Write down how you would decide where each risk belongs - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four positions taken in rooms like this, and what each one costs

• "Push every risk back onto the builder." The instinct of anyone protecting public money, and it feels like the strong negotiating position. It is about to turn out to be the expensive one, for a reason that is not obvious until you see it - and it is the single most common error in these contracts.

• "Accept the draft; their lawyers do this for a living." They do, and they are paid by the other party. The draft is not dishonest - it is one side's opening position, and treating it as a technical document rather than a negotiating one is how R4.26 billion of exposure gets signed.

• "Split them down the middle - fair is fair." Sounds equitable and allocates by arithmetic instead of by anything real. Half the risks would land on a party that can do nothing whatever about them.

• "Refuse the deal - it is too exposed." Defensible, and 180,000 people keep queuing at tankers for another five years. That is also a decision with a cost, and it lands on people who are not in this room.

• What none of these has is a reason that can be written in a column. "We want it" and "they want it" are positions, and a negotiation between positions is won by whoever has more time and better lawyers - which is not you. What settles a row defensibly is asking something about the risk itself rather than about either party's preference: who is actually able to do something about this one?

T2: The Tool in Action

Take the eleven risks one at a time and ask of each, before anything else, who is actually able to control it or absorb it more cheaply. Write the reason in its own column, so every row can be argued with individually rather than as a package. See it run on the Mthonjeni deal.

The two harder calls behind those rows: the rand falling against imported equipment is private during build (the builder can hedge or buy forward), but a defined extreme move beyond a band is shared; a change in national water law is public, because government controls the law and must carry the risk of changing it.

Step back and check the balance: risk is spread by control, not by who has the loudest lawyers. The builder carries what builders control; the public carries what only government controls; the wild, uncontrollable risks are shared by clear rules or insured. The headline finding for the memo: the draft contract pushes drought, demand and law-change risk onto the municipality even though, under the golden rule, demand and law belong to the public but drought should be shared and priced - the draft over-allocates to the public, and three rows need renegotiating.

See what asking one question of each row produced. The draft had put drought, demand and law-change all on the municipality; under the control test demand and law belong to the public and are correctly placed, while drought belongs to neither side and should be shared by a stated rule - so exactly three rows need renegotiating, and you can say which three and why. And notice what happened to the instinct to push everything onto the builder. A party handed a risk it cannot control does not absorb it for nothing; it prices it into the monthly charge. Dumping drought risk on the builder would not have protected the public - it would have made the public pay for it twice, once in a higher charge every month for 25 years and again if the drought came anyway. You go into Thursday with a reason in every row rather than a position.

T3: The Blueprint

Two linked questions sit behind the matrix and the sums you just saw. Each is taught here as the question it answers.

  • 1. What is a PPP, and why does risk need a home? A Public-Private Partnership (PPP) is a long contract where a private firm funds, builds and runs a public asset, and the government pays over time. It only works if risk sits in the right hands.
  • 2. Who should carry each risk? The golden rule, written into PPP guidance worldwide:
    Allocate each risk to the party best able to control it, or to bear it at lowest cost.
    If a risk is something the builder can prevent or fix - like Mthonjeni's construction delay - the builder carries it, because it controls it. If neither side controls it but the public can absorb it more cheaply - like a change in national law - government often carries it. Some risks, like Mthonjeni's drought, are shared, split by a rule agreed up front.
  • 3. Why does this matter for money, not just fairness? A party handed a risk it cannot control does not absorb it for free - it prices it in. The builder will pad the monthly charge to cover a drought risk it cannot stop. Dumping every risk on the private side does not protect the public; it makes the public pay twice - once in the higher charge, and again if the risk lands anyway.
  • 4. What does each row of the matrix need? A name, an owner (public, private, or shared), a why (the reason that owner is best placed), and a treatment (what that owner will do about it) - exactly the four columns Mthonjeni's table used.
  • 5. When does this fit, and what does it not answer? Use a risk-allocation matrix on any PPP, concession or long service contract - it is cheap, it takes an afternoon, and it always pays for itself. It is overkill for a short purchase order. And be clear about the question it does not answer: a perfectly allocated matrix tells you who carries what if this goes ahead, and says nothing at all about whether it should. Whether the deal is worth it to society - counting the queuing time and the illness that never shows up as cash - is a separate assessment with its own arithmetic and its own honesty problems, and t-0283 is where that one lives. Doing the matrix and skipping that question gives you accountability without value; doing that question and skipping the matrix gives you value with nobody on the hook.

T4: Your Turn

Take a PPP, concession or long service contract of your own - a real one you know, or one you can imagine in detail - and build its matrix. Do not copy the Mthonjeni rows; every risk should be one that could genuinely happen to your project. Write your answers on paper before you look at any feedback below; the skill is in the deciding, not in the reading.

The matrix. One row per risk. For the Owner column, answer the golden rule out loud: who can best control this, or bear it at lowest cost? - then write Public, Private, or Shared.

Risk (name it)Owner (Public / Private / Shared)Why this owner is best placedTreatment: what will the owner do about it?

Balance check: Does each row sit by control, not by who has the loudest lawyers? Are the wild, uncontrollable risks shared by a rule or insured, rather than dumped on one side? Which rows does the other party's draft over-allocate - and which will you renegotiate?

Task 1 - One risk row (warm-up). A PPP to build a toll road faces the risk that traffic is lower than forecast, so toll income falls short. Decide the owner (public / private / shared), give the "why", and a treatment. Attempt it, then open the reveal.

What good looks like

Owner = often shared, sometimes public. Why: traffic depends on the wider economy and on government's own road network, which the builder cannot control - but the builder controls road quality, which affects use. Treatment: a minimum-traffic guarantee band from the public side, with upside shared above a ceiling. The error to avoid is dumping all traffic risk on the builder; they will price it into a higher toll and the public pays anyway.

Task 2 - A four-row matrix. A municipality plans a PPP waste-to-energy plant. Build a four-row matrix for: (a) construction delay, (b) waste volumes lower than promised, (c) the electricity buy-back tariff being cut by the national regulator, (d) a major fire at the plant. Give owner, why, treatment for each.

What good looks like

(a) Construction delay - Private, controls the build, fixed-date penalty. (b) Waste volumes low - Public, the municipality controls what waste is collected and delivered, so it carries or guarantees a minimum tonnage. (c) Tariff cut by regulator - Public, government controls the regulator and the policy. (d) Fire - Shared via insurance, builder insures, premium is a known cost line. Marks lost for putting waste-volume or tariff risk on the private side - those are public-controlled.

Task 3 - What a moved risk costs (numbers). Your draft puts drought risk on the municipality. You ask the builder to take it instead, and their revised monthly charge comes back at R16.1 million instead of R14.2 million. Over the 25-year contract, what has that transfer cost the public, and what does the figure tell you about what the builder thinks the risk is worth?

Hint: work in plain monthly terms first - you are after the size of the premium, not a discounted valuation.

What good looks like

The premium is R1.9 million a month, which is R22.8 million a year and about R570 million across 25 years in plain terms. Two readings, and you need both. First, the builder has priced the drought risk at R570 million - and they cannot make rain, so they are not buying the ability to prevent it, only the obligation to absorb it. Second, and this is the one to write in the memo: the public now pays that R570 million whether or not a drought ever happens, where under a shared rule it would pay only if one did. Transferring a risk nobody controls converts an uncertain cost into a certain one, usually at a mark-up. A complete answer also says what to do instead: define the drought threshold and the reduced charge in the contract, so the risk is shared by a rule rather than bought.

Task 4 - Catch the trap (judgement). A consultant hands you a matrix for a hospital PPP. Every one of the fourteen rows says Private in the Owner column, the Why column reads "transferred to contractor" on all fourteen, and the Treatment column is blank on nine of them. The covering note calls it "full risk transfer, maximum protection for the public". What do you challenge, and what do you predict about the price?

What good looks like

Three challenges. (1) "Transferred to contractor" is not a reason - it restates the Owner column. A Why has to say what this party can actually do about this risk, and fourteen identical entries mean the question was never asked row by row. (2) Some of those rows cannot belong to the private side at all - a change in health regulation, or patient numbers driven by a provincial referral policy, are controlled by government, and no contract makes a builder able to influence them. (3) Nine blank treatments mean nine risks with an owner and no plan, which is a list rather than an allocation. And the prediction: the price will be high, and higher than the risks are worth, because the builder has priced fourteen risks including several it can do nothing about. "Full risk transfer" is not maximum protection - it is maximum premium, paid every month whether anything goes wrong or not.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your matrix true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Symptom: your matrix puts almost every risk on the private builder, and their monthly charge comes back very high. Cause: you allocated by "make someone else liable" instead of by control. Fix: re-test each row against the golden rule - who can best control it, or bear it at lowest cost? Move uncontrollable, un-insurable risks to shared rules or to the public side. The charge should fall.

• Symptom: a risk has no obvious owner - neither side really controls it (a currency crash, a 50-year flood). Cause: you are forcing a binary choice on a risk that is genuinely external. Fix: use the third option - shared by a stated rule, or insured so a wild risk becomes a small fixed cost. "Shared" is not a cop-out; it is the correct answer for external risks.

• Symptom: the Why column says the same thing on every row. Cause: the allocation was decided as a package and the reasons written in afterwards. Fix: a Why has to say what this party can do about this risk. If you cannot write a different sentence for each row, you have not allocated anything - and a negotiator will take the matrix apart one row at a time.

• Symptom: rows have an owner but the Treatment column is empty. Cause: you stopped at naming who is liable. Fix: liability is not management - an owner with no treatment is a risk nobody is actually doing anything about, and it will arrive with a contract argument attached. Every row needs something the owner will do.

You came into the council chamber with a one-page draft you could not defend and a CFO warning you that the public would carry almost everything. You can leave with eleven rows, each carrying an owner, a reason that is different from every other reason on the page, and something the owner will actually do - and with the three rows the draft got wrong, named, with the argument for each. A reason in every row beats a position, because a position is negotiated and a reason is either true or it is not. On Thursday, when the builder's lawyers push back on drought, the answer is not a preference. It is a fact. Neither party can make rain, so the risk is shared by a stated rule. The alternative - for them to carry a risk they cannot control - is not free. It costs the public R570 million, paid every month for 25 years, whether the drought comes or not. That is the choice on the table.
