The second signature

T1: The Ache

On paper, the controls were all there. Every large spares order needed a second, independent signature. The stores issues were reconciled against the work orders every month. An auditor had signed off the system. By the book, the money and the assets were protected.

And still it happened: an inflated order went through to a favoured supplier, the money was gone, and the trail led back through a control that had been followed to the letter. The second signature was on the form. The reconciliation had been done. The system did not fail on paper - it was defeated, from the inside, by someone with the authority to lean past it and the will to do so, and a junior who did not feel free to say no.

The hard defences were real, and they were not enough - because the soft foundation underneath them was not there.

We had all the controls and it happened anyway. If a signature and a reconciliation cannot stop a determined insider, what actually protects an organisation - and what is the point of the controls at all?

Before you read on, decide. The order was for R240,000 of parts that never arrived at the store. The second signature is on the form. The monthly reconciliation was done, on time, by the book. Nothing failed on paper. What happened is that the section head stood at the young storeman's desk and asked him to sign it now, and said he would sort the paperwork out later — and the storeman signed, because the last person who queried something of his was moved to another site within the month, and everybody knows it. Write down what actually protects an organisation — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what every control has in common

• “Add a third signature.” Another person who can be asked to sign now and sort it out later. If the second signature could be leaned past, so can the third — you would be adding cost and delay and no protection.

• “Automate it — take the human out.” Moves the discretion rather than removing it. Somebody configures the system, somebody holds an override, and the same conversation happens about a screen instead of a form.

• “Audit more often.” The reconciliation was done and revealed nothing, because the paperwork was complete. More frequent inspection of correct-looking documents finds correct-looking documents.

• “Discipline the storeman — he signed it.” He did, under pressure from somebody senior, in an organisation that had already shown him what querying costs. Punish him and you will have taught the next storeman the same lesson more firmly.

• “You cannot stop a determined insider.” Comfortable and half true, and it ignores that this insider needed somebody else's compliance — which was available, and did not have to be.

• Understand the defences properly first. They come in two kinds: those that stop the bad thing — separated duties so no one person controls a whole risky transaction, authorisation limits, a second signature, a locked store — and those that reveal it afterwards — reconciliations, exception reports, the figure that does not fit. The second kind prevents nothing, and the knowledge that it will reveal is itself a deterrent. But every single one of them is executed by a person, and that is where the limit sits. A signature is a control only if the signatory is free to refuse. A separation of duties holds only while neither party will simply ask the other to look away. So the whole structure rests on something softer than itself: whether people believe refusal is survivable, whether authority can be questioned, whether the quiet no is backed or punished. That foundation is not built by writing a code of conduct, though one helps — it is built by what visibly happens the first time somebody uses it. Your organisation has already run that experiment once, and everybody watched the result.

Here is how controls and ethics hold each other up.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how the hard defences and the soft foundation work together - what internal controls are, where they reach their limit, how ethics is the ground they stand on, how a code of ethics supports them, and how you design ethics in.

1. What internal controls are

START WITH WHAT THESE THINGS ACTUALLY ARE, BECAUSE THE WORD CONTROLS SOUNDS BUREAUCRATIC UNTIL YOU SEE THE JOB THEY DO. INTERNAL CONTROLS ARE THE DELIBERATE DEFENCES AN ORGANISATION BUILDS TO PROTECT ITS ASSETS, ITS RECORDS AND ITS PEOPLE - to stop error, waste, fraud and non-compliance before they cause harm, and to catch them when they slip through. They come in TWO KINDS, and a good system uses both together. PREVENTIVE CONTROLS STOP THE BAD THING HAPPENING: the SEGREGATION OF DUTIES (no single person controls a whole risky transaction, so the person who orders cannot also pay, and the person who records cannot also approve), the AUTHORISATION LIMIT, the SECOND SIGNATURE on a large order, the physically LOCKED STORE. They put a barrier in the path of the mistake or the theft. DETECTIVE CONTROLS CATCH IT AFTER THE FACT: the monthly RECONCILIATION (do the stores issues match the work orders?), the EXCEPTION REPORT (flag the transaction that breaks the pattern), the ANALYTICAL REVIEW (the number that does not fit). They do not prevent, they reveal - and the knowledge that they will reveal is itself a deterrent. Together, preventive and detective controls SAFEGUARD THE ASSETS AND KEEP THE RECORDS HONEST, and it is worth being clear about who they work on: the ORDINARY run of risk - the tired mistake, the duplicate order, the small opportunistic temptation. For all of that, controls work well, and they are worth every bit of the effort to build and maintain. Hold onto that, because the next box is about where they stop - but it is not a reason to think them pointless; it is a reason to know what they must be built upon.

2. The limit of controls - reasonable, not absolute

NOW THE TRUTH THE WHOLE UNIT TURNS ON, AND IT IS NOT A FLAW TO BE PATCHED BY ADDING MORE CONTROLS - IT IS INHERENT AND PERMANENT. INTERNAL CONTROLS PROVIDE REASONABLE ASSURANCE, NEVER ABSOLUTE, because they have limitations that no control can design away. Four limitations, and TWO OF THEM MATTER MOST because they are the ones that break a system that looks perfect on paper. MANAGEMENT OVERRIDE: a person with enough authority simply overrules the control - and the second signature means NOTHING when the person demanding it outranks the person giving it, because the independence the control assumed has been crushed by the hierarchy. COLLUSION: a segregation of duties assumes the two people are INDEPENDENT and will check each other, but two people who quietly AGREE to defeat it can walk straight through, each providing the cover the other needs. Add the ordinary two - HUMAN ERROR (people make mistakes even inside good controls) and COST (you cannot afford infinite controls) - and the conclusion is unavoidable: A CONTROL IS ONLY AS GOOD AS THE INTEGRITY OF THE PEOPLE OPERATING IT. Here is the precise reach of a control: it STOPS THE HONEST PERSON FROM AN ACCIDENTAL SLIP, and it DETERS THE CASUAL, OPPORTUNISTIC CHEAT who fears being caught - genuinely valuable, and most of your risk. BUT A DETERMINED, DISHONEST INSIDER WITH AUTHORITY CAN DEFEAT ALMOST ANY CONTROL. This is exactly why a fraud can go through a system that was followed to the letter: nothing in the mechanism itself can stop a person with power leaning on a person without it. Controls are necessary and never sufficient - the hard defences rest on a soft foundation, which is the rest of this unit.

3. Ethics is the foundation - the control environment

IF CONTROLS CANNOT SOLVE THE OVERRIDE-AND-COLLUSION PROBLEM, WHAT DOES? The answer is the thing the entire framework quietly rests on, and it is not another control - it is the CONTROL ENVIRONMENT: the TONE AT THE TOP, the ethical values, the integrity and the culture of the organisation. This is the FOUNDATIONAL component of any internal-control system, the ground on which every specific control stands, and it is foundational for one exact reason: IT DECIDES WHETHER PEOPLE OPERATE THE CONTROLS IN GOOD FAITH OR QUIETLY WORK AROUND THEM - and that single fact is covered by NO control on the list. You can mandate a second signature; you cannot mandate that the two people care whether the order is honest. An ethical culture makes controls actually EFFECTIVE, because people follow them out of shared integrity, not merely out of fear of being caught, and because a leader who visibly MODELS integrity makes the very pressure that defeated the control in the ache story unthinkable to apply - and unsupported if anyone tried. THIS IS THE ONLY REAL ANSWER TO OVERRIDE AND COLLUSION. The defence against a senior manager leaning on a junior authoriser is not a fourth signature (they would override that too); it is an organisation where the manager does not lean in the first place, and where, if they did, the junior would be immediately and unquestionably backed by everyone above. YOU CANNOT CONTROL YOUR WAY TO INTEGRITY. The hard defences of internal controls must rest on the soft foundation of an ethical culture, and one without the other is a performance - controls without ethics are a locked door in a wall with no roof, and ethics without controls is good intentions with no way to catch the honest mistake. You need both, and the controls stand on the ethics, never the other way round.

4. The code of ethics - and how it supports the controls

A CULTURE HAS TO BE WRITTEN DOWN TO BE SHARED, TAUGHT AND ENFORCED, AND THAT IS WHAT A CODE OF ETHICS IS: THE PLAIN ARTICULATION OF THE INTEGRITY STANDARD EVERYONE IN THE ORGANISATION IS HELD TO - usually with a WHISTLE-BLOWER CHANNEL and ETHICS TRAINING alongside it, so the standard is not just stated but supported and practised. Now the specific point the outcome asks for: HOW A CODE OF ETHICS SUPPORTS THE INTERNAL CONTROLS, in three concrete ways. FIRST, IT STATES THE STANDARD, plainly and in advance, so no one can later claim they did not know where the line was - the expectation is explicit, not assumed. SECOND, IT CREATES A SHARED EXPECTATION, so operating the controls honestly is the visible, normal thing that everyone is seen to do, not a personal quirk of the unusually scrupulous - which makes the dishonest act socially isolated rather than quietly tolerated. THIRD, AND MOST IMPORTANT FOR THE PERSON UNDER PRESSURE: IT GIVES THEM THE AUTHORITY AND THE PERMISSION TO REFUSE. A junior authoriser asked to approve the inflated order has, in the code, an EXPLICIT ORGANISATIONAL LINE to stand on ('orders to connected suppliers are prohibited') and a SAFE CHANNEL to raise it - rather than only their own lonely courage set against a senior person career-shaping pressure. THE CODE TURNS 'I PERSONALLY DO NOT WANT TO DO THIS' INTO 'THE ORGANISATION DOES NOT PERMIT THIS, AND HERE IS WHERE I REPORT IT' - which is a vastly stronger and safer place to stand, because now the junior is enforcing the organisation policy, not defying their boss. A code that does this is a real support to the control system; a code that is merely a poster on a wall, unlived and unenforced, supports nothing and, worse, signals that the standard is for show.

5. Design ethics in - at the pressure points

THIS IS WHERE THE ROLE OF ETHICS IN DEVELOPING CONTROLS BECOMES CONCRETE, AND IT IS THE HEART OF THE OUTCOME. YOU DO NOT DESIGN CONTROLS EVENLY ACROSS EVERYTHING - that wastes effort on the low-risk and under-protects the high-risk. YOU DESIGN THEM AROUND WHERE THE ETHICAL PRESSURE POINTS ARE: the places where money, power and temptation concentrate, where someone will predictably be leaned on to sign what they should not - PROCUREMENT, PAYMENTS, APPROVALS AND AUTHORISATIONS, SAFETY SIGN-OFFS, THE DISPOSAL OF VALUABLE ASSETS. At those points you place BOTH the strongest CONTROLS (independent authorisation, tight reconciliation, rotation of duties so no one can be reliably leaned on, declared conflicts of interest) AND the clearest ETHICAL LINES (an explicit code provision, a named safe channel), because that is exactly where the control environment will be tested. So DESIGNING A CONTROL SYSTEM IS NOT MERELY AN ACCOUNTING EXERCISE - IT IS AN ETHICAL ONE: the design question is 'WHERE WILL A GOOD PERSON BE PUT UNDER PRESSURE TO DO A BAD THING?', and you build both the hard defence and the soft support precisely there. AND HOLD THE TWO DIGNITY TRUTHS THAT MUST TRAVEL WITH ALL OF THIS. FIRST: INTERNAL CONTROLS ARE NOT A SIGN OF DISTRUST. THEY ARE A SIGN OF PROFESSIONALISM. THEY PROTECT THE ORGANISATION FROM ERROR AND FRAUD, AND THEY PROTECT THE HONEST PERSON FROM FALSE ACCUSATION AND FROM THE BURDEN OF UNCHECKED TEMPTATION. A CLEAN RECONCILIATION IS YOUR SHIELD - the second signature is not an insult to your honesty, it is what lets you prove it. SECOND: A CONTROL SYSTEM THAT LEAVES THE MOST JUNIOR PERSON TO FACE AN ETHICAL TEST ALONE IS A FAILED DESIGN. THE PURPOSE OF A STRONG CONTROL ENVIRONMENT AND A CLEAR CODE OF ETHICS IS TO ENSURE THAT THE BURDEN OF INTEGRITY IS CARRIED BY THE ORGANISATION, NOT BY THE INDIVIDUAL UNDER PRESSURE. Design the world where the junior is backed before they ever have to be brave.

T3: The Blueprint

Controls catch the honest slip and deter the casual cheat, but only an ethical culture stops the determined insider. You cannot control your way to integrity. Five questions:

  • What are internal controls? The deliberate defences for assets, records and people - preventive (segregation of duties, authorisation) and detective (reconciliations, exception reports).
  • What is their limit? Reasonable assurance, never absolute. Management override and collusion defeat them; a control is only as good as the integrity of the people operating it.
  • What holds them up? The control environment - the tone at the top and the ethical culture. It decides whether people honour the controls or work around them.
  • What does the code of ethics do? States the standard, gives a shared line, and permission to refuse. It supports the controls and protects the person under pressure.
  • How do I use ethics in design? Put the strongest controls and clearest lines at the pressure points - where money, power and temptation concentrate - and never leave the junior alone.

What internal controls are. Internal controls are the deliberate defences an organisation builds to protect its assets, its records and its people - to stop error, waste, fraud and non-compliance before they cause harm, and to catch them when they slip through. They come in two kinds, and a good system uses both. Preventive controls stop the bad thing happening: the segregation of duties (no single person controls a whole risky transaction, so no one can both order and pay), the authorisation limit, the second signature, the locked store. Detective controls catch it after the fact: the monthly reconciliation, the exception report, the analytical review that flags the number that does not fit. Together they safeguard the assets and keep the records honest - and, crucially, they do this for the ordinary run of risk: the tired mistake, the duplicate order, the small temptation. For that, they work, and they are worth every bit of the effort.

The limit of controls. But here is the truth the T1 story turns on, and it is not a flaw to be fixed by adding more controls - it is inherent. Internal controls provide reasonable assurance, never absolute, because they have limitations no control can design away. The two that matter most: management override - a person with enough authority simply overrules the control, and the second signature means nothing when the person demanding it outranks the person giving it - and collusion - a segregation of duties assumes the two people are independent, and two people who agree to defeat it can walk straight through. Add the ordinary limits (human error, the cost of controls) and the conclusion is unavoidable: a control is only as good as the integrity of the people operating it. Controls stop the honest person from an accidental slip and deter the casual, opportunistic cheat who fears being caught - but a determined, dishonest insider with authority can defeat almost any control. This is why the T1 fraud went through a system that was followed to the letter: nothing in the mechanism could stop a person with power leaning on a person without it.

Ethics is the foundation, not a bolt-on. So if controls cannot solve the override-and-collusion problem, what does? The answer is the thing the whole framework quietly rests on: the control environment - the tone at the top, the ethical values, the integrity and the culture of the organisation - which is the foundational component of any internal-control system, the ground everything else stands on. It matters because it decides whether people operate the controls in good faith or quietly work around them, and that single fact is not covered by any control on the list. An ethical culture makes controls effective: people follow them because they are right, not only because they might be caught, and a leader who models integrity makes the pressure that defeated the T1 control simply unacceptable to apply. This is the only real answer to override and collusion - not a fourth signature, but an organisation where the manager does not lean on the junior in the first place, and where, if they did, the junior would be backed. You cannot control your way to integrity; the hard defences must rest on a soft foundation, and one without the other is a performance.

The code of ethics, and how it supports the controls. A culture needs to be written down to be shared and taught, and that is what a code of ethics is: the plain articulation of the integrity standard everyone in the organisation is held to, usually with a whistle-blower channel and ethics training alongside it. And it supports the internal controls in three concrete ways. It states the standard, so no one can claim they did not know the line. It creates a shared expectation, so operating the controls honestly is the visible norm, not a personal quirk. And - most important for the person in the T1 story - it gives them the authority and the permission to refuse: a junior asked to approve the inflated order has, in the code, an explicit organisational line to stand on and a safe channel to raise it, rather than only their own lonely courage against a senior's pressure. The code turns "I personally do not want to" into "the organisation does not permit this, and here is where I report it" - which is a far stronger place to stand.

One honest boundary, and it decides how this whole unit lands. First, on what controls are for: internal controls are not a sign of distrust. They are a sign of professionalism. They protect the organisation from error and fraud, and they protect the honest person from false accusation and from the burden of unchecked temptation. A clean reconciliation is your shield. The second signature is not an insult to your honesty; it is what lets you prove your honesty, and what removes the weight of temptation from your shoulders. Second, on who carries the ethical load: a control system that leaves the most junior person to face an ethical test alone is a failed design. The purpose of a strong control environment and a clear code of ethics is to ensure that the burden of integrity is carried by the organisation, not by the individual under pressure. The design-side answer to the pressured junior is not "be braver" - it is an organisation that made sure they never had to face it alone.

Design ethics in - at the pressure points. This is where the role of ethics in developing controls becomes concrete. You do not design controls evenly across everything; you design them around where the ethical pressure points are - the places where money, power and temptation concentrate, where someone will be leaned on to sign what they should not: procurement, payments, approvals, safety sign-offs, the disposal of valuable assets. There you place the strongest controls (independent authorisation, tight reconciliation, rotation of duties) and the clearest ethical lines (an explicit code provision, a named safe channel), because that is exactly where the environment will be tested. Designing a control system is therefore not just an accounting exercise - it is an ethical one: you ask "where will a good person be put under pressure to do a bad thing?", and you build both the hard defence and the soft support precisely there.

Does it scale? A household can have a rule that big purchases need both partners to agree (a control), and it will stop the impulsive mistake - but it cannot survive one partner hiding money from the other, because that is not a rule problem, it is a trust problem, and no rule fixes a broken trust. The rules keep an honest relationship tidy; only honesty keeps it whole. A home and an organisation both learn that a control is a help to integrity and never a substitute for it.

T4: Your Turn

Here is a control and two threats to it.

The control: at the utility, any spares order over R50,000 needs a second, independent authoriser (a segregation of duties), and the stores issues are reconciled against work orders each month.

Threat A: a busy planner accidentally raises a duplicate order for the same pump seals.

Threat B: a senior manager pressures the junior second-authoriser to approve an inflated R80,000 order to a supplier the manager is quietly connected to.

  1. Which threat does the control stop, and which does it not? Say why in one line each.
  2. Name the limitations. Which two inherent limitations of internal controls are at work in Threat B?
  3. What actually stops Threat B? Name what in the control environment and in the code of ethics would stop it where the control alone could not.
  4. Design. Given that procurement is a known pressure point, name one change to the design - a control or an ethical line - that would make Threat B harder, and say who should carry the responsibility (and why not the junior alone).

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the control stops Threat A - the second signature and the reconciliation catch a duplicate order easily, because that is an honest slip and everyone in the chain is free to flag it. It does not stop Threat B on its own, because the person demanding the approval has authority over the person operating the control, so the control's independence is broken by the pressure - the signature happens, and it is worthless.

Second, the limitations in Threat B are management override (a person with authority overruling the control) and, given the manager's hidden supplier connection, the makings of collusion (two parties conspiring to defeat the segregation). These are precisely the two limitations that no additional control can fully remove.

Third, what stops Threat B is the soft foundation: a control environment with a genuine tone at the top - where leaders visibly model integrity, so applying that pressure is unthinkable and, if attempted, unsupported by anyone above; and a code of ethics that gives the junior an explicit line ("orders to connected suppliers are prohibited; you may and must refuse") and a safe channel to report the pressure without fear. Together they turn a lonely act of courage into a backed organisational stance.

Fourth, the design: because procurement is a pressure point, sensible changes include rotating the second authoriser so no one can be reliably leaned on, requiring declared conflicts of interest on suppliers, or escalating orders above a threshold to a panel rather than a single junior. And the responsibility must sit above - with the leadership that owns the control environment and the code - because a system that leaves the most junior person to carry the ethical test alone is a failed design: the burden of integrity belongs to the organisation, not to the youngest person in the chain.

T5: The Guardrails

A signature on a form is not proof the right thing was done. Controls catch the honest slip and deter the casual cheat - but only an ethical culture stops the determined insider.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Trusting controls as absolute. They give reasonable assurance, never certainty. A system with every control in place can still be defeated from the inside.

• Ignoring override and collusion. The two failures that matter most cannot be fixed by adding a control - a person with authority overrules it, and two people conspiring walk through it. Only the ethical environment answers them.

• Bolting ethics on as an afterthought. The control environment is the foundation, not the decoration. Design ethics in from the start, at the pressure points.

• A code of ethics that is a poster, not a practice. A code nobody lives is worse than none, because it signals the standard is for show. It must be modelled, trained, and enforced to mean anything.

• Leaving the junior to carry it alone. A design that puts the ethical test on the most junior person has failed. The burden of integrity belongs to the organisation and its leaders.

• Designing controls away from the pressure points. Put the strongest controls and clearest lines where money, power and temptation concentrate - not evenly across everything.

• Treating controls as distrust. Controls protect the honest as much as they deter the dishonest - a clean reconciliation is your defence against a false accusation. That is professionalism, not suspicion.

• A tone at the top in words but not in deeds. Culture is set by what leaders do under pressure, not what the values statement says. Integrity is modelled or it is absent.

Every control was in place and the fraud went through anyway, because the controls assumed the people operating them were independent and free to refuse, and a determined insider with authority simply leaned past them. So understand what controls can and cannot do: they are the deliberate defences for your assets, records and people, preventive and detective, and they stop the honest slip and deter the casual cheat superbly - but they give reasonable assurance, never absolute, because management override and collusion can defeat them, so a control is only as good as the integrity operating it. The foundation those hard defences rest on is the control environment, the tone at the top and the ethical culture that decides whether controls are honoured or worked around; a code of ethics writes that standard down, supports the controls, and gives the person under pressure a line to stand on and a channel to raise it; and you design ethics in by putting the strongest controls and clearest lines exactly where money, power and temptation concentrate. You cannot control your way to integrity - so build the hard defences on the soft foundation, and make sure the burden of that integrity is carried by the organisation, never dumped on the most junior person in the chain. The next time you are shown a system of controls, do not only ask whether the signatures are in place - ask what happens the day someone with authority tells the junior to sign.
