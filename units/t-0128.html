The pump that looked fine

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you are doing this properly. Next year's maintenance budget is R1.2 million and you refuse to allocate it on hunches, so you export every work order from the maintenance system and write a script to calculate mean time between failures for every pump on the plant. Worst assets first, money follows evidence.

The script runs perfectly. The table is neat, sorted, and quietly satisfying. Pump 1 sits in the middle of it at 1,752 hours - unremarkable, nothing to see, no action.

Months later, Pump 1 fails catastrophically and takes the supply down with it.

You go back to the raw export, and there it is. That pump appears under three different names - "Pump 1", "P-01" and "PUMP #1" - because three different people typed it three different ways over the years. Its failures were split across all three, so no single name ever looked bad. Two jobs had also been exported twice. Once you resolve the names and remove the duplicates, the pump did not fail five times. It failed ten. Its real MTBF was 876 hours - exactly half of what your table said, and one of the worst figures on the entire site.

Your code was flawless. Every line did precisely what you asked. Your data was filthy - and nothing, anywhere, warned you. Dirty data does not crash. It does not turn red. It simply answers wrong, with total confidence, and lets you spend a budget on the wrong machines.

My script ran perfectly and gave me an answer that was exactly half the truth, because the data underneath it was a mess. How do I clean an asset dataset so the answers I build on it can be trusted?

Before you read on, decide. The script ran perfectly. It ranked the plant's worst assets from four thousand rows of maintenance history, and the budget went to the machines at the top. Pump 1 was not among them. Pump 1 had failed ten times, but it appears in the export as “Pump 1”, “P-01” and “PUMP #1”, so your analysis counted three unremarkable machines instead of one bad one. Nothing crashed. Nothing turned red. There are 287 distinct asset names in that file for a plant with about 180 assets, and you never looked. Write down how you would clean a dataset so answers built on it can be trusted — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why dirty data never warns you

• “Fix the names in the spreadsheet and re-run it.” It gets today's answer and destroys your evidence. Once the export is edited, nobody — including you in six months — can say what the source actually said or what you changed, and the analysis becomes unrepeatable and undefendable.

• “Drop the incomplete rows so the data is clean.” The most dangerous answer on the list, because it feels like tidying. The messiest records are usually the worst events — the long, ugly repairs nobody wrote up properly — so deleting them makes the plant look healthier than it is, and does so silently.

• “Fix the source system so it stops happening.” Right, and it is next year's project. It does nothing about the four thousand rows of history you must answer from today.

• “Check the totals reconcile.” Your totals were fine. Three fragments of one machine sum to exactly the same number of jobs as one machine; the total is the one view in which this error is perfectly invisible.

• “Eyeball the names and merge the ones that obviously match.” Half right, and by hunch — which is where invented data gets in. “P-01” and “P-1” may be the same machine or two machines in different buildings, and the difference between checking and assuming is the difference between analysis and fiction.

• The reframe is that cleaning is not preparation for the work — it is the work, and it is analysis rather than tidying, because every decision inside it moves the answer. Profile before touching anything: you cannot clean what you have not measured, and 287 names for 180 assets is itself a finding. Resolve identity by a written rule you can defend, checked against the asset register, never by which names look alike. Decide and declare what happens to what is missing or duplicated — drop it, fill it, or flag it, but say which and why, and prefer flagging to dropping for the reason above. And never edit the original: it is evidence. Every step lives in a script and a log, so the result is repeatable and every judgement is visible to whoever has to defend it — which, this time, was you.

There is a one-page discipline for this, and its first rule is that you never touch the original. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the cleaning record that would have found Pump 1 before it found her - every decision visible, and the last box asking the only question that really matters.

1. Profile the raw data - what have I actually got?

Before touching anything: 4,312 rows; 287 distinct asset names (for a plant with about 180 assets - a warning in itself); 41 blank completion dates; 6 rows with text in the hours column; dates in two different formats. You cannot clean what you have not measured, and you cannot later prove what you changed.

2. Standardise identity - is this one asset, or three?

The same pump appears as 'Pump 1' (5 rows), 'P-01' (4 rows) and 'PUMP #1' (3 rows) - 12 rows, one machine. Resolve them to a single asset ID with a written mapping table and a stated rule, checked against the asset register. Identity resolution needs a rule you can defend, never a hunch about which names look similar.

3. Duplicates and missing values - decide, and DECLARE

Two of those 12 rows are the same job exported twice - removed on a stated key (asset + date + order number), leaving 10 real failures. The 41 blank completion dates are FLAGGED, not dropped: dropping them would delete the longest, messiest repairs and quietly make the plant look healthier than it is. Every choice is declared: drop, impute, or flag.

4. The script and the change log - repeatable and auditable

The raw export is never edited - it is evidence. Every step lives in a script that runs again, unchanged, on next month's export, beside a change log: what was changed, why, and by whom. Hand-cleaning in a spreadsheet has to be redone from memory every month, and nobody can check it.

5. Verify - and did the ANSWER change?

Re-profile and compare: 4,312 rows in, 4,310 after de-duplication; 287 distinct names down to 181. Then the question that matters most: MTBF for this pump moved from 1,752 hours to 876 - exactly half. It was never mid-table; it was one of the worst assets on site. Cleaning is not tidying: it changed the answer.

Look at the last line. The answer changed - by a factor of two. That is the whole argument of this unit: cleaning is not tidying up before the real work, it is the work, because every choice in those middle boxes moves the number you are about to make decisions with. And notice the third box - the missing dates were flagged, not quietly dropped. Now let us name the craft.

T3: The Blueprint

What lied to Onalenna was not one big fault but three ordinary, nameable ones - and naming them is the first step to catching them:

  • Inconsistent identity - one real asset recorded under several names. It understates failures, because each fragment looks harmless on its own.
  • Duplicate records - the same job captured twice. It overstates failures.
  • Missing values - a blank completion date, an empty hours field. It silently removes work from whatever you calculate.

Notice that the first two push in opposite directions, and both are completely invisible in the final number. That is why you cannot judge data by looking at the answer - a wrong answer looks exactly like a right one.

So the craft, each step answering a question:

  • What have I actually got? Profile before you change anything: row count, distinct asset names, blanks per column, date range. If a plant with 180 assets shows 287 distinct names in its work orders, you have found your identity problem before writing a line of cleaning code - and you now have the evidence of your starting state.
  • Is this one asset or three? Identity resolution: build a mapping table from a stated rule, checked against the asset register. A rule you can defend, never a hunch about which names look similar - because "P-01" and "P-10" look similar too.
  • Which rows are real, and what do I do with what I cannot fix? Remove duplicates on a stated key. Then, for every value you cannot repair, choose openly: drop, impute, or flag - and say which. This matters more than it sounds. Silently dropping every row with a missing completion date feels tidy and is quietly the most dangerous thing in this unit: those blanks cluster on the long, messy, complicated repairs - exactly the failures that matter most - so dropping them makes the whole plant look healthier than it is.
  • Can anyone repeat this? Never edit the raw export - it is evidence. Put every step in a script that re-runs unchanged on next month's export, with a change log of what and why. Hand-cleaning in a spreadsheet must be redone from memory every month, and nobody - including you in six months - can check what you did.
  • Did the answer change? Re-profile, compare, and state it plainly. Here: 1,752 hours became 876. If cleaning never changes an answer, you probably have not looked hard enough.

And test the cleaning itself, exactly as you would test any other engineering calculation - a known-answer case with three name variants that must collapse to one asset is a perfectly good test.

Does it scale? In a materials laboratory you never write on the original sample, and you log every preparation step so another engineer can repeat it. Your raw export is the sample; your cleaning script is the lab notebook - and a result reported without either is not a result anyone should act on.

T4: Your Turn

Now clean something real and small. Take a messy dataset from your own world - a club or society membership list, a shared expenses sheet, a training log, a spreadsheet someone else built.

  1. Profile it first. How many rows? How many distinct names or entries? How many blanks, and in which columns? Write the numbers down before you change anything.
  2. Find an identity clash. Almost every real list has one - the same person or item entered two ways. State the rule you would use to resolve it.
  3. Find a duplicate - and say what key you used to decide it was one.
  4. Find one missing value and DECLARE your choice in a single sentence, including the consequence. For example: "I chose to FLAG the row with the missing date rather than drop it, because dropping it would have quietly improved the average." This sentence is compulsory.
  5. Answer the question. Re-profile, and say whether your headline number changed - and by how much.

T5: The Guardrails

Data cleaning is where good analysis is quietly won or lost. Glance through these before you trust a number.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Silently dropping rows with missing values. The most dangerous default in this unit, because it feels like tidiness. Blanks cluster on the awkward, long, complicated jobs - the very ones you needed to see - so dropping them biases every result towards optimism. Flag them, or drop them and say so out loud.

• Editing the raw export. The moment you type over the original, nobody can reproduce or check your work, and your evidence is gone. Treat it as read-only, always.

• Cleaning by hand in a spreadsheet. It works once, and then next month arrives and you must remember what you did. Script it, or you have not really solved anything.

• Guessing at identity. Assuming similar names are the same asset - or that different-looking names are different assets. "P-01" and "P-10" are one character apart and are not the same pump. Use a rule and the asset register.

• Imputing and forgetting. Filling a gap with an average is sometimes right, but an invented value that nobody records as invented becomes a fact by the next meeting. Mark it.

• Never re-profiling. If you cannot say how many rows and names you started and finished with, you cannot prove what your cleaning did - or catch it if it went wrong.

• Not testing the cleaning code. It is engineering code like any other. Give it a small known-answer case and check it does what you claim.

Onalenna's script never made a mistake; it faithfully computed the wrong answer from data nobody had questioned, and a pump that was among the worst on the plant sat quietly mid-table until it failed. Now you hold the discipline that prevents it: profile before you touch anything, resolve identity by rule, de-duplicate on a stated key, declare every choice about what you cannot fix, script it all so it can be re-run and checked - and then say plainly whether the answer changed. Do that, and the numbers you carry into a budget meeting will deserve the confidence everyone is about to place in them.
