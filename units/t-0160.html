It has always been on the list

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and in front of you is the maintenance plan for the clarifier drive unit at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. Forty tasks. A hundred and twenty labour-hours a year. Eighteen years of accretion.

You ask an artisan why a particular point is greased every week, and you get the only honest answer available: it has always been on the list. You go looking for where it came from. Some tasks trace to a vendor manual written for a machine that was modified a decade ago. Some were added after an incident in 2009 that nobody present remembers. Some arrived with a colleague who has since retired, and left no note.

And the drive keeps failing - reliably, expensively - in ways this plan does not appear to touch at all.

You know what the rigorous answer is. It is a full RCM analysis: functions, failure modes, consequences, tasks derived from evidence. You also know now what that costs. And doing it here - on an old, well-understood machine sitting on eighteen years of failure history in your own CMMS - would mean throwing away everything already known in order to derive it again from a blank sheet.

It feels like being ordered to demolish a structurally sound wall in order to verify its dimensions.

This asset already has a plan and a history, and nobody can defend either. How do I rationalise the plan I have - without the cost of starting from nothing, and without deleting the one task that was quietly holding the thing together?

Before you read on, decide. Forty recurring tasks on this clarifier drive. Some came from the original supplier, some were added after incidents nobody now remembers, some were copied from another plant, and one is a two-minute check a fitter has done faithfully every week for years because he says it matters. Nobody can defend the plan. You are being told to derive it again properly from first principles — on a well-understood machine sitting on eighteen years of failure history in your own records. It feels like being ordered to demolish a structurally sound wall to verify its dimensions. Write down how you would rationalise this plan — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and which direction the method should run

• “Do the full first-principles analysis as instructed.” Correct on a new machine and wasteful here, because it discards eighteen years of evidence in order to re-derive what those years already recorded. You would pay a great deal to arrive at conclusions you could have read.

• “Cut the tasks nobody can justify.” Fast, defensible-sounding, and it will eventually delete the fitter's two-minute check — which looks exactly like the waste and may be the one thing holding the machine together. An unexamined cull is as dangerous as leaving it alone.

• “Leave it — it works, do not touch it.” Preserves the load-bearing task and every wasteful one with it, and concedes that nobody will ever be able to defend the plan. It also costs real money every week.

• “Ask the supplier what they recommend.” Their recommendation is one of the sources that got you here, written for the machine as a type and for their liability rather than your eighteen years.

• “Benchmark against another plant's plan.” Substitutes somebody else's accretion for yours. Their plan has the same problem and none of your history.

• The point is direction. Deriving from first principles runs forwards — from functions, through failure modes, to tasks, on a blank sheet — and it is the right instrument when you have no history. You have eighteen years of it, so run backwards instead: start from the tasks you actually perform and the failures you have actually had. That direction carries its own honest precondition, and it is what keeps this from being a shortcut — it requires an existing plan and a real failure history, so it could not be used on a new machine at all. Two moves make it work. Surface every task from every source first — the system schedule, the operator round sheet, the statutory list, the supplier's manual — because an accreted plan does not live in one place and cannot be rationalised while parts of it are invisible. Then test each task against the failures this machine has actually had. That converts an argument about opinions into an examination of evidence, and it is what lets you delete genuine waste and keep the unglamorous two-minute check, once the record shows what it has been quietly preventing.

There is a method that starts from what you already do. Here it is, for the clarifier drive.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the PM Optimisation pass - the same forty tasks, interrogated backward.

1. Why this method and not RCM? (justify the instrument first)

The choice of method is part of the answer, so justify it. RCM works FORWARD - from functions, through failure modes, to tasks, on a blank sheet. PMO works BACKWARD - from the tasks you already do and the failures you have already had. That direction sets the precondition: PMO REQUIRES an existing PM programme and failure history, and without one it is very difficult if not impossible. This clarifier drive has eighteen years of both, so PMO is the right instrument - and the corollary is that we could NOT use it on a new machine. RCM is reserved for critical assets where failure-mode data is LACKING or a coverage gap would be dangerous. On the economics: published benchmarks suggest PMO delivers the majority of RCM's benefits for roughly ONE-SIXTH of the analytical effort. Local experience varies with asset complexity and data quality - a ten-to-one ratio is a valid local figure - but both tell the same story: for reviewing an existing plan, PMO is an order of magnitude more efficient.

2. What are we actually doing? (compile every task, from every source)

You cannot rationalise what you have not surfaced, and a legacy plan does not come from one place - it ACCRETES. Pull every task into a single master list, from all of it: the CMMS schedule, the operator round sheet, the statutory inspection list, the vendor manual (written for a machine that has since been modified), the actions added after an incident years ago, and the unwritten one the crew does every Friday because a colleague who has now retired always did. That last category is the one desk-based reviews miss entirely, so go and ask the people who do the work. The clarifier drive comes to 40 TASKS consuming 120 LABOUR-HOURS A YEAR - and this is the first time anyone has seen them all on one page.

3. Which failure mode does this address? (the question that does all the work)

Now the one question nobody asked when these tasks were written. For EVERY task, in its own column: WHICH FAILURE MODE DOES THIS ADDRESS? If you cannot write a plausible failure mode in that box, the task is unjustified by default and is flagged for action - and 'it has always been on the list' is not a failure mode. Here 12 of the 40 tasks fail that test and come off the plan, releasing 30 hours (12 of 40 is 30 per cent, squarely in the 30 to 40 per cent that structured reviews typically find unjustified). A further 6 tasks turn out to address failure modes ALREADY covered by other tasks - the same mode attacked from three different sources over eighteen years - and merge into 3, a net saving of 3 tasks and 6 hours. THE CRITICAL PMO SAFETY CHECK, before ANY task is deleted: is it a STATUTORY task; and is it a FAILURE-FINDING task for a HIDDEN failure - a standby pump, a trip, an alarm that only acts on demand? Those tasks look pointless precisely because their purpose is to confirm a protective function still works. Deleting one because nothing ever happens is a critical error. IF IN DOUBT, THE TASK STAYS.

4. What does the plan never protect? (find the gaps, then re-decide the policy)

This is the half everyone skips, and it is where the value is. Turn the question around: go to the failure HISTORY and list the modes this asset actually suffers - then ask which of them NO task addresses. Here there are 5, and five tasks are added, costing 14 hours. That is why the drive kept failing in ways the plan never touched. Then re-decide the policy on every surviving task: evaluate the CONSEQUENCE of its failure mode (hidden, safety, environmental, operational, non-operational), and ask whether this task is appropriate and effective and whether the interval is right - or whether the honest answer is condition-based monitoring, a redesign, or an accepted run-to-failure. A task that survives this box has a named failure mode, a graded consequence and a defended interval. It is no longer on the list because it has always been on the list.

5. The rationalised plan (and what it really bought you)

GROUP the survivors by trade, frequency and access so the plan can actually be executed, get it APPROVED by someone accountable, and set the REVIEW TRIGGER so it does not accrete again. Then report the result honestly: 40 tasks and 120 hours becomes 30 TASKS AND 98 HOURS - a saving of 22 hours a year, about 18 PER CENT. Read that number carefully, because it is the point. The saving is MODEST. What the pass actually bought was five failure modes that were destroying this machine and are now covered, twelve tasks nobody could defend removed, and a plan every line of which can be justified out loud. PMO IS NOT A COST-CUTTING EXERCISE. A PMO that only deletes tasks has not found the gaps - which means it is incomplete, and on a plan that was already failing to protect the asset, that is dangerous.

Look at the numbers in box 5 before you read on. Twenty-two hours a year. That is the entire cash saving from rationalising an eighteen-year-old plan, and if that were the point, this would barely be worth doing. Now look at what else changed in box 4. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

When a legacy asset has a maintenance history, you do not need a blank sheet. You rationalise the plan you have by working backward from each task to the failure mode it prevents. Five questions:

  • Why this method and not RCM? Direction is the whole difference. RCM works forward - functions, failure modes, tasks, from a blank sheet. PM Optimisation works backward - from the tasks you already perform and the failures you have already had. Hence its precondition: PMO requires an existing PM programme and failure history, and without one it is very difficult if not impossible - which is exactly why it fits a legacy asset and cannot be used on a new one. RCM stays reserved for critical assets where failure-mode data is lacking or a coverage gap would be dangerous.
  • What are we actually doing? Task compilation from every source - CMMS schedule, operator rounds, statutory list, vendor manual, post-incident additions, and the unwritten work only the crews can tell you about. You cannot rationalise what you have not surfaced.
  • Which failure mode does this address? The engine of the whole technique, asked of every single task, in its own column. No plausible failure mode means unjustified; two tasks on one mode means duplication. Structured reviews commonly find 30 to 40 per cent of existing tasks have no technically justified basis.
  • What does the plan never protect? Turn the question around and go to the failure history: which modes does this asset actually suffer that no task addresses? Then re-decide the policy on the survivors - grade the consequence (hidden, safety, environmental, operational, non-operational), and confirm the task type and interval, or move to condition-based, redesign or accepted run-to-failure.
  • What did it really buy? Group the survivors so the plan is executable, have it approved by someone accountable, set the review trigger, and report before and after honestly - including new coverage, not only hours saved.

The critical PMO safety check. A task with no obvious purpose is not automatically waste. Before any task is deleted, ask two questions. Is this a statutory task - a pressure-vessel inspection required by law? Is this a failure-finding task for a hidden failure - testing a standby pump, a trip or an alarm that only functions on demand? These tasks prevent failures whose consequences are not visible during normal operation. They often look pointless precisely because their purpose is to confirm that a protective function is still available. Deleting them because "nothing ever happens" is a critical error that re-introduces the very risk the task was designed to manage. If in doubt, the task stays.

One honest boundary. You are rationalising an existing plan by working backward from its tasks to the failure modes they address, and justifying that choice of method. You are not performing a full RCM analysis forward from functions, not building a plan from scratch for a new machine, and not deriving individual intervals from first principles. Those are separate techniques - and choosing between them is itself the first thing this one asks you to do.

Does it scale? RCM is a commission to write a new instruction manual from first principles. PMO hands you an eighteen-year-old manual - full of handwritten margin notes, superseded sections and pages for parts that are no longer fitted - and asks you to edit it into a rational, effective document for the machine that exists today. You are the editor, not the original author. And a good editor does two things: cuts what cannot be defended, and notices what was never written down at all.

T4: Your Turn

An old screw conveyor has accumulated eight PM tasks over eleven years. Its failure history shows three recurring failures: bearing seizure at the tail end, shaft coupling wear, and trough liner wear leading to leakage.

6. Statutory guard and interlock inspectionAnnually3Regulatory

8. Inspect and log trough liner thicknessQuarterly6Added after 2019 leak
  1. Justify the method. In two or three sentences, say why PMO is the right instrument for this conveyor and why you are not running full RCM.
  2. Work backward. For each of the eight tasks, name the failure mode it addresses - or write "none identified".
  3. Rationalise. Say which tasks you delete, which you merge, and which you keep, applying the critical PMO safety check before any deletion. Total the hours before and after.
  4. Find the gaps. Compare your surviving tasks against the three recurring failures. Which is not properly addressed, and what task would you add?
  5. Report it. State the hours saved and the coverage gained.

The test of a real answer: there are two ways to fail this, and both feel like success at the time.

The first is task 5. It is monthly, it costs six hours a year, and nothing has ever been found - which is exactly what a working protective device looks like. It is a failure-finding task for a hidden failure, and the emergency stop is discovered to be broken at the only moment it matters. Deleting it because "nothing ever happens" would be the classic PMO fatality. Task 6 is statutory and equally untouchable. Meanwhile task 4 costs eight hours a year and addresses no failure mode at all, and task 7 collects data nobody reads - which is either a task to delete or, more interestingly, a condition-monitoring task nobody ever finished building.

The second failure is quieter. If your answer reports hours saved and does not list a single added task, you have not done a PM Optimisation - you have done a cost-cut. Look again at the three recurring failures and ask which one your plan still does not properly address.

T5: The Guardrails

PM Optimisation fails in two opposite directions, and the dangerous one looks like a win.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Treating PMO as a deletion exercise. The central error. If the output is a shorter list and nothing else, the gaps were never looked for, and the plan that was already failing to protect the asset is now shorter and still failing.

• Deleting a hidden-failure or statutory task. They look pointless because their purpose is to prove a protective function still works. Run the safety check before every deletion, and if in doubt, the task stays.

• Never asking what is missing. Half the method runs backward from the tasks; the other half runs from the failure history to the tasks that do not exist. Skipping it is skipping the value.

• Using PMO where there is no plan or history. On a new machine there is nothing to interrogate. That is an RCM job, and no amount of enthusiasm makes PMO work without its precondition.

• Using PMO on a critical asset where the failure-mode data is lacking. The economics are attractive and the answer is still no - that is precisely the case RCM is reserved for.

• Accepting "it has always been on the list". It is a description of how the task survived, not of what it prevents.

• Merging tasks that look similar but address different failure modes. Similar wording is not the same purpose, and the merge quietly drops the mode nobody noticed.

• Reviewing the paperwork and never asking the crews. The unwritten tasks are invisible from a desk, and they are often the ones keeping the machine alive.

• Letting it accrete again. Without a review trigger and a rule for adding tasks, you are simply resetting an eighteen-year clock.

Onalenna's plan had forty tasks and no defence. Justify the instrument before you use it - PMO for a legacy asset with history, RCM where the data is thin and the consequences are severe. Compile every task from every source, including the unwritten ones. Ask of each one which failure mode it addresses, and let the ones with no answer go - after the safety check, because statutory and hidden-failure tasks are not deletable and look most deletable of all. Then turn it around and find the failures nobody was protecting against. Twenty-two hours a year was never the prize. The prize was a plan whose every line can be said out loud. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take one real legacy plan and add the column - just the column - and see how many boxes come back empty.
