A rumour with a total

T1: The Ache

You are the practitioner, and the monthly maintenance cost report for the pump section is due before the budget meeting in an hour. You run the extract from the system, and the headline is wonderful: the section is 40 per cent under budget - R300,000 spent against a R500,000 budget. Your manager needs good news for that meeting, and here it is, in black and white, straight from a system that cost millions.

Submitting it would make you the hero of the morning. And yet something stops your hand. Forty per cent under, in a month you know had three big pump overhauls in it? That is not a triumph - that is a number too good to be true, and a number too good to be true almost always is.

Because the total will add up perfectly, and it will still be a lie - and once it is in the meeting, a decision will be made on it before anyone thinks to ask what is missing from underneath.

The system gave me a precise, tidy total that says we are doing brilliantly - so why don't I trust it enough to submit it? How do I turn a raw extract into a number I can stand behind?

Before you read on, decide. The extract is beautiful. Neat columns, a precise total, and the pump section twenty per cent under budget — in a month you personally watched contain three major pump overhauls. You know the contractor bills quarterly. You know one purchase order has not been drawn against yet. The budget meeting is on Thursday and somebody will make a decision on this figure. It will add up perfectly. Nobody in that room will be able to see what is missing underneath it. Write down what you would do before submitting it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what a report actually is

• “Submit it — it is what the system says.” The system reported what it was told, accurately. Passing it on transfers a figure you already disbelieve to somebody who has no way of disbelieving it.

• “Submit it with a caveat that it may be incomplete.” Better, and a caveat beside a precise number loses every time. The total is quantified and the warning is not, so the meeting will use the total.

• “Adjust the number to what it feels like it should be.” Replaces a wrong figure you can trace with a wrong figure you cannot. Now nobody, including you, can reconstruct where it came from.

• “Wait until the invoices arrive and report late.” The decision happens Thursday with or without you. Being right a month later is being absent from the decision.

• “Report it and explain the variance if anybody asks.” Nobody will ask, because a favourable variance never gets challenged. That is precisely why an implausibly good number is more dangerous than an implausibly bad one.

• The reframe that changes the job: this is not an output you run off and pass on — it is a claim about reality, and the moment you submit it somebody believes it and acts on it. They cannot see the errors under a tidy total, because a figure from an expensive system looks exactly as authoritative when it is wrong as when it is right: the confidence is in the formatting, not the figure. Which makes a wrong report worse than no report — with none you go and find out, and with a wrong one you confidently decide the wrong thing and never learn why it failed. So your actual job is not running the extract, which takes a second. It is making the claim true before you make it — and that means reconciling it against reality from more than one direction: against what was committed, against what was physically done, against what you know happened, because each catches errors the others cannot. And notice the specific cause here: this is timing rather than performance. The work was done and the cost exists; the invoices simply have not landed. Including what has been incurred but not yet billed is what turns an accidental snapshot into a period figure somebody can compare.

Here is how you produce a report you can trust.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you turn an extract into a cost report a decision-maker can act on - treating it as a claim, knowing what feeds it, extracting the right data, reconciling it against reality, and presenting it so it is read right.

1. The report is a claim - make it true before you make it

START HERE, BECAUSE IT CHANGES THE WHOLE JOB FROM A CLERICAL TASK INTO A PROFESSIONAL RESPONSIBILITY. A COST REPORT IS NOT AN OUTPUT YOU RUN OFF AND PASS ON. IT IS A CLAIM ABOUT REALITY - 'THE PUMP SECTION COST THIS MUCH THIS MONTH' - AND THE MOMENT YOU SUBMIT IT, SOMEONE WILL BELIEVE IT AND ACT ON IT. A manager will carry it into a budget meeting, defend a position on the strength of it, cut or approve on the strength of it - and here is the trap: THEY CANNOT SEE THE ERRORS UNDERNEATH A TIDY TOTAL, because a total from a system that cost millions looks exactly as authoritative when it is wrong as when it is right. The confidence is in the formatting, not the figure. SO A WRONG REPORT IS WORSE THAN NO REPORT: with no report you go and find out; with a wrong report you confidently decide the wrong thing and never know why it failed. THIS REFRAMES YOUR ACTUAL JOB. It is not to run the extract - the system does that in a second. It is to MAKE THE CLAIM TRUE BEFORE YOU MAKE IT, and that takes a discipline of four parts, which are the rest of this unit: KNOW WHAT FEEDS THE REPORT, EXTRACT THE RIGHT DATA, RECONCILE IT AGAINST REALITY, and PRESENT IT SO IT IS READ CORRECTLY. THE SINGLE HARDEST TRUTH OF THE UNIT: A COST REPORT IS NOT THE OUTPUT OF A SYSTEM; IT IS A VERIFIABLE CLAIM ABOUT REALITY THAT A PROFESSIONAL STANDS BEHIND.

2. Know what feeds the number

YOU CANNOT TRUST A TOTAL WHOSE INGREDIENTS YOU CANNOT NAME, SO KNOW THE STREAMS THAT FEED A MAINTENANCE COST REPORT - because knowing what SHOULD be in it is the only way you will ever notice when something is missing. There are a few, and they all flow through the ERP charged against a cost centre, a work order, or an asset number. LABOUR: the hours booked to work orders, valued at a labour rate. MATERIALS AND SPARES: issues from the store, plus items bought directly for a job. CONTRACTORS AND SERVICES: outside work, which travels a specific path - a PURCHASE ORDER is raised, a GOODS RECEIPT confirms the work or delivery, and finally an INVOICE posts - and it is NOT a real, actual cost in your report until that invoice posts, which is the source of a great deal of trouble. And sometimes ALLOCATIONS OR OVERHEADS charged in from elsewhere (workshop time, stores handling). These costs also sit in a HIERARCHY that rolls upward - equipment into cost centre, cost centre into department, department into plant - so the same cost can be viewed at several levels. Two reasons this matters concretely: first, if you do not know a stream exists, you will not notice when it is absent (the missing contractor invoice); second, the hierarchy means you must later extract at the RIGHT level, because pulling too high sweeps in other sections and pulling too low misses charges. Name the streams, and you have a checklist of what a true report must contain.

3. Extract the right data - the silent errors live here

PULLING THE FIGURES FROM THE ERP IS NOT ONE BUTTON - IT IS A SET OF CHOICES, AND THE WRONG CHOICE RETURNS A WRONG NUMBER THAT LOOKS EXACTLY AS CLEAN AS A RIGHT ONE. This is where the silent errors live, the ones that throw no error message and simply hand you a confident, wrong total. You must set THREE things correctly. THE DIMENSION: which cost centre, asset class, project, or work-order type you are reporting - and at which LEVEL of the hierarchy (too high sweeps in other sections, too low misses charges). THE PERIOD: the months you are reporting. And - THE ONE THAT CATCHES EVERYONE - THE DATE BASIS: the POSTING date, the DOCUMENT date, and the date the WORK WAS ACTUALLY DONE are three different dates, and choosing the wrong one mis-assigns cost between periods, so a cost belonging to March lands in April. You must also never confuse COMMITTED cost - a purchase order raised, money promised - with ACTUAL cost - an invoice posted, money spent - because treating a commitment as a cost overstates, and ignoring it can understate a forecast. THE COMMON EXTRACTION ERRORS ARE ALL OF THIS SHAPE, AND NONE OF THEM THROWS AN ERROR: the wrong date basis (timing); committed-versus-actual confusion; a missing cost element or GL account (a whole category left out); a double-counted allocation; extracting at the wrong cost-centre node; and open or incomplete postings caught mid-flight. Learn to distrust the extract at exactly this step, because a report is only ever as right as the parameters that pulled it, and the parameters are invisible in the output.

4. Reconcile - the step that catches the lie

THIS IS THE HEART OF THE UNIT AND THE STEP EVERYONE SKIPS, SO HOLD THE CRUCIAL TRUTH FIRST: THE DANGEROUS ERROR IN A COST REPORT IS ALMOST NEVER AN ARITHMETIC MISTAKE - THE TOTALS ALWAYS ADD UP. It is a TIMING, CODING, OR COMPLETENESS error in the SOURCE DATA that the report faithfully totals into a confident, precise, wrong number: the invoice not yet posted, the overhaul miscoded to the wrong cost centre, the stores issue that never reached the report. The extract ran, so it was trusted. RECONCILIATION IS THE ONE STEP THAT CATCHES ALL OF THESE, and there are THREE, each asking a different question of the number. BUDGET VERSUS ACTUAL: is this variance REAL, or is it a timing artefact - a missing accrual, an invoice not yet in? A variance too large to be plausible is a data flag, not a result. ERP VERSUS PHYSICAL ACTIVITY: does the money match the WORK THAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED - the work orders closed, the spares issued - or has cost appeared with no work behind it, or has real work happened with no cost yet booked? For the second case you ACCRUE: you put the cost of work done but not yet invoiced into the period the work happened, because that is where it honestly belongs. PERIOD ON PERIOD: does this month make sense against last month, because A SUDDEN DROP IS FAR MORE OFTEN MISSING DATA THAN A REAL SAVING - the pleasant surprise is usually the loudest alarm. Run all three, resolve what they surface (re-code the miscoding, accrue the unposted, chase the missing), and only THEN do you have a number instead of a rumour with a total. A COST REPORT WITHOUT RECONCILIATION IS A RUMOUR WITH A TOTAL.

5. Present in the standard template - and a variance is a question

FINALLY, PRESENT THE RECONCILED TRUTH IN A FORM THAT IS READ CORRECTLY, BECAUSE A TRUE NUMBER IN A CONFUSING SHAPE CAN STILL MISLEAD. USE THE STANDARD TEMPLATES, the ones the reader already knows how to read. THE PERIOD COST REPORT: cost by cost centre and by element (labour, materials, contractors) for the period - what was spent, and on what. THE BUDGET-VERSUS-ACTUAL REPORT: budget, actual, the variance and its percentage, WITH a line of commentary explaining the variance - the most-used report in the budget meeting. THE ASSET LIFECYCLE-COST SUMMARY: the cumulative cost of an asset across its whole life, for repair-versus-replace and end-of-life decisions. Why standard forms matter beyond neatness: A TEMPLATE THE READER KNOWS LETS THEM SPOT THE ANOMALY THEMSELVES - a figure in the wrong place, a variance that does not look right - so the familiar format is part of the accuracy, not just the presentation. NOW THE DIGNITY THAT MUST TRAVEL WITH EVERY REPORT, AND IT IS NON-NEGOTIABLE. FIRST, ABOUT THE WORK: A WRONG FIRST-PASS REPORT IS NOT THE ANALYST BEING CARELESS - TIMING LAGS, POSTING DELAYS AND CODING SLIPS ARE HOW ERPS ACTUALLY BEHAVE, AND RECONCILIATION IS PRECISELY THE DISCIPLINED THING A PROFESSIONAL DOES BECAUSE THEY KNOW THE RAW EXTRACT LIES; the skilled reporter is not the one whose extract is never wrong, but the one who never submits it unreconciled. SECOND, ABOUT THE READER: A VARIANCE IS A QUESTION, NOT A VERDICT. 'Under budget' can mean missing invoices as easily as good management; 'over budget' can mean necessary work was done - so a cost report must never be used to judge a person before it is reconciled and understood. The report exists to help someone act on reality, not to catch anyone out. Write the commentary to open an inquiry, not to close a case.

T3: The Blueprint

A cost report is not the output of a system; it is a verifiable claim about reality that a professional stands behind. A cost report without reconciliation is a rumour with a total. Five questions:

  • What am I actually producing? A claim, not an extract. A wrong report is worse than no report, because it is believed and acted on. Make the claim true before you make it.
  • What feeds the number? Labour, materials and spares, contractors and services, allocations - flowing through the ERP against cost centres, work orders and assets. You cannot trust a total whose sources you cannot name.
  • Did I extract the right thing? The right dimension, period and date basis. Committed is not actual; the wrong date or the wrong cost-centre node silently returns the wrong number.
  • Does it match reality? Reconcile three ways - budget vs actual, ERP vs the physical work, and period-on-period - and accrue for work done but not yet invoiced.
  • Will it be read correctly? Use the standard template - and remember a variance is a question, not a verdict.

Does it scale? A student checks their banking app and sees far more money than they expected, and feels rich for a day - until they remember the rent debit order and the loan repayment have not gone off yet. The balance was a real number and a false picture, for exactly the reason the cost report was: the costs already incurred had not yet posted. Reconcile the balance against what you know is coming, and you have the truth; act on the raw figure, and you overspend. A plant and a bank account are read the same way.

T4: Your Turn

Here is the very report from your desk. The pump section, this month:

ItemFigure
Budget for the monthR500,000
Raw extract "actual"R300,000 (shown as 40% under budget)

You know three facts the extract does not show: (i) three large overhaul work orders were completed this month; (ii) the stores issues for those overhauls - R120,000 of spares - were booked to the wrong cost centre and are missing from this report; (iii) the biggest contractor's invoice, R90,000 for work already done, has not yet posted.

  1. Why is the headline suspect? In one sentence, say why "40% under budget" is a red flag, not good news.
  2. Run the three reconciliations. Name what each one (budget-vs-actual, ERP-vs-physical, period-on-period) tells you here.
  3. The accrual. State the one accrual you would make, and why an accrual is the honest thing to do.
  4. The corrected figure. Give the true actual for the pump section this month, and the variance against budget.
  5. The commentary. Write the one line you would put on the budget-versus-actual template - phrasing the variance as a question, not a verdict.

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the suspicion: 40 per cent under budget in a month with three major overhauls is implausible - real work costs real money, so a big underspend almost certainly means cost is missing from the report, not saved.

Second, the three reconciliations. Budget-vs-actual: the 40 per cent gap is too large to be real - a timing/completeness flag. ERP-vs-physical: three overhauls were completed, yet almost no material cost appears - the money does not match the work, so cost is miscoded or unbooked. Period-on-period: last month, with similar work, was near budget, so a sudden drop signals missing data, not a saving. All three point the same way: the report is incomplete.

Third, the accrual: accrue the R90,000 contractor invoice, because the work was done this month and the cost belongs to this month even though the invoice has not posted yet - leaving it out would understate this period and overstate the next. Accruing puts the cost where the work happened.

Fourth, the corrected figure: restore the miscoded R120,000 of spares and add the R90,000 accrual: R300,000 + R120,000 + R90,000 = R510,000, which against a R500,000 budget is R10,000, or about 2 per cent, over - a plausible, honest result for a heavy-overhaul month, and the exact opposite of the story the raw extract told.

Fifth, the commentary reports the truth and opens an inquiry. For example: "Pump section 2% over budget for the month, driven by three planned overhauls; is the overhaul programme running to plan, or is this the start of a trend we should look at?" This phrasing reports the fact and poses a question for management, rather than either hiding behind a false underspend or landing a blame for the overspend.

T5: The Guardrails

The total always adds up. That is exactly why the total is not the thing to check. A cost report without reconciliation is a rumour with a total.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Submitting the raw extract. The extract is a first draft, not a report. It is always incomplete at first - posting lags and coding slips are how ERPs behave. Reconcile before you submit.

• Trusting a tidy total. A precise number from a big system looks authoritative whether it is right or wrong. The confidence is in the formatting, not the figure.

• The wrong date basis. Posting date, document date and work date are different, and choosing wrong mis-assigns cost between months. Know which date your extract used.

• Committed mistaken for actual. A purchase order raised is not a cost incurred. Mixing committed and actual over- or under-states the report.

• Extracting at the wrong cost-centre node. Too high in the hierarchy sweeps in other sections; too low misses charges. Pull at the level that matches the report.

• Missing an accrual. Work done but not yet invoiced still belongs to the period it happened in. Accrue it, or this month looks cheap and next month looks dear.

• Reading a too-good variance as good news. A large underspend in a busy month is usually missing cost, not saved cost. Treat the pleasant surprise as the loudest alarm.

• Using a variance as a stick. A variance is a question, not a verdict. Over budget can mean necessary work; under budget can mean unposted invoices. Reconcile and understand before anyone is judged.

• A non-standard template. A report in an unfamiliar shape hides its own anomalies. The standard template lets the reader spot what is wrong, which is part of the accuracy.

The extract said the pump section was 40 per cent under budget, and it was tidy, precise, and false - because the biggest invoice had not posted and R120,000 of spares were miscoded away. Treat a cost report as a claim about reality and make it true before you make it: know the streams that feed it - labour, materials, contractors, allocations - so you notice what is missing; extract the right data, minding the dimension, the period, the date basis, and committed-versus-actual, because the silent errors live there; reconcile three ways - budget against actual, the money against the physical work, this period against the last - and accrue for work done but not yet invoiced; and present the reconciled truth in the standard template. A cost report without reconciliation is a rumour with a total - and a variance is a question, not a verdict, never a stick. The next time a number too good to be true lands on your screen an hour before the meeting, do not submit it and do not re-add it - reconcile it, and hand over a figure your manager can safely stand on.
