Forty pumps on paper

T1: The Ache

You are the practitioner, and the pump-station asset register says you have 40 pumps. You walk the station with a tag scanner, and you find 38. Two are simply not there.

You could shrug at a gap of two. But the gap is worse than it looks, and in two directions at once. Two of the missing pumps were scrapped after failures a year ago and never taken off the register - so the company is still insuring them, still depreciating them, still planning maintenance for machines that no longer exist. And while you are looking, you find two other transfer pumps in the workshop that are running real duty and are not on the register at all - bought on a work order, never captured, so they are uninsured, unmaintained, and invisible to every plan you make.

The register is not a small two short. It is paying for ghosts and blind to real machines - and every plan, budget and valuation you have built on it is quietly wrong.

My register says forty and the plant has thirty-eight - and even that gap lies both ways. How do I know what kinds of assets I actually have, and how do I make the record match the reality again?

Before you read on, decide. The register says forty pumps. You walked the plant and found thirty-eight. Two short, easily fixed — except it is not. Two of the entries are for machines scrapped years ago and still being carried, insured and depreciated. And there are pumps in the workshop running real duty that are not on the register at all — bought on a work order, never captured, so they are uninsured, unmaintained, and invisible to every plan you make. Nobody was careless; each happened through something ordinary nobody thought to record. Every budget, plan and valuation you have built rests on this. Write down how you would make the record match reality — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the totals prove nothing

• “Delete two entries and the numbers agree.” Makes the totals match and leaves the record more wrong than before — you would be deleting two arbitrary lines while real machines stay invisible and the actual ghosts may well survive the cull.

• “It is only two out of forty — five per cent, close enough.” The net gap is two. The gross error is much larger, because it runs in both directions and the two partly cancel. Counting totals cannot detect that; only comparing item by item can.

• “Do a full physical count once and fix it.” Necessary, and treated as a one-off it decays immediately — the register did not break, it drifts continuously through work-order purchases, transfers, cannibalisations and quiet scrappings.

• “Tell people to update the register properly.” Asks for diligence against events nobody currently recognises as register events. The person who bought a pump on a work order was doing their job.

• “Trust the plant, not the paper — we know what we have.” You did not: you have just found machines nobody could name from the record, and it is the record that drives the insurance, the maintenance plan and the valuation.

• The scale is the first correction: a small net difference conceals large errors in both directions — paying for ghosts while blind to real machines — and every plan built on it is quietly wrong both ways at once. Then two disciplines. First, name each thing as the kind of thing it truly is, because the classification is not filing: it is the first decision determining how something is tracked, valued, maintained and protected, and getting it wrong makes every downstream choice wrong to match. Second, reconcile the record against the plant deliberately and periodically, item by item — because a register does not decay through negligence but through perfectly ordinary events: bought on a work order, moved between sites, cannibalised for parts, quietly scrapped. A register is a map, and a map is useful only if it names things correctly and matches the territory. Drift is its normal condition rather than a scandal, which is exactly why the reconciliation has to be a routine rather than a reaction.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you come to know your asset base - seeing that an asset is not one thing, naming the kinds and how each is managed, understanding how the register drifts, reconciling it against reality, and resolving what you find.

1. An asset is not one thing - name the kind

START BY REFUSING THE LAZY WORD. 'ASSET' HIDES REAL DIFFERENCES, AND YOU CANNOT MANAGE WHAT YOU HAVE NOT CORRECTLY IDENTIFIED. Think of the register as your MAP of what you own - and a map is only useful for two reasons, both of which this unit is about: it must NAME each thing as the KIND it truly is, and it must MATCH the territory. This box is the first of those: naming. A PHYSICAL ASSET is the umbrella term - a tangible thing you can touch, as opposed to a financial or intangible asset - and underneath that umbrella live several distinct KINDS, and the whole reason to tell them apart is that EACH IS MANAGED DIFFERENTLY (which is the next box). The classification is not an academic filing exercise; it is the first decision that determines how the asset is tracked, valued, maintained and protected. Get the kind wrong at the start and every management choice downstream is wrong to match: you will track a thing that cannot move as though it might, and fail to track a thing that walks; you will maintain a single machine when you should be managing a whole network's service. So before you manage an asset, CLASSIFY it - decide honestly what kind of thing it is - because the kind is not a label on the record, it is the instruction for how the record must be kept and how the asset must be looked after. Name it as the kind it is, and the right way to manage it follows; misname it, and you have quietly chosen to manage it wrongly.

2. The kinds - and how each is managed differently

HERE ARE THE KINDS, AND - THE POINT OF THE WHOLE OUTCOME - HOW THE MANAGEMENT OF EACH GENUINELY DIFFERS. A FIXED ASSET IS IMMOVABLE AND LONG-LIVED: a building, a structure, a bolted-down machine. You manage it by CONDITION AND LIFECYCLE - tracking its health across years, planning its major interventions and eventual replacement - and it is carried on the balance sheet and depreciated over its long life. A MOVABLE ASSET IS PORTABLE: a transfer pump, a vehicle, a laptop, a test instrument. You manage it by CUSTODY AND LOCATION - who has it, where it is - and you VERIFY IT MORE OFTEN, because it can be moved, borrowed, or stolen, and a movable asset carries a real, standing risk of loss that a fixed one does not. INFRASTRUCTURE IS A DISTRIBUTED NETWORK: a pipeline, a cable run, a road, a water reticulation system. You manage it AS A SYSTEM DELIVERING A LEVEL OF SERVICE, not as a heap of individual parts, because its value is in the WHOLE network doing its job - a single length of pipe means nothing; the water arriving at pressure means everything. FACILITIES ARE THE BUILDINGS AND GROUNDS THAT HOUSE OPERATIONS: the workshops, offices, stores, the site itself. You manage them FOR THE PEOPLE AND FUNCTIONS THEY SHELTER - safety, access, the operations they enable. NOW THE LESSON UNDERNEATH THE LIST: MISNAMING THE KIND MEANS MISMANAGING THE ASSET. Call a movable pump 'fixed' and you stop tracking its custody, so it quietly disappears and no one notices until it is needed. Treat a pipeline network as if it were one machine and you miss that the SERVICE, not the pipe, is the thing you are managing, and you optimise the part while the system fails. The kind is the instruction for the management; match them, and the asset is looked after correctly.

3. A register is a claim about reality - and it drifts

NOW THE SHARPER HALF OF THE UNIT, THE ONE THAT CHANGES HOW YOU SEE A REGISTER FOREVER: A REGISTER IS NOT A STATIC, RELIABLE DOCUMENT. IT IS A CLAIM ABOUT REALITY, AND IT BEGINS TO DRIFT FROM THE REAL WORLD THE DAY IT IS CREATED, because the physical world changes faster than the paper can be updated. Assets get moved, scrapped, added, cannibalised for parts - and every change that is not written down opens a gap between what the register says and what the plant holds. So a register you have been trusting is quietly wrong in TWO DIRECTIONS AT ONCE, and you must learn to look for both. GHOST ASSETS ARE ON THE REGISTER BUT NOT IN THE PLANT: scrapped, stolen, or transferred away long ago, and never retired from the record - so they are STILL being insured, STILL being depreciated, STILL having maintenance planned and even scheduled for a machine that no longer exists. They quietly burn money and effort on nothing. UNRECORDED ASSETS ARE THE MIRROR IMAGE - IN THE PLANT BUT NOT ON THE REGISTER: real, working machines (often movable ones bought on a work order and never captured) that are UNINSURED, UNMAINTAINED, and invisible to every plan and every valuation - live risks that nobody is managing precisely because nobody knows they are there. And beneath these two are the quieter errors: assets recorded at the WRONG LOCATION, and DUPLICATES counted twice. Here is the trap that catches the careless: a register can even have the RIGHT TOTAL COUNT while being wrong, if a ghost and an unrecorded asset happen to cancel out - which is exactly why a simple stock-take that matches the total is not proof of anything. Left alone, the drift only grows, until the register faithfully describes a plant that no longer exists.

4. Reconcile - verify against reality, in three truths

RECONCILIATION IS THE DISCIPLINE THAT CATCHES THE DRIFT, AND THE FIRST THING TO BE CLEAR ABOUT IS WHAT IT IS NOT: it is not scoring the register's data quality against a standard (that is a different, valuable job). RECONCILIATION IS CHECKING THE REGISTER AGAINST THE PHYSICAL WORLD - the record against the reality. The process. You PHYSICALLY VERIFY: a walk-down of the plant, a count, a tag or barcode scan of what is actually, physically there right now. You COMPARE that physical truth against the register, and you find the discrepancies IN BOTH DIRECTIONS - the GHOSTS (on paper, not present) and the UNRECORDED (present, not on paper) - and this two-way looking is the crux, because A RECONCILIATION THAT ONLY HUNTS FOR WHAT IS MISSING WILL NEVER FIND THE REAL PUMPS NOBODY WROTE DOWN, and those are often the bigger risk. You also catch the mislocations and duplicates. Then, for each discrepancy, you INVESTIGATE THE CAUSE - not to blame, but to understand which process step let the drift in. AND THE STRONGEST RECONCILIATION RECONCILES THREE TRUTHS AT ONCE: WHAT EXISTS PHYSICALLY, WHAT THE REGISTER SAYS, AND WHAT THE FINANCIAL RECORDS (the general ledger) SAY. This three-way check matters because the three can disagree in revealing ways: a pump can be gone from the plant, removed from the register, and STILL sitting in the accounts being depreciated - a financial ghost - and only lining up all three makes the picture whole. ONE MORE THING: THIS IS A ROUTINE, NOT A ONE-OFF ACT OF HEROICS. A register reconciled once and never again simply begins drifting from the moment you finish, so reconciliation is a recurring discipline built into how the organisation runs, not a project you complete and forget.

5. Resolve - and why it matters (a service, not a blame-hunt)

FINALLY, RESOLVE WHAT YOU FOUND, AND HOLD ONTO WHY IT MATTERS AND HOW IT MUST BE DONE. THE RESOLUTIONS ARE SPECIFIC: RETIRE THE GHOSTS (remove the scrapped and transferred from the register, and stop the insurance and depreciation they were drawing), CAPTURE THE UNRECORDED (add the real machines to the register so they enter insurance, maintenance and valuation), and CORRECT THE MISLOCATED AND THE DUPLICATED (fix the location, merge the duplicates). Now the corrected register matches the plant - and WHY THIS MATTERS IS THE WHOLE POINT: EVERY DOWNSTREAM DECISION IS ONLY AS TRUE AS THE REGISTER BENEATH IT. The maintenance plan schedules work against it; the insurance premium is priced on it; the depreciation and the asset value flow from it; the capital plan and the audit rest on it. A drifted register silently corrupts all of them at once, so keeping it true is not clerical tidiness - it is the foundation the rest of asset management stands on. AND HOW IT MUST BE DONE, WHICH IS NON-NEGOTIABLE: AN ASSET REGISTER'S DRIFT FROM REALITY IS A NATURAL PROCESS, NOT A PERSONAL FAILING - IT HAPPENS BECAUSE THE PHYSICAL WORLD CHANGES FASTER THAN RECORDS CAN BE UPDATED, AND RECONCILIATION IS THE PROFESSIONAL DISCIPLINE THAT CORRECTS THIS DRIFT. WHEN WE FIND A DISCREPANCY, WE INVESTIGATE THE CAUSE TO IMPROVE THE PROCESS, NOT TO ASSIGN BLAME TO A PERSON - the goal is a system that makes it easy to keep the register true (for instance, linking every disposal to a register retirement, so ghosts cannot breed). A team that gets blamed for discrepancies will hide them, and a register you cannot trust is worse than one you know is drifting. And keep the purpose in view: A TRUE ASSET REGISTER SERVES EVERYONE WHO DEPENDS ON IT - PLANNERS, MAINTENANCE CREWS, FINANCE, AND AUDITORS - SO KEEPING IT ACCURATE IS A SERVICE TO THE WHOLE COMMUNITY.

T3: The Blueprint

A register is a claim about reality. Reconciliation is the discipline that keeps the claim true. An unreconciled asset register is not a record of your assets; it is a photograph of the past, decaying into fiction. Five questions:

  • Is an asset one thing? No - name the kind. The register is your map of what you own, and it is only useful if it names each thing as the kind it is.
  • How is each kind managed? Differently. Fixed by condition and lifecycle; movable by custody and location; infrastructure as a system to a level of service; facilities for the people they shelter.
  • Does the register stay true? No - it drifts. From the day it is made, assets move, get scrapped, and get added, so it goes wrong in two directions: ghosts and unrecorded.
  • How do I make it true again? Reconcile. Physically verify the register against reality, find both kinds of discrepancy, investigate the cause, and reconcile three truths - physical, register, financial.
  • Why does it matter? Every downstream decision rests on it. The plan, the insurance, the depreciation, the valuation - all only as true as the register beneath them.

An asset is not one thing - name the kind. The word "asset" hides real differences, and you cannot manage what you have not correctly identified. A physical asset is the umbrella - a tangible thing you can touch - and within it live several kinds, each managed in its own way. A fixed asset is immovable and long-lived - a building, a structure, a bolted-down machine - and you manage it by condition and lifecycle, tracking its health over years and carrying it on the balance sheet. A movable asset is portable - a transfer pump, a vehicle, a laptop, a test instrument - and you manage it by custody and location, verifying it more often, because it can walk and it carries a real risk of loss or theft. Infrastructure is a distributed network - a pipeline, a cable run, a road, a water reticulation system - and you manage it as a system delivering a level of service, because its value is in the whole network doing its job, not in any single length of pipe. And facilities are the buildings and grounds that house operations, managed for the people and functions they shelter. The point is that misnaming the kind means mismanaging the asset: treat a movable pump as if it were fixed and you stop tracking its custody, so it quietly disappears; treat a pipeline network as if it were one machine and you miss that the service, not the pipe, is the thing you are managing.

A register is a claim about reality - and it drifts. Now the sharper truth. A register is not a static, reliable document; it is a claim about reality, and it begins to drift from the real world the day it is created, because the physical world changes faster than the paper. Assets get moved, scrapped, added, cannibalised for parts - and each change that is not written down opens a gap between the register and the plant. So a register you trust is quietly wrong in two directions at once. Ghost assets are on the register but not in the plant - scrapped, stolen or transferred long ago, yet still being insured, still depreciated, still having maintenance planned for a thing that no longer exists; they cost money and effort spent on nothing. Unrecorded assets are the opposite - in the plant but not on the register - real, working machines that are uninsured, unmaintained, and invisible to every plan and every valuation; they are risks nobody is managing because nobody knows they are there. And a register also carries the quieter errors: assets at the wrong location, and duplicates counted twice. Left alone, the drift only grows, until the register describes a plant that no longer exists.

Reconcile - verify against reality, in three truths. Reconciliation is the discipline that catches the drift, and it is not the same as scoring the register's data quality - it is checking the register against the physical world. You physically verify: a walk-down, a count, a tag or barcode scan of what is actually there. You compare that against the register and find the discrepancies both ways - the ghosts (on paper, not present) and the unrecorded (present, not on paper) - because a reconciliation that only looks for what is missing will never find the real pumps nobody wrote down. Then you investigate the cause of each discrepancy, and you resolve it. And the strongest reconciliation goes further, reconciling three truths at once: what physically exists, what the register says, and what the financial records say - because a ghost that is gone from the plant may still be sitting in the accounts being depreciated, and only lining up all three makes the picture whole. (This is a routine, not a one-off heroics: a register reconciled once and never again simply starts drifting from the day you finish.)

One honest boundary. Do not turn reconciliation into a hunt for who is to blame. An asset register's drift from reality is a natural process, not a personal failing - it happens because the physical world changes faster than records can be updated, and reconciliation is the professional discipline that corrects this drift. When we find a discrepancy, we investigate the cause to improve the process, not to assign blame to a person - the goal is a system that makes it easy to keep the register true. Ask why scrapped assets were not retired, and fix that step - do not go looking for a culprit. And keep the purpose in view: a true asset register serves everyone who depends on it - planners, maintenance crews, finance, and auditors - so keeping it accurate is a service to the whole community. Reconciliation restores the truth that all of them build on.

Does it scale? Anyone who has ever trusted their banking app's balance while three debit orders were still to come knows the feeling of a record that has drifted from reality. A personal example is a house inventory for insurance: the list you made when you moved in is a photograph of the past - the television you sold is a ghost you are still paying to insure, and the new laptop you never added is a real thing you would not be paid out for. Walk your own rooms against your own list and you are doing exactly what an asset manager does to a plant. A register and a life are both claims about reality, and both decay into fiction unless you keep them true.

Here is a small slice of the pump-station register, and the result of a physical walk-down with a tag scanner.

The register says:

IDDescriptionKindRegister location
P-01Main transfer pump(you decide)Bay 1

The walk-down found: P-01 present in Bay 1; P-02 not found anywhere (records show it was scrapped last year); P-03 present but in Bay 5, not Bay 3; the pump house present; and one extra portable pump, P-99, running in the workshop and not on the register.

  1. Classify by kind. Give the asset kind for P-01 (a transfer pump), P-04 (the pump house building), and the station's buried delivery pipeline - and say in a few words how each is managed differently.
  2. Build the reconciliation report. For each discrepancy, give the type (ghost / unrecorded / mislocated), the asset ID, and the action (retire / capture / correct location).
  3. Count it. The register listed 4 assets. How many assets truly exist, and what does the corrected register hold?
  4. Cause, not culprit. For the ghost, name the process step you would investigate and fix - not the person you would blame.
  5. The cost of the drift. Name one specific downstream decision that was wrong because of P-02 (the ghost) and one because of P-99 (the unrecorded).

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the kinds. P-01, a transfer pump, is a movable asset - managed by custody and location, verified often because it can be moved or lost. P-04, the pump house, is a facility (a building) - managed for the operations and people it shelters, on a long lifecycle. The buried delivery pipeline is infrastructure - managed as a networked system to a level of service, not as one discrete item.

Second, the reconciliation report. P-02: ghost, retire (on the register, scrapped, not present). P-03: mislocated, correct location to Bay 5 (present, but the register is wrong). P-99: unrecorded, capture (present and running, not on the register).

Third, the count. The register listed 4. Physically, P-01, P-03, P-04 and P-99 exist - 4 real assets - but they are not the same 4: the register loses the ghost P-02 and gains the unrecorded P-99, so the corrected register holds P-01, P-03 (relocated), P-04 and P-99. The count happens to stay 4, which is exactly why counting alone is not reconciliation - a matching total can still hide a ghost and an unrecorded asset cancelling out.

Fourth, cause not culprit: the step to investigate is why a scrapped asset was not retired from the register - most likely there is no link between the disposal process and the register update. Fix that step (make retirement part of disposal), and you stop breeding ghosts - which is far more useful than finding whoever forgot P-02.

Fifth, the cost of the drift. P-02 the ghost: the company kept paying insurance and booking depreciation on a scrapped pump, and maintenance was planned and possibly scheduled for a machine that was not there. P-99 the unrecorded: a real, running pump was uninsured and outside every maintenance plan, so it could fail with no cover and no preventive work - a live risk nobody was managing.

T5: The Guardrails

A register begins to drift from reality the day it is created. Reconciliation is the discipline that realigns your records with the real world, so you stop paying for ghosts and start managing the assets you actually have.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Managing every asset the same way. A movable pump, a fixed building, a pipeline network and a facility are managed by different means. Name the kind, then choose the management to match.

• Losing a movable asset by treating it as fixed. A portable asset needs custody and location tracking. Treat it like a bolted-down machine and it walks off unnoticed.

• Managing a network as one machine. Infrastructure delivers a service across a whole system; its value is in the network, not the part. Manage it to a level of service.

• Trusting an unreconciled register. It is a photograph of the past, not a record of the present. It has been drifting since the day it was made.

• Paying for ghosts. Assets scrapped or transferred but never retired keep drawing insurance, depreciation and maintenance planning for nothing. Retire them.

• Running unrecorded assets blind. Real machines not on the register are uninsured, unmaintained and invisible to every plan. Capture them.

• Reconciling only one direction. Looking only for what is missing never finds the real assets nobody wrote down. Check both ways, and against the financials too.

• Treating reconciliation as a blame-hunt. Drift is what registers do; it is not a person's failing. Investigate the cause to fix the process, not to find a culprit.

• A one-off count. A register reconciled once and never again just starts drifting from the day you finish. Reconciliation is a routine, not an event.

The register said forty and the plant held thirty-eight, and the gap lied in both directions - ghosts being insured and depreciated for nothing, and real pumps running uninsured and unplanned. So come to know what you own: see that an asset is not one thing and name each as the kind it is - fixed managed by condition, movable by custody, infrastructure as a system to a level of service, facilities for the people they shelter - because the kind decides the management; understand that a register is a claim about reality that drifts from the day it is made, going wrong as ghosts and as unrecorded assets at once; reconcile by physically verifying the register against the plant and against the financials, finding both kinds of discrepancy and investigating the cause; and resolve each - retire the ghosts, capture the unrecorded, correct the mislocations - as a routine, not an event. A register is a claim about reality, and reconciliation is the discipline that keeps the claim true - a service to everyone who depends on it, and never a hunt for who is to blame. The next time you trust an asset register, ask when it was last walked against the plant - because the day that answer is "too long ago" is the day it stopped being a record of your assets and started being a photograph of the past.
