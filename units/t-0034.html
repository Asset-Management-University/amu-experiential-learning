Step 1 of 5

The crusher bearing is hot 40 minutes into the shift, the oil report shows iron jumping 35 to 180 ppm, and my supervisor wants to know: will it make the weekend, or do we stop now (R220,000) before it seizes (R1.8 million)?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday at the Kopano Aggregates crushing plant in the dry interior. You are the reliability engineer. You are standing next to the secondary cone crusher, your hand resting on the drive-end housing of its main-shaft bearing. Through your glove you feel a fine, fast buzz - and the housing is warm, warmer than the gearbox beside it. The plant started at 06:00. It is 40 minutes in, and this bearing should still be cool.

In your other hand is yesterday's oil-analysis report. One line is circled in red: iron particles 180 ppm (parts per million - how much metal is floating in the oil), up from 35 ppm last month. Underneath, a second number: ISO 4406 cleanliness 22/20/17. You are not yet sure what that one is telling you, but the red circle says it matters.

The shift supervisor, Phenyo, walks up with her radio. "Is it going to make it to the weekend shutdown, or do we stop now?" Behind her, the day's first tipper truck is already reversing toward the feed hopper. Stop the crusher today and the plant loses about R220,000 in throughput - and you are the one who called it. Keep running and the bearing seizes, the shaft scores, and that is closer to R1.8 million and three weeks down. The smell of warm oil is just coming off the housing - that faint, hot-metal sweetness you have learned not to like.

You can guess. You have guessed before. But a guess at 06:40 with R1.8 million on the line is not good enough, and you know it.

I need to read what this bearing is telling me - to NAME what is wearing it out, and to put a real NUMBER on how long it has left. Not a feeling. A method.

Before you read on, decide. It is 06:40. The bearing is running warm — you can smell it off the housing. Change it now and you take a planned stop. Leave it and if it seizes, the shaft scores and you are looking at about R1.8 million and three weeks down. You have the bearing’s designation, its load and speed, and you can look at the machine. Write down how you would decide — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question that has to come first

• “Change it now. R1.8 million against one planned stop is not a close call.” Sound risk reasoning, and if the thing wearing this bearing out is still there afterwards, you have bought a few months and learnt nothing — and you will be standing here again.

• “Look up its rated life and see whether it is due.” A real calculation with a real answer, and it answers a question about a bearing that is being fatigued by load. If this one is being destroyed by contamination or poor lubrication, the rated life describes a machine you do not have.

• “Run it to failure and be ready to swap it fast.” A defensible strategy for a cheap, harmless part. This one takes the shaft with it.

• “Add grease and watch the temperature.” Might help, might be the very thing killing it — over-greasing destroys bearings — and either way it is an action taken before the cause is named.

• “Get a vibration reading and see what it says.” Good, and it tells you what is happening now. It does not tell you how long you have, which is the half of the question the R1.8 million actually turns on.

• There are two questions here and they must be taken in order. Why is it wearing out? — because the bearing carries the evidence, and each way of dying leaves its own marks: fatigue, contamination, poor lubrication and electrical damage do not look alike, and the remedy differs completely. Only once you have named the mechanism does the second question mean anything: how long has it got? The standard life calculation is a good answer to a question about load-driven fatigue and a misleading answer to anything else — so running it first, as almost everyone does, produces a confident number about a machine that is dying of something the formula never modelled. Name the mechanism, then calculate. In the other order the arithmetic is impeccable and the answer is about somebody else’s bearing.

There is a clear method for each half - why it is wearing, and how long it has. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is tribology - the science of friction, wear and lubrication - put to work as a diagnosis. It answers two questions in order: why is the metal being destroyed? (the wear mechanism), and how long has it got? (a defensible life number from the standard, ISO 281). The trick is that the second answer is only honest once you have the first. Watch it run on the warm crusher bearing.

Step 1 - Check the lubrication regime (Stribeck, kappa)

The crusher bearing: at 350 rev/min and this bore size the maker's chart gives a required viscosity nu1 = 70 mm2/s. The housing runs hot (IR thermometer 78 C), and from the oil's chart the VG 220 oil at 78 C gives an actual viscosity nu = 49 mm2/s. So kappa = nu / nu1 = 49 / 70 = 0.70 - below 1. On the Stribeck curve you are not in full-film; you have slid into the boundary region. The oil is too thin at this temperature to lift the rollers off the races. Finding: the lubrication regime is wrong - metal is touching metal.

Step 2 - Read the oil's cleanliness (ISO 4406, eC)

The report says ISO 4406 22/20/17 - dirty, well above the 18/16/13 you would want for this bearing. From the ISO 281 contamination table for this cleanliness and bearing size, the contamination factor eC = 0.2. A clean bearing would be near 1.0; 0.2 means the dirt is doing serious harm to the bearing's life.

Step 3 - Diagnose the wear mechanism (oil data + surface)

Iron jumped 35 to 180 ppm in one month - metal removed now, not slowly fatiguing. The particles skew large (the 14-micron count is high - abrasion and adhesion make big ragged particles; fatigue spalls are fewer and rounder). kappa = 0.70 is boundary contact, the textbook condition for adhesion; the dirty oil means hard particles, the textbook condition for abrasion. Pull the magnetic drain plug: fine parallel scratches (abrasion) with smeared torn patches (adhesion), no rust, few round pits. Diagnosis: combined abrasion and adhesion from dirty oil running too thin - NOT normal fatigue.

Step 4 - Calculate the bearing life (ISO 281: L10 then L10m)

L10 = (850 / 190)^(10/3) = 4.47^(10/3) = about 148 million revolutions. At 350 rev/min that is 350 x 60 = 21,000 rev/hour, so L10 = 148,000,000 / 21,000 = about 7,000 hours - the clean-world fatigue life, the number a careless engineer would quote. Now the real oil: with kappa = 0.70 and eC = 0.2 the chart gives a-iso = 0.3, so L10m = 0.3 x 7,000 = about 2,100 hours. The dirt and thin oil cut the fatigue life to under a third - but remember the diagnosis: this bearing is dying of abrasion, which ISO 281 does not model, so even 2,100 hours is an optimistic ceiling, not a promise.

Step 5 - Make the call, and fix the cause

The call: it will not safely make the weekend (boundary lubrication, dirty oil, abrasion not fatigue, the fatigue clock already cut to a third and the real damage faster). Schedule a controlled stop for tonight's quiet shift - lower lost-throughput than a daytime stop, far below the R1.8 million seizure. Then fix the cause: find and close the source of dirt (a failed seal or breather), flush and refill to ISO 4406 18/16/13, and move to a VG 320 oil so at 78 C the viscosity ratio climbs back above kappa = 1. You hand Phenyo one sentence: 'Boundary lubrication plus dirty oil - abrasion, not fatigue. Stop it tonight, fix the seal, change to a thicker, cleaner oil.'

See what the method did. It turned a hot housing and a red-circled number into a sentence with evidence behind every word: boundary lubrication, dirty oil, abrasion - not fatigue - stop it tonight. And see the mission in it, because this is the discipline that turns anxiety into authority and a part-swap into a cure. A guess at 06:40, with R1.8 million riding on it, is no way to treat a machine or the people who depend on it; the method lets the engineer stand in front of Phenyo with numbers, not a feeling. But its deepest honesty is harder than that. The standard will hand you a life number - about 2,100 hours - and a careless engineer would quote it and feel safe. The method forbids that, because the bearing is not dying of fatigue, which is all that number measures; it is dying of abrasion, which is faster and which the standard does not model at all. So the honest answer is not "2,100 hours" but "2,100 hours is an optimistic ceiling, and the real damage is running ahead of it." That refusal to quote a comforting but false number is the whole integrity of the trade. And because the method names the mechanism, it points past the bearing to the cause - the failed seal letting dirt in, the oil too thin at temperature - so you cure the disease instead of replacing the symptom every few weeks. The money - R220,000 against R1.8 million - is the measure; the aim is the machine kept alive, the truth told plainly, and the cause fixed so it does not come back.

T3: The Blueprint

You work any worrying bearing down five steps.

  • 1. Check the lubrication regime. The Stribeck curve and the viscosity ratio kappa - is the oil keeping the metal apart?
  • 2. Read the oil's cleanliness. The ISO 4406 code, and the contamination factor it gives.
  • 3. Diagnose the wear mechanism. Oil data and the surface look - abrasion, adhesion, fatigue or corrosion.
  • 4. Calculate the bearing life. ISO 281: basic L10, then adjusted L10m for the real oil.
  • 5. Make the call, and fix the cause. The defensible decision, and the root-cause repair.

The ideas underneath. The Stribeck curve shows how friction changes with oil thickness, speed and load, across three zones - boundary (film too thin, surfaces scrape), mixed, and full-film (the oil fully lifts the surfaces apart, where you want to live); the viscosity ratio kappa turns it into a number (your oil's actual thickness divided by the thickness the bearing needs - below 1 means boundary). The wear mechanisms (grouped in the ASM Handbook Vol. 18) are abrasion (hard dirt, parallel scratches), adhesion (micro-welding and tearing in boundary contact), fatigue/spalling (rolling load cracking the surface from below - the normal end-of-life) and corrosion; you read them from the oil data and the surface together. ISO 281 gives the life: basic L10 = (C / P)^p (the life 90 of 100 bearings reach before fatigue; C the catalogue load rating, P the service load, p 3 for ball or 10/3 for roller), then adjusted L10m = a-iso x L10, where the factor a-iso (from the chart, using kappa and the contamination factor eC from the ISO 4406 code) accounts for the real oil. Two more standards label the oil: ISO 3448 names its thickness by viscosity grade (VG 220, VG 320 - the thickness at 40 C), and ISO 4406 is the cleanliness code. It fits rolling-element bearings under known loads and speeds where you can sample oil and see the surface; it does not fit when the cause is not fatigue - if the wear is abrasion from a broken seal, ISO 281 hands you a falsely long life, because the standard assumes clean oil. So you diagnose the mechanism first, and only trust a life number once you know the metal is dying of fatigue.

Take a bearing you are worried about, and run it down the five steps - in order, no skipping to the life number. Read its viscosity ratio at running temperature; read the cleanliness code; diagnose the mechanism from the oil data and the drain-plug chip; calculate L10 and L10m; then make the call and name the cause.

Two disciplines keep the diagnosis honest. First, diagnose the mechanism before you trust any life number - the ISO 281 number only measures fatigue, so quoting it on a bearing that is dying of abrasion or corrosion is the most common and most dangerous mistake in the field; if the metal is being removed by dirt, the standard's clean-oil life is a comforting fiction, and your real answer is "sooner than this, and here is why." Second, fix the cause, not the symptom - a new bearing dropped into dirty, thin oil simply dies the same death a little later; once the mechanism names the cause (the seal, the breather, the wrong oil grade, the running temperature), repair that, or you will be standing at this housing again next month with the same red-circled number.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your diagnosis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A life number is only as honest as the mechanism. ISO 281 measures fatigue. On a bearing dying of abrasion or corrosion, its clean-oil life is a fiction - diagnose the mechanism first.

• Check kappa at running temperature, not when cold. A thick oil can still be too thin once the housing is hot. kappa below 1 means boundary contact - metal touching metal - whatever the drum says.

• Read the oil and the surface together. The metals and particle sizes in the oil, and the scratches, smears or pits on the chip - one without the other can mislead. Two sources, one diagnosis.

• Fix the cause, not just the bearing. A new bearing in dirty, thin oil dies the same death. Find the dirt source, the breather, the oil grade - cure the disease.

• Quote the ceiling, not a promise. When the active wear is faster than fatigue, even the adjusted life is an optimistic ceiling. Say so - an honest "sooner than this" beats a comforting wrong number.

You came to the housing with a buzz under your glove, a red-circled number, and a supervisor who needed an answer you did not have. You can leave with a diagnosis behind every word: the oil too thin at temperature, the cleanliness code dirty, the chip showing abrasion and adhesion, the fatigue clock already cut to a third and the real damage faster still - so a controlled stop tonight, a seal fixed, a thicker and cleaner oil. Name the mechanism before you trust the life number, and fix the cause, not just the bearing. That is the difference between a guess and a method. That is how a machine is kept alive and a hard call is made well: not by the brave guess or the comforting number, but by reading what the metal is telling you, and curing what is killing it.
