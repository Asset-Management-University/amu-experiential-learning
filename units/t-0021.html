T1: The Ache

You are on the control-room mezzanine at Nala Reformer at 06:40, in an inland industrial town. Through the window stands Reformer R-201, its stack shimmering in the cold air - a fired vessel running at 22 bar and 840 C. If the feed-gas flow drops while the burners stay lit, the tubes overheat and can rupture, releasing hot gas next to an operator walkway.

In your hand is an incident report from a sister plant in the same region: three weeks ago their reformer lost feed-gas flow for ninety seconds while the burners stayed lit. Two tubes bulged. No one was hurt - barely. Mzwakhe, the plant manager, taps it: "That could have been us. And there's more." He slides a second page across. The IT team found that a contractor's maintenance laptop, plugged straight into the network that talks to R-201's controller, carried malware. Nothing happened this time. "But the same wire that lets a fixer fix can let an attacker reach the burners."

Your name is on the design package for the burner upgrade, due before the September shutdown. If R-201 overheats - by accident or by attack - and someone is hurt, the design is the first thing examined. There is a tempting first move: bolt an extra high-temperature trip onto the existing controller, lock the network cabinet, call it done. Quick, cheap - it would look finished. But a trip you have not sized may be too slow, or fail silently the day you need it; and a locked cabinet does nothing about the laptop reaching across the wider network. You are guessing, and guessing is what put two bulges in a sister plant's tubes.

I know a process failure could kill someone - and now I know an attacker could disable the very system meant to prevent it. How do I design the physical safety and the digital security together, as one defence, so my last line of protection actually holds?

Before you read on, decide. If the feed gas to reformer R-201 drops away, the burners must be shut within 3 seconds; past about 8 seconds the tubes are damaged. Low flow happens roughly once every two years, an operator is often on the walkway, and a sister plant has already lost its tubes. The trip system that prevents it can be reached from the plant network, and the vendor dials in to support it. Write down how you would design the protection — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question each of them only half answers

• “Design the trip to the required reliability and get it certified.” Half the job, and the half that has a standard to follow — which is exactly why it is the half that gets done. A certified trip that somebody can reach and switch off has a certificate and no protection.

• “Air-gap the plant network.” The answer nearly everyone reaches for and almost nobody keeps. Somebody has to update it, fix it, read data off it — so the gap closes again quietly, one cable at a time, and now the connections exist and nobody has a list of them.

• “Put a firewall between the office and the plant.” Necessary and nowhere near sufficient. It treats security as a device you install rather than a property of how the thing is laid out.

• “Let IT handle the security — that is their job.” IT understands security and not the process; the people who understand the process do not do security. The trip ends up in the gap between them, which is where it has been all along.

• “Add a second independent trip — defence in depth.” A good instinct that can double the wrong thing: if both are reachable by the same route, you have bought two of something an attacker switches off once.

• The two questions have to be answered together, because each one can quietly cancel the other. On the safety side that means a defined function with a clock on it (close both fuel valves within 3 seconds, inside an 8-second process safety time), a required risk reduction worked out from the actual consequence and exposure rather than assumed, a design that beats it with margin, and a proof test that will catch a valve stuck between shutdowns. On the security side it means laying the network out so that a person who needs to fix something can fix it without that same route reaching the burners. A safety system somebody can switch off is not a safety system, whatever its certificate says — and a locked-down plant nobody can maintain will be quietly unlocked by the people who have to keep it running.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to design the safety protection and the security protection together - how strong each layer must be, how to lay out the network so a fixer can fix without an attacker reaching the burners, and how the last line of defence is kept from being switched off. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

You must answer two questions at once: "is our last line of defence reliable enough?" and "is it protected from being switched off, by accident or attack?" The process industry has a standard method for each, and they must be used together: the Safety Instrumented System (SIS) designed to IEC 61511, and the Operational Technology (OT) secured to IEC 62443. Watch Farida and Wei run both on R-201's low-feed-gas hazard.

Step 1 - Define the safety function and size the SIL (IEC 61511)

SIF-201-01: on low feed-gas flow, close the two burner fuel valves within 3 seconds. Trip at 40% of 5,000 Nm3/h normal = 2,000 Nm3/h; the process safety time (hazard to tube damage) is 8 seconds, so 3 leaves margin. A risk graph - severe consequence, an operator often on the walkway, little chance to escape, low-flow about once in 2 years - lands a required risk reduction of about 300, which is SIL 2 (band 100-1,000); the design must beat a PFDavg of 0.01.

Step 2 - Design the SIS to meet the SIL, and plan to prove it

2oo3 on the flow sensors (any two must agree - catches one failed transmitter while keeping false trips low) and 1oo2 on the fuel valves (either valve closing stops firing). With certified failure data this gives PFDavg = 0.004 - inside the SIL 2 band (0.001-0.01) with margin. Prove it: a full proof test every 12 months, timed to the shutdown, plus automatic partial-stroke valve testing every 3 months so a stuck valve is caught between shutdowns.

Step 3 - Draw the zones (IEC 62443: the SIS in its own most-protected zone)

Zones: Enterprise (office network), Site-Ops (historian, engineering workstation), Control (the basic process control system + screens), SIS (the safety logic solver ALONE - its own zone), Field (sensors and valves). Security levels rise toward the SIS: Enterprise SL 1, Site-Ops and Control SL 2, and Zone SIS SL 3 - the highest on site, because it is the last line of defence.

Step 4 - Guard the conduits (the absolute rule: no write path into the SIS)

Office to Site-Ops: business data only. Site-Ops to Control: engineering changes only through a logged, authenticated path. Control to SIS: a ONE-WAY DATA DIODE - the control system can READ the SIS, but there is NO write path in; any change to the safety logic needs a person at the cabinet with a physical key. This single decision - no network write path into the SIS - is what would have stopped the contractor's malware-laden laptop from ever reaching the burners.

Step 5 - Secure the edges (IIoT and the human access points)

The new wireless vibration sensor (an IIoT device) is NOT allowed to talk to the control layer directly; it routes through a gateway in the Site-Ops zone, authenticated and encrypted, sending data UP only. And contractor laptops now connect only to a locked, monitored engineering workstation through a managed access point - never a free wire into the control network. The fixer can fix; the attacker cannot reach the burners.

See what the five steps did. The first two made the protection real: a defined function, a derived SIL 2, an architecture proven to PFDavg 0.004, and a test that catches silent failure. The last three made it unkillable: the SIS sealed in its own most-protected zone, and one absolute rule - no network path may write to the safety system - that turns the contractor's laptop from a doorway into a dead end. This is the whole truth of it: a safety system an attacker can switch off is not a safety system at all. The SIS is both your most safety-critical function and your most security-critical zone - which is exactly why you design them as one act, not two. Not a bolt-on that looks finished, but a defence that holds.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the defence in five moves - two for safety, three for security.

  • 1. Define the safety function, size the SIL. State what the shutdown must do (the SIF); derive how reliable it must be (the SIL) from the harm it prevents, never a guess.
  • 2. Design the SIS, plan to prove it. Pick the voting architecture (e.g. 2oo3 sensors, 1oo2 valves) to meet the SIL without false trips; schedule the proof test, because a safety system can fail silently.
  • 3. Draw the zones. Map the systems on the plant’s network levels; group them into zones by trust; the SIS gets its own, most-protected zone with the highest security level.
  • 4. Guard the conduits. Allow only controlled pathways, each firewalled - and never a write path into the SIS (a one-way data diode out; a physical key for any change).
  • 5. Secure the edges. Route new IIoT devices through a gateway sending data up only; let contractors in only via a locked, monitored workstation.

The ideas underneath: a SIF is one protective action ("on low flow, trip the burners"); the SIL (1-4) is how reliable it must be, measured as PFDavg (the average probability it fails when called); the voting architecture (1oo2, 2oo3) trades a hidden dangerous failure against a costly false trip; a zone groups systems that share a trust level and a conduit is the only firewalled pathway between zones; and a data diode is a one-way link - data can flow out to be read, but nothing can flow back in to change the logic. This whole method fits any plant whose safety depends on programmable controllers reachable by a network; it is not needed for a purely mechanical, air-gapped protection with no digital path (though those are rare now).

Two disciplines keep the defence honest. First, derive, never guess - a SIL chosen by feel, an architecture not proven against certified failure data, or a proof interval picked for convenience is a number that lies; the whole value of the method is that each layer's strength is calculated and testable. Second, treat the no-write-path rule as absolute - the moment anyone argues for "just a small remote-change path, it's convenient", the last line of defence has a doorway in it; convenience that can switch off the safety system is never worth it. The fixer can always fix at the cabinet; the attacker must never reach it down a wire.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your safety-and-security design true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• "Bolt it on and lock the cabinet" is not a design. An unsized trip and a locked door look finished and protect nothing real. Size the function, prove the architecture, secure the network - or you are guessing.

• Never give the SIS a network write path. Read-only out, physical key in. The single most important line in the whole design is the one an attacker cannot cross to reprogram your last line of defence.

• Do not put the SIS in the control zone. If the safety system shares a network with normal control, one breach or one fault can take out both. Its own zone, its own highest security level - always.

• Do not trust a safety system you have not proof-tested. A SIL is a promise that only holds if the function is tested at its planned interval. An untested trip is a barrier that may already have failed silently.

• Do not let convenience reopen the door. Contractor laptops on a free wire, an IIoT device wired straight to control, a "temporary" remote link - each is how the next attacker gets in. Route every edge through a controlled, monitored path.

You came up to that mezzanine with a sister plant's near-miss in one hand and a malware-laden laptop in the other, and the temptation to make it all look solved by September. You can do far better: a safety function sized to the harm, an architecture proven to meet it, a proof test that catches silent failure, and a network where the last line of defence sits sealed in its own zone with no way in to switch it off. A safety system an attacker can disable is not a safety system. Design the protection from accident and the protection from attack as one single, indivisible act - because the operator on that walkway is protected only if both hold at once.
