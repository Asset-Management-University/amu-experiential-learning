The council-approved plan promises to move all 48 critical pumps to condition-based care and cut failures 40%, but it assumes six trained technicians and a reliability engineer - and I have three untrained fitters, a frozen post, and a pump already failing; the first progress report is due Friday, so what do I actually need to know before I write it?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Phiwe, six weeks into the job of maintenance manager at the regional water works - the plant that treats and pumps drinking water for the 240,000 people of the district. It is a Tuesday in early winter and you are standing in the pump hall with a glossy document in your hands. The cover says Asset Management Strategy and Plan 2025-2030. The council signed it off three months before you arrived. It is forty pages long and it reads beautifully: it promises that every one of the works' 48 critical pumps and motors will move from "fix it when it breaks" to planned, condition-based care - measuring vibration, oil and temperature on a fixed schedule, catching faults early, cutting unplanned failures by 40% inside two years.

You believe in the plan. It is the right idea. That is not the problem. The problem is the page headed "Resourcing." It says the new way of working needs six condition-monitoring technicians, each trained and certified to read vibration data. You walk down to the workshop and count heads. You have three maintenance fitters. None of them has ever held a vibration meter. The technician who used to do the rounds retired in March and his post was frozen in the budget freeze. The plan also names a "Reliability Engineer" who owns the whole programme. You check the structure chart on the wall: there is no such post, no such person - just a dotted-line box with a question-mark someone drew in pencil.

Through the open door you can hear Pump 3 running rough - a low, uneven growl under the normal hum, the sound a bearing makes when it is tired. Nobody is measuring it. Nobody is trained to. The plan says you should have caught it two weeks ago. Your phone buzzes - the Municipal Manager: "Council wants the first quarterly report on the new strategy by Friday. They're proud of it. Show them progress."

Friday is in three days. You have a forty-page plan that assumes six trained technicians and a reliability engineer, and you have three untrained fitters, a frozen post, and a pump that is starting to fail. A small, tempting voice says: just write the report so it looks like it's on track - tick the boxes, buy the vibration meters, and the rest will sort itself out. You put the document down on the workbench. The growl from Pump 3 does not stop.

The council-approved plan promises condition-based care on all 48 pumps and a 40% cut in failures, but it assumes six trained technicians and a reliability engineer I don't have - and a pump is already failing; what do I need to know before I write Friday's report?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The equipment is already budgeted and the plan reads beautifully - yet something is badly wrong before a single meter is bought. "Just write the report so it looks on track." But the pump will fail, the report will be exposed, and the plan itself will be blamed when the plan is sound - a dishonest report protects you for a week and sinks you for a year. "Buy the six vibration meters - that's the visible action." But buying the meter does not buy the skill to read it, or the person whose job it is to act on it; you would have six meters and still nobody trained to hold them. "Hire and train six technicians with the budget." Closer - but who authorises their schedules, who owns the readings, who is accountable when a pump fails? The plan names a Reliability Engineer for exactly that, and the post does not exist. So before you read on: name the four kinds of thing a plan can quietly assume you already have, and which single missing one - left unfixed - would still block the plan even if you solved the others.

I am Phiwe, six weeks into the new maintenance-manager job at the regional water works that treats and pumps drinking water for 240,000 people. In my hands is a glossy 'Asset Management Strategy and Plan' the council signed off three months before I arrived. It reads beautifully and it is the RIGHT idea: move every one of our 48 critical pumps and motors from 'fix it when it breaks' to planned, condition-based care - vibration, oil and temperature measured on a schedule - cutting unplanned failures by 40% inside two years. That is not the problem. The problem is the 'Resourcing' page: it needs six condition-monitoring technicians, each trained and certified to read vibration data. I count heads in the workshop: three maintenance fitters, none of whom has ever held a vibration meter; the technician who used to do the rounds retired in March and his post was frozen in a budget freeze. The plan also names a 'Reliability Engineer' who owns the whole programme - but the structure chart has no such post, no such person, just a dotted-line box with a pencilled question-mark. Meanwhile Pump 3 is running rough - a tired-bearing growl - nobody is measuring it, nobody is trained to, and the plan says it should have been caught two weeks ago. The Municipal Manager wants the first quarterly report by Friday: 'show them progress.' A small voice says just write it so it looks on track, tick the boxes, buy the meters, the rest will sort itself out. Where do I start before Friday?
Don't write the report yet. Set a success line first: this plan is on track ONLY if, for every resource it names, the thing it assumes is matched by something you actually have - the people, the skills and the owner; if even one is missing, 'on track' is not true. Then make the invisible gap visible the cheapest honest way - count and observe what is already in front of you, in one small table: WHAT THE PLAN NEEDS vs WHAT WE HAVE vs THE GAP. Six trained technicians vs three untrained fitters (short 3 people AND short 6 skill-sets); one Reliability Engineer with a post vs zero (short 1 role AND the structure to hold it); condition-based rounds on 48 assets vs reactive fixing only (the whole new way of working). The plan was never wrong - it was never TESTED against the organisation that has to deliver it; someone costed the meters and forgot to ask whether there were hands trained to hold them and a person whose job it was to care. That is a CAPABILITY GAP, and a strategy only delivers if the organisation has the STRUCTURE, ROLES, COMPETENCES and NUMBERS to execute it. The real question is not 'how do I make Friday's report look good?' but 'can we, the people we actually are, deliver this - and which missing piece, left unfixed, blocks all the others?'
Five moves turn a glossy wish into an honest, deliverable plan. (1) SET A SUCCESS LINE AND MAP THE GAP - assumed vs actual across people, skills and owner; a plan that assumes resources it does not have is not a plan, it is a wish. (2) FIND THE KEYSTONE - of the three shortfalls (count, skill, owner), the OWNER is the keystone: hire and train six people, but with no engineer post, who authorises the schedules, who owns the readings, who is accountable when a pump fails? It stalls. The missing structural owner is the only gap you cannot see by counting heads. (3) TEST YOUR OWN 'WE CAN START NOW' LINE - you planned to start on the 8 most critical pumps with the three fitters; before promising it, you put a borrowed meter on Pump 3 (bad bearing confirmed) and timed your best fitter walking one honest round - and it SURPRISED you: a single proper round on eight assets took most of a day, and the three fitters were already swallowed whole by breakdowns; 'deliverable now on eight' was itself a wish, so you cut the scope from 8 to the 3 most critical and brought the contractor in sooner. (4) SPLIT HONESTLY - deliverable now (the 3 most critical pumps; send the best fitter on the 5-day vibration Category I course, ~R18,000; a contract reliability specialist 2 days a month, ~R12,000/month, to set schedules and carry the rounds your fitters have no hours for; a meter on Pump 3 today) versus blocked-and-named (the other 45 assets, the full team of six, the in-house engineer - blocked until council unfreezes the post and funds training). (5) PRICE THE GAP AND REPORT THE HONEST TRADE-OFF - training 4 staff x R18,000 = R72,000; the bridge R12,000 x 24 months = R288,000; +~R60,000 contingency = ~R420,000 over two years, against the plan's own R2.1m avoided-failure saving (about five rand back for every rand in). The report does not say 'on track'; it says the strategy is sound, here is what full delivery needs, here is what we are starting now, and here is the decision council must make - and because the gap finally has a number and a consequence, the post gets unfrozen.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You did not start by writing the report. You started by deciding, in advance, what you would need to SEE to know the plan was deliverable as written - and then you went looking for the evidence the cheapest honest way: by counting and observing what was already in front of you. Five moves:

  • Set a success line, and map the capability gap: Phiwe does not start with the report. She starts by deciding, in advance, what she would need to SEE to know the plan was deliverable as written, and writes it on the back of the document: this plan is on track only if, for every resource it names, the thing it assumes is matched by something we actually have - the people, the skills and the owner; if even one is missing, 'on track' is not true, and the honest report has to say so. Then she goes looking by the cheapest honest means - counting and observing what is already in front of her - and builds the smallest instrument that makes an invisible gap visible: a single table, WHAT THE PLAN NEEDS against WHAT WE HAVE against THE GAP. Six trained condition-monitoring technicians against three untrained fitters: short three people and short six skill-sets. One Reliability Engineer with a real post against zero: short one role and the structure to hold it. Condition-based rounds on 48 assets against reactive fixing only: the whole new way of working. Laid out like that, the truth is plain - the plan was not wrong, it was never tested against the organisation that has to deliver it. This has a name: a capability gap, and a strategy only delivers if the organisation has the structure, roles, competences and workforce numbers to execute it (testing those four before committing is an organisational capability and resource assessment; in ISO 55001 terms, determine the resources and competence the objectives need, and provide them). A plan that assumes resources it does not have is not a plan; it is a wish. (Naming and assessing the role competences themselves is its own discipline; this unit is the wider readiness test around it.)
  • Find the keystone - the missing structural owner: the gap is not one thing; it is three, and they are not equal. Pull the shortfall into its honest parts: short on HOW MANY people, short on WHAT SKILL, and short on WHO IS RESPONSIBLE. Then run a test you can do on the page: for each shortfall ask, 'if I fix the other two but NOT this one, can the plan actually proceed?' Hire six people and train them up to read vibration - but with no engineer post, who authorises the schedules, who owns the readings, who is accountable when a pump fails? It stalls. Do the same for the others and they can be worked around; only one fails every 'fix-the-others-first' test. The keystone is the missing OWNER - the Reliability Engineer post that does not exist, the pencil question-mark on the structure chart. No post means no owner; no owner means the work falls to whoever has a spare moment, which is no one. This is the gap every plan forgets and the only one you cannot see by counting heads, because it is not a person or a skill you are short of - it is a box on the chart that was never created. Find the missing owner first, and the rest of the gap comes into focus.
  • Test your own 'we can start now' line against reality: the table proved the full plan was not deliverable - but the honest report still needed a 'deliverable now', and Phiwe nearly made the very mistake she had just caught the plan making. Her draft said 'start condition-based care on the 8 most critical pumps with the three fitters we have' - which quietly ASSUMED the fitters had spare hours. So before writing it as a promise, she tested it, with a success line: if the fitters can take on the eight rounds without dropping breakdown work, the 'deliverable now' line is honest. She put a borrowed meter on Pump 3 that afternoon (the bearing reading was bad, confirming what her ear had told her - the instrument works and the danger is real) and asked Zola, her most capable fitter, to walk the first round and time it honestly. The surprise: a single proper round on eight assets - measure, log, compare to the last reading - took Zola most of a day, and the three fitters were already swallowed whole by breakdowns. There was no slack at all. 'Deliverable now on eight assets' was, quietly, another wish - a smaller version of the very mistake she had caught the plan making. So she cut the starting scope from 8 assets to the 3 most critical and brought the contract specialist in sooner, to carry the rounds her fitters genuinely could not. The honest 'now' got smaller, and truer - because a timed round, not a hope, set it.
  • Split the plan - deliverable-now vs blocked-and-named: Phiwe does not write a report saying everything is on track - that voice leads somewhere she knows: the pump fails, the report is exposed, and the sound plan gets blamed; the dishonest report protects you for a week and sinks you for a year. Instead she splits the plan in two. DELIVERABLE NOW, with three fitters and the test-corrected scope: start condition-based care on the 3 most critical pumps - not all 48, and not the 8 she first hoped; send Zola, her most capable fitter, on the 5-day vibration Category I course (about R18,000); bring a contract reliability specialist in two days a month (about R12,000 a month) to set the schedules, read the early data and carry the rounds the fitters have no hours for, until a real post exists; and get a meter onto Pump 3 today, because she already knows it is sick and now she has measured it. BLOCKED, and named AS blocked: the other 45 assets, the full team of six, and the in-house Reliability Engineer - none of which can happen until council unfreezes the post and funds the training. She cannot fix that from the workshop floor, but she CAN make the cost of leaving it frozen visible to the people who can. Naming the blocked part as blocked is not failure; it is the difference between a bridge you call a bridge (fine) and a bridge you pretend is a building (it collapses).
  • Price the gap, and write the honest one-line report: a gap without a number is easy to ignore, so Phiwe does the sums in plain sight, the way anyone could check them. Training to close the skills gap: four staff still need the certified course (the three current fitters plus one new hire) at about R18,000 each - R18,000 x 4 = R72,000. The bridge until a real post exists: the contract specialist at about R12,000 a month, over the two years it takes to stand up the in-house post - R12,000 x 24 = R288,000. Add a little room for the unexpected and round up: R72,000 + R288,000 = R360,000, plus about R60,000 contingency, roughly R420,000 over two years. Then she sets that against what the plan itself already promised it would save - an avoided-failure saving of R2.1 million - so about R2.1m saved for about R420,000 spent, roughly five rand back for every rand in, a ratio taken straight off the plan's own page. Her Friday report does not say 'on track'; it says the strategy is sound, full delivery needs six trained technicians and a Reliability Engineer, we have three untrained fitters and an unfunded post, we are starting now on the three most critical assets (the number we TESTED our fitters could carry) and training one technician, and to reach the promised 40% across all 48 the council must unfreeze the post and fund training for three more - about R420,000 over two years against the plan's own R2.1m saving. The Municipal Manager does not love hearing it, but council unfreezes the post next budget cycle - because for the first time the gap has a number and a consequence beside it - and Pump 3's bearing is caught and changed before it seizes: the first save the new programme can point to. You delivered less, sooner, truthfully, instead of more, never, falsely.

See where it lands. Three pumps cared for properly, truthfully, beats forty-eight cared for on paper; the keystone gap - the missing owner - is named and priced so the people who can fix it must decide; and Pump 3's bearing is caught before it seizes. A plan is only as real as the people, skills and structure standing behind it. A plan that assumes resources it does not have is not a plan - it is a wish. So before you promise the result, test the plan against the organisation you actually are: its structure, its roles, its competences and its numbers - and test your own "we can start now" line the same way, with the cheapest real round timed honestly, because that too can be a wish. Find the single named owner the plan forgot - the gap you cannot see by counting heads - put a number on what it costs to close, and make that gap the most important thing your report tells the truth about, not the thing it hides.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You've seen it done once, cleanly. The next one is dressed up to look like a different problem - and that disguise is the whole point.

See the clinic whose vaccination target the grant 'already paid for'

You manage a small community clinic. The district hands you a one-page target: vaccinate 5,000 children in three months as part of a regional drive. There's money attached, and the team is excited. The target assumes the clinic will run outreach - going out to creches, taprooms and informal settlements where families can't easily reach you. On paper it adds up: the district has costed the vaccines, the cold boxes and the fuel. You read it twice. Something is off, the same way Pump 3 was off - a growl under the hum.

Start where you started before: for this target to come true, what would have to be already true about the people and how they're organised? The plan assumes outreach happens - so who, exactly, does outreach? You have nurses, but they run the clinic floor all day, and you have no community outreach workers at all; that role simply isn't on your staff list. The district costed the THINGS and assumed the PEOPLE were there - the pump hall again, someone budgeting the meters and forgetting whose hands would hold them. Now resist the easy fix ("just hire outreach workers with the grant money") and pull the shortfall apart the way you did at the works. Three gaps, not one. NUMBERS: no outreach staff. SKILLS: outreach to wary families is its own competence - trust-building, record-keeping in the field, the cold chain on the move - not the same as clinic nursing. And the one that HIDES: who owns the 5,000 number? If a nurse "also does outreach when she can", no single person is accountable when the count stalls - that missing owner is exactly the pencil question-mark on the works' structure chart, the easiest gap for any plan to forget because it is the only one you can't see by counting heads. So close it honestly. Deliverable now: run the drive inside the clinic and at two nearby creches your nurses can reach, naming one nurse - Sister Lelethu - as the owner of the count so someone holds the number. Blocked until: the full 5,000 across the district needs trained outreach workers the clinic doesn't have - that's the district's to fund and hire. The honest line: "The drive is worth doing. We can reach about 1,800 children with the staff we have, with Sister Lelethu owning the count. Reaching all 5,000 needs four trained outreach workers for the three-month drive, costing about R120,000, which the district must fund - without them, the number on paper won't be the number in arms." Two completely different worlds, a pump hall and a clinic, machines and children - and underneath, the same problem: a plan that assumes capability the organisation does not have, whose deepest part is not the people you can count but the single named owner of the result. Find that missing owner first, and the rest of the gap comes into focus.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The same trap is waiting wherever a plan was written by people who did not check whether the team could deliver it. Find it where you are:

  1. Pick a plan, strategy or target you've been handed - at work, in a club, a project, a household goal. Read it for what it promises, then ask the buried question: what does this quietly assume we already have? Write down the people, skills and structure it depends on.
  2. Count the heads and the skills, honestly. For each thing the plan assumes, write what you actually have beside it. Where the two columns don't match, you've found a capability gap - don't soften it; a gap you can see is a gap you can manage.
  3. Find the missing owner. Plans fail most often where no single named person owns the result. Is there a real role - a real person, with the time and the authority - responsible for delivering this? Or is it a pencil question-mark on the chart? If no one owns it, name that as the first gap to close.
  4. Split it into "deliverable now" and "blocked until", and test the "now". What could you genuinely start with the people and skills you have today - and have you timed one real round to be sure they can carry it without dropping today's work? What is honestly blocked until someone with budget or authority acts, and who must do exactly what?
R

Then draft the single honest sentence you'd say to whoever is proud of this plan - the one that tells the truth about the gap and shows real progress on what you can do. Notice how much harder, and how much more useful, it is than "on track". (Working with an AMU facilitator? Take one real plan from your world and work it all the way through - name what it assumes, measure the gap, find the missing owner, price it, and write the honest report.)

This is the same problem-type as the pump hall - a plan that assumes capability the organisation does not yet have - moved into your own context. The water and the pumps change; the trap does not.
