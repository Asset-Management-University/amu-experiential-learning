One bad night could take it all

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and tonight you cannot sleep. Across town, a man you know - a good trader, years older, who built up a real little business of his own - lost everything last week. A fire in the night. No insurance. A decade of work turned to ash and wet soot by morning, and he is starting again from nothing, at an age when starting again breaks a person.

And lying there in the dark, you do a sum you have never let yourself do. The depot, full of stock and cylinders. The bakkies. The tools. The savings. The name. Everything you and Ibraaheem have built, brick by patient brick - and you realise it could all be gone by tomorrow morning too. One fire. One bad accident on the road. One break-in that clears you out. You have been "saving money" by not paying for any protection - because nothing ever went wrong, so it felt like money for nothing.

But it wasn't saving. It was a bet. You have been betting everything you own, every single night, that tonight is not the night - and never once admitting to yourself that you were gambling.

I've spent years building this, and one bad night could take it all. How do I protect what I've built from the rare disaster - without wasting money insuring every little thing?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Two answers pull at you, and both are wrong. One says: carry on as before - it's rare, it costs money, nothing's happened yet. (That is the exact bet your friend just lost.) The other, born of last night's fear, says: insure EVERYTHING, every tyre and tool. (That would bleed you dry protecting against things you could easily survive.)

Before you read on: look at the difference between a flat tyre and the fire that ruined your friend. They are not the same kind of risk at all. What single question separates the things you must protect against from the things you can simply weather?

I've been skipping protection because nothing ever goes wrong and it feels like money for nothing. But that's a bet - and my friend just lost that exact bet, and everything with it. I'm gambling without admitting I'm gambling.
Not every risk is equal. A flat tyre I can shrug off. A fire that takes the whole depot, or an accident I'm liable for, I could NEVER come back from. Those two things can't possibly need the same plan.
So I ask one question of each risk: could I recover, or is it the END? The ones I can recover from, I absorb. The ones that would finish me, I protect against - even though it costs a little now and nothing may ever happen.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You don't protect against everything, and you don't protect against nothing. You protect against the things that could END you - and to find them, you ask one question of every risk:

  • Ask one question of every risk: if this happens, can I RECOVER from it - or is it the END? That single question sorts everything into two very different piles.
  • RECOVERABLE -> absorb it yourself: a flat tyre, a small theft, a slow month - painful but survivable. Your emergency cushion handles these. Don't waste money insuring the small stuff.
  • UNRECOVERABLE -> you MUST act: a fire that takes everything, an accident you're liable for, losing the one person it all depends on. You can't simply absorb these - so TRANSFER the risk (insurance), REDUCE it (prevention), or BUFFER it (reserves).
  • The trade that makes it worth it: a small, certain cost now - one you'll mostly 'waste' because nothing goes wrong - buys you out of the rare event that would END you. That isn't waste; it's the price of lasting.
  • The rule: insure the unrecoverable, not the trivial: most people do it backwards - they cover small annoyances and leave the one fatal gap wide open. Find your fatal gaps first.

That one question - recover, or the end? - does all the work. It stops you wasting money insuring flat tyres, and it stops you doing what your friend did: leaving the one fatal gap wide open while feeling careful about small things. The fire that ruined him cost a fortune; the cover that would have saved him cost a little each month. He didn't lack money. He lacked the question.

One honest word: this is about how to THINK, not what to buy. AMU does not sell you a policy or name a company. Once you know your fatal gaps, a reputable, regulated provider helps you close them - and some you close not with insurance at all, but by prevention or a reserve.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad sits with Ibraaheem and runs every risk they can think of through the one question.

See Tank You sort its risks - recover, or the end?

Can recover -> ABSORB (the cushion handles it):

• A flat tyre or a breakdown - a bad day, not a disaster.
• A stolen toolbox - replaceable.
• A slow month - that's what the emergency cushion is for.

Would be the END -> PROTECT (transfer / reduce / buffer):

• The depot burns with all the stock inside (everything in one place - a huge concentration of risk) → TRANSFER (cover it) + REDUCE (an alarm, safe storage, and not keeping every egg in one basket).
• A serious road accident Tank You is liable for → TRANSFER (liability cover - the very thing from before) + REDUCE (the safe-driving limits the drivers now keep).
• Muhammad himself badly hurt for months → BUFFER (a reserve, and a second person who can run things - so it isn't all on one pair of hands).

The maths that settles it: the cover for the catastrophic pile costs a manageable amount each month - money that, year after year, seems "wasted" because nothing burns down. But the day something does, it is the difference between a hard season and the end of everything. Muhammad's friend paid the small saving and lost the whole decade. Never again, not for Tank You.

(How much of each, and with whom, is a conversation for a reputable, regulated adviser - the point here is knowing WHICH risks to take to them.)

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The gamble you were making in the dark only became visible the moment you named it. One question turned a vague dread into a short, clear list - and a clear list is something you can actually act on. For your own life and work:

  1. List what could go wrong - then ask each one: could I recover from this, or would it be the end?
  2. Absorb the recoverable. Small, survivable setbacks are what your emergency cushion is for - don't waste money insuring them.
  3. Protect the unrecoverable. For the few that could finish you, act now: transfer the risk, reduce it, or buffer it. A small certain cost to avoid a fatal one.
