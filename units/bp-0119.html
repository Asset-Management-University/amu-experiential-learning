Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Zinhle, the new safety lead at a commercial steam laundry that washes the linen for the local hospitals. On your first walk-around you stop at the big steam boiler - a pressure vessel the size of a bus - and notice a piece of cardboard taped over its flashing high-pressure alarm. "Oh, that," says the operator. "It's a nuisance alarm - trips if the pressure even thinks about climbing. We just silence it to get on with the work."

It feels wrong. You open the control cabinet, and tucked behind the door is a small jumper wire bypassing the high-pressure trip - the automatic shutdown that should fire if the alarm is ignored. A faded tag on the wire reads: "Temp fix - jammed valve - 6 months ago." Your stomach tightens: that is two layers of protection gone. You ask for the proof-test log of the last line of defence, the mechanical safety relief valve on top of the boiler. It should be tested every year. The last signature is from two and a half years ago.

Each action was reasonable on its own. Silence a nuisance alarm. Bypass an interlock to keep the line running. Defer a test because the schedule is full. But together they have quietly hollowed out the protection. You look at the huge machine, humming with immense energy, and one question burns.

Layer by layer our safety systems have been quietly switched off, each for a good reason - what is actually stopping this from going wrong right now, and how do I rebuild protection we can trust?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fix it fast - but each attempt makes it worse. The crackdown: you order every bypass removed at once. The plant grinds to a halt - the nuisance alarm stops production every twenty minutes, the team cannot clear a common jam without the interlock bypass, and they start to see you as the enemy who does not understand the job. Within a week you suspect new, hidden bypasses are in place; the problem has not gone away, it has gone underground. The blame game: you try to find who fitted the jumper wire. People get defensive - whoever did it was just trying to get the work done under pressure - and now everyone is afraid to talk to you. You have built a culture of fear, not safety. Trust the paperwork: you look at the engineering drawing, which shows three robust layers of protection. You could show a manager and say "the design is safe" - but you know that is a lie. The drawing is a story; the plant floor is the reality.

You realise the real question is not "who is to blame?" or "what does the drawing say?". So before you read on: what is the question that would actually let you find - and fix - the thinning protection?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop seeing the bypasses as failures of discipline and start seeing them as symptoms of a system under pressure. The operators are not villains; they are problem-solvers. Your job is to solve the problems that force them to choose between production and safety. Five moves:

  • See protection as a claim to be proven, not assumed: the breakthrough is to stop treating a safeguard as a fact and start treating it as a CLAIM that must be proven. 'The boiler is protected' is a hope; 'we proved the boiler's protection works today' is knowledge. A guard that is present on the drawing, or was working last year, or is assumed fine because nothing has gone wrong, is not protection - it is a story you are telling yourself. Every protective layer is either KNOWN-good (recently tested to actually trip) or it is unknown, and unknown must be treated as absent until proven. This single shift in language - from 'it is safe' to 'how did we prove it is safe?' - is what makes the silent erosion impossible to ignore.
  • Make the erosion visible - a Safeguard Register: the danger is that each bypass is invisible to everyone but the person who made it. So make it visible: one honest, public Safeguard Register (a whiteboard will do) where every active bypass, override and deferred test is logged with a name, a reason, an OWNER and an EXPIRY date. It is not for shaming - it is for honesty: a bypass is a DEBT the operation owes to safety, and a debt with no expiry is a debt that is never repaid. The moment the hidden becomes shared, the team can see the true thinness of the protection and start paying the debt down together, instead of each person carrying a private risk alone.
  • Concentrate on the few that matter most: you cannot proof-test and watch everything at once, and not every safeguard guards a catastrophe. So mark the few whose failure would be disastrous - here, the boiler's pressure protection, where failure risks lives - in red on the register, and protect THOSE first: checked every shift, proven most often, never allowed to run on a bypass. This is the same focusing discipline as bp-0158 - defence-in-depth for the worst hazards before the minor ones - so your limited attention lands where the harm would be greatest, not spread so thin it protects nothing well.
  • Restore the proof-test discipline - buy back certainty: a protective device is only known-good if it is regularly tested to actually do its job - to trip, to vent, to shut down - not merely inspected or signed off. So restore the proof test: schedule the planned shutdown and test the safety relief valve for real. Yes it costs - perhaps R200,000 in lost production for the shutdown - but frame it honestly: you are spending R200,000 to BUY BACK your certainty. When the valve is proven to lift at the right pressure, the relief is real - you now have a known-good guard, not a hope. Certainty is not free, but it is far cheaper than the catastrophe an unproven guard invites.
  • Fix the pressure that drives the bypass: re-enabling a guard without fixing why it was bypassed just guarantees it gets bypassed again. So treat every bypass as a SYMPTOM and chase its root. The nuisance alarm that gets cardboarded? The pressure sensor was old and drifting - a R5,000 replacement stops the false trips, and the reason to bypass it is gone. The interlock jumpered to clear a jam? Fix the jamming valve. If a safeguard is bypassed every single shift, then the safeguard, or the process around it, is wrong - and fixing the root is the only thing that makes the safe way the easy way. Make honesty safe, fix the pressures, and the safety debt falls while the proven guards rise.

See it transform Zinhle's plant. She stops saying "the boiler is protected" and starts asking "how did we prove it today?". She puts up a public Safeguard Register - every bypass, override and deferred test, each with a name, a reason, an owner and an expiry - and marks the boiler's pressure protection in red. She schedules the shutdown and proof-tests the relief valve for real (R200,000 to buy back certainty), and it lifts at the right pressure - a known-good guard at last. She chases the nuisance alarm to its root: an old, drifting sensor, replaced for R5,000, and the reason to bypass it is simply gone. Slowly the board becomes the team's shared, honest picture of risk - people start saying "if we fixed that pump seal, we would not need this bypass". The safety debt falls; the proven guards rise. The plant does not just feel safer - it is safer. A safeguard you have not proven is not protection; it is a hope. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This happens in our own homes too, one reasonable shortcut at a time.

See the same erosion at home

Think of the smoke alarm in your kitchen. It chirps every time you make toast, so you take the battery out "just for now" - you have bypassed a nuisance alarm. Then you forget to put it back, and you have silently removed a layer of protection for your whole family. The real fix is not yelling at yourself to remember the battery; it is moving the alarm to a spot where it does its job without being a nuisance - fix the pressure that drove the bypass.

Or the seatbelt warning that beeps until you buckle up. It is tempting to silence it and feel the nuisance is gone - but you have overridden an interlock. The car now "thinks" you are belted; in a crash the airbag that expects a belted body can do more harm. The protection looks present and is quietly hollow - exactly the trap, in miniature: a guard you defeat for convenience is a guard you no longer have.

The same moves apply: make the shortcut visible, see protection as something proven not assumed, and fix the real reason it felt necessary.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Where in your world are the safeguards quietly thin - where are you running on hope instead of proof? It need not be a big machine. It might be a critical backup that has never been test-restored, a fire escape sometimes used for "temporary" storage, or a two-person sign-off that one person does by signing twice to save time.

  1. Name one critical process and its 3-5 key safeguards - the layers meant to stop a disaster.
  2. Ask the hard question: when was each one last PROVEN to work in the real world - not just signed off, but actually tested?
  3. Run a quiet experiment: for one week, privately note every time a check is skipped, a procedure shortened, or a limit pushed "just this once". Do not judge - just see.
  4. Look for the pattern. Pick the most common shortcut and ask the people who do the work: what pressure makes this feel necessary, and what would make the right way the easy way?

This is not about finding fault, and it was never a sign that anyone was reckless - good people bypass good guards when the system forces a choice between getting the work done and keeping it safe. The front line almost always knows where the silent risks hide; your mission is to make it safe for them to tell the truth, so you can restore the integrity of your protection together. People-safety comes before throughput, always - and a safeguard you have not proven is just a story you tell yourself.
