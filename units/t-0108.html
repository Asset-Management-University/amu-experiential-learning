We assumed they could

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. You have done the hard work of ownership: every critical task now has a single, clear owner. The weekly standby-pump test belongs to one of your most trusted operators - twenty years of service, steady hands, never let you down.

Then the main pump trips. You reach for the standby. It is dead.

And the reason, when you find it, is quietly devastating. Last year a new pump model was installed, with a different test sequence to the old one. Your veteran operator - genuinely brilliant on the old pump - was never actually shown the new procedure. He had been running the old test on the new machine, and it never caught the fault. Nobody doubted him; everyone simply assumed that twenty years of experience must surely cover it. The task was owned. The owner was not competent for this version of it. And no one ever checked. A good man was quietly set up to fail, and a half-million-rand asset sat useless when the town needed it most.

The task had a clear owner I trusted completely - and it still failed, because I assumed he could do it instead of checking he actually could. How do I make sure the person on the hook for a task genuinely has the specific skills it needs - without it ever feeling like an insult?

Before you read on, decide. The weekly standby-pump test was owned, clearly, by a technician with twenty years here whom you would trust with anything. The test was logged as done. When the main pump failed, the standby did not start — and it turns out the new pump's test sequence is nothing like the old one, and nobody ever showed him. He is expert on the machine that was there before. A half-million-rand asset sat useless when the town needed it. Write down what you would do — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why nobody asked the obvious question

• “Have a word with him about the logged test.” He did what he understood the test to be, on a machine he has run for two decades. A conversation about the log addresses honesty, which was never in question, and leaves the actual gap exactly where it is.

• “Move the task to somebody else.” To whom, and on what basis? You would be making the same assumption again about a different person, and you would be taking a duty away from somebody as a punishment for something nobody told him.

• “Send everybody on training.” Generous, expensive, and unaimed. Most of them do not need most of it, and there is no way to tell afterwards whether the one gap that mattered was closed.

• “Write a better procedure for the new pump.” Worth doing and not sufficient. A procedure helps somebody who knows they need it; he had no reason to think this test was different from the one he has run for years.

• “Supervise the test until I am satisfied.” That works for this task while you watch it, and says nothing about the dozen other tasks where the same assumption is sitting unexamined.

• The uncomfortable part is why nobody asked. Asking a man with twenty years whether he can do something feels like an insult — so the question that would have caught this was never available, and his long service is precisely what made the gap invisible. Which tells you the check cannot be about the person at all. Ask instead what this task actually requires, named specifically enough to be demonstrated rather than as a general standard of being good at the job, and then look at what each person has actually shown they can do — not years served. Done that way the picture is nearly always uneven: strong on most of it, short on one part. And that is what makes it possible to run without insult, because what comes out is a fact about a task rather than a verdict on a man — a gap found before it fails again, which is a win rather than a reprimand.

There is a tool for exactly this, and it is built on dignity, not judgement. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the one page that would have caught the gap before the pump ever failed - an honest look at what the job needs and what the person can actually, demonstrably do.

1. The Role and its task

The role: the technician who runs the weekly test on the new standby pump. The task it must deliver: prove the standby will start the day the main pump fails.

2. The competencies it really needs (specific, not 'be good at the job')

The real competencies (specific, not vague): (1) isolate and lock off the pump safely; (2) run the NEW pump's test sequence; (3) read the vibration trace; (4) log and escalate a fail in the system.

3. Honest assessment: required level vs current (by demonstrated evidence)

Rate by what they have shown they can DO (say 1-4), not by years served. Isolation: needs 3, has 4. New test sequence: needs 3, has 1. Vibration: needs 2, has 2. Logging: needs 3, has 3.

4. The gap (where current is below required)

The gap: the new test sequence - required 3, current 1, a gap of -2. He is brilliant on the OLD pump after 20 years, but was never shown the new one. The gap is a FACT, not a fault.

5. The development plan (close it, and keep the evidence)

Close it: a half-day on the new test procedure, then a practical check signed off by Onalenna - and keep the record as evidence of competence. A gap found before it failed again is a WIN.

See what it does that "twenty years of service" never could: it names the specific skills the task needs, and rates them by what the operator has actually shown he can do. And there it is - a glaring gap on the new test sequence, invisible until now, with a simple half-day fix beside it. The gap is not a verdict on a good man; it is a training need the works simply failed to meet - and now can. Now let us walk through the thinking.

T3: The Blueprint

This is a competency framework (a competency matrix), and it comes from ISO 55001, clause 7.2. Last unit's RACI answered who is on the hook for a task. This answers a different and equally vital question: can the person on the hook actually do it? Authority and ability are not the same thing - and assuming the second from the first is exactly how the standby pump died.

Read the page you just saw as a method, each box a question. What is the role, and its critical task? What specific skills does it really need? (Not "be a good operator" - "run the new test sequence".) What level does the job demand, and what has the person demonstrated? (Rated by evidence, never by job title or years served.) Where is the gap? And what one action closes it? - keeping the record as proof of competence.

And here is the heart of it, the part that makes the tool humane: a gap is not a failing of the person - it is a training need the organisation is responsible for filling. You are measuring the fit between a person's skills and a role's demands, never their worth. Finding a gap is a success: it means you can close it before it causes a failure, and help a good person do their job with confidence. That is the dignity of it.

Does it scale? The Corathyl matrix making sure an operator can test this new pump is the exact same discipline as the regulator that certifies a pilot to fly one specific aircraft type - not just "an aeroplane". Where it truly matters, you may not do the task until your competence on that task is proven.

T4: Your Turn

Now turn it, gently, on yourself. Take a role you actually hold - a part-time job, a position in a society, captain of a team. Build a small competency matrix for it:

  1. Name the real competencies - the 3 or 4 specific skills the role truly needs (not "be organised" - "reconcile the society's account each month").
  2. Rate yourself honestly - the level the role needs against the level you have actually demonstrated. Evidence, not ego.
  3. Find your biggest gap - and write one concrete action you could take this month to start closing it.

This one is for you, no one else - so be honest. A gap you can see is a gap you can close.

T5: The Guardrails

A competency framework is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust your assessment.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The assumption. Rating someone competent because of their title, their degree, or their years served. That was the standby-pump trap exactly. Competence is what a person can demonstrate, today, on this task.

• The vague skill. Listing "good communication" or "good with computers". You cannot assess or train a fog. Name the specific competency: "log and escalate a fault in the asset system".

• The shame trap. Treating a gap as a black mark against the person. A gap is a failure of the system to provide training - and your job is to find it and bridge it. Handle it with dignity, or people will hide their gaps from you, and the gaps will surface as failures instead.

• The one-and-done. Assessing competence once and assuming it lasts forever. The moment the equipment, the procedure, or the standard changes, the competence must be checked again. The new pump proved that in the cruellest way.

The standby pump did not die because a good operator was bad at his job. It died because the works gave him a task and assumed - never checked - that he could do its new version. Now you hold the tool that closes that gap with care: name the real skills a role needs, measure them by honest evidence, and meet every gap with training, not blame. Do that, and "we assumed they could" stops being the quiet sentence behind so many failures - and becomes "we made sure they could, and helped them when they could not yet."
