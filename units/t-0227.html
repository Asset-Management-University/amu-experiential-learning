A claim, not a portrait

T1: The Ache

The role that plans the maintenance of the treatment plant has sat empty for four months. The maintenance backlog is growing, jobs are slipping, and every capable planner who applied was screened out at the first gate - because the job specification demands a four-year engineering degree, and they did not have one. They could do the job. The spec would not let them near it.

Where did that degree requirement come from? Nobody knows. It was in the last version of the spec, and the version before that. The last person to hold the role happened to have a degree, so the degree went into the spec - not because the work needs one, but because the last person had one. No one ever tested it. It just kept getting copied forward, quietly turning away good people for a reason that had nothing to do with whether they could plan maintenance.

And here is the mirror of the same failure. When the plant last recruited into this role, the spec never named the one thing that mattered most - the ability to read condition and failure data - because no one had ever written it down. So they hired against the spec, got someone who looked perfect on paper, and discovered too late that the person could not do the part of the job the asset most needed.

My job specification is turning away people who could do the work and letting through people who cannot - because it was copied, not tested. How do I make a spec that matches the actual job?

Before you read on, decide. The best applicant for the reliability post has been screened out: the specification demands a particular degree, and when you ask three people why, nobody can say — it has been in there for years. Meanwhile the mirror. When this role was last filled, the specification never mentioned the ability to read condition and failure data, which is the thing the asset most needs, because nobody had ever written it down. So they hired against the spec, got somebody who looked perfect on paper, and discovered too late that he could not do that part of the job at all. Write down how you would make a specification that matches the work — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and where the requirements actually came from

• “Make an exception for this candidate.” Rescues one applicant this month and leaves the document to do the same thing next time, to somebody you never see — and exceptions granted case by case are exactly how a requirement nobody believes in survives for years.

• “Add the data-reading requirement.” Right and half the job. You would have fixed the omission you know about and kept every other untested line, including the degree that just cost you the best applicant.

• “Widen it — ask for a degree or equivalent experience.” Softens a requirement nobody can justify rather than deciding whether the work needs it, and pushes the judgement onto whoever screens, with no basis to screen on.

• “Use the standard template from the sector.” Somebody else's inherited assumptions instead of your own. Their spec was built the same way yours was.

• “Interview more thoroughly to catch the gaps.” A better interview can only assess the people who reach it, and the best applicant was removed before anybody spoke to her.

• See what the document actually is: a claim about what the job needs — and most of the claims in most specifications have never been tested. Ask of each line where did this come from, and the honest answer is rarely the work needs it. It was inherited, copied from the last version which was copied from the one before. Or inflated, because writing down what you want makes it feel safe to ask for everything. Or — most quietly — built around the last person who held the role: their degree, their years, their background became the requirements, as though the job needed whatever that one human happened to have. None of these is the job. Assembled that way it is a portrait of a person, often one who has left, dressed up as a description of work — and it is an untested claim in a document that decides both who gets a livelihood and whether your asset gets the capability it needs. So test every line against the actual work: what does somebody in this role really do, and what must they be able to do to do it? That one test catches both your failures at once.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you evaluate and test a job specification against the real requirements of the job - seeing it as a claim, testing it against the actual work, and catching both the missing requirement and the needless barrier.

1. A job spec is a claim - and most are untested

START BY SEEING WHAT A JOB SPECIFICATION ACTUALLY IS: A CLAIM ABOUT WHAT THE JOB NEEDS - AND MOST OF THE CLAIMS IN MOST SPECS HAVE NEVER BEEN TESTED. Open almost any job specification and ask a simple question of each line: WHERE DID THIS COME FROM? The honest answer is usually not 'the work needs it.' It is that the spec was INHERITED - copied from the last version, which was copied from the one before. Or it was INFLATED into a wish-list, because when you are writing down what you want it feels safe to ask for everything. Or, most quietly, it was BUILT AROUND THE LAST PERSON who held the role: their degree, their years, their particular background became 'the requirements,' as though the job needed whatever that one human happened to have. NONE OF THESE IS THE JOB. A specification assembled this way is A PORTRAIT OF A PERSON - often a person who has left - DRESSED UP AS A DESCRIPTION OF WORK. And an untested claim, in a document that decides who gets a livelihood AND whether your asset gets the capability it needs, is dangerous. This is why a spec must be tested, not just written: it is a set of high-stakes claims, not a neutral form to copy forward.

2. Test it against the real requirements of the job

NOW THE TEST, AND IT HAS ONE ANCHOR: THE ACTUAL WORK. What does this role really do, day to day? And what does the ASSET OR THE ACCOUNTABILITY IT SERVES genuinely demand of the person doing it? You DERIVE THE REQUIREMENTS FROM THE WORK - from the tasks, the decisions, the competency the asset needs - NOT from tradition, NOT from the incumbent, NOT from habit. The competency framework (the discipline of naming the skills a role needs and assessing them by evidence - taught elsewhere) is one of your instruments here: it lets you state, in terms of DEMONSTRABLE CAPABILITY, what the job actually requires, and then check whether the spec matches. For the plant-maintenance planner, the real work is PLANNING AND SCHEDULING MAINTENANCE, READING CONDITION AND FAILURE DATA, AND COORDINATING CREWS AND CONTRACTORS. THAT is the requirement, and everything in the spec should trace back to it. The moment you anchor the spec to the work rather than to the old document, you can see which requirements belong and which drifted in - which is the whole point of testing rather than copying.

3. Under-specification - the spec asks too little and misses what the asset needs

THE FIRST FAILURE THE TEST CATCHES IS THE SILENT GAP - UNDER-SPECIFICATION - AND IT IS EASY TO MISS BECAUSE THE SPEC LOOKS FULL. A specification can be crowded with requirements and STILL LEAVE OUT THE ONE THAT MATTERS MOST, because no one ever wrote it down. The plant's role most needed THE ABILITY TO READ CONDITION AND FAILURE DATA - that is the competency that keeps the asset reliable - and the inherited spec NEVER MENTIONED IT. So the recruitment optimised for the things the spec DID name (the degree, the years) and never tested for the thing the ASSET ACTUALLY NEEDED. You hire someone who satisfies the spec on paper and cannot do the critical part of the job, AND THE ASSET PAYS FOR IT - a bearing fails that a data-literate technician would have caught. Testing the spec against the real work is exactly what surfaces the MISSING ESSENTIAL before you hire, not after it fails. Ask of the asset: what is the one competency this role exists to provide, and does the spec name it? If not, you have found an under-specification, and it is the most expensive kind of gap.

4. Over-specification and job-relatedness - the needless requirement is a barrier

THE SECOND FAILURE IS MORE COMMON AND MORE DAMAGING, BECAUSE IT LOOKS LIKE RIGOUR: OVER-SPECIFICATION. The spec demands A FOUR-YEAR DEGREE THE WORK DOES NOT NEED; TEN YEARS OF EXPERIENCE WHERE THREE WOULD DO; A CERTIFICATION UNRELATED TO THE ACTUAL TASK. Each of these FEELS like a high standard - and each is actually A BARRIER THAT EXCLUDES CAPABLE PEOPLE FOR A REASON THAT HAS NOTHING TO DO WITH WHETHER THEY CAN DO THE JOB. Here is the discipline that fixes it, and it has two moves. FIRST, SEPARATE ESSENTIAL FROM DESIRABLE: essential is the MINIMUM a person must have to do the job at all; desirable is what would make someone likely to do it BETTER. Do not let a desirable creep into the essential column, BECAUSE EVERYTHING IN THE ESSENTIAL COLUMN IS A GATE. SECOND, AND THIS IS THE WHOLE TEST: JUSTIFY EVERY ESSENTIAL REQUIREMENT BY JOB-RELATEDNESS - be able to point at a SPECIFIC TASK, ACCOUNTABILITY OR ASSET OUTCOME that genuinely requires it. A requirement you can justify that way is a REAL STANDARD. A REQUIREMENT YOU CANNOT IS A BARRIER WEARING A STANDARD'S CLOTHES - and it is turning away exactly the capable people the asset needs.

5. The test itself - where quality and fairness are the same act

END ON THE ONE IDEA THAT MAKES THIS A MISSION ACT, NOT JUST A PROCEDURE, BECAUSE A JOB SPECIFICATION IS ONE OF THE QUIETEST PLACES AN ORGANISATION BUILDS IN EXCLUSION - ALWAYS WITH GOOD INTENTIONS AND THE PHRASE 'HIGH STANDARDS.' Hold the frame exactly. A JOB SPECIFICATION IS A CLAIM ABOUT THE WORK, NOT A PORTRAIT OF A PERSON. EVERY REQUIREMENT MUST EARN ITS PLACE BY BEING GENUINELY REQUIRED BY THE JOB. A REQUIREMENT THAT CANNOT BE JUSTIFIED BY THE WORK IS NOT A STANDARD, IT IS A BARRIER. So when you strike out the needless degree, you are NOT lowering the bar - TESTING A SPECIFICATION AGAINST THE JOB REPLACES A FALSE STANDARD WITH A REAL ONE. The degree was never a real standard; it was a barrier that happened to look like one, turning away the capable planners the plant needed while the role sat empty. This is the barrier-removal of diversity management made concrete: YOU WIDEN THE POOL NOT BY RELAXING ANYTHING REAL, BUT BY DELETING THE FAKE REQUIREMENTS THAT WERE NEVER ABOUT THE WORK. And here is what makes it powerful - THE QUALITY ACT AND THE FAIRNESS ACT ARE THE SAME ACT. When you test every requirement against the job, you SIMULTANEOUSLY hire for the real requirements (quality) AND remove the unjustified barriers (fairness). You do not trade one for the other; you get BOTH, from one honest test.

T3: The Blueprint

  • What is a job spec, really? A claim - and most are untested. Inherited, copied, inflated into a wish-list, or built around the last person who held the role rather than the work it does.
  • Test it against what? The real requirements of the job. What does this role actually do, and what does the asset it serves actually demand? Derive every requirement from the work - the competency framework is one of your instruments.
  • What if it asks too little? Under-specification. The spec misses a competency the asset genuinely needs, so you hire someone who cannot do the critical part - and find out when it fails.
  • What if it asks too much? Over-specification. Requirements the job does not truly need are barriers that exclude capable people for no job-related reason. Separate essential from desirable, and justify every essential by the work.
  • So how do I test it? Job-relatedness. Hold every requirement against the actual tasks; keep the ones the work justifies, strike the ones it does not. That single act is both quality and fairness.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. At Corathyl, a vacancy has opened for the reliability technician who keeps the pump station running - monitoring condition, spotting early failure signs, and carrying out first-line repairs. The inherited job specification reads: "Essential: a four-year mechanical engineering degree; eight years' experience; a valid driver's licence; membership of a professional engineering body." The role has been vacant for months and good applicants keep being screened out.

  1. Name the real requirements. From the actual work (monitor condition, spot early failure, first-line repairs), name the two or three capabilities the job genuinely needs.
  2. Find the barrier. Name one requirement in the inherited spec that is over-specified - a barrier, not a standard - and say in a line why it is not job-related.
  3. Find the missing essential. Name the competency the asset most needs that the inherited spec does not mention at all.
  4. Rebuild it. Write the corrected spec as two lists - essential and desirable - and for each essential, write one sentence justifying its job-relatedness by linking it to a specific task or asset outcome.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the real requirements: the job needs condition-monitoring and early-fault-detection capability (the ability to read the signs an asset is starting to fail), hands-on first-line maintenance skill, and the reliability-data literacy to know what "normal" looks like and when it has changed. These come from the work, not the old spec.

Second, the barrier: the four-year engineering degree (or the eight years, or the professional-body membership) is the clearest one - a reliability technician doing condition monitoring and first-line repair does not need a four-year degree to do the job well, so the degree is not job-related; it is a barrier that screens out capable, experienced technicians for a reason unconnected to the work. (The driver's licence may be genuinely job-related if the role travels between sites - which shows the test in action: you ask of each line, does the work require it?)

Third, the missing essential: the inherited spec never names condition-monitoring / early-failure-detection literacy - the very competency the pump station most needs - so it would let through someone who cannot do the part of the job that keeps the asset reliable. Naming it is the under-specification catch.

Fourth, the rebuilt spec: essential = condition-monitoring capability ("the role must read vibration and temperature trends to catch a failing bearing before it fails"), first-line maintenance skill ("the role carries out the repairs that keep the station running between planned interventions"), reliability-data literacy ("the role must know normal from abnormal to raise the right alarm"); desirable = a formal qualification, wider experience, professional-body membership - genuinely useful, but not gates. The mark of a real answer is that every essential is justified by a task or an asset outcome, and nothing sits in the essential column that the work does not actually demand.

T5: The Guardrails

A job specification is a claim about the work, not a portrait of a person. Test every requirement against what the job actually does, or you are hiring for a fiction.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Inheriting the spec unquestioned. "It was in the last version" is not a justification. Every requirement earns its place by being required by the work, or it goes.

• Building the spec around the last incumbent. The job does not need whatever the previous post-holder happened to have. Describe the work, not the person who used to do it.

• Wish-listing. Asking for everything anyone might want feels safe and quietly excludes almost everyone. Ask only for what the job genuinely requires.

• Letting desirable creep into essential. Everything in the essential column is a gate. If it only makes someone likely to do better, it is desirable, not essential.

• A requirement with no job-relatedness. If you cannot point to a task, accountability or asset outcome that needs it, it is not a standard - it is a barrier. Strike it out.

• Under-specifying the asset-critical competency. A spec full of requirements can still miss the one that matters most. Test against the asset's real need, and name the competency it depends on.

• Mistaking barrier-removal for lowering the bar. Deleting a fake requirement raises the standard - it replaces a false one with a real one. You lose nothing the work needed.

• Forgetting the spec serves the asset. The specification exists so the asset gets the capability it needs. Tradition, habit and the old document do not get a vote; the work does.

The maintenance-planner role sat empty for four months while capable people were turned away by a degree the job never needed, and the last time the plant recruited it hired against a spec that never named the competency the asset most required. So do the one thing the old document never had done to it: test it. Read every requirement and ask where it came from and whether the work justifies it; derive the real requirements from the actual job and the asset it serves; catch the missing essential and strike the needless barrier; separate essential from desirable and justify every essential by a task or an outcome. A job specification is a claim about the work, not a portrait of a person - a requirement that cannot be justified by the work is not a standard, it is a barrier, and testing the spec against the job replaces a false standard with a real one. Do it, and in a single honest act you hire for the real job and open the door to everyone who can actually do it.
