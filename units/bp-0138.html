Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Thulisile, the shift maintenance planner at a manganese mine on the red desert sand of the dry interior. It is 04:40 on a cold Tuesday, six weeks into the job. The night air smells of diesel and dust through the open roller door, and the floodlights buzz over the wash bay.

The big 150-tonne haul truck - fleet number HT-12 - is down. It carries ore from the pit to the crusher; every hour it stands idle, the pit loses roughly 640 tonnes of moved ore. Your supervisor, Vuyokazi, is standing very close to you with her arms folded. She has been here twenty-two years and she does not raise her voice, which somehow makes it worse. "The front brake pack," she says. "Get me the torque figures and the right part number, and we'll have her turning by day shift."

You go to the steel filing cabinet by the tea station. There is a folder marked "HT-12 - Brakes" in faded marker. Inside are three different brake assembly drawings. One is a printout with a coffee ring on it. One is a photocopy of a photocopy, so grey you can barely read the bolt callouts. One came off the email of a fitter who left last year. The torque value on the first sheet says 540 Nm. The second says 610 Nm. The third has no torque value at all - someone has written "see updated spec" in pen, but there is no updated spec in the folder. You feel your stomach tighten. You do not know which sheet is the real one. The part number on two of them differs by one digit. If you torque the brake bolts wrong, the pack can fail under a 150-tonne load on the haul ramp - with a driver in the cab.

Across the workshop, the storeman calls over, "Just use the one on the wall, hey, that's what we always use." On the wall is a laminated sheet, curled at the corners, dated - you squint - 2019. Vuyokazi checks her watch. "Day shift is in three hours," she says quietly. "The mine manager already phoned me once." You have three sheets, a wall poster, and a storeman's memory. None of them agree. One of them might be right.

The brake is down and I need the torque figure by day shift, but the folder has three conflicting drawings, the wall poster is dated 2019, the storeman swears by memory, and none can prove which is real - do I pick the likeliest number and let a spanner near a brake under a 150-tonne load?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Vuyokazi wants a number and day shift is in three hours. "Use the wall poster - that's what we always use." But it's dated 2019 with no sign it was ever checked since; "what we always use" is exactly how a stale sheet survives, and this one sits over a brake under a 150-tonne load. "Average the two folder sheets, or take the middle value." But a torque spec is not a vote; if both are wrong, the average is wrong too, and a guessed brake torque is a person's life, not a rounding decision. "Just send the most official-looking sheet to the fitter and get the truck turning." But none of these can prove an issue date, a version, an author or an approval - "official-looking" is not the same as authoritative, and tomorrow you'll be back at the same cabinet with the same four disagreeing copies. So before you read on: name what every one of these documents is missing that would let you trust it, and the first thing you'd do to find out how many uncontrolled copies are really loose in this workshop.

A wrong brake torque is not a paperwork problem - it is a person's life - so do NOT let anyone torque those bolts on a guess. Stop and ask a better question than 'which sheet?': ask 'which of these can I PROVE is correct?' None of them can be proved - not one sheet has an issue date, a version number, an author, or an approval signature you can trace (the 2019 poster has a date but no sign it was ever checked since). So get the truth from a source that can prove itself - phone the equipment manufacturer's technical line and the mine's engineering records office for the CURRENT service bulletin. But understand the real fault: it was never the brake. It was that no one could say which document was the true one - because there is no ONE controlled master, the documents carry no identity (number, version, date, approval) on their face, and the superseded copies were never retired so they keep lying to people. The real question is not 'which of these four numbers do I trust tonight?' but 'why can nobody here prove what the current, approved document is - and how many uncontrolled copies of it are loose in this workshop?' Before you fix the system, count: you cannot control copies you have never counted.
Five moves turn a dawn guess into a controlled, trustworthy answer. (1) STOP THE GUESSWORK, ASK WHICH IS PROVABLY CURRENT - none of the sheets can prove an issue date, version, author or approval; phone the manufacturer for the current bulletin (the right torque is 580 Nm, NOT 540 or 610 - both folder sheets were wrong - and the odd part number was a SUPERSEDED part, the bracket redesigned in 2021). (2) COUNT BEFORE YOU CONTROL - build the cheapest instrument, a copy census, with a success line set out loud; walking the workshop one shift, you expected four copies but found NINE (folder sheets, the 2019 poster, two in lockers, one on the planning PC, one on an old phone group-chat, and one riding in the truck's cab) - the most dangerous one closest to the work, and the one TRUE answer not even stored on site. (3) ONE MASTER, ONE HOME, IDENTITY ON ITS FACE - exactly one official version of each document in one agreed home; every controlled document shows a number, a revision, an issue date, who approved it, and what it replaces; everything else is an uncontrolled copy, not to be trusted for the job. (4) A NEW VERSION RETIRES THE OLD - when the bracket was redesigned, the spec should have been re-issued as a new revision, the old marked 'superseded - do not use', and the dead copies PULLED from circulation (the step the mine had skipped for years); plus a retention rule - keep what you must, where, for how long. (5) MAKE THE CONTROLLED PATH THE EASIEST, AND RE-COUNT TO PROVE IT - set it up cheap (a shared folder, the bulletin stored on site, every copy stamped 'controlled copy - valid on day of print only'), pull and bin every uncontrolled copy, and re-run the census six weeks later: nine down to TWO, each able to prove who it is. The danger is not the truth you cannot find, but the out-of-date copy you can find, sitting closer to hand with nothing on its face to warn it is no longer true.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You make the only honest call available at 04:40: you do not let anyone torque those bolts on a guess. A wrong torque on a haul-truck brake is not a paperwork problem; it is a person's life. So first you stop, and ask a better question than "which sheet?" - you ask "which of these can I prove is correct?" Five moves:

  • Stop the guesswork - ask which is provably current: you make the only honest call available at 04:40: you do not let anyone torque those bolts on a guess, because a wrong torque on a haul-truck brake is not a paperwork problem, it is a person's life. So you stop, and ask a better question than 'which sheet?' - you ask 'which of these can I PROVE is correct?' And none of them can. Not one sheet has an issue date, a version number, an author, or an approval signature you can trace; the wall poster has a date - 2019 - but no sign it was ever checked since. A document that cannot tell you who it is and when it was born has no authority, however confident the number on it looks. So you do the safe thing: you ring the equipment manufacturer's technical line and the mine's engineering records office, and within forty minutes you have the manufacturer's current service bulletin. The correct torque is 580 Nm - not 540, not 610; both folder sheets were wrong. The part number with the changed digit was a superseded part - the bracket was redesigned in 2021. HT-12 is back turning by 07:50. But you got lucky with a phone call at dawn, and you and Vuyokazi both know it: the real fault was never the brake, it was that no one in the workshop could say which document was the true one.
  • Count before you control - the copy census: you cannot control copies you have never counted, so before buying anything you find out how bad it really is by building the smallest instrument that makes the invisible visible: a one-page copy census. You set a success line in advance and say it to Vuyokazi out loud, so you cannot move the goalposts later - 'if document control is mostly working, for HT-12 I should find roughly one or two copies of each key document, and at least one carrying a number, a date and an approval; if it is broken, I'll find a scatter and not one of them able to prove who it is.' Then you walk the workshop for one shift with a clipboard, tallying every place an HT-12 brake document lives. The count lands harder than you expected: you had told Vuyokazi 'three sheets and a wall poster' - four copies - but the honest census finds NINE: the three folder sheets, the 2019 poster, two more taped inside fitters' lockers, one on the planning PC desktop, one on an eighteen-month-old phone group-chat, and one laminated copy riding in the cab of HT-12 itself. The surprise is that last one - the very machine you were trying to fix safely was carrying its own out-of-date brake sheet, the one a driver or roadside fitter would reach for first. The dangerous copies were the ones closest to the work, in lockers and the cab, not in the folder. And a second jolt: the manufacturer's bulletin, the one true answer, was not stored anywhere on site - you were one disconnected phone line from having no truth to find. So you change course: capturing the master and sweeping the point-of-use copies becomes the first job, ahead of any clever system.
  • One master, one home, and an identity on its face: what you build is called document control - a simple, enforced discipline for how technical documents are created, approved, issued, found and retired - and it rests on a few plain ideas. ONE CONTROLLED MASTER, ONE PLACE: for every asset and task there is exactly one official version of each document, kept in one agreed home (here, the mine's engineering document register); everything else - the folder copies, the wall poster, the fitter's email - is an uncontrolled copy, and is treated as not to be trusted for the job. EVERY DOCUMENT CARRIES ITS OWN IDENTITY: a controlled document shows, on its face, a document number, a version or revision number (Rev 4, Rev 5...), an issue date, who approved it, and ideally a line saying which version it replaces. If a sheet cannot tell you who it is and when it was born, it is not a controlled document - it is just paper. This is the move that would have dissolved the whole 04:40 crisis before it began: faced with three sheets, you could have read their faces and known in seconds which (if any) was the current, approved master - instead of phoning a manufacturer in the dark, hoping someone answered. Identity on the face of the document is what turns a pile of paper into a thing you can trust.
  • A new version retires the old - and retention: the step the mine had been skipping for years is the one that matters most: a change makes a new version, and KILLS the old one. When the manufacturer redesigned that bracket in 2021, the spec should have been re-issued as a new revision, the old revision marked 'superseded - do not use', and the dead copies pulled from circulation. Retiring the old version so it stops lying to people is the discipline that was missing - which is exactly why a 2019 poster and a cab sheet were still quietly offering wrong torque values years later. A master that is updated but whose old copies are left in the wild gives you the worst of both worlds: a true answer that nobody can find, surrounded by false answers that everybody can. Alongside this sits RETENTION: some records - the brake's maintenance history, a pressure part's requalification, a safety sign-off - must be kept for a defined period, in a defined place, not forever in a random folder and not binned at a clear-up; a retention rule says what to keep, where, and for how long. Together these two rules - retire the superseded, keep the required - are what stop the document set from rotting back into a scatter of half-truths the moment your attention moves on.
  • Make the controlled path the easiest, and re-count to prove it: the point of control is one trustworthy answer to 'what is current?' - so that anyone, a new fitter or an auditor, can go to the register and get one reliable answer. You set it up the cheap, honest way first, with no big software on day one: a shared, access-controlled folder on the mine server; the manufacturer's bulletin stored IN it so the truth lives on site, not on a phone line; a number and a revision on every live document; each issued copy stamped 'CONTROLLED COPY - valid on day of print only'; and then you walk the census route again and pull and bin every uncontrolled copy - the lockers, the planning PC, the group-chat print, the cab sheet, and, with a little ceremony, the curled 2019 wall poster. A short list on the register names, per critical asset, the current revision of each key document; when the manufacturer issues a new bulletin, one person updates the master, bumps the revision, and marks the old superseded. Then you do not declare victory - you re-run the instrument. Six weeks later you walk the census route a second time against the same success line, and the HT-12 brake tally reads TWO: the controlled master in the register and one stamped controlled-copy in the folder, both showing the new revision, both dated, both approved, and the cab is clear. Nine down to two, every survivor able to prove who it is - the count is the proof. Be honest about the trade-off: document control is a small, steady tax of discipline (someone owns the register, every change costs a few minutes, fitters grumble), and over-engineer it - forty fields, three sign-offs for a typo - and people route around it; the skill is to make the controlled path the easiest path, so doing it right is less effort than the dawn phone call. See where it lands. HT-12 was turning by 07:50; the census of nine copies became a controlled two, each able to prove its own identity; and the manufacturer's bulletin now lives on site, not one disconnected phone line away. The breakdown was loud, but the real failure was quiet - you could not find the right document, and the version in circulation was out of date. Having a master is not the same as having control. A single trustworthy answer only protects people if every other copy is either retired or visibly marked as not-the-truth - because people act on whatever is nearest and easiest. So keep one controlled master in one home, give every document an identity on its face (number, version, date, approval), and when it changes issue a new version AND pull the old one from circulation. The danger is not the truth you cannot find; it is the out-of-date copy you can find, sitting closer to hand, with nothing on its face to warn it is no longer true.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You've seen the clean case: at the mine, nothing was controlled, so the danger was loud. Now take one rep with a trickier version - because in the real world the danger is usually quieter, and it hides behind something that looks already fixed.

See the paint shop that made one master and still lost money

Anelisiwe runs a small hardware-and-paint shop in a small town. After a bad week of arguments at the till, she did the sensible thing: she made one master price list, a single spreadsheet on the shop laptop, and told everyone, "That is the only price list now." She even printed a fresh copy each Monday and taped it next to the till so staff could glance at it. This morning a customer is buying ten litres of exterior paint. The Saturday assistant, Luyanda, reads the price off a slip taped to the counter - R489 - and the customer pays. An hour later Anelisiwe checks the laptop master: that paint went up to R549 three weeks ago when the supplier's cost rose. The shop just lost R60 on the tin, and it has been quietly losing it on every tin of that paint for three weeks. Here is the thing to sit with: Anelisiwe did the hard part. She made a single master. So why is she still losing money?

Look at what Luyanda actually touched. Anelisiwe has one master on the laptop - but Luyanda read the price from a slip taped to the counter; where did that slip come from, and when? There is not one document in this shop - there are two in circulation: the laptop master (correct, R549) and an old printed slip taped to the counter (wrong, R489). The slip is a copy that used to match the master and then drifted, because when Anelisiwe updated the price on the laptop she never pulled the old printout. It is the exact same fault as the 2019 wall poster at the mine - an uncontrolled copy left in circulation after the master moved on. She even reprints a fresh copy each Monday, but nobody removes last week's, so the old slips pile up and the newest is not always the one nearest the till. She nailed "one master, one home" but skipped "find every copy" and "new version retires the old", and her "what's current?" answer is unreliable because the thing nearest Luyanda's hand is not the master - it just looks like it. Notice what both stories share: somebody HAD access to the right answer (the manufacturer's bulletin; the laptop master); the harm came not from the truth being missing but from a stale copy sitting closer to hand than the truth, with nothing on its face to warn anyone it was out of date - at the mine it nearly cost a life, at the shop it quietly bled cash. The deep lesson: having a master is not the same as having control. A single master only protects you if every other copy is either retired or visibly marked not-the-truth, because the dangerous version is rarely the one you can't find - it is the one you can find, that's wrong, and that looks fine.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The brake folder was the shape of the problem, not the problem itself. The same shape turns up far from a mine workshop, and the trap is always the same: many copies, none of them provably the true one, and the wrong one still in circulation. Find it where you are:

  1. Name your "HT-12 folder". Where do several versions of the same important document live in different places - a contract, a price list, a recipe, a policy, a procedure, an assembly drawing, a medication list? Which version would someone grab in a hurry, and could you prove it is the current one?
  2. Hunt the silent out-of-date copy. Find one document still being used but no longer correct - an old price sheet, a superseded form, last year's safety rules pinned to a wall. Who is relying on it right now without knowing? What could it cost?
  3. Test the "what's current?" question. Pick one important document and ask: who or what can I trust to tell me the single correct version right now? If the honest answer is "nobody" or "I'd have to ask around", you have found the gap.
  4. Design the cheapest control, and set one retention rule. Without buying software, how would you give your most important documents an identity (number, version, date, owner) and a single home - and make sure the old version gets pulled when a new one is issued? Then pick one record you must keep and decide for how long, where, and who checks it is still there.
R

Run the smallest test this week: pick your one most important document and do the cheapest move - count every copy of it in circulation (printouts, files, screens, walls, lockers, phones) and write the number down. If it is more than one or two, or any copy can't show a date and a version, your control has a hole. Then, after you clean up, count again - the drop is your proof, not our say-so. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build the controlled master, the identity, and the retire-the-old rule for your own document together.)

On a mine it was a brake under load; for you it might be a wrong dose, a bad quote, an out-of-date policy, or simply an hour lost hunting for the right file. The discipline that fixes it is the same wherever you meet it: one controlled master, a clear identity on every document, and the old version retired before it can mislead anyone.
