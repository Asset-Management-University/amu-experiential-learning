I have done this job safely for two years - now I have a fortnight to prove it on paper to a stranger who has never seen me work. How do I make everything I have done visible, and make it count?

T1: The Ache

It is a Thursday afternoon at the Tank You filling yard. You are Thando, and for two years you have been the person who keeps the cylinder side of this business safe. You check every 9 kg LPG cylinder before it goes out, you spot the rusted footrings, you pull the dented ones off the line, and you keep the little notebook where you log what you found and what you did.

Muhammad walks over with a printed email, its corner creased from folding. A bigger supplier wants Tank You to fill and maintain cylinders for a chain of takeaway shops - good, steady money, about R18,000 a month. But there is a condition: the cylinder work must be signed off by someone holding a recognised competence certificate - a formal piece of paper saying an outside body has checked your skills against a written standard and agrees you meet it. "They want proof," Muhammad says. "Not that we say you're good. Every part of the standard, ticked off." On the second page is a list - fourteen things a competent cylinder handler must be able to do.

Your stomach tightens. You can do the work - you have always been able to do the work, hundreds of times. But the assessor will not watch you for two years; they will sit with you for one afternoon and ask you to show them. And right now your proof lives in fourteen places: that notebook, photos on your phone, a few text messages, things only Ibraaheem remembers. You have a fortnight before the assessor comes.

I have done this job safely for two years - now I have a fortnight to prove it on paper to a stranger who has never seen me work. How do I make everything I have already done visible, and make it count?

Before you read on, decide. Fourteen things a competent cylinder handler must be able to do, and you can do all of them — hundreds of times over two years. But the assessor will not watch you for two years. He will sit with you for one afternoon and ask you to show him, and your proof is in a notebook, on your phone, in a few text messages, and in what Ibraaheem happens to remember. A fortnight. Write down what you would do with it. Your own answer, in your own words, not a choice from a list.

Now compare — the four answers, and what each one leaves you holding on the day

• “Practise hard for two weeks so I’m sharp on the day.” The natural instinct, and it is aimed at a problem you do not have. You are not short of skill. Two weeks of practising will not put a single piece of proof in front of the assessor.

• “Write out everything I’ve done over the two years.” Better, and it produces a story about how hard you work. He is not asking what you have done in general — he has fourteen specific lines, and a long account that does not answer them line by line leaves him to hunt for the answers himself.

• “Get Ibraaheem to write a letter saying I’m good.” Worth having for one or two points, and on its own it is somebody’s opinion of you. The strongest proof is the ordinary thing you actually did — the photo, the signed sheet, the record with a date on it — because nobody has to take that on trust.

• “If I’m short on something, write it up as though I’ve done it.” Nobody says this out loud, and a fortnight is exactly when it whispers. Do not. An honest gap with real proof around it survives an assessor pushing on it; an invented one collapses the moment he asks a second question — and it takes the true thirteen down with it.

The task is smaller and stranger than it looks: not to become better, but to make what you have already done visible to somebody who has never seen you work.

You do not need to become better at the job - you are already good at it. You need a reliable way to gather what you have already done and lay it out so it answers the standard, point by point, and to stand behind it when the assessor pushes back. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is a portfolio of evidence: an organised collection of proof that you can do what a standard says you must. It is not a story about how hard you work - it is a map, where for every line in the standard there is the proof. Watch it built with Thando, on her real fourteen-point standard.

Step 1 - Turn the standard into your checklist (the table of contents)

Thando copies the supplier's fourteen requirements verbatim into a list, C1 to C14 - C1 inspect a 9 kg cylinder for external damage; C3 apply the correct rejection criteria; C4 keep auditable records; C5 handle and store safely; C9 use correct PPE. The standard hands her the table of contents she did not have to invent.

Step 2 - Gather the proof you already have

Thando's proof lived in fourteen scattered places: a 14-month dated inspection notebook (serial number, fault, action); 47 phone photos of rejected cylinders; text messages reporting rejections to Ibraaheem; the rejection-bin tally; a safety induction certificate from 16 months ago. She lays each out and asks of it: which competence does this prove?

Step 3 - Build the evidence matrix (the gaps stand out)

Thando draws the table and marks a Y where each piece proves a competence: the notebook covers C1 to C4; the clear photos add weight to C1 and C3; the safety certificate touches C5. She fills it down to C14 - and three rows light up nearly empty. The matrix has found her gaps for her, before the assessor could.

Step 4 - Test every piece against VARC

The notebook passes all four - valid, in her hand, 14 steady months, current. The safety certificate is valid and authentic but 16 months old, so it FAILS Current (the standard wants within 12 months) - amber. Some photos are blurry and prove nothing, so they FAIL Valid for 'spot the damage' - she keeps only the 31 clear ones. VARC turns a pile of proof into proof that will hold.

Step 5 - Fill the gaps with real proof, then defend the link

Three real gaps: C5 leans only on a stale certificate; C9 (PPE) is empty; C12 (leak response) is only mentioned. Thando fills them honestly - Ibraaheem takes three dated photos of her inspecting in full PPE and signs a witness statement; she re-books the safety refresher so the certificate is current; she writes a dated account of the last real leak she handled, countersigned. She orders the folder C1 to C14, an index line per competence, the matrix at the front as the map. When the assessor pushes - 'how do I know you did not just copy this log?' - she points to the dated entries and the signed witness statement. Every line answered; the contract signed.

See what the five steps did. They took two years of real, careful work - scattered across a notebook, a phone and someone's memory - and turned it into a folder that answers the standard line by line, every claim backed by proof that holds. And see the heart of it: the portfolio did not make Thando competent. She already was. It made her competence VISIBLE - in a currency an outside assessor could read - so that what she could already do could finally be recognised, and could open a door. This is AMU's whole promise in one technique: you can; here is how to prove it, honestly, and own it. The discipline that keeps it honest is simple - you gather what you have truly done and never invent a thing; an honest gap, filled with real proof, beats a polished fiction every time.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the portfolio in five moves.

  • 1. Turn the standard into a checklist. Copy its exact wording into a numbered list; it is your table of contents, line by line.
  • 2. Gather the proof you already have. List every place it lives and pull it together; you are collecting real work, not creating it.
  • 3. Build the evidence matrix. Competences down, evidence across, a mark where a piece proves a competence - the gaps stand out on their own.
  • 4. Test every piece against VARC. Valid, Authentic, Reliable, Current - cull or strengthen anything that fails one.
  • 5. Fill the gaps and defend. Real current proof for the empty rows; order it to the standard; walk the assessor through the link.

The ideas underneath: a competence is a single, clearly described thing you must be able to do, and a standard is just a list of these - so you never invent the structure, you answer it; VARC is the four-question test every assessor applies (Valid - does it actually show this competence? Authentic - is it genuinely yours? Reliable - a steady record, not one lucky day? Current - recent enough to still count?); the evidence matrix is the map that proves at a glance that every line is covered and nothing rests on a single weak photo; and to defend is to explain the link calmly ("this piece proves that competence, because..."), not to argue. This technique fits whenever someone must judge your competence but cannot watch you work over time - an assessor, a client, an auditor, a new employer - and the judgement is against a written standard you can gather real proof for. It does not fit when there is no standard to map to (find or agree one first), or when the thing judged is a one-off opinion rather than a repeatable skill.

Two disciplines keep the portfolio honest and strong. First, never invent or inflate - fill a gap with real, current proof or not at all; the whole worth of a portfolio is that it is true, and a single fabricated piece, once an assessor catches it, poisons every honest one beside it. An honest gap you go and fill with real work is the right answer, always. Second, let the matrix and VARC find your weaknesses before the assessor does - an empty row, or a piece that fails Current, is not a failure but a gift: it tells you exactly what to go and evidence this week, while you still have time.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your portfolio true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The standard is the structure - do not invent your own. Copy its exact wording and answer it line by line; an assessor marks against their words, not your paraphrase.

• Never fabricate, never inflate. Fill a gap with real, current proof or leave it open and go earn it. One invented piece, once caught, sinks the whole portfolio - and it is not who you are.

• One lucky example is not evidence. A single photo or a one-day record is weak (it fails Reliable). Show a steady pattern of the real work over time.

• Check Current before the assessor does. A certificate or record that has aged out fails the test no matter how good it once was. Re-book, re-do, refresh - in time.

• Defend by guiding, not arguing. When pushed, point to the proof and the link, calmly. You are walking them along a map you built, not winning a fight.

You came in able to do the work and unable to prove it - two careful years scattered across fourteen places, and a fortnight on the clock. You can walk into that assessment with something solid instead: a folder ordered to the standard, every line answered by real proof that passes all four tests, the gaps found and filled honestly, and the calm ability to show the assessor exactly why each piece counts. This is the method to own the capability you already have - to make real, careful work visible in a currency the world can read, and so turn what you can do into what you are recognised, trusted and paid to do.
