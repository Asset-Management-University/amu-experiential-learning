Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Katlego, and you run a regional sawmill - the main employer for your town, cutting and kiln-drying the timber the whole area builds with. Your job is simple: keep the saw line running and the kilns loaded. One morning a technician servicing the control panel for the main line points at the screen on the PLC, the little computer that runs the saws. "Funny thing," he says. "From here, I can see the office printers." Your blood runs cold. The office printers - from the heart of the plant?

You ask a few questions. It turns out that years ago someone connected the plant's control network to the office network to make it "easier to get the data". The supplier who did it is long gone. You walk to the main control computer your operators use, and ask your lead engineer for its password. He shrugs. "It was never changed from the factory default. It's been like that forever."

You look out over the line and the kilns - 200 people's livelihoods, and the timber a region is waiting to build with. If a single email virus reaches the office network, it could now find its way to the machines that run your whole plant. If they stop, the line stops, the workers go home, and the orders go unfilled. You are one click from a catastrophe, and you realise with a sickening certainty that nobody is watching the door.

A cyber attack could now stop my whole plant - the control systems are wide open and nobody owns their security; if it isn't IT and it isn't the engineers, who does?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to call the head of IT. "The plant network is wide open." He is sympathetic but firm: "Katlego, we can't touch that - that's OT, operational technology. Our team manages the business systems. If we changed something and your line shut down, we'd be liable. It's your system." A dead end. Next you think money can solve it - a vendor promises a "next-generation industrial firewall" for R450,000. But when you ask who will configure it and keep it updated for your twenty-year-old controllers, the salesman goes vague. It is a magic box with no owner, and a box without a person responsible for it is just expensive decoration. In desperation you consider the simplest fix: unplug the cable to the office. But finance pulls live energy data through that link for cost reports, and your biggest customer gets a live feed to meet their standards. Disconnecting completely would break contracts and leave you flying blind. The plant was connected for a reason; you cannot just go back.

It is not IT's job. An expensive box will not solve it. And you cannot unplug it. So before you read on: where is the real gap here - and what is the first move that actually closes it?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop looking at the technology and start looking at the empty chair. The problem is not the network cable; it is the OWNERSHIP VACUUM. The answer is not a product you buy; it is a person you name. Five moves:

  • Name an owner - the vacuum is the real risk: the breakthrough is to stop looking at the technology and start looking at the empty chair. The problem is not the network cable; it is the OWNERSHIP VACUUM. The control system's security fell into the gap between the IT team and the engineering team - and because it was 'everyone's' problem, it was no one's responsibility, so it quietly drifted open. The first and most important move is therefore not technical at all: name a single accountable owner for the plant's control-system security, in writing, as a real and resourced part of their job (say a quarter of a senior engineer's time, in their performance review). 'Everyone is responsible' means no one is; one named owner fills the vacuum.
  • Know what is connected - you can't protect what you can't see: the new owner's first task is to map reality: every controller, every network switch, every laptop that plugs in, every remote connection a supplier or vendor uses to dial in. This almost always surfaces forgotten doors - an old modem, a supplier's remote-access line nobody remembers, a link added years ago 'just to get data'. You cannot protect what you cannot see, and the most dangerous exposure is the connection no one knew was still there. A simple, honest map of what touches the control system is the foundation everything else stands on.
  • Separate the plant from the office - guard the few doors: with a clear map you can draw a boundary. The aim is not to unplug - the plant was connected for real reasons (live data finance and customers depend on) and you cannot go back. The aim is to SEPARATE the plant network from the office and the internet so a problem on one cannot freely reach the other, allowing only the exact data you need (a few readings) to cross through a single, guarded gateway. Bring a specialist in to help you build that boundary - not to sell you a box, but to control the few necessary doors so an office email virus can no longer wander into the machines that run the plant.
  • Get the basics right on the critical few: do not try to fix everything at once - concentrate on the few controllers whose loss is catastrophic (here, the main saw line and the kiln controllers). On those, get the boring basics right, because they stop the great majority of trouble: change every password that is still on a factory default; remove old and unused user accounts; and take a full backup of each critical control configuration, kept OFFLINE (printed and locked away, or on a disconnected drive) so you can rebuild even if the live system is lost. This is the same focusing discipline: the cheap, dull basics on the vital few beat expensive sophistication spread thin.
  • Assume a breach - rehearse running and stopping safely: you cannot promise nothing will ever get through, so build the muscle to survive it. Assume a breach will happen one day and rehearse the response: practise running the critical machines manually, or bringing the plant to a SAFE stop, with the control system assumed lost; test the call-out list (who do we phone, in what order?); confirm who holds the keys to any manual bypasses. A drill turns panic into a plan. Resilience is not hoping you are safe - it is knowing, because you have practised, that you can keep people safe and the plant under control even on the worst day.

See it transform Katlego's mill. A year on, the fear is gone, replaced by quiet confidence. One senior engineer now owns control-system security - in writing, a real slice of her job - so the vacuum is filled. She mapped everything and found two forgotten links, including a supplier's remote line nobody remembered. A specialist helped separate the plant network from the office, letting only the few needed readings cross one guarded gateway. On the critical few - the saw line and the kiln controllers - the default passwords are changed, old accounts removed, and a known-good backup sits offline in the safe. And the team has drilled the worst day: run the line by hand, or bring it to a safe stop, and the call-out list works. They are no longer hoping they are safe; they have a plan that makes them resilient. Security is not a product you buy; it is an owner you name and a few basic disciplines you keep. The real danger is not the clever attacker but the critical system nobody is responsible for - so fill the ownership vacuum first (one named, accountable owner), then map what is connected (you cannot protect what you cannot see), separate the plant from the office through a few guarded doors, get the boring basics right on the catastrophic few (passwords, unused access, an offline backup), and assume a breach so you have rehearsed how to keep people safe and the plant under control on the worst day.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This is not a new idea - you already do it to protect your own home.

See the same five moves at home

• Name an owner: you do not wait for the police to lock your doors - YOU own your home's security.
• Know what's connected: you know every door and window, and where the spare key is hidden.
• Separate: you give visitors the guest wi-fi password, not the one for the network holding your own devices and files.
• Get the basics right: you lock the front door, you do not leave the key in the lock, and you keep copies of important papers somewhere safe.
• Assume a breach: you know what you would do if you lost your keys or your wallet - who to call to cancel the cards, how to get a locksmith.

The principles are exactly the same. The only difference is that at the plant, the 'house' you are protecting serves the whole community.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

That dawning realisation - that a critical system is exposed and unowned - it is time to look for it in your own world. It might be the factory floor, the building's air conditioning, or the water pumps for your site. You do not need a big budget or a team of experts to start; you just need to start asking questions.

  1. Find the vacuum. Ask your IT manager, "who is responsible for the security of the plant's control systems?" Then ask your head of engineering the same. If they point at each other, you have found the gap.
  2. Propose an owner. The single most important step is to get one person named as the accountable owner - it could be you. A one-page proposal to your boss is enough to start.
  3. Map one critical system. Pick the one machine whose loss would be catastrophic and draw, simply, what it connects to. Is it on the office network? Does a supplier reach it remotely? Put it on paper.
  4. Ask about one password. For that same system, ask: "what is the password, and when was it last changed?" The answer tells you how deep the problem runs.

This is not really about 'cyber', and it was never a failing that the gap existed - control systems were built for an isolated world and then quietly connected, one reasonable convenience at a time, while no one was ever made responsible for defending them. It is about operational discipline and ownership: protecting these systems is not an IT task but a core part of making sure the plant can do its job, safely and reliably. It is how we keep the lights on, the water clean, and food on people's tables - it is about protecting people.
