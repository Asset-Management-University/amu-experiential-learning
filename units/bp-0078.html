Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Onele, the reliability engineer on a ferrochrome smelter's Furnace 4 rebuild - the R840-million project that is meant to lift the plant out of three bad years. It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday and you are standing on the casting floor; the night shift is hosing down, steam lifting off the wet concrete with that warm, metallic, burnt-dust smell that never quite leaves your overalls. In your hand is a single sheet, still warm from the printer: the draft Output Guarantee the commercial team wants you to sign off before the board meets on Thursday. One line is highlighted in yellow: "The rebuilt Furnace 4 system will deliver a production availability of no less than 92%, measured over any rolling twelve months." Ninety-two percent. Someone typed that number. You do not know where it came from.

Across the desk, the project director, Naledihle, is already in her coat, car keys in her fist. "I just need your name under that line," she says. "The export customer wants a hard availability figure in the contract. No figure, no contract. No contract, no rebuild. The whole business case rests on us shipping 92% of nameplate. You're the reliability person. Tell me it holds." Your stomach drops, because you know what 92% has to survive. Furnace 4 is not one machine. It is a CHAIN: raw ore and reductant come in on a feed system, go through the furnace itself, the molten product is tapped and handled by the casting line, then the cooling-and-crushing plant, then out - and running across all of it is the electrical supply and the water-cooling system. If any one of those drops, the furnace can stand idle while everything else runs perfectly.

You have the equipment list, and the numbers are real and checked: the feed system is up about 97%; the furnace transformer about 96%; the tapping-and-casting line about 95%; the cooling-and-crushing plant about 98%; the water-cooling circuit about 99%. Every single one is above 92%, so the commercial team looked at the list, saw nothing below 95%, and called 92% "conservative". Naledihle is half-convinced: "Each part is better than 92. So the whole thing is better than 92. Surely." But that does not feel right, and you cannot yet say why in words she will accept. Something about a chain - if the feed is down, it does not matter that the furnace is healthy; the losses do not sit politely side by side, they STACK.

And the list hides more. You have TWO water-cooling pumps, not one - if the duty pump trips, the standby cuts in, so the circuit barely notices; that should HELP the number. But the two pumps share ONE electrical board and ONE raw-water dam: if the board faults, both die together; if the dam runs dry, both are useless at once. Last February, both pumps stopped within the same hour - not because both broke, but because one thing they both depended on broke. And the repair times are nowhere on the sheet: a tripped pump that restarts in twenty minutes is a scratch; a furnace transformer that fails and takes eleven weeks to replace because it ships from Europe is season-ending. "Up 96%" hides whether the 4% missing is a thousand short blinks or one catastrophic eleven-week hole. "Just give me a number you believe," Naledihle says, opening the door. "Even a range. But I need it grounded in something, not a feeling."

They want me to sign that the furnace will run at 92% availability, and every sub-system is above 95% - so surely the whole thing clears 92% - but something about a chain is screaming at me not to put my name to that number; where do I start before it goes near the contract?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The five tidy percentages all sit comfortably above 92, and the pull is to agree. "Each part is above 92%, so the whole must be too - sign it." But the parts are in series, and series losses don't average, they stack; you can't reason a chain from its best link. "Take the lowest part, 95%, with a margin - call it 92%." But the whole chain finishes BELOW even the weakest single link, not above it, so 92% is already optimistic before you start. "We have two water pumps, so the water circuit is basically certain - tick it as safe." But two pumps that share one board and one dam are not two independent chances; last February they died together. So before you read on: reason out whether the whole-chain figure lands above or below 92% and why, and name the one question that turns "we have two pumps" from a comfort into a real, capped number.

The equipment list (real, checked): feed ~97%, transformer ~96%, casting ~95%, cooling/crushing ~98%, water ~99% - every one above 92%, so the commercial team saw nothing below 95% and called 92% 'conservative' ('each part is better than 92, so the whole is better than 92, surely'). But the losses don't sit side by side - they STACK. And the list hides two things: there are two water pumps (duty + standby) that should help, but they share one electrical board and one dam, so last February both stopped within the same hour from one shared cause; and repair times are nowhere - 'up 96%' hides whether the 4% missing is a thousand short blinks or one transformer that ships eleven weeks from Europe. Where do I start before any number goes near that contract?
The five tidy percentages are quietly lying to you, and you can't multiply your way out of it in your head. The sub-systems are in SERIES - the furnace makes product only when EVERY link works at once - and probabilities in series MULTIPLY, they do not average: 0.97 x 0.96 x 0.95 x 0.98 x 0.99 is about 0.86, roughly 86% - already BELOW the 92% someone typed, because five small 2-5% holes stack into a 14% hole. That is a reliability model, and its first rule is series links multiply, not average. Then handle the redundancy honestly: two parallel pumps look like 99.99% (both must fail by chance, 0.01 x 0.01 = 0.0001) - but they share one board and one dam, and a COMMON-CAUSE failure of that shared thing takes both at once, capping the real water availability nearer 99.3%; redundancy only helps to the extent the parts fail INDEPENDENTLY. And split every percentage into how-OFTEN it fails and how-LONG to recover - a transformer that fails rarely but takes eleven weeks (with no spare on site) is the real threat to steady monthly tonnes, even though it barely dents the annual figure. The real question is not 'is each part above 92%?' but 'what does the whole CHAIN come to once I multiply the series, cap the redundancy by what the backups share, and expose the rare-but-slow hole - and does the plant's own record agree?'

(2) CREDIT REDUNDANCY, THEN CAP IT - two parallel pumps look like 99.99% (both must fail by chance, 0.0001), but a COMMON-CAUSE failure (the shared board, the shared dam that took both last February) caps the real figure nearer 99.3%; redundancy only helps as far as the parts fail independently. (3) SPLIT EVERY PERCENTAGE INTO HOW-OFTEN x HOW-LONG - availability is roughly uptime / (uptime + repair); a transformer that fails rarely but takes eleven weeks from Europe with no on-site spare is the real threat to steady monthly output, hidden inside a comfortable 96%. (4) GROUND THE MODEL IN THE RECORD - set a success line first ('the model is fitting only if the plant's own history sits in the mid-to-high 80s, not the low 90s'), then pull the old furnace's three-year recorded availability (~84%, which agrees with the modelled 86%) and phone the supplier - who reveals the surprise: the worst-case transformer lead time is nine MONTHS, not eleven weeks, so the standby transformer is no longer a nice-to-have but make-or-break. (5) OFFER THE LEVER, NOT JUST THE NUMBER - don't sign 92%; give a defensible range (86-88%) checked against the record, name the single cheapest lever (a standby transformer on site lifts it to ~90-91% and shrinks the worst hole to two weeks), and promise only what you can prove (contract 88%, with the spare funded in the rebuild scope). A whole-system number is the product of its series links, capped by what its backups secretly share.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is how you got to a number you could actually sign - and, just as important, how you tested it against reality before it went anywhere near the contract. Five moves:

  • Model the chain, not the list: the first thing you do is correct the commercial team's instinct. They saw five numbers all above 92% and assumed the whole was above 92% too. But the sub-systems are in SERIES - the furnace only makes product when every link is working at once - and probabilities in series MULTIPLY, they do not average. So you take the five 'up' fractions and multiply them: feed 0.97 x transformer 0.96 x casting 0.95 x cooling/crushing 0.98 x water 0.99 = about 0.858, roughly 86%. You stare at it: every single part is above 95%, and the chain as a whole is 86% - already below the 92% someone typed into the contract. This is the heart of the trap - in a series chain, small losses STACK: five small 2-to-5% holes do not stay small, multiplied together they open a 14% hole, and 'surely it's better than 92' was exactly backwards. This multiplying-up of part reliabilities into one whole-system number is the technique you were missing - it is called building a reliability model of the system, and its first rule is: series links multiply, they do not average. The weakest single part sets a ceiling the whole can never beat, and the chain always finishes below even that.
  • Credit the redundancy, then cap it by what is shared: now the good news, and the trap inside it. The water-cooling circuit is not one pump but two in PARALLEL - duty and standby - and for the circuit to fail by chance, BOTH must be down at once: if each pump is down 1% of the time, the chance of both down at the same random moment is roughly 0.01 x 0.01 = 0.0001, about 0.01%, so on paper the water circuit is not 99% but 99.99%; parallel redundancy turns a 1-in-100 risk into a 1-in-10,000 one. That is the reward, and you fold it in - but you refuse the FULL reward, because you remember last February: both pumps stopped within the same hour, not two unlucky failures but ONE failure they shared. The pumps sit on one electrical board and draw from one dam; if the board faults, both die; if the dam runs dry, both are useless. A cause that takes out 'redundant' units together is a COMMON-CAUSE FAILURE, and it quietly cancels the redundancy you paid for. So the true risk is not the tiny 0.01% of two independent pumps failing together - it is dominated by the shared risks: how often that board faults, how often the dam fails. You dig those out (the board has a small but real fault rate, the dam has run critically low once in five drought-prone years), fold them in, and the water circuit's HONEST availability is not 99.99% but closer to 99.3% - still excellent, but the redundancy did not buy you four nines; the shared dam and board capped it. Redundancy only helps to the extent the redundant parts fail independently.
  • Split every percentage into how-often x how-long: now you attack the eleven-week problem, because a single availability percentage hides two completely different numbers: how OFTEN a thing fails (the average run between failures) and how LONG it takes to get back (the average repair time) - availability is roughly uptime divided by (uptime plus repair time). The transformer's 96% could be lots of quick trips, or one rare but brutal failure that takes eleven weeks because the spare ships from Europe. You pull the maintenance history and the spares strategy: the transformer fails RARELY, but when it does the repair is enormous, because there is NO spare on site. That single fact is the biggest threat to a STEADY MONTHLY output, even though it barely dents the annual percentage - the export customer does not just need 86% averaged over a year, they need to know you will not vanish for eleven weeks in March. So you give Naledihle two numbers behind every link, not one: a failure frequency AND a repair time. The same comfortable 96% can be a thousand twenty-minute scratches or one season-ending injury, and only the split tells you which - a rare, slow failure is the one that empties a month of tonnage while hiding inside a healthy-looking annual figure.
  • Ground the model in the record - and the surprise: here is the move that separates an honest engineer from a confident one: you have a number, about 86%, but a number from a model is only a belief about the real world, so before you carry it to Thursday you decide IN ADVANCE what you would have to see for the model to be trustworthy, and then find out by the cheapest honest means. Your success line, written before you check anything: 'this model is fitting if the plant's own history roughly agrees - if recorded production-availability over the last good run sits in the mid-to-high 80s, not the low 90s; if the real record is 92% or higher, my model is wrong and I must not sign 86%.' Then you find out the cheap way - you do NOT run an experiment on an R840-million furnace; you pull the OLD Furnace 4's actual recorded availability over its last three steady years from the plant historian, and you phone the transformer supplier to confirm one real fact, the genuine lead time on a replacement core. Two things come back, and one surprises you. The record lands at about 84% over three years - close to your modelled 86%, near enough to trust the METHOD; good, the model isn't fantasy. But the SURPRISE is the second check: you'd assumed the eleven-week transformer was your single worst hole - the supplier confirms eleven weeks, but only if a REFURBISHED core is in their pool; with a new-build core in the current market the honest lead time is closer to NINE MONTHS. Your 'season-ending injury' was, in the worst real case, a YEAR-ending one - the thing you were most confident about was the thing you'd most underestimated. That does not change the arithmetic (86% stands), but it changes what you must DO: a standby transformer on site is no longer a nice-to-have, it is the difference between a recoverable bad year and a contract you physically cannot honour - so you re-point the whole recommendation around it.
  • Offer the lever, not just the number: you roll it all up into an honest, reality-checked model: the base chain is ~86% and the plant's own three-year record (~84%) agrees; the parallel water pumps lift the water link but common-cause (board plus dam) caps the gain at ~99.3% not 99.99%; and the transformer's rare-but-slow failure is the single biggest swing, verified worse than the sheet implied. Your best estimate for whole-system production availability lands around 86-88%, NOT 92%, and it is fragile in one specific place: the transformer spare. So you do not sign 92%. Instead you give Naledihle three things. First, an honest figure with a range, checked against the record: 'whole-system availability is about 86-88%, not 92%; the plant's own three-year history says about 84%; here is the model behind it and the record that agrees.' Second, the single lever that changes the answer: 'if we keep a standby transformer on site, the worst hole shrinks from a verified nine months to about two weeks and the credible figure rises to roughly 90-91%; that one spare is worth far less than a single penalty event, and it is the difference between a contract we can honour and one we can't.' Third, a defendable promise: 'let's contract 88%, with the standby transformer funded in the rebuild scope - that we can prove, and it survives a drought and a transformer failure in the same year.' Naledihle does not get the comfortable 92% she walked in wanting, but she gets a number with a model under it, a real production record that backs it, a clear reason it is what it is, and one concrete investment that lifts it - a figure you can put your name to, and one the customer will still be receiving product under in March. (A failure of the unowned joints between these links, rather than the links themselves, is its own problem; managing the single constraint that caps throughput is its own problem.)

See where it lands. You do not sign 92%; you hand Naledihle an honest 86-88% with a model and a three-year record under it, one lever that lifts it (the standby transformer), and a promise of 88% you can actually prove. A defensible whole-system availability is not an average of its parts and is usually worse than the weakest of them: series links MULTIPLY their losses, so the chain finishes below even its best link; redundancy only helps as far as the redundant parts fail independently, because a shared cause cancels it; and a single rare-but-slow failure hides inside a comfortable percentage. So model the whole system honestly - multiply the series chain, cap the redundancy by what the backups secretly share, and split every availability into how-often times how-long - then test the number against the asset's own record and the real supplier facts, and only ever promise the range you can prove.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You have seen the model worked once, on a furnace. Now take one rep on a problem that looks nothing like a smelter, so you have to find the structure underneath, not copy the surface.

See the food-garden co-op with "two boreholes, so water's sorted"

Nokwanda runs a community food-garden co-op in a township outside town - fifteen families, a patch of land, and a small market stall they supply twice a week. No furnace, no R840-million budget, no off-take contract. Just vegetables that have to keep coming. The whole thing depends on a short chain: water, then seedlings, then a labour rota, then getting produce to market. If any one link drops, the stall stands empty that week, and the families who depend on the income feel it straight away. Nokwanda is proud of one thing in particular. "We won't run dry," she tells you. "We dug a second borehole last year. So now we have two. If one borehole has a problem, we just use the other. Water is the one thing I'm not worried about." It sounds exactly like the standby pump at the smelter, doesn't it? Two boreholes instead of one. Redundancy. The comfortable feeling that water is "sorted".

Think back to the furnace. Two water pumps looked safe too - right up until last February. The question that broke the comfort wasn't "do we have two?" It was: what do the two boreholes share? Ask it, and the comfort wobbles. Both boreholes are pumped by electric pumps, and both pumps run off the same little pump-house, on the same single power feed from the same pole at the gate. When the power to that pump-house goes - load-shedding, a tripped feed, a stolen cable, which on that line happens more than anyone likes - NEITHER borehole gives a drop. Two boreholes, but one thing they both depend on. It is the common-cause failure again, wearing different clothes: at the smelter, two pumps, one electrical board, one dam; here, two boreholes, one power feed - both failures coming from the same single cause. Nokwanda didn't really buy two independent water sources; she bought two boreholes that fail together the moment the shared power feed fails. And the second half of the lesson hides here too: "we have two boreholes" tells you nothing about how LONG an outage lasts - a two-hour load-shedding slot is a scratch, but a stolen feeder cable that takes the municipality three weeks to replace is her "eleven-week transformer", a rare, slow failure that empties the stall for a month no matter how many boreholes she has. So the cheapest lever almost writes itself: not a third borehole (more of the same shared weakness), but BREAKING the shared dependency - a small solar pump on one borehole, or a water tank holding three weeks' supply, so the two boreholes stop failing as one. The deep lesson: redundancy is never worth what it looks like on paper - its real value is only as strong as the weakest thing the backup and the main secretly share, and one rare, slow failure of that shared thing can wipe out a whole month no matter how many spares you count. Whether it is two pumps and a dam or two boreholes and a power feed, don't ask "how many do I have?" - ask "what do they share, how often does THAT fail, and how long does it take to come back?"

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The smelter case is about a furnace, but the problem-type is everywhere a system made of parts has to promise an output for the whole: you are asked to commit to a whole-system performance number - or to prove a proposed new system will hit one - and you only have numbers for the separate parts. Find it where you are:

  1. Find the chain, and multiply - don't average. Name a system you must answer for, and list the links that must all work at once. Get each link's "up most of the time" figure and multiply the series links together (never average). Is the whole-system number lower than every individual part - and by how much does that surprise the people who set the target?
  2. Test your redundancy honestly. Wherever you have a spare or backup, ask the uncomfortable question: what do the "redundant" parts secretly share - one power board, one fuel tank, one internet line, one key person? When did they last fail together? That shared thing is where your redundancy quietly disappears.
  3. Split every percentage into how-often and how-long. Take one important link: behind its availability number, is it many quick recoveries or one rare, slow disaster? Where is your "eleven-week transformer" - the failure that barely dents the annual percentage but would wipe out a whole month - and how far away is the spare?
  4. Find the one lever, and make a defensible promise. Which single change moves the whole-system number most for the least money, and what is it worth against the cost of missing the promise? Instead of one comfortable number someone typed, what range can you defend, with a model under it and the assumptions named?
R

Run the smallest test this week: take the single biggest assumption in your figure - most likely your slowest-to-replace part, or the "shared thing" behind your redundancy - and check it against reality by the cheapest honest means: pull your system's own recorded availability and compare it to your multiplied figure, and phone one supplier for one real lead time. You'll know it worked when your modelled number and your recorded history land within a few points of each other, and no real lead time comes back worse than you'd assumed; if either surprises you, you've found the number you must NOT sign yet. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Pressure-test the figure you would put your name to together.)

The arithmetic is the same as the smelter's - rolling part-by-part reliability up into one whole-system figure you can stand behind - but your world supplies the parts, the chain, the shared dependencies and the slow failures.
