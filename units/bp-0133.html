The paper is fiction and the practice just nearly hurt someone - which do I trust?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and on the office wall hangs the Delivery & Safety Checklist you wrote yourself years ago. You're proud of it - it's your promise to every family that a 9kg gas cylinder arrives and is connected safely. Then one afternoon a customer rings, her voice tight: she can smell gas. "The young man just left," she says, "and the smell is getting stronger." Your heart turns to ice. You talk her through shutting the cylinder off from a safe distance and opening the windows, and you drive straight there. You find it: a loose connection, slowly hissing into the kitchen. One twist with the right tool, and it's safe. A near-miss - a serious one.

Back at the depot, Leo - the stand-in covering for Sipho - is shaken. "I followed the checklist on the wall," he says. "Well... mostly. Step 4 says use the 'Model B' spanner, but there isn't one in the truck. And Sipho told me we don't really do the soapy-water leak check any more - it takes too long." You look at your proud checklist: it names a tool you stopped using a year ago. And your experienced drivers' faster real way had quietly dropped the one check that would have caught this. The paper is fiction; the practice has a dangerous hole.

The paper is out of date and the practice just nearly hurt someone - so which do I even trust, and how do I keep families safe?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to find someone to blame - "just follow the procedure I wrote, to the letter!" But you can't: the procedure is broken, it asks for a tool that doesn't exist, and the drivers would rightly ignore it. So you swing the other way: "fine - let's just write down what Sipho really does and call that the standard." But that would mean officially blessing the dropped leak check - signing a dangerous shortcut into the rules. Unthinkable. And blaming the people is plainly unfair when the system failed them.

You're stuck between enforcing a fiction and approving a risk. So before you read on: if the paper is wrong and the practice has a hidden hole, neither one alone can be trusted - where do you go to find the truth?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You can't trust the paper or the practice blindly - you must go to the work and build one source of truth that is both real and safe. Five moves:

  • See the gap for what it is: accept that the official written way and the real, unwritten way have drifted apart. This is not a sign of bad people - it's a broken feedback loop: the work changed, and the paper didn't. The real method works, but it lives only in a few skilled heads and hands, untaught and unchecked.
  • Go to where the work is done: you won't find the truth at your desk or in your memory of how things 'should' be. Put on your boots and ride along: sit in the passenger seat and watch your skilled people actually do the job. Your only task is to understand - 'show me how you do that; why that way?' - and capture the real, living method.
  • Sort improvements from dangerous drift: compare the real way to the paper, difference by difference, asking WHY each time. A genuine improvement (a smarter, safer way someone worked out) - keep it. A dropped safety step - dig deeper: was it pointless, or was it made impossible? ('The spray bottles broke months ago and were never replaced, and we're measured on deliveries an hour - there was never time.') That's not laziness; that's a system that broke the step.
  • Rebuild one true and safe standard: write the single new checklist that keeps the real improvements AND restores every genuine safety step - and fix what broke it: put the tool back in every vehicle, build the time for the check into the standard. The new procedure is now both true to reality and safe by design - one source of truth people can actually follow.
  • Prove it, and keep the loop open: don't just pin it up - hand it to a newcomer and have them follow it cold; if they can do it, it's real. Then add a standing way for the people doing the work to flag anything wrong ('this step can't be done - tell me'), so they become the guardians of the procedure and paper and practice can never silently part again.

Watch how it resolves. You ride along with Sipho and ask why at every turn. His smarter way of strapping the cylinders so they don't rattle? A genuine improvement - keep it. The dropped leak check? "Boss, the spray bottles broke months ago and were never replaced - and we're judged on deliveries an hour, so there was never time." That's the key: not laziness, a system that made the safe way impossible. So you rebuild ONE checklist - Sipho's improvement kept, the soapy-water check restored - and you fix what broke it: new spray bottles in every truck, and ninety seconds added to the standard delivery time so the check can actually be done. Then you hand it to Leo to follow cold (he does, perfectly), and add a box at the bottom: "anything wrong with this - tell Muhammad." The truth was never on the wall or only in Sipho's hands - it was out where the work happens; your job was to go there, and build a standard that is both true and safe.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This gap - the official way drifting from the real way - lives in every workplace. Watch it on a hospital ward.

See the same trap (and fix) on a hospital ward

The official protocol for changing a dressing is a 20-step form nobody uses; the experienced nurses have a faster, real-world custom. But their custom has quietly dropped one step - checking the patient's wristband against the chart before applying a medicated cream - because the ward is short-staffed and it "seems small". A junior nurse, following the custom, almost applies a cream to a patient allergic to it; a senior catches it just in time. A near-miss.

The head nurse blames no one. She goes to the work: watches a real dressing change, feels the time pressure, sees the risk. With her team she rebuilds a short, practical, wipe-clean card - fast enough to actually use, with the non-negotiable step restored: "check wristband against chart." She has closed the gap - the new way is both true to the real ward and safe by design.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of a procedure on a wall, a checklist on a server, or a rule in a handbook that nobody actually follows - a job really done from memory and "the way we've always done it". That gap between the paper and the practice is a risk waiting to happen. Don't trust the paper; don't blindly trust the practice; go to where the work is done:

  1. Go and watch. See how the job is really done, by the people who know it best - and capture the good of it.
  2. Ask 'why', and sort. For each difference, separate genuine improvements (keep them) from dangerous drift - and look for the system cause of any dropped step (a missing tool, no time, a broken form).
  3. Rebuild one true-and-safe standard, then prove it. Write the single version that keeps the real improvements and restores every safety step - and provide the tool and time it needs. Have a newcomer follow it cold; if they can, it's real. Then keep a loop open so the people doing the work can flag drift before it bites.

A procedure is not bureaucracy - it is a promise: that the work will be done with care, and that the people it touches will be kept safe. When the paper and the practice drift apart, that promise quietly becomes a lie, and one day a stand-in or a tired hand pays for it. You don't fix it from a desk by force, or by blessing every shortcut. You fix it by going to where the work truly lives, honouring the skill you find there, restoring what keeps people safe, and writing it down as one truth everyone can follow. Go and make the promise real again.
