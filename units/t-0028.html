Step 1 of 5

You keep telling me these pumps last four years on average - but this one died at eleven months, and 62 of my 120 are still running. How do I answer without throwing away more than half my fleet?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a Monday at the Dinaledi Colliery, a coal mine that runs a fleet of 120 slurry dewatering pumps - they pump the dirty, gritty water out of the underground sumps, and when one fails, that section floods, production stops, and people stand idle. You are Phetogo, the reliability engineer, in the reliability office: a portable cabin, the kettle not boiled, the air thick with diesel and wet coal dust off the night shift's overalls.

In your hand is a printed failure log - one row per pump, 120 rows, coffee-ringed at the corner. The section foreman, Tholwana, leans in the doorway, arms folded, jaw tight. Last night Pump 47 failed in 3 South, the sump overflowed, and the mine lost five hours of production worth about R180,000.

"You keep telling me these pumps last four years on average," Tholwana says. "Pump 47 was eleven months old. The one before it was fourteen months. Average is no use to me. I need to know which pumps are about to go, and why."

You look at the log, and the four-year figure is not a lie exactly - it is worse than a lie, because it is arithmetic. It is the true average of the pumps that have died. And 62 of the 120 are still running right now. They have not failed, so they have no lifespan to average, so every calculation this department has ever done has quietly left them out. More than half the fleet, deleted, to produce a tidy number that failed Tholwana last night.

More than half my fleet has not failed yet, so it never appears in any figure I produce. How do I get the truth out of a history that is only half finished?

Before you read on, answer Tholwana. One hundred and twenty pumps, 58 with a failure age, 62 still running with no failure age at all. Write down how you would work out what these pumps really do, and which one to look at next - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the three answers, and what each one costs you

• "Average the 58 that failed." This is what has been happening, and it is arithmetically flawless. It also answers a question nobody asked: how long did the pumps that died last? The 62 still running are exactly the pumps doing well, so removing them makes the fleet look worse - and any figure that gets better as more pumps survive is measuring the wrong thing.

• "Wait until they have all failed, then the data will be complete." Perfectly honest, and it is what a lot of reporting quietly does by simply never mentioning the runners. It costs several years and, by the time it arrives, describes a fleet that has been replaced.

• "Assume the 62 will last about as long as the others did." The most reasonable-sounding of the three, and it decides the answer before it starts - you have assumed the very thing you were trying to find out, and the number will faithfully agree with your assumption.

• Notice what is common to all three. Each one treats a pump that has not failed as a blank. But a pump that has run 26 months and is still running is not missing information - it is a fact: this pump made it past 26 months. That is a real, usable statement about how long these pumps last, and it is sitting in 62 of your 120 rows. The whole method is built on refusing to throw it away.

T2: The Tool in Action

Give every pump two facts - how long you have watched it, and whether it failed or is still going. Then step down a curve at each failure while letting the still-running ones quietly reduce the pool they were part of. What comes out describes all 120 pumps, not 58 of them. Watch it run on the fleet.

Step 1 - Lay out the data with a censoring flag

Each of the 120 pumps gets two columns - months observed, and status (1 = failed, 0 = still running/censored). Across the fleet, 58 pumps failed and 62 are still running (censored). That is exactly why a plain average is wrong: it would ignore the 62, more than half the data. Pump 47 failed at 11 months (status 1); Pump 63 has run 26 months and counting (status 0).

Step 2 - Draw the survival curve (Kaplan-Meier)

At month 9: 120 at risk, 1 fails -> 119/120 = 0.992. At month 11: 119 at risk, 2 fail -> 0.992 x (117/119) = 0.975. At month 14: 117 at risk, 1 fails -> 0.975 x (116/117) = 0.967. Carried to the end: survival at 12 months = 0.94 (6% fail in their first year), at 24 months = 0.71, and the median life is 38 months (where survival drops through 0.50). The honest headline is not 'four years average' but '6% die in the first year, and half are gone by 38 months' - and Pump 47 at 11 months sits inside that early-failure 6%, unusual but not impossible.

Step 3 - Find what drives the risk, and rank the pumps still running

Phetogo adds three covariates - make (A or B), sump water pH, duty hours per day - and fits the model. The hazard ratios: Make B vs A = 1.9 (fails about 90% more often), water pH below 5.5 = 2.4 (acidic sumps more than double the risk), each extra duty hour/day = 1.08 (about 8% more). The pH assumption passes its residual check. Ranking the 62 running pumps by combined risk, Pump 19 (Make B, pH 5.1, 20 duty hours) tops the list - that is the pump to inspect next, not the oldest.

Step 4 - Turn the curve and the ranking into what the foreman asked for

Tholwana asked two things: which pumps are about to go, and why. The curve answers neither on its own - it describes the fleet, not any one pump - and the ranking answers neither on its own either, because a hazard ratio says what multiplies risk, not what a pump will do. Together they say something a foreman can use at 06:40: half this fleet is gone by 38 months, so an eleven-month failure is unusual but well inside what this fleet does; acidic sump water more than doubles the risk of failing; and of the 62 pumps still running, number 19 carries the most of that risk - not the oldest pump, which is what anyone would have opened first. Phetogo also says the part that is easy to leave out: this ranking is a place to look, not a prediction, and the pH finding needs confirming at the sump before anyone spends money on it.

See what refusing to delete the survivors bought. The mean said four years; the curve says 6 per cent die inside their first year and half the fleet is gone by 38 months - which is a different fleet, and the one Tholwana actually has. Pump 47's eleven months stops being an outrage and becomes what it really was: unusual, and well inside what this fleet does. And the ranking points at Pump 19 rather than the oldest pump, which is what anyone in that cabin would have opened first. There is integrity in what this method refuses to throw away - the 62 pumps still turning are not blanks in a spreadsheet, they are the half of the truth that a tidy average deletes to look neat.

T3: The Blueprint

Four moves, and the first one is the whole idea.

  • 1. Two columns for every asset - how long observed, and whether that ended in failure. The still-running ones stay in.
  • 2. Step the curve down at each failure, letting the still-running assets shrink the pool without a step. Read the halfway point, not the average.
  • 3. Add the factors you suspect and see what each multiplies the risk by. Rank the assets still running.
  • 4. Answer the actual question - which one to look at, and what to confirm before spending.

This family of methods is survival analysis, and now that you have run it its vocabulary is just names for what you did. An asset still running is censored: you know it lasted at least this long and not its full life, and the entire field exists to use that properly rather than discard it. The stepped curve is the Kaplan-Meier estimator, which drops at each failure by the share of the at-risk pool that failed, while a censored asset leaves the pool without causing a drop - which is exactly how it stays in the sum without pretending to have died. The halfway point you read off it is the median life, and it is the honest headline because it survives the early deaths that the mean flattens away. The factors you added are covariates, the model that weighs them is Cox proportional hazards, and what it gives back for each factor is a hazard ratio - 2.4 means the risk of failing at any moment is multiplied by 2.4 when that factor is present. It holds only while one thing stays true, and it is worth seeing what happens when it does not. Suppose the acidic water attacked seals hard in the first year, but any seal that survived that year had effectively proved itself and was no worse off afterwards. Then the acid does not multiply the risk by 2.4 throughout - it multiplies it enormously early and barely at all later, and a single number claiming to cover the whole life would send you to inspect the wrong pumps. That constancy over time is the proportional-hazards assumption, and checking it is how you find out whether one ratio is honestly describing the whole story or averaging away two different ones.

It fits anywhere you have times-until-something with a live population still running - equipment, contracts, customers, employees, patients, tenancies. It is the wrong instrument when you have only counts and no times, and it ranks rather than proves: a hazard ratio tells you where to look, never what caused what.

Two disciplines keep the answer honest. First, keep the survivors, and quote the median rather than the mean - the assets still running are the half of your evidence that the usual average silently deletes, and the giveaway is that such an average gets worse as more of your fleet survives. "Half are gone by 38 months" is a sentence a foreman can plan against; "four years on average" is the sentence that failed him. Second, say plainly that a ranking is a place to look, not a prophecy. A hazard ratio of 2.4 on acidic water tells you where to spend your next hour, and it does not establish that acid is the cause; go to the sump, confirm it, and then spend. The method's authority comes from its honesty about the survivors, and it is lost the moment you let a ranking be quoted as a prediction.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your analysis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Keep the survivors. An asset still running is censored data, not a blank. Delete them and you have measured only the ones that died - and made your fleet look worse the better it performs.

• Read the median, not the mean. "Four years average" hides the early deaths that hurt. "Half are gone by 38 months" is the honest headline, and the early part of the curve is where the pain actually lives.

• A hazard ratio ranks; it does not prove. It says where to look first. Confirm the cause on the ground before anyone spends money on it.

• Do not assume the runners will behave like the dead. That assumption decides the answer before the analysis starts, and the numbers will agree with it obediently.

• Watch for the assets that left for other reasons. A pump removed in a refurbishment did not fail - it is censored too, and counting it as a failure is the same error in reverse.

• The curve describes the fleet, not the pump. It cannot tell you that pump 19 will fail. It tells you where the risk is concentrated, which is a different and more useful kind of help.

You came to the cabin at 06:40 with a coffee-ringed log, a four-year average that had just failed a foreman, and 62 pumps that no calculation in this department had ever counted. You can leave with the fleet as it actually is: 6 per cent gone inside a year, half gone by 38 months, acidic sump water carrying more than twice the risk, and Pump 19 - not the oldest one - as the pump to open next, said out loud as a place to look rather than a prophecy. This is what it means to refuse to throw away more than half your evidence for the sake of a tidy number. Down in 3 South, it is simpler than that: the section floods less often, and nobody stands idle for five hours on a Monday. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
