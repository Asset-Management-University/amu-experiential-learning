Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Ibraaheem. It is a Tuesday morning at the Tank You filling yard, the roller door half up, the faint sharp tang of LPG that never quite leaves the place, the slow hiss of the filling line as Sicelo works the cylinders one by one. You and Muhammad now run three delivery vans, the filling plant, and four staff: Sicelo on the line, two drivers - Sipho and Thabo - and Noma who takes the orders. Last month you filled and delivered 1,840 cylinders. The business is real now.

In your hand is a single sheet: a letter from the gas regulator. A routine inspection found two pressure cylinders in your stock past their requalification date - the 10-year safety re-test that every LPG cylinder must pass. You did not catch it. Nobody did. The letter gives you 14 days to prove that someone competent is checking cylinder integrity, or your filling licence is suspended. You feel the cold drop in your stomach: a suspended licence means no filling, no deliveries - by your own quick sum, the better part of half a million rand of revenue gone in the first month (1,840 cylinders at your price) - and customers who phone the next supplier and never come back.

You walk over to Sicelo. He is good - fast, careful, never drops a cylinder, never spills. You ask: "How do we check if a cylinder is past its re-test date?" He looks at you, honestly puzzled, and shrugs. "I fill them, boss. I check for dents and rust, like you showed me. The date stamp - I didn't know that was a thing we watch." And he is right. You never showed him. You never showed anyone, because two years ago when it was just you and Muhammad and your parents' car, you carried all of that in your own heads - the dents, the rust, the smell of a leak. But you never wrote down what a person on this line actually needs to know and be able to do: not just "fill cylinders" but the full list - read a date stamp, judge corrosion, spot a damaged valve, know when to reject a tank.

You look at Noma taking an order, at Sipho and Thabo loading a van, and realise you hired four people for what you CALL their jobs - "filler", "driver", "order-taker" - but never once wrote down the actual skills each job needs to keep people safe and the licence valid. You hired for warm bodies and good attitudes, and hoped the knowledge would rub off. And next year you want a fourth van and a night shift. More people, more cylinders, more date stamps nobody is watching. Muhammad phones: "What do we do - send Sicelo on a course? Hire someone who knows compliance? Just check the dates ourselves this month and hope?" Three easy answers, and none of them feels like it fixes the real thing.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The quick fix is tempting - book Sicelo on a one-day course, or hire a "compliance person", and move on. "Send Sicelo on a gas-safety course." But which skills does the course teach, and are they the ones you're actually missing? You'd be buying a bundle and hoping the right thing is inside it. "Hire a compliance person." But a whole new salary, every month forever, on a hunch that you're a whole person short - when you don't yet know whether the gap is one skill or thirty. "Just check the dates ourselves this month and hope." But that fixes this month and not the cause; next year's night shift inherits the same blind spot, and the next letter is already on its way. Each answer spends money before you know what you're buying.

So before you read on: resist the quick fix. Name what you've actually been hiring for all this time (a word that isn't "skills"), and the one thing you'd have to write down before you could spend a single rand wisely.

Three easy answers pull at you - send Sicelo on a course, hire a 'compliance person', or just check the dates yourselves this month and hope - and none fixes the real thing. The real thing is that you hired for job TITLES ('filler', 'driver') and never named what those jobs actually require a person to be able to DO. So before you spend, set a success line out loud so reality, not your hunch, decides: 'my hunch is we're missing a whole person - a compliance officer; if that's right the gap will be everywhere and the answer is hire; but if the gap is small and specific, a list will show it and the fix is cheaper.' Then build the smallest instrument that makes the invisible visible: write down what the filling-line ROLE actually requires - not 'fill cylinders' but the real list (read and interpret the date stamp and work out the re-test due date; judge corrosion, cosmetic vs reject; inspect the valve and collar; operate the line safely and spot an overfill; detect a leak by smell and soapy water; know the legal rule that a cylinder past its date may NOT be filled). That list of specific things a person must know and be able to do is a COMPETENCE; the whole list for a role is a COMPETENCE REQUIREMENT. You have named JOBS; you have never named COMPETENCES. The real question is not 'course or hire?' but 'what exactly does this role need a person to be able to do, and who currently can?'
Five moves turn a regulator's scramble into a plan. (1) DEFINE THE ROLE BY ITS REQUIRED ACTIONS - list what the person must actually DO to be safe, legal and useful, ignoring the title. (2) NAME THE COMPETENCE FOR EACH ACTION - the specific knowledge and ability behind each task ('fill cylinders' becomes 'read a date stamp and work out the re-test due date'); the whole list is the role's competence requirement. (3) ASSESS YOUR PEOPLE AGAINST THE STANDARD - a simple grid, competences down the side, people across the top, marked honestly Yes / Partly / No (a 'Partly' that should be a 'No' is how the next letter arrives). (4) TARGET THE GAPS WITH A PRECISION FIX - aim spend only at the No's. Here the grid surprises Ibraaheem: Sicelo is strong or fine on four of the six competences; the whole crisis is just TWO (read the date stamp, know the no-fill rule), not a missing person. So a R18,000-a-month compliance hire (R216,000 a year) would buy 30 skills to fix 2 - the wrong tool; the aimed fix is R2,500 for a half-day with a requalification assessor teaching exactly those two, plus a one-page check sheet every cylinder now passes through. R2,500, once - licence saved. (5) EMBED IT AS A SYSTEM - a competence requirement for every role, revisited when technology or rules change, so you find your gaps on a page in time to act, not through a regulator's letter, a breakdown or a resignation. A list of job titles is not a list of capabilities.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Ibraaheem did not book the course. Not yet. He did something slower and, it turned out, far more powerful: before he spent a rand, he set a line to test against - "if I'm really a whole person short, the gap will be everywhere; if it's small and specific, a list will show it" - and then he built the smallest instrument that would make the invisible visible. Five moves:

  • Define the role by its required actions, not its title: Ibraaheem did not book the course. First he set a line to test against, said out loud so reality not his hunch would decide: 'my hunch is we are missing a whole person, a compliance officer; if that is right the gap will be everywhere and the answer is hire; but if it is small and specific, a list will show it and the fix is cheaper.' Then he built the smallest instrument that would make the invisible visible: a blank page on which he wrote what the filling-line ROLE actually requires a person to be able to DO - not 'fill cylinders', the title, but the real tasks behind it. Because that is the root of the whole crisis: he had hired four people for what he CALLS their jobs ('filler', 'driver', 'order-taker') and never once written down the actual things each job needs a person to do to keep people safe and the licence valid - he hired warm bodies and good attitudes and hoped the knowledge would rub off. A title names a slot; two people with the same title can differ completely in what they can actually do. So you start by ignoring the title and listing the actions: the honest list, kept going until it feels true, not until it feels short.
  • Name the competence for each action: now turn each action into the specific thing a person must KNOW and be ABLE TO DO to perform it safely and well - that is a COMPETENCE, and the whole list for a role is its COMPETENCE REQUIREMENT. For the filling line, Ibraaheem, Muhammad and Sicelo wrote a short, honest list: read and interpret the date stamp and work out the requalification due date; judge surface corrosion (when rust is cosmetic and when it means reject); inspect the valve and collar for damage; operate the line safely and spot an overfill; detect a leak by smell and the soapy-water test; and know the legal rule that a cylinder past its re-test date may NOT be filled, full stop. Notice the shift: 'fill cylinders' is a job; 'read a date stamp and work out the re-test due date' is a competence - a thing you can name, demonstrate, teach and check. Naming competences is the move Ibraaheem had never made; he had named jobs, and a job name is a box you cannot inspect. A competence is a box you can.
  • Assess your people honestly against the standard: the first half names what the role NEEDS; the second half checks what your people HAVE - a COMPETENCE ASSESSMENT, comparing the two, one competence at a time. Build the simplest grid: the competences down the side, your people across the top, and against each, mark honestly - Yes, Partly, or No. Do not be kind: a 'Partly' that should be a 'No' is exactly how the next regulator's letter arrives. The grid for the filling line was uncomfortable and clear - Sicelo strong on operating the line, spotting dents and rust, leak detection; partly on the valve inspection; and a flat NO on reading the date stamp and on knowing the no-fill rule. Written down, the gap stops being a vague worry and becomes two named, visible, fixable things. This is the whole power of the assessment: a skill held only in one person's head, or missing entirely, is invisible until you ask the question against a named standard - and an unasked question is a gap you will meet through failure instead of on paper.
  • Target the gaps with a precision fix: here reality surprised Ibraaheem and changed his plan, which is exactly why he set the success line first. He had walked in certain he was a whole person short, ready to commit to an R18,000-a-month compliance hire - but the grid would not lie: Sicelo was strong or fine on FOUR of the six competences, and the gap was just TWO, read-the-date-stamp and know-the-no-fill-rule. The whole crisis came from two items on a list nobody had written down - not a missing person, not a missing course. His hunch had failed his own test, and it was a good thing he checked before signing an employment contract. Now the spending was easy because it was AIMED: a full compliance hire (about R18,000 a month, R216,000 a year) would have been buying thirty skills to fix two - the wrong tool; a generic one-day gas-safety course (R2,500) covered some of it but not the date-stamp arithmetic specifically; what they did was spend R2,500 on a half-day with a requalification assessor who taught Sicelo exactly those two competences, plus wrote a one-page check sheet (date stamp, due date, reject rule) that every cylinder now passes through. R2,500 and one afternoon; licence saved. That is the case for naming the gap before you spend: you stop paying for skills you already have, and you buy only the ones you lack.
  • Embed it as a system - find gaps on paper, not through failure: the real win was never the R2,500 - it was that Ibraaheem now had a competence requirement for EVERY role in the business. When he hires the night-shift filler next year, he will not hire a warm body and hope; he will hire and train against the named list. When a new technology arrives - the smart cylinder valves the supplier keeps mentioning - he will ask first 'what new competences will that need, and who has them?' and find the gap on paper, before it is found in a regulator's letter. And he made the fix visible rather than assumed: for a month, every cylinder leaving the line carried a signed check-sheet row for the date stamp and the no-fill rule, and a spot-check of ten random cylinders four weeks later found ten clean date stamps and ten signed rows - the gap that nearly cost the licence now closed AND visible, not held in anyone's head. The honest trade-off is real: writing the competence requirements took two evenings of arguing about what 'good enough' means for each task, and it feels like paperwork when there are vans to load - but the alternative is the one they nearly lived, discovering the capability gap THROUGH FAILURE, one expensive surprise at a time. Naming the competences turns a reactive scramble into a plan you can hire, train and budget against.

Your assets demand specific competences - safety, a licence, a working machine - so name what each role must actually be able to do, assess your people honestly against that standard (Yes, Partly, No), and aim your spend only at the gaps you find. An organisation that names and assesses its competences finds its skill gaps on a page, in time to act - instead of through a regulator's letter, a breakdown, or a resignation. Name the competence, not the job; check who holds it; close the gap on purpose.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know the clean version. The disguise is harder - because in real life the gap usually wears a costume of order.

See the family stokvel whose neat wall-list hides the gap

A family stokvel: twelve households put money in every month; once a year they pay out and reinvest some in a small chicken-and-egg operation they run together. It feels nothing like a gas plant - no licence, no regulator, no machinery. And here is the twist: they already have a list. Pinned to the kitchen wall is a sheet headed "Who does what" - Aunt Aphiwe: money. Cousin Mbuso: feed and birds. Gogo: the books. Sized, signed, sorted. So this can't be the same problem... can it?

Look at what the list actually says. "Aunt Aphiwe: money." Is "money" a task someone must be able to DO, or is it a title - like "filler" or "driver"? It is a title. Underneath "money" sits a real list nobody has written: reconcile what came in against what was promised; chase a late member; keep the bank slips; work out each year's payout; spot when the numbers don't add up. The family did step one of Ibraaheem's recipe - they named PEOPLE - and stopped; the wall sheet looks like the work is done, and that is the disguise. A list of names feels like safety and hides the same gap a blank page would. Now ask the second question: take the competence hidden under "money" - working out the payout, reconciling the books - and ask who ELSE can do it. Nobody. The whole stokvel's trust sits on one person, and if she is in hospital at payout time, it collapses. The list said the role was covered; it never said the competence was held by only one person, because it never named the competence, so it could never be assessed - the honest Yes / Partly / No that Ibraaheem did and the family skipped. So they do what you now know how to do: turn "money" into its real tasks, each task into a competence, and mark who can do each. The grid shows three competences with one Yes each and No's everywhere else. The fix is cheap and aimed: Aunt Aphiwe spends two Sundays teaching a younger cousin to reconcile the books. No course, no hire, the gap closed on paper, in time. A list of names is not a list of competences, and an unassessed list still hides the gap - so when something already looks organised, ask not "is there a list?" but "does it name what each person must be able to DO, and have we honestly checked who else can do it?"

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This same problem-type lives almost everywhere people look after important things and the knowledge sits in someone's head instead of on a page. The trap is always the same: you hire and train by job title, the real competences a role needs are never written down, and the gap is discovered through a failure - a breakdown, an audit, a resignation, a near-miss. Find it where you are:

  1. Pick one role in your organisation, team or household where someone "just knows how to do it". Write down, task by task, what that person actually needs to know and be able to do. How long is the real list - and is any of it written down anywhere?
  2. Name the one competence in that list that, if the person left tomorrow, nobody else could do. What would it cost to discover that gap the hard way - through failure - instead of on paper today?
  3. Think about a change coming - a new tool, system, rule or technology. What new competences will it demand, and who currently has them? Are you planning for that gap, or will it find you?
  4. Spot where you hire or train for a title rather than a named list of competences. What is the most reactive part of your skills planning right now - and what would make it one item more deliberate?
R

Build the grid for one critical role, count the empty cells, and name the cheapest aimed fix for each "No". (Working with an AMU facilitator? Map the competence requirement for one of your real roles and assess your people against it together.)

The world changes - gas cylinders, a delivery business, a hospital ward, a school, a server room, a water plant - but the problem-type is identical: capability requirements go unnamed, people are matched to titles not competences, and the gap is found through failure. The fix is always the same shape: name what each role must be able to do, compare it honestly to who you have, and close the gap on purpose - on paper, in time.
