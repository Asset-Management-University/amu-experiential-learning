Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tebatso, the engineering manager at a speciality-chemicals plant. It is 06:40 on a Tuesday in the control room; the air smells faintly of stale coffee and warm electronics. Behind the glass, the night-shift operator, Hlengiwe, is rubbing her eyes - her shift ends in twenty minutes and she has been watching Reactor 3 climb all night. Reactor 3 runs an exothermic reaction: it makes its own heat, and if that heat runs away, the pressure climbs fast. Standing between a normal day and a vapour cloud over the highway is one thing - an automatic system that watches the temperature and pressure, and if they cross the line, slams the feed valves shut and dumps the batch to a safe quench tank. No human in the loop. It must work on its own, every single time.

On the desk in front of you is a thin, blue lever-arch file. The cover says SIS Verification Pack - Reactor 3 - Due for Audit. The external auditor, a precise man from your insurer, arrives at 09:00, and he wants two things from you in writing. First: proof that this shut-down system is reliable ENOUGH. Your licence-to-operate, and your insurance, both demand that this safety function carries a specific rated level of protection - the kind of level where the system is allowed to fail dangerously, on average, less than once in a thousand demands. You open the file. There are wiring diagrams, a sensor datasheet, a maintenance log with a six-month gap in it, and a single faded certificate from the original installer dated to when it was put in fourteen years ago. There is no calculation. Nobody has ever added up the failure rates of the actual sensors, logic box and valves as they are installed today and shown they still hit the number. You realise, with a cold feeling in your stomach, that you have been ASSUMING the rating, not PROVING it.

Second: the auditor emailed last night asking a new question, one that was never on the old audit. "Please confirm," he wrote, "that the safety controller cannot be reached or altered from your business network or the internet." You walk to the cabinet and look. The safety logic box has a network cable. It runs into the same switch as the plant's general network - the one that connects to the office, to the maintenance laptops, to the supplier who dials in remotely to update the recipes. Last month a contractor plugged a USB stick into the engineering workstation to load a software patch. Nobody logged it. Nobody scanned it.

Your phone buzzes. It is the plant manager, Mpilo: "Auditor at 9. Are we covered? Board wants a yes." On the wall, the trend line for Reactor 3 ticks up another half a degree. Hlengiwe looks over. "Sir," she says quietly, "if that thing trips today - does it actually still work? It's never tripped on a real run since I started." You have two hours and twenty minutes. The temptation is loud and simple: write "system meets required integrity level, isolated from external networks" on the cover sheet, sign it, and hand it over at nine. It would be true ENOUGH, probably. It has run for fourteen years without a disaster. But you cannot show the calculation. And you cannot honestly say the controller is cut off from the outside world.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The board wants a yes and it has run fourteen years without a disaster. "Sign it - 'meets the integrity level, isolated from external networks' is true enough." But you cannot show the calculation and you cannot honestly say the controller is cut off; a signature you cannot back up is a lie that protects no one. "It's reliable, so the network risk does not matter - focus on the SIL." But a barrier anyone can quietly switch off is not a barrier, however reliable the parts; a perfect SIL number on a reachable controller is meaningless. "The network is now the priority - lock it down and the reliability looks after itself." But a perfectly locked-down box whose valves no longer close on demand is exactly as deadly; securing it proves nothing about whether it still trips. So before you read on: name why the auditor's two questions are really ONE problem, and the two separate things you must do before you can honestly sign anything.

I am Tebatso, the engineering manager at a speciality-chemicals plant. It is 06:40 and the night operator, Hlengiwe, has watched Reactor 3 climb all night. Reactor 3 runs an exothermic reaction - it makes its own heat, and if that heat runs away the pressure climbs fast. The only thing between a normal day and a vapour cloud over the highway is an automatic system: it watches temperature and pressure, and if they cross the line it slams the feed valves shut and dumps the batch to a quench tank, with no human in the loop. It must work on its own, every time. On my desk is the SIS Verification Pack, due for audit at 09:00. The auditor wants two things in writing. First: proof this shut-down is reliable ENOUGH - our licence and insurance demand a rated level of protection, the kind where it is allowed to fail dangerously less than once in a thousand demands. But the file has only wiring diagrams, a datasheet, a maintenance log with a six-month gap, and a faded installer certificate from fourteen years ago. There is NO calculation. Nobody has ever added up the failure rates of the actual sensors, logic box and valves as installed today and shown they still hit the number - I have been ASSUMING the rating, not proving it. Second: he asks me to confirm the safety controller cannot be reached or altered from our business network or the internet. I look: the logic box has a network cable into the same switch as the office, the maintenance laptops, and the supplier who dials in to update recipes; last month a contractor plugged an unlogged USB stick into the workstation. The plant manager, Mpilo, texts: the board wants a yes. Do I sign?
The auditor's two questions look different - 'is it reliable enough?' and 'is it protected from attackers?' - but run the thought-experiments: (a) the box is perfectly reliable, but someone quietly switches it off - is the plant safe? No. (b) the box is perfectly locked down, but the valves no longer close on demand - is the plant safe? No. Both failures end in the same place: the valves do not do their safety job. So functional safety and OT cybersecurity are the SAME governance problem wearing two hats - can this last barrier be trusted to ACT when it must? You cannot answer 'yes' to one and 'we do not know' to the other and still claim the plant is safe. That means you do two things, not one. PROVE the integrity: the shut-down is a safety-instrumented system carrying a Safety Integrity Level (a reliability band) - but a SIL is a living property, not a birth certificate; do not trust the fourteen-year-old paper, recalculate the probability of failure on demand on the function AS INSTALLED TODAY (the real parts, the real proof-test interval, any bypass currently sitting on it) and check it against the historian and the maintenance log. DEFEND the integrity: list every path that can reach and disable that controller - the shared switch, the remote dial-in, the USB - and for each decide remove it or strictly control and log it. The real question is not 'do I sign the form?' but 'can I PROVE this barrier is reliable enough as it stands today, and have I closed every path by which it could be reached and switched off?'
Five moves replace a comfortable signature with a trustworthy barrier. (1) THE TWO QUESTIONS ARE ONE PROBLEM: reliable? and secure? both end at the valves failing their safety job, so govern functional safety and OT security as one - can the last barrier be trusted to ACT? (2) PROVE THE INTEGRITY - A SIL IS A LIVING NUMBER, NOT A CERTIFICATE: set a success line first (genuinely SIL 2 today only if the recalculated number is in-band, the proof testing happened on schedule, and no bypass sits on it), then recalculate as-installed - the valves were due a stroke-test every 12 months but were last tested 18 months ago, drifting PFDavg from about 2.1x10-3 to 3.0x10-3 (still inside the SIL 2 band of 10-3 to 10-2, but eating the margin). THE SURPRISE: the historian shows one of the two temperature transmitters left in 'maintenance override' for THREE WEEKS - so the redundant pair was secretly a single sensor, dropping the function OUT of SIL 2 right now. The certificate was real fourteen years ago; it is not real this morning. (3) DEFEND THE INTEGRITY: at least three open paths reach the controller (shared switch, standing remote dial-in, open USB); segregate it behind a one-way data diode (the office can SEE the data, nothing can REACH IN), kill the standing remote access, lock USB, require a two-person logged change. (4) HUNT THE FORGOTTEN BYPASS: any override or 'temporary' fix still on is the barrier quietly halved - and here it exposed BOTH gaps (the 'prove' process never found it, the 'defend' process never prevented it). (5) WRITE THE HONEST LINE, NOT A COMFORTABLE SIGNATURE: prove what you can (corrected and recalculated today), and convert what you cannot into a dated, owned plan with names - a signature you cannot back up protects no one. A last barrier is trustworthy only when you can both prove AND defend it; a confident answer on one face is never a quiet pass on the other.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You do not sign the cover sheet at 06:50. Instead you call two people - Rendani, your control-and-instrumentation engineer, and Nkateko, who looks after plant IT - put the blue file on the table, and say: "We are not going to assume anything today. We are going to either show it, or write down honestly that we cannot yet." Five moves:

  • The two faces of trust - prove AND defend: you do not sign the cover sheet at 06:50. You call Rendani, your control-and-instrumentation engineer, and Nkateko, who looks after plant IT, put the blue file on the table and say: 'We are not going to assume anything today. We are going to either show it, or write down honestly that we cannot yet.' Rendani names the thing first: the shut-down on Reactor 3 is a SAFETY-INSTRUMENTED SYSTEM - sensors, a logic solver, and final elements (the valves) whose only job is to take the plant to a safe state when a dangerous condition appears. It is the LAST automated barrier before a major accident, and it carries a SAFETY INTEGRITY LEVEL (SIL) - a band saying how reliable it must be (Reactor 3's was specified SIL 2). Then Nkateko adds the second face: the same logic box that must trip reliably can also be REACHED. If an attacker - or a careless update - can change its settings, freeze its inputs, or stop it tripping, the SIL number on paper is meaningless; a barrier that can be quietly switched off is not a barrier. So functional safety and OT cybersecurity are the same governance problem wearing two hats: can this last barrier be trusted to ACT when it must? You cannot answer 'yes' to one and 'we do not know' to the other and still call the plant safe. (Splitting reliability and security across two files owned by two departments leaves the joint between them unowned - the systems-integration failure of bp-0070.)
  • Prove the integrity - a SIL is a living number, not a birth certificate: Rendani does not trust the certificate and does not guess. She writes down, in advance, what would have to be TRUE for the SIL 2 claim to still hold this morning: 'If this function is genuinely SIL 2 as installed today, three things must be true - the recalculated probability of failure on demand lands inside the SIL 2 band, the proof testing has actually happened on schedule, and there is no bypass sitting on the function right now. If any one is missing, the certificate is lying for us.' That is her success line, set before she opens a record. Then she finds out the cheapest honest way - not by running the reactor to a trip (insane on a live plant) but by doing the calculation nobody had done, on the function as installed today, end to end, checked against the historian and the maintenance log. The sensors are two temperature transmitters wired so EITHER tripping is enough (good - that redundancy is what buys the reliability), about 300 FIT dangerous-undetected each; the logic solver is certified, a small share; the weak point is the valves - mechanical, exposed to process fluid, proof-tested rarely. And the PROOF-TEST INTERVAL matters enormously, because a SIL number is not just how good the parts are, it is how often you TEST them to catch hidden dangerous failures: the valves were due a stroke-test every 12 months, but the last full proof test was 18 months ago. At 12 months the PFDavg comes to about 2.1x10-3 (comfortably inside the SIL 2 band of 10-3 to 10-2); at the actual 18-month gap it drifts to about 3.0x10-3 - still inside SIL 2, but eating the margin and trending the wrong way. THE SURPRISE breaks her own success line: the historian shows one of the two temperature transmitters has carried a 'maintenance override' flag for THREE WEEKS - someone bypassed it during a calibration and never cleared it - so the 'redundant' pair has been a SINGLE sensor, which drops the function out of SIL 2 altogether right now. The certificate was real fourteen years ago; it is not real this morning. So she changes the plan on the spot: the bypass is cleared and the pair restored before anyone writes anything, the overdue proof test is booked and a standing 12-month regime goes into the maintenance plan, and the SIL 2 claim moves from assumed-true to true-because-corrected-and-recalculated-today. The certificate did not earn the signature; the corrected, re-read function did. A SIL is a living property - it degrades with missed tests, forgotten bypasses and drift - and proving it means recalculating it against the plant as it actually is.
  • Defend the integrity - isolate the controller, control every path: Nkateko draws the network on the whiteboard, and it is ugly: the safety controller shares a switch with the business network; the recipe supplier dials in over a remote-access tool not updated in two years; the engineering workstation accepts any USB stick. At least THREE open paths reach the last barrier. He applies a hard principle - the safety function must be defended IN DEPTH, and each path that can reach it must be removed or strictly controlled. SEGREGATE the network: the safety controller and the basic process control come off the shared switch onto their own isolated zone, behind a one-way data diode for the historian feed the office needs - the office can SEE the data, but nothing from the office can REACH IN and change the controller (the direct answer to the auditor's email). KILL the standing remote access: the supplier's always-on dial-in is removed; when they genuinely need to update a recipe it becomes a scheduled, supervised, logged session an engineer opens and closes. LOCK the workstation and USB: ports disabled by default, patches through a controlled, scanned process with a logged approval, not a stick in a pocket. And MAKE CHANGES LEAVE A TRACE: any change to the safety logic now requires a logged, two-person authorisation - under which the three-week sensor bypass could never have lasted three weeks unnoticed. None of this is exotic; it is the same idea as the SIL discipline - the barrier is only as trustworthy as the controls that keep it whole over its whole life.
  • Hunt the forgotten bypass - the barrier quietly halved: stand back and look at what the three-week override actually was. A 'temporary' change, made for a perfectly good reason during a calibration, never closed out, in a system nobody re-checked because 'it has always been fine' - and it had quietly turned a redundant, trustworthy barrier into a single-sensor, sleeping one. Any override, mute, or 'temporary' fix still in place is the barrier halved, and the danger is that it is invisible: the screen still shows green. (This hidden-failure dynamic - that a protective system you do not actively test is a promise unkept, and that two 'redundant' things sharing one cause are really one - is the dormant-protection lesson of bp-0153.) But notice what makes this one worse than a simple untested backup: the same forgotten bypass exposed BOTH governance faces at once. The PROVE process should have caught it (a real SIL recalculation reads the override log) and did not, because nobody recalculated. The DEFEND process should have prevented it (a two-person change authorisation never lets an override live for three weeks) and did not, because there was no change control. One small uncleared maintenance action is the single thread that, pulled, unravels both the reliability case and the security case - which is exactly why you hunt it, every time, as the first evidence that a barrier you were about to vouch for is not whole.
  • Write the honest line - not a comfortable signature: you do NOT write 'system meets required integrity level, isolated from external networks.' That would be a comfortable lie. You write, and the auditor, Dakalo, respects it far more: 'Reactor 3 shut-down function is rated SIL 2. Recalculated PFDavg as currently installed is 3.0x10-3 - within SIL 2 but degraded by an overdue proof test and a sensor bypass left in place; both corrected as of today. A standing 12-month proof-test and bypass-clearance regime is now in the maintenance plan, owned by C&I. Network segregation of the safety controller is INCOMPLETE; three external access paths identified; isolation and supervised-access controls scheduled with dates and owners. Interim: remote dial-in disabled today, USB disabled today.' And you name the trade-offs honestly: tighter proof-testing costs production (stroke-testing valves means short, planned shut-downs - downtime now versus a runaway reactor later, and you choose downtime); network isolation costs convenience (the supplier loved their always-on dial-in, the office liked the live link - but convenience is exactly the door an attacker walks through, so you close it and make access deliberate); and you cannot prove everything in two hours, so you convert what you cannot prove into a dated, owned plan, which is itself a form of honesty an auditor and a board can act on. A signature you cannot back up protects no one. The deeper move: you stopped treating 'is it reliable?' and 'is it secure?' as two boxes for two departments, and started governing the safety-instrumented system as ONE trusted barrier - proving its integrity with numbers and defending its integrity with controls, across its whole life.

See where it lands. Not a comfortable signature, but a corrected barrier and an honest, dated account the auditor respects more than a confident lie. A critical automated last barrier is trustworthy only when you can BOTH prove its integrity - recalculate its rated reliability as installed today, a living number and not a certificate - AND defend its integrity - remove or strictly control every path by which it could be reached and switched off - governed as one thing across its whole life. A confident answer on one face is never a quiet pass on the other; and the most dangerous moment is not when a barrier obviously looks broken, but when one half has just been polished, because that polish is exactly what stops anyone checking the other half. When you feel safest, ask the question you have not asked yet.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You have seen Rendani and Nkateko work the clean case. Now take one step with a guide beside you - because the real world rarely arrives as cleanly as Reactor 3 did. This next one is the same deep problem, dressed up to look like something it is not.

See the water utility that secured its barrier - and never proved it

You are the works manager at a small-town water utility - nothing like a chemicals plant. Your last barrier is the chlorine-dosing cut-out: an automatic system that shuts the chlorine feed if the dose climbs dangerously high, so the town is never sent over-chlorinated water. Three months ago you had a proper scare - a security consultant showed that your dosing controller was reachable from the office network. So you acted. You paid for a project, segregated the controller onto its own network, killed the old remote login, and now every change is logged with two names against it. There is a tidy folder proving it. Your board congratulated you. When the regulator asks "is your dosing safety system secure?", you can say yes, with evidence, and mean it. So you feel safe - safer than the chemicals team felt at 06:40, honestly - because you fixed the thing the expert flagged.

Look back at the deep lesson from the worked solution. The barrier had TWO faces, not one. Which face did this water utility just spend three months making airtight - and which face has nobody mentioned since the scare? The team poured everything into DEFENDING the barrier (the network, the access, the logging) and not one hour into PROVING it. Nobody has asked the reliability question at all. When was the chlorine cut-out last TESTED in a way that would reveal a hidden, dangerous failure? Has anyone calculated whether it still meets its required level as installed today, with the actual sensors and the actual test interval? The shiny security folder is real - and it is only HALF the barrier. A cut-out that is perfectly locked down but no longer trips when the dose runs away is exactly as dangerous as one anybody could switch off. So go and look, the way Rendani did. You pull the dosing-cut-out maintenance record and the alarm settings, and you find that the high-dose overflow alarm was put into "test mode" during a sensor swap FIVE MONTHS ago and never taken out. Where have you seen this exact thing before? It is the three-week sensor bypass on Reactor 3, wearing different clothes - a "temporary" change made for a good reason during maintenance, never cleared, quietly turning a live barrier into a sleeping one. Both come from the same place: a maintenance action that was never closed out, in a system nobody re-checks because "it has always been fine." Notice the trap you nearly walked into: the secured network made the barrier FEEL trustworthy - a reassuring, paid-for, board-praised fix hid the fact that only one of the two faces was ever handled, and that a forgotten bypass had been eating the other for five months. You would have signed "dosing safety system secure" and been telling the truth - about the half you looked at. A last barrier is trustworthy only when you can both prove its integrity and defend its integrity, and a confident answer on one face is not a quiet pass on the other. The most dangerous moment is not when a barrier obviously looks broken; it is when one half has just been polished, because that polish is exactly what stops anyone checking the other half.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The plant, the reactor and the auditor are not your world, but the problem-type almost certainly is: you depend on an automated last-line-of-defence to protect against a rare catastrophe, and you have neither proven it is reliable enough nor confirmed it cannot be reached and disabled. It works today, so nobody checks - and the risk hides in that confidence. Find it where you are:

  1. Name your last barrier. The automated system whose quiet failure would be a disaster, not an inconvenience - a pressure relief on a vessel, an interlock on a machine, a fire-suppression trigger, a high-level alarm-and-trip on a tank, the brake-and-cut-out on a hoist, a software safety check in a critical service. Pick the one whose quiet failure would hurt people or the business most.
  2. Can you prove it is reliable enough - with a number, not a feeling? Is there a required reliability level for it? Has anyone calculated it as installed today, or are you trusting a certificate from years ago? When was it last tested in a way that would reveal a hidden, dangerous failure - and is that test actually happening on schedule?
  3. Map every path that could reach it and switch it off. Who or what can change its settings, bypass it, or stop it acting - a maintenance override left on, a shared network, a remote login, a USB port, a default password, a supplier with standing access? Which of those paths should exist at all, and which is just a door left open out of convenience?
  4. Hunt the forgotten bypass, and decide what you would honestly write. Where might a temporary fix, a silenced alarm, or a disabled check have become permanent without anyone deciding it should? And if an auditor or your board asked you today "is this barrier trustworthy?", what could you prove, what would you have to admit you cannot yet prove, and what would become a dated, owned plan rather than a comfortable signature?

Run the smallest test this week: pick your one last barrier, pull its maintenance record and its override log, and read off the two real numbers - the date of its last hidden-failure test (is it overdue against the schedule?), and how many overrides or "test mode" flags are still live on it right now. You will know it worked when you are holding evidence, not a feeling, about whether that barrier could be signed for today - and a non-zero second number is a barrier you cannot yet sign for.
