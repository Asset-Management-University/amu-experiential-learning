Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Zimkhitha, the site manager for a steel pressure-pipework fabrication firm, building and installing the pipework and the two pressure skids on a regional water board's new pump station. It is a wet Tuesday on the lay-down yard, your boots caked in red mud; diesel and wet concrete hang in the air, and a generator runs flat out behind you. In your hand is a thin, damp site diary - a soft-cover notebook with a coffee ring on the front, most pages half-blank. The signed contract for this job is R8.4 million, and it lives in a lever-arch file back at the office. You have not opened that file since the day you signed it four months ago. Out here, the work runs on memory and on what people tell you.

The client's engineer, Mlungisi, walks across the yard towards you. He is calm, well-dressed, and he never raises his voice - which somehow makes him harder to argue with. "Morning," he says. "Small thing. The board has changed the inlet layout. We need the two skids turned ninety degrees and the manifold re-routed to the north wall. Can your team start today? We're behind, and I'd hate for the delay to be on your side." You feel the familiar pull to be the helpful contractor. The client is standing right here. Saying "yes, no problem" keeps him happy, keeps the job moving, keeps you off the hook for the schedule. Your foreman is already nodding. It would be so easy to point the welders at the new layout and sort the paperwork out "later, when things calm down."

But a cold thought arrives with the rain. Turning those skids is not a small thing. It means cutting out 14 metres of pipe you already welded and tested, re-fabricating it, and three days of crane time you did not plan for. You guess it is somewhere north of R200,000 of extra work - R215,000, maybe more, you genuinely do not know yet. And this is the fourth "small change" Mlungisi has asked for verbally this month. The first three are already done. You never wrote down what they cost. You never said, in writing, that they would cost anything at all.

You remember the contract has something in it about changes - a clause about giving notice, a number of days. You cannot remember the number. You are not even sure where that page is. And you have a sick feeling that whatever that deadline was, it has already passed on the first three. If you say yes now and bill it all at the end, you can already hear the answer six months from now: "We never agreed any extra cost. You carried on without telling us. Prove it." And the only proof you would have is a damp notebook with half-blank pages. The crane is booked for the afternoon. Mlungisi is waiting. Your foreman is waiting. The rain is getting heavier.

The engineer is asking me to turn two welded skids ninety degrees today - the fourth verbal "small change" this month, north of R200,000 I've never put in writing, the crane booked for the afternoon - do I just say yes and sort the paperwork later?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The client is standing right there, and being helpful feels like the safe move. "Say 'yes, no problem' and sort the paperwork later." But "later" is the year-end fight: you'll have done R200,000 of work with a damp notebook as your only proof, and "we never agreed any extra cost - prove it" is an answer you cannot beat. "Refuse until a formal variation order is signed." But that stops the job dead in the rain, breaks the relationship with a calm client who holds your next contract, and isn't even necessary - you can keep working AND protect the claim. "Just remember it all and add it up at the end." But memory is not evidence; a number reconstructed in six months is your word against theirs, and the three earlier changes already prove how fast "I'll remember" becomes "I can't prove it." So before you read on: name what you've been running this live R8.4m contract on instead of records, and the one ten-minute action that would turn this spoken instruction into something you could prove in three months.

I am Zimkhitha, the site manager for a steel pressure-pipework fabrication firm building the pipework and two pressure skids on a water board's new pump station. The signed contract is R8.4 million - it has been sitting unopened in a lever-arch file back at the office for four months, and out here the work runs on memory. It is a wet Tuesday and the client's engineer, Mlungisi - calm, never raises his voice, which makes him harder to argue with - walks over: 'small thing, the board's changed the inlet layout, we need the two skids turned ninety degrees and the manifold re-routed to the north wall, can your team start today? we're behind, I'd hate the delay to be on your side.' I feel the pull to be the helpful contractor - say 'yes, no problem', keep him happy, sort the paperwork later. But turning the skids means cutting out 14 metres of pipe I've already welded and tested, re-fabricating it, and three days of unplanned crane time - somewhere north of R200,000 (I'd estimate R215,000), and this is the FOURTH verbal 'small change' this month; the first three are done, their cost never written down, never even said in writing that they'd cost anything. The contract has a clause about changes - notice within some number of days - but I can't remember the number or find the page, and I have a sick feeling the deadline has already passed on the first three. If I say yes and bill at the end, in six months I'll hear: 'we never agreed any extra cost, you carried on without telling us, prove it' - and my only proof is a damp notebook with half-blank pages. Where do I start before a single welder lifts a grinder?
Don't give a blind yes, and don't refuse - buy a PAUSE to do it right. Say: 'we can absolutely do this, my team's ready; give me till end of day - I'll send you one short note confirming the change you've asked for, what it does to our programme and cost, and then we crack on. Same with the three earlier ones.' That sentence - 'I'll send you one short note confirming the change' - is the whole solution. The fault was never the change; it was running a live R8.4m contract by MEMORY instead of by RECORD. The discipline is CONTRACT ADMINISTRATION, and its core is the variation-and-claim process: three habits done AS THE CHANGE HAPPENS, not at the end. (1) Turn every verbal instruction into a dated WRITTEN record the same day - a 'confirming your instruction today to do X; we are treating this as a variation; estimated cost R___, estimated time +___ days; proceeding on this basis unless you advise otherwise', copied to your own director: a contemporaneous record no one can later pretend did not happen. (2) Watch the notification deadline like a hawk - many contracts say that if you don't give notice of a change and its cost within the time limit, you LOSE the right to claim it, however fair. (3) Keep a live cost-and-time record as it happens, from real timesheets and delivery notes, not memory six months later. The real question is not 'do I say yes?' but 'how do I turn every change into a priced record the same day, so it stays a change and never matures into a fight I can't prove?'
Five moves keep a change from becoming a fight. (1) BUY THE PAUSE AND OPEN THE CONTRACT - neither a blind yes nor a flat no; 'give me till end of day for one short confirming note, then we crack on' - and finally open the file and find the clause (here: a variation must be notified in writing within 14 days, with a cost and time estimate). (2) HABIT 1 - TURN EVERY WORD INTO A WRITTEN RECORD THE SAME DAY: one dated email confirming the instruction, the R215,000 estimated cost and +3 days, treated as a variation, copied to your director - a contemporaneous record. (3) HABIT 2 - WATCH THE NOTIFICATION DEADLINE LIKE A HAWK: a table of every variation - instructed date, notice-due date, notice-sent date - so no change quietly ages past its deadline and is lost. (4) HABIT 3 - KEEP A LIVE COST-AND-TIME RECORD: a one-page sheet per change (labour, crane, the 14m re-fabricated pipe, the re-test) updated daily from real timesheets and delivery notes - the difference between a claim you can PROVE and one you can only ARGUE. (5) TEST PAST CHANGES WITH TWO GATES, NOT YOUR GUT: for the three earlier changes, set a success line first - recoverable only if (gate 1) you can still assemble real evidence AND (gate 2) the notice deadline hasn't passed - then score each against the real records. The surprise: Zimkhitha was certain the OLDEST change was lost, but a forgotten same-day photo-email made it the most provable - the evidence trail, not the age, decided. She recovered about R180,000 she'd been ready to surrender, and honestly wrote off the one change (R95,000) a sub-contractor's missing timesheet left unprovable. A variation left to memory becomes a claim, and a claim becomes a fight; a variation recorded the same day stays a priced change.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

That morning Zimkhitha did not say no, and she did not say a blind yes. She said something that bought her the most valuable thing she had: a pause to do it right. "We can absolutely do this - my team's ready. Give me till the end of the day. I'll send you one short note confirming the change you've asked for, what it does to our programme and our cost, and then we crack on. Same with the three earlier ones." He shrugged. "Fine. Send it." Five moves:

  • Buy the pause, and open the contract: Zimkhitha did not say no, and she did not say a blind yes. She said the thing that bought her the most valuable thing she had - a pause to do it right: 'Mr engineer, we can absolutely do this, my team's ready; give me till the end of the day. I'll send you one short note confirming the change you've asked for, what it does to our programme and our cost, and then we crack on. Same with the three earlier ones - I owe you a tidy record on those too.' He shrugged: 'Fine. Send it.' That sentence - 'I'll send you one short note confirming the change' - is the whole solution in miniature. Then she did what she had not done in four months: she walked into the site container, opened the contract file she had been ignoring, and found the clause. It was there in black and white - a VARIATION (a change to the agreed scope) had to be notified IN WRITING within 14 days, with an estimate of cost and time impact as soon as practicable. The pause is the move that makes every other move possible: a blind yes throws away your right to be paid, a flat no breaks the relationship and the job, but a short, confident 'let me confirm this in writing and we proceed today' keeps the work moving AND protects the claim. You cannot administer a contract you have never opened.
  • Habit 1 - turn every word into a written record, the same day: the discipline she reached for is CONTRACT ADMINISTRATION: actively managing the agreement during the work, instead of signing it and running the job on memory - and within it, the VARIATION-AND-CLAIM PROCESS, three habits done AS THE CHANGE HAPPENS. The first: turn every verbal instruction into a written record the same day. She wrote one short, dated email: 'Confirming your verbal instruction today to rotate both skids 90 degrees and re-route the manifold to the north wall. We are treating this as a variation under the contract. Estimated cost impact R215,000; estimated programme impact +3 days. Work proceeding on this basis unless you advise otherwise.' She copied her own director and saved it. Now there was a CONTEMPORANEOUS record - a record made AT THE TIME, which no one can later pretend did not happen. This is the single habit that dissolves the whole six-months-from-now nightmare ('we never agreed any extra cost, prove it'): the proof is made on the day the change is asked for, in one short note, before a welder lifts a grinder - not reconstructed from a damp notebook after the fight has already started.
  • Habit 2 - watch the notification deadline like a hawk: the 14-day clock was the trap she had already half-fallen into. Many contracts say that if you do not give notice of a change and its cost within the time limit, you LOSE the right to claim for it - full stop, no matter how fair the claim. This is the cruellest part of contract administration and the most overlooked: a perfectly legitimate R200,000 of extra work can become legally unrecoverable not because it wasn't done, but because nobody sent a note inside a fortnight. So Zimkhitha made a simple table: every variation, the date it was instructed, the date notice was due, the date notice was sent. From now on, no change would quietly age past its deadline in silence. The deadline exists to protect the CLIENT - to stop a contractor ambushing them at the end with a pile of surprise costs they could have queried or avoided at the time - which is exactly why it is enforced strictly, and exactly why the contractor who watches it is the one who gets paid. A clock you cannot see is a clock that runs out.
  • Habit 3 - keep a live record, to prove not just argue: for the skid rotation she opened a one-page sheet: labour hours, crane hire, the 14 metres of re-fabricated pipe, the re-test. She updated it daily from real timesheets and delivery notes - not from memory six months later. This is the difference between a claim you can PROVE and a claim you can only ARGUE, and at the end of a job, when the money is being fought over, only proof survives. A number written down TODAY, against a real timesheet and a real delivery note, is worth far more than the same number reconstructed at the end from recollection - because the contemporaneous number is evidence, and the remembered number is just your word against theirs. The habit costs almost nothing in the moment (the timesheets and delivery notes already exist; you are only attaching them to the change) and is nearly impossible to recreate after the fact. Record the cost as it accrues, and the claim writes itself; leave it to memory, and you are left arguing about money you genuinely spent and genuinely cannot prove.
  • Test past changes with two gates, not your gut: the three earlier changes - were they lost? She refused to guess, and tested it, setting a success line first, in writing, before she opened a single folder: 'a past change is recoverable only if it passes two gates - (1) I can still assemble real evidence of what it cost, AND (2) its 14-day notice deadline has not run out; I'll score all three against both gates, chase the count that clear both, and write off the rest without a fight.' Then she went to the cheapest honest source of truth - the actual timesheets, delivery notes and contract dates, not her memory of how it felt. And here came the surprise that changed her plan: she had walked in CERTAIN the recoverable ones would be the recent changes and the lost one would be the oldest. But pulling the file on the oldest change, she found something she had completely forgotten - she had emailed the engineer a PHOTO of the re-routed drain on the day, with one line, 'as you asked this morning.' That email was a contemporaneous record, and it was itself the notice. The 'oldest, hopeless' change was not hopeless at all: the evidence trail, not the age, decided it. So she changed course - submitted all three, flagging plainly where records were late - and the client accepted two in full, about R180,000 recovered that she had been ready to surrender. The one she could not honestly defend (a sub-contractor's support-frame re-work with no separate timesheet, so no real evidence of cost) failed gate 1, and she wrote it off - roughly R95,000 - and told her director straight. The test, run against the real records, returned a different verdict than her instinct, and she held to the test, not the instinct. (An intelligent client expects exactly this discipline of its contractors - see bp-0089; and bp-0064 covers getting the scope right before the contract is signed.)

See where it lands. The discipline costs maybe twenty minutes a day of records and the small social discomfort of sending a "confirming the cost" note to a client who would rather you stayed quiet. Against it: on this one R8.4m job, sloppy administration had already cost R95,000 written off, nearly lost another R180,000, and had the firm heading towards a year-end fight over the full R215,000 skid change. A variation only becomes a disputed claim because the change was managed by memory instead of by record. Caught the same day, in writing, with the cost attached, a change is just a change; left to mature in silence, every change becomes a claim and every claim becomes a fight - one you usually lose, because you cannot prove what was agreed. So administer the live agreement by record: turn every verbal instruction into a dated written record the same day, watch the notification deadline like a hawk, and keep a live cost-and-time record as the change happens - and when you judge a past claim, let the two-gate test (real evidence + the deadline), not your gut, decide.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You've seen the clean version on a big construction site. Now take one step on a messier, smaller one - the kind that hides the same trap behind a friendly face.

See the rent drop a kind landlord never wrote down

You rent a small flat. Your landlord, Sandiso, is genuinely kind - he drops off oranges from his garden, he asks after your family. One evening he phones. "Listen," he says warmly, "your geyser's leaking, I know. I'll get it fixed properly next month, my cousin does plumbing. And honestly, you've been a good tenant - let's drop your rent by R600 a month while the geyser's a hassle. Sound fair?" You say yes, gratefully. You like him. Nothing is written down - it would feel cold to ask. Your lease, signed two years ago, is in a drawer somewhere; you think it says something about repairs needing to be requested "in writing", but you have never read it closely. This feels nothing like the muddy yard - no R8.4m contract, no calm engineer applying pressure, no crane booked. It is a kind man and a leaky geyser.

Look past the warmth to the shape. What actually got recorded in that phone call? Nothing. A change to your agreement - the rent, and a repair promise - was made by speaking, run on memory, with the actual contract sitting unread in a drawer. That is the exact shape of the muddy-yard problem; the warmth is the disguise, and a friendly verbal change is still a verbal change. Now find the quiet clocks - there are two. The repair: your lease may require the request in writing within some period before you have any right to push for it. And the rent drop: if it is never written down, what stops Sandiso, or a new owner if he sells, from one day saying "we never agreed any reduction - you owe me the R600 for every month"? The kindness today does not protect you from the dispute tomorrow - it is the same trap as "we never agreed any extra cost - prove it." So apply Zimkhitha's one move, kept warm: you send Sandiso a friendly message that evening. "Thanks so much, Sandiso - really appreciate it. Just so we both have it straight: rent drops by R600/month from the 1st while the geyser is sorted, and you'll have your cousin fix it next month. Shout if I've got anything wrong." That is Habit 1 - turn the verbal change into a dated written record, the same day, without refusing or accusing anyone - and it protects both of you. The skid rotation and the geyser look like different worlds, millions of rand versus R600 - but they are the same problem: a live agreement run on memory instead of by record, a change drifting unwritten, a deadline ticking in silence. The disguise here is friendliness - it whispers that writing it down would be rude. It would not. A change you confirm in writing, the same day, stays a change; a change left to memory matures, quietly, into a fight you cannot win - whether the page says R8.4 million or R600.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This problem-type is everywhere a deal is struck and then run on memory. The setting changes; the trap does not. Find it where you work:

  1. Read the changes clause. Think of an agreement you are responsible for right now - a contract, a supplier arrangement, a project scope, a service promise. When did you last open it and read the part about changes? If you can't remember, that's the warning sign Zimkhitha ignored for four months.
  2. Catch a verbal change. Find a place where someone instructs a change by speaking - a phone call, a corridor chat, a "can you just also..." - and the work starts before anything is written down. That is where the next dispute is being born. What would your "one short note, same day" look like?
  3. Find the hidden deadline. Look for a notice period or claim window inside an agreement you might be quietly blowing past. Whose interest does it protect, and what do you lose if you miss it?
  4. Reconstruct one past change, and name the record you're not keeping. Pick one change you handled "on memory" - could you honestly prove what it cost in time and money, or is it already a damp-notebook situation? And where would twenty minutes a day of recording save you a year-end fight?

Run the smallest test this week: pick the one change you're least sure about and run Zimkhitha's two-gate test on it - pull the actual records (not your memory) and answer, yes or no, can I still assemble real evidence? and has the notice deadline passed? The yes-sign you're looking for: at least one change clears both gates, which means money you thought was lost is actually still recoverable. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build your variation tracker and your same-day confirming note for a real agreement together.)

This is the same problem-type - managing a live agreement by record instead of by memory, so a change stays a change and never matures into a fight you cannot win. Your world supplies the contract, the deadline, and the person across the yard asking for "just a small thing".
