Your asset plan on one page

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. A year ago, you helped the works agree its promise: safe, reliable water for the community, every day. It is framed on the wall by the door. Everyone nodded. It was a good day.

This morning you are staring at the pump breakdown log - and your stomach sinks. The same pumps. The same failures. The same Friday-night scramble to keep water in the reservoir. A year on, nothing has actually changed. The team is still doing what it has always done: waiting for something to break, then running to fix it.

And you see why. The promise on the wall is real, but it is just a promise. Nobody ever turned it into a plan - nobody decided which pump to fix first, in which year, with what money, before it fails. Without that, "reliable water" is a hope, not a commitment. Everyone is busy, everyone means well, and yet the works only ever reacts.

We made a clear promise about our assets - and a year later nothing has changed, because a promise with no plan changes nothing. How do I turn "we will look after these assets" into who does what, in what order, and when?

Before you read on, decide. A year ago this works published a clear promise about looking after its assets, signed at the top, and everybody meant it. Twelve months on: Pump 1 is fifteen years old and in poor condition, Pump 2 is ten years old and fair, Pump 3 is four years old and good. Nothing has been done to any of them except repairs after breakdowns. Everybody has been busy the whole year. There is about R180,000 available in the coming year and nothing agreed beyond it. Write down what you would do so that next year is different — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why a year of good intentions produced nothing

• “Restate the commitment and get everybody behind it properly this time.” The commitment was already clear and people were already behind it. Saying it again with more conviction addresses a failure of will that did not occur.

• “Ask for a bigger budget — we cannot do this on R180,000.” Probably true, and it is also what has been said every year, and the answer arrives too late to change the year. A plan that only works if the money grows is a plan for somebody else's works.

• “Draw up a full list of everything that needs doing.” A list is not a plan. Everything on it is real, none of it is sequenced, and it will be worked through in the order things break — which is the order you already have.

• “Start with the oldest pump.” Reasonable, and age is not risk. Sometimes the oldest is the one nothing depends on, and the plan quietly spends the year's money where failure would have cost least.

• “Fix whichever fails first — at least that is money well spent.” This is precisely what has happened for twelve months, described as though it were a decision.

• The promise is not the problem and neither is the effort. A statement of intent contains no decisions — it says what matters and is silent on which thing happens first, in which year, with whose money, done by whom — and in that silence an organisation reverts to the only ordering it has ever had, which is whatever fails next. Turning it into something that changes a year means making those decisions explicitly and in a particular way: ordered by what would hurt most if it failed rather than by age or noise; costed and staged against the money that actually exists, this year approved and later years indicative; each job carrying the name of who will do it, because work given to the organisation is given to nobody; owned by a role rather than a person, so it survives them leaving; and reviewed both on a date and whenever something fails unexpectedly — because a plan that cannot absorb a surprise is abandoned at the first one and never rebuilt.

There is a tool for exactly this: one page that turns a promise into a prioritised, costed plan of action. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is what turns Corathyl's promise into something that actually happens: the whole plan for its most critical assets - the raw-water pumps - on one page. Read it slowly. Notice that it does not try to fix everything at once; it decides what comes first, and why.

1. Our Objective (from the strategy)

Keep our raw-water pumps available at least 99.5% of the time - the level we need to keep the 'safe, reliable water' promise made in our Policy. (This target comes from our long-term strategy, the SAMP.)

2. The Assets - Condition and Risk

Pump 1: 15 years old, condition poor - HIGH risk of failure. Pump 2: 10 years, condition fair - MEDIUM risk. Pump 3: 4 years, condition good - LOW risk.

3. What We'll Do, and In What Order (worst risk first)

Year 1: refurbish Pump 1 (the highest risk) - R180,000. Year 2: major overhaul of Pump 2 - R120,000. Year 3: fit condition-monitoring to Pump 3 - R40,000. Year 4: scope and budget Pump 1's eventual replacement.

4. What It Will Take (money and people)

About R340,000 across the first three years, staged year by year (Pump 1's replacement is budgeted later). Year 1's R180,000 is approved. Each job names the crew or contractor who will carry it out.

5. Owner and Review

Owned by the works' asset engineer (a role, not one person's name). Reviewed every December - and again the moment a pump fails unexpectedly or the budget changes. A plan is a living document, not a poster.

Five boxes. It names a real target, faces the true state of each pump, and then makes a choice: fix the riskiest one first, in a set year, with a set budget, owned by a real person. That is the difference between a poster and a plan. Now let us walk through it, so you can build one yourself.

T3: The Blueprint

This one-page document has a name: it is an Asset Management Plan - an AMP. It is the operational "how" plan, set out in ISO 55001 clause 6.2.3. It turns strategy into specific actions for one specific group of assets.

But notice the very first box: the AMP does not invent its own goal. It inherits it. That target - "99.5% pump availability" - comes from the works' long-term strategy, the Strategic Asset Management Plan (SAMP), clause 6.2.1. The SAMP is the organisation's big, long-horizon plan (often twenty years and more) for how all its assets will deliver the Policy's promises. So the cascade is simple and worth holding onto: the Policy says why (our promise); the SAMP says what, long-term (the strategy and the targets); the AMP says how, here and now (the actions, for these assets). Why to what to how.

Now each box of the AMP, as the question it answers:

  • 1. Our Objective - What must these assets achieve? Not a vague wish - a measurable target handed down by the strategy (99.5% availability). Every action below must serve it.
  • 2. The Assets - Condition and Risk - Where are we now? The age and condition of each asset, and - the part that matters most - the risk if it fails. Everything that follows is driven by this.
  • 3. What We'll Do, and In What Order - What will we do, in what order, and when? This is the heart of it, and it is not a guess. You spend your limited money on the biggest risk to the objective first. Pump 1 is refurbished this year because its failure is the greatest threat to reliable water - not because it is the oldest, or the nearest.
  • 4. What It Will Take - What will it cost, in money and people, and is it funded? A plan with no budget is a fantasy. Naming the cost - and who will do the work - is what makes it real.
  • 5. Owner and Review - Who is accountable, and when do we look again? One owner (a role, not a name), and a review date - because the works changes, pumps fail early, budgets shift, and a plan that never updates slowly becomes a lie.

Does this really scale? Look at the two side by side. Just as Onalenna's plan prioritises which pump to refurbish this year to keep Corathyl's water reliable, a refinery's plan for its cracking units decides which reactor to overhaul at the next shutdown to protect production and safety. Same instrument: a costed, risk-prioritised plan that turns a strategy into action for a group of assets. The assets change; the thinking does not.

T4: Your Turn

Now you build one. Choose a group of assets you actually know, with more than one item so you have to decide what comes first: a society's sound or sports equipment, a family business's vehicles or machines, the computers in a lab, the tools in a workshop. Give it a simple objective (what must these assets achieve?), face each one's condition and risk honestly, then make a prioritised, costed, three-year plan - worst risk first.

Five boxes feels like a lot? Start with boxes 2 and 3 - the risks, then what you'd do first. The order of your actions is the plan; the rest supports it.

T5: The Guardrails

A one-page plan is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then go back and strengthen any box that needs it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The wish-list. Actions with no order, no dates, and no owner are a list of nice-to-haves, not a plan. The moment you put them in order of risk, with years and a cost, it becomes one.

• Mixing up the SAMP and the AMP. A long, vague twenty-year vision is a SAMP. The task here is an AMP: specific, costed, a few years, for a handful of real assets. If your "plan" has no costs or dates, you have drifted up into strategy.

• The peanut-butter plan. Spreading the money thinly and equally ("service every pump a little") feels fair but fixes nothing. Concentrate your force on the highest-risk asset. Risk decides the order - not age, not fairness.

• The whole-life blind spot. Counting only this year's repair bill and ignoring the far bigger cost of running, maintaining and one day replacing the asset. The cheapest fix today can be the most expensive choice over the asset's life.

• The static plan. Treating the plan as finished. It is a living document - when a pump fails early or the budget changes, the plan changes. An un-reviewed plan slowly stops being true.

The promise on Corathyl's wall did not fail because it was a bad promise. It failed because no one turned it into a plan. Now you hold the tool that closes that gap - the one page that decides what comes first, when, and at what cost, so a good intention finally becomes action. Master it here, and you can turn any promise, for any group of assets, into something that actually gets done.
