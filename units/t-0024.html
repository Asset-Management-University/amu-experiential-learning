The spare costs little and we might not use it for a year - yet without it on the shelf a furnace sits cold at a fortune an hour. How do I decide which spares to hold, and how many?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning at a ferrochrome smelter, and you are the new MRO planner - MRO being Maintenance, Repair and Operations, the whole world of spare parts a plant needs to keep running, from a giant motor bearing down to a box of welding rods. You are standing in the spares store, a long shed of grey steel shelving that runs further than you can see; the air smells of grease and warm cardboard, and a forklift's reverse alarm beeps somewhere behind you.

In your hand is a work order. Furnace 2's main feed conveyor has tripped, and the fitter, a calm older man named Mandlenkosi, stands in front of you with oil on his forearms and a look that says the clock is running. He needs one part: a gearbox drive coupling. Every hour Furnace 2 sits cold, the plant loses about R180,000 in lost production, and it has been down forty minutes already. You walk the aisle. Three different couplings look almost the same; two labels are faded. The store computer lists "coupling, drive, assorted" - quantity 4 - with no part number and no shelf location. At the back you find a box from a supplier sticker you have never seen, bought cheap last year. You have no idea if it is the genuine part or a copy.

The temptation is loud and simple: grab the one that looks closest, fit it, sort the paperwork later. But you have felt this before - last month a "close enough" part failed in six days and took the furnace down twice. Grabbing the nearest box is not a method; it is a gamble dressed up as speed. The real failure happened long before today: nobody decided, in the calm, what this store should hold.

The spare costs little and we might not use it for a year - yet without it on the shelf a furnace sits cold at a fortune an hour. How do I decide which spares to hold, and how many?

Before you read on, decide. The coupling costs R12,500 and gets used about 2.3 times a year. If one is not on the shelf when a furnace trips, the supplier takes five working days — about 40 production hours at roughly R180,000 an hour. The history shows seven couplings used across thirty-six months, in months 4, 9, 17, 28 and 33, in ones and twos. One box in the store came cheap from a supplier nobody can name. Write down whether you would hold this part, and how many — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the average hides

• “Do not hold it. About R29,000 a year of spend on a part used twice a year is money tied up on a shelf.” Sound stores discipline, and it is the disease itself: judged on cost, this part looks unimportant. Judged on what its absence costs, it is one of the most important things in the building.

• “Hold one.” Much better, and the history is quietly telling you otherwise: the worst single event needed two at once. One is enough for the average and not for the event that matters.

• “Hold plenty — it is only R12,500.” Correct for this part and impossible as a rule. A store that stocks everything cheap is the other failure, and it is how a yard fills with parts for machines nobody runs any more.

• “Set a reorder point from the average demand.” The right instrument pointed at the wrong shape. An average of about a fifth of a coupling a month describes nothing that ever actually happens: most months are zero, and then two are needed in a morning.

• “Find a supplier who can deliver next day.” The best answer of the five, if it is real — and the unnamed cheap box already in the store is the reason a promise is not the same as a source you can rely on.

• The move is to size the bet by consequence rather than by cost: not what it costs to hold, but what it costs to lack — about R7.2 million for one event, against R12,500 on a shelf. Then read the demand for its shape and not its average, because a lumpy history with a worst event of two says that one on the shelf is a store that will fail on the day it is tested. Then check how fast and how trustworthy the supply really is, since a critical part with a five-day lead time and a source nobody can name is not truly stocked at all.

The answer is to design the store in the calm, before the breakdown - to decide what to hold not by what a part costs, but by what its absence would cost. Here is that tool, worked on the very coupling that bit you.

T2: The Tool in Action

A spares store is a set of bets placed in advance. The disease is betting on cost - holding lots of cheap fast-movers and none of the cheap part that stops a furnace. The cure is to size each bet by consequence. Watch the four boxes turn the coupling from a guess into a designed decision.

Box 1 - Criticality: the consequence if it is MISSING

The coupling costs R12,500 and is used about 2.3 times a year - roughly R29,000 of spend, so on cost alone it looks like a low-value part to hold none of. But if one is missing when a furnace trips, the furnace waits 5 days (about 40 production hours) for delivery: 40 x R180,000 = R7.2 MILLION lost for one event. Cheap to buy, catastrophic to lack - CRITICAL.

Box 2 - The demand pattern: steady, or lumpy?

Lumpy: 7 couplings across 36 months (months 4, 9, 17, 28, 33), mostly zeros. Croston: average demand size (1+2+1+1+2)/5 = 1.4; average gap (4+5+8+11+5)/5 = 6.6 months; rate 1.4 / 6.6 = 0.21 per month. But the worst single event was 2-at-once - so a '0.21 a month' average must not lull you into stocking just one.

Box 3 - Lead time and source: how fast, and how trusted?

Held in stock, a coupling is fitted in 1 hour. Held nowhere, the supplier delivers in 5 working days - far too long for a cold furnace. And one box was bought cheap from an unknown supplier: it might be a counterfeit that fails in six days, the way a 'close enough' part did last month. A long lead time on a critical part means you must hold it.

Box 4 - The stocking decision - and make it findable

Critical + 5-day lead + a worst event of 2 -> hold a min/max of 2 (reorder back to 2 the moment one is used). Tied-up cash 2 x R12,500 = R25,000, against a single avoided stock-out of R7.2m - it pays for itself 288 times over on the first event it prevents. Then make it findable: one part number (FC-CPL-160-A), a shelf location (Aisle 3, Bay B, Bin 12), and a photo - so the fitter types one number and walks straight to it.

Look at what the boxes did. On cost alone, this R29,000-a-year part screamed "hold none". But the moment you asked what its absence costs - R7.2 million for one cold-furnace event - the decision inverted: hold two, for R25,000 of tied-up cash that pays for itself 288 times over on the first stock-out it prevents. The cheapness of a part tells you almost nothing about whether to stock it; only the consequence of its absence does. And the lumpy-demand maths matters: a "0.21 a month" average would have whispered "one is plenty" - but the worst real event was two at once, and stocking for the average, not the worst, is exactly how a calm store fails on its worst day. And the rand figures were never the real point - they are the measure. A cold furnace is broken promises downstream: orders missed, shifts sent home, customers let down. Holding the right spare is how the plant keeps its word.

T3: The Blueprint

You design the stocking policy for a part - in the calm, not the crisis - by walking four boxes.

  • 1. Criticality - the consequence if it is missing. Do not ask what the part costs; ask what its absence costs. A cheap part whose stock-out halts production is critical. Stock to consequence, not to cost.
  • 2. The demand pattern. Steady, or lumpy (random bursts, long zero gaps)? For lumpy intermittent demand, the Croston method forecasts the average size of a demand and the average gap between demands separately, then size divided by gap gives a steady rate - but always note the worst single event, because that is what a stock-out is made of.
  • 3. Lead time and source. How long to get one if you hold none, and from a trusted, genuine source? A long lead time on a critical part means you must hold it; an unknown cheap supplier risks a counterfeit that fails early.
  • 4. The stocking decision - and make it findable. Critical + long-lead + lumpy means a safety buffer sized to the worst event (a simple min/max); cheap + non-critical + short-lead means order on demand. Then give it one exact part number, a shelf location and a photo.

That decision is the heart of MRO, but it lives inside a wider system worth knowing by name. A good catalogue gives every part one number, one description, one location and a photo (and a supersession note saying which new part replaces an old one) - so "coupling, assorted" can never strand a fitter again. The SCOR model (Plan, Source, Make/Repair, Deliver, Return) maps the whole flow end-to-end so you can see where parts get stuck - often at Source, the slow lead time that holding stock merely hides. A performance-based contract pays a supplier for an outcome ("on our shelf 98% of the time") rather than for boxes delivered, so they share your goal of uptime. And supply-chain-security and anti-counterfeit standards (ISO 28000, SAE AS6174) keep fakes and tampering out of the store - because a counterfeit bearing is cheaper today and ruinous tomorrow.

The decisive question is always Box 1: not "what does this cost?" but "what does its absence cost?" If a cheap part can stop something expensive, it earns a buffer however rarely you use it. Size that buffer to the worst demand you have actually seen, not the comfortable average - and the moment you decide to hold it, give it one exact number, a location and a photo, so the readiness you designed in the calm is still there, findable, on the worst morning.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your spares strategy honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Stock to consequence, not cost. The single most expensive mistake in a store is holding none of a cheap part whose absence stops production. Always size the bet by what the stock-out would cost, never by the price on the box.

• Forecast the worst event, not the average. Intermittent demand has a comfortable average and a dangerous worst case. If two have ever arrived at once, a buffer of one is a future breakdown. Let the worst real event set the minimum.

• "Assorted" is a breakdown waiting to happen. A part with no exact number, location or photo is unfindable on the day it matters. One part number, one meaning, one shelf - and a supersession note so nobody fits a part that no longer exists.

• Cheap from an unknown source is dear. A counterfeit that fails in six days costs you far more than the genuine part. Buy critical spares only from trusted, proven suppliers, and treat a bargain from a stranger as a risk, not a saving.

• Holding stock hides a slow supply chain - it does not fix it. A safety buffer buys you time; it does not cure a five-day lead time. Where the consequence is high, also work the source (a faster contract, a local repair) so you are not one bad month from an empty shelf.

The breakdown in the Frame was not really about a coupling - it was about a decision nobody made in the calm, so it fell to you in the crisis, with a fitter waiting and a furnace going cold. Design that decision in advance and the crisis never comes: the right part is on the shelf, in the right number, found in seconds, and proven genuine. Replace a shelf of guesses with a system of readiness: stock to consequence, not to cost, so you never have to gamble with uptime. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring three real spares and their usage history, and design each one's policy through the boxes together.)
