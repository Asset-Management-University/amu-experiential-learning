Step 1 of 5

The contractor says we need a full trip system and the plant manager says another alarm will do - how do I settle it with a number instead of two confident opinions?

T1: The Ache

The machine room at Roodepan Cold Chain is loud and cold and smells faintly of ammonia, the way every machine room does. Six hundred tonnes of other people's food sit behind the doors down the corridor. A fitter is up a ladder in the corner with a torch in his teeth.

Two quotations are on the desk in the office next door, and two people who both know this plant cannot agree.

The engineering contractor says the receiver needs a full instrumented trip system - a certified, tested, automatic shutdown - and has quoted R1.4 million. Mr Bekker, who has run this plant for eleven years and has never had a release, says that is money the business does not have, and that a second high-pressure alarm for R40,000 gives you the same warning twice over. He is not being reckless. He genuinely believes it, and he can point at the drawing and show you two alarms where there was one.

They both turn to you. And you realise, standing there, that you have no way to settle it. You can argue that more protection is better, and he can argue that two alarms are two alarms, and neither of you can show the other anything. There is a fitter up a ladder twelve metres away while the two of you have opinions.

Two protections on a drawing is not the same as enough protection. How do I work out how much is actually needed - and tell a real layer from one that only looks like one?

Before you read on, decide. Which of them would you back, and what would you say to the other one? Write it down - your answer, not a shortlist to choose from. It matters that you commit, because the whole value of what follows is in what it does to the answer you just gave.

Now compare - the three ways people settle this, and why all three leave the fitter where he is

• "Buy the trip system - you cannot put a price on safety." Honourable, and unanswerable in the wrong way: it cannot be argued with, so it cannot be checked either. It also loses the argument in most plants, because the person holding the budget hears it as "I have no number".

• "Two alarms are better than one - go with Bekker." This is the answer that feels most reasonable, and it is the one worth being frightened of. Hold on to it. It is about to turn out to buy nothing at all, for a reason nobody in that room could see.

• "Nothing has ever happened here, so what we have is clearly working." Eleven years without a release is real evidence, and it is not evidence about how often the chain would run all the way through - because the chain has to start before anything can stop it, and it may simply not have started yet.

• And the honest position: "I cannot settle this, because I have no way to say how much protection is enough." That is exactly the gap. What is missing is not more caution and not more experience. It is a way to take one chain of events, count what would actually stop it, and compare the answer with a limit somebody has written down and signed.

T2: The Tool in Action

Take one chain of events. Say how rare it has to be. Find how often it starts. Then test every claimed protection with one hard question - could this fail for the same reason? - and multiply only what survives. What is left over is not a score; it is the specification for whatever you buy next. Watch it run on Roodepan's machine room.

Step 1 - Take ONE scenario, and write down how rare it has to be

You write one line on the board: 'High-side pressure control fails, the receiver over-pressures, ammonia releases into the machine room while a fitter is working in it.' Not 'an ammonia risk' - that cannot be counted. Then the target: for an event that could kill someone, Roodepan Cold Chain's own risk criteria say 1 in 100,000 years - 0.00001 per year. Mr Bekker, the plant manager, goes quiet at that. It is the first honest thing on the whiteboard, because it is the first line anyone could disagree with.

Step 2 - How often does the chain START? Take it from the record, not the room

The high-side pressure control has failed twice in nineteen years on this plant, which is about 0.1 per year - once a decade. The maintenance log is on the table, open, and Mr Bekker and the contractor both read the same two entries. Nobody argues with this number, and that is exactly why it goes first: it is the one thing in the room that is not an opinion.

Step 3 - Test every protection for independence, and credit only what survives

Four claims go up, and two come down. The basic control loop cannot count - it IS the cause. The second high-pressure alarm Mr Bekker wants to add cannot count either, because it reads the same transmitter as the control that fails; when that transmitter lies, both alarms stay silent together. What survives: the existing high-pressure alarm on its own transmitter with an operator who must respond in time, which fails to act about 1 time in 10 (0.1); and the relief valve to the water scrubber, on ammonia duty and conservatively 1 in 10 as well. Mr Bekker: 'So my second alarm buys nothing.' The contractor does not gloat. Neither of them knew before this line was drawn.

Step 4 - Multiply: how often does the scenario get all the way through?

0.1 per year, times 0.1 for the alarm, times 0.1 for the relief valve, is 0.001 per year - about once in a thousand years with what is installed today. Under it on the board goes the target: once in 100,000 years. Two numbers, one above the other, and the whole argument has already changed shape. Note what would have happened if the second alarm had been credited: 0.0001 - a plant that looks ten times safer on paper and is not one bit safer in the machine room.

Step 5 - The gap IS the requirement - and it names how good the new protection must be

0.001 divided by 0.00001 is 100. You are 100 times short. So the new automatic trip must remove 99% of what is left - and a factor of 100 sits in the second of the four reliability bands (10-100 is the first, 100-1,000 the second), which is what the contractor's quotation said. The contractor was right, and now it is right on paper where the board can see it. What Mr Bekker gets is not a defeat: it is a page showing exactly why R1.4 million is the number, what it buys, and what he would be signing for if he chose the alarm instead - once in a thousand years, against a limit of once in a hundred thousand, with a fitter in that room.

See what the five steps did to the argument. Mr Bekker's second alarm turned out to buy nothing - not because it is a bad alarm, but because it reads the same transmitter as the failure it is meant to catch, so when that transmitter lies both alarms stay silent together. And the contractor's expensive answer turned out to be right, for a reason he had never had to write down. Neither of them was foolish; they were both reasoning from a drawing, and a drawing shows you what exists, never what is independent. Look at what Mr Bekker holds now instead of a losing argument: a page that says exactly why R1.4 million is the number, what it buys, and what he would be putting his name to if he chose otherwise - with a fitter up a ladder in that room.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the third one is where the whole method earns its keep.

  • 1. One scenario, one target. This cause, this consequence, these people - and the frequency your organisation will accept for harm that severe, written as a number per year.
  • 2. How often it starts, from the record, with the source on the table.
  • 3. Test every claimed protection for independence. Could it fail for the same reason as the cause, or as another layer you are counting? If so, it does not count. For each survivor, how often does it fail to act when called?
  • 4. Multiply the starting frequency by each survivor's failure chance - that is where you stand today.
  • 5. Divide what you have by what you need. The shortfall is the requirement for whatever you add.

This is LOPA - Layer of Protection Analysis (IEC 61511) - and now that you have run it, its words are just names for what you did. The chain you named is the scenario; the thing that starts it is the initiating cause. A protection that survives the independence test is an Independent Protection Layer, and the chance it fails to act when called is its Probability of Failure on Demand - the 0.1 you wrote next to the alarm. The limit you set in Step 1 is the tolerable frequency. And the shortfall you read off at the end has a name too: it is the Safety Integrity Level, the band the required risk reduction falls into - 10 to 100 is SIL 1, 100 to 1,000 is SIL 2, 1,000 to 10,000 is SIL 3. A SIL is not a quality rating and not a compliment; it is a statement of how much of the remaining risk one new function must carry, and therefore how hard it must be proven and how often it must be tested for the rest of its life.

The method fits when you have a named hazard, several claimed protections, and a decision to make about buying more. It cannot tell you what your hazards are - something has to have found the scenario before you can size it - and it is the wrong instrument entirely for a system with no hazardous energy or inventory, where demanding a SIL wastes money and buries the real risks in noise.

Get the starting frequency from your own record. Then list every protection anyone would claim, and put each one through the independence question out loud. Multiply what survives. Read the gap.

Two disciplines keep the number honest, and the first one is the entire method. Be ruthless about independence, and be ruthless out loud, in front of the people who proposed the layer. The most common error in this work is crediting something that shares a sensor, a valve, a signal, a power supply or a single person with the cause or with another layer - and it is a comfortable error, because it makes the page look better and nobody has to spend anything. That is precisely what makes it dangerous: an inflated study is not a neutral mistake, it is a written record telling people they are protected when they are not. Second, name the consequence plainly before you count anything. If you write "possible upset" where the truth is "a release into a room with a fitter in it", you will size the protection to the softened words - and the method will faithfully give you the answer to the gentler question you asked it.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your study true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Counting a layer that is not independent is worse than counting nothing. Nothing leaves you knowing you are exposed. A false layer leaves you with a document saying you are safe. Ask of every claimed layer: what does it share with the cause?

• "It has never happened" is not a frequency. Eleven safe years tell you the chain has not started yet, not how well you would stop it if it did. Use the record for how often it starts, and the layers for what happens next.

• Do not soften the consequence. Write who is in the room. The arithmetic is obedient - it will answer the question you actually asked it, including the gentle one.

• The target is a value decision, not a calculation. Somebody has to say what frequency of harm this organisation will accept, and put their name to it. If nobody will, that is the finding - and it belongs in your report, not in your assumptions.

• A SIL is a promise with a maintenance schedule attached. The band only holds if the function is proof-tested at the right interval for its whole life. An automatic trip that nobody tests is a barrier that has quietly fallen, and the page still says you are protected.

• Do not over-protect a low-energy system. Sizing is a two-way discipline. Demanding a high band where nothing can hurt anyone spends money that the genuinely dangerous scenario down the corridor needed.

You walked into that machine room with two capable people, two quotations and no way to choose between them. You can put one page on the desk instead: the scenario in a single line, the target somebody signed, the starting frequency straight out of the log, the two layers that survived and the two that did not, the once-in-a-thousand-years you stand at against the once-in-a-hundred-thousand you need - and the factor of 100 that says what the new trip must do. This is how "how safe is safe enough" stops being an argument between confident people and becomes a number that can be checked, challenged and signed. And it is how a second alarm that everyone believed in gets caught before it is bought - while there is still a fitter up that ladder. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
