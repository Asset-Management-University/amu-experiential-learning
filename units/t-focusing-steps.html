Everyone's flat out - so why aren't we serving more people?

T1: The Ache

Everyone is flat out. Phones ring, people move, there's a constant hum of work - and you keep saying, "let's work smarter, let's improve!" So you do: a faster van here, a tidier stockroom there, a new spreadsheet. The improvements are real. And yet the number that actually matters - the people you serve, the problems you truly solve - barely moves. Effort everywhere; mission stuck.

It is a maddening, almost soul-crushing feeling, and it can make you doubt yourself. But it isn't a sign you're failing. It's a sign your effort is aimed at the wrong target: you're straining to strengthen a whole chain when only one link is holding you back. The hidden truth is that, right now, just ONE thing is limiting how much of your mission you can deliver - and almost all your hard work is going somewhere else, some of it even creating busywork that serves no one. This technique is how you find that one link, and put your energy where it actually frees the mission.

We're working harder than ever - so why aren't we serving any more people than before?

Before you read on, decide. Everybody is flat out. The improvements you made are real ones. And the number that actually matters — the people you serve — has not moved. Write down what you would do next. Your own answer, in your own words, not a choice from a list.

Now compare — the four answers, and what each one really does

• “Work harder. Everyone must push more.” They are already pushing. And here is the uncomfortable part: pushing harder in the wrong place does not just fail to help — it makes things worse, because the extra work piles up somewhere as half-finished jobs that serve nobody.

• “Improve everything we can, everywhere.” This is what you have been doing, and every one of those improvements was genuine. A faster van, a tidier store, a better spreadsheet — all real. And if the one thing holding you back was not any of them, the number does not move. You have been strengthening a whole chain when only one link is holding.

• “Buy something. We need more capacity.” Sometimes true, and it is the most expensive place to start. Most people spend money on capacity they already had and were simply wasting — and you cannot know which until you have found the one thing that is actually setting the pace.

• “Keep everyone busy — idle people are wasted money.” The hardest belief to give up, and it is the one doing the damage. Somebody working flat out on something that does not help is not producing value; they are producing waste, and it costs fuel, materials and their goodwill.

Hold on to the thing you already know and have not yet used: you are working harder than ever and serving no more people. That is not a sign you are failing. It is a sign your effort is aimed at the wrong target — and that there is a target.

T2: The Tool in Action

Make it real. Muhammad's mission with Tank You is simple: get cooking gas to families so they can have a hot meal - that's the problem he solves, and his real output is families served each day. He, Sipho and Thabo are working all hours with a good fleet of bakkies, yet they're stuck at about 30 families a day. Why? They walk the five steps:

1. Find the limit. "What one thing stops us serving the 31st family?" Not the vans - they often sit waiting. Not the gas. Watching the whole flow, it's clear: Muhammad's single phone. Every order must pass through him to be taken, confirmed and scheduled - so intake is capped at ~30, even though the fleet could deliver ~50.

2. Squeeze it dry. Before buying anything: "How do we get the most from that phone right now?" From 8am to 4pm, Muhammad does nothing but answer the order phone; every other call - suppliers, admin - goes to Ibraaheem. They protect the limit's time fiercely. Intake jumps to ~40 a day, not a cent spent.

3. Serve the limit (stop the busywork). The hard step. Sipho and Thabo feel restless parked - they want to be doing. But their job is no longer "to drive"; it's "to serve the limit": wait for a confirmed, paid order, then go. They stop driving around "looking for customers", which was just burning fuel and creating waste. Their pace is now set by real orders, not restlessness.

4. Lift the limit. Only now, with the phone still ringing off the hook, do they spend: a second order-phone, with Ibraaheem trained to take orders too. Intake capacity rises to about 65 a day.

5. Find the new limit. Suddenly orders flood in - but the fleet can only deliver ~50 a day. The bottleneck has moved: it's now the vans, not the phone. So they begin again - and they drop the old rule ("the phone is our problem"), because it isn't any more.

T3: The Blueprint

This is a five-step loop you can run on any system that serves people - a business, a clinic, a kitchen, a charity, even your own day. The aim is always the same: deliver more of your mission.

  1. Find the limit. Look at the whole flow and find the single weakest link - the one spot that sets the pace for everything else. Ask: if I could improve just one part, which would let me serve more people today? That's your limit.
  2. Squeeze it dry. Before you spend or hire, get the absolute most from the limit as it is: protect its time, stop it doing work others could do, never let it sit idle.
  3. Serve the limit (stop creating waste). The most powerful, most counter-intuitive step: line every other part up to serve the limit's pace. If they race ahead, they only pile up half-finished work and busywork - that isn't efficiency, it's waste. Subordinate everything to what truly serves the mission.
  4. Lift the limit. Only after squeezing (2) and aligning everything to support it (3) - if it's still the limit - do you invest to raise its capacity.
  5. Find the new limit. Lift one limit and the bottleneck moves elsewhere; a new weakest link appears. Go straight back to step 1 - and beware inertia: don't keep obeying the old rules you made for the old limit.

T5: The Guardrails

As you use this tool, hold to these truths - they keep you from going astray:

  • The limit always moves. Fix one and another appears - that's not failure, it's progress. The aim is a stronger chain, not a chain with no weakest link.
  • Improving a non-limit is a mirage. Making a part that isn't the bottleneck "better" adds nothing to your mission - it just creates more sophisticated waste. This is why working harder so often doesn't work.
  • "Serve" is not "abandon". Subordinating the other parts doesn't mean neglecting them; it means their success is now measured by how well they support the limit, not by their own busyness.
  • Money is the shadow, not the sun. The goal is to serve the mission - to solve real problems for real people. The money that follows is only the measure that you're solving them well; never mistake the measure for the aim. Aim only at money and you may build what no one needs; aim at the problem worth solving, and sustainable money follows.
  • You are not lazy - your focus was scattered. This technique honours your hard work; it simply gives it a single point to push against, turning effort into leverage.

So whenever it feels like you're pouring all you have into a bucket that never fills, stop pouring faster. Find the one hole. Serve the mission through it. Then find the next. You have the will and the energy already - now you have the focus.
