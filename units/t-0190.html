Step 1 of 5

The liability that outlives your career

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna. The old sludge lagoons and the original filter house come out of service next year, after thirty-four years, and you have been asked for the decommissioning plan.

You write a good one. Demolition sequence, isolations, waste routes, contractor scope. Eighteen months, R28.4 million. It is a proper project plan and you are pleased with it.

Then somebody asks what happens in year three.

And year ten. And year thirty - because the lagoon liner is still down there, the groundwater still has to be monitored, the cap still has to shed water, and the site is still the works' responsibility long after the last contractor has driven out of the gate.

You have planned the part that looks like a project. You have not planned the part that is longer than your career.

The demolition is eighteen months and the obligation is thirty years. Who owns that, who funds it, and what does a maintenance plan for a capped lagoon and a buried liner even look like?

Before you read on, decide. The demolition is eighteen months: contractors, programme, completion date, all of it properly planned. Then there is the rest. The lagoon liner is staying where it is, because it cannot be taken out. So the groundwater has to be monitored, the cap has to go on shedding water, and the site remains this works' responsibility at year five, year ten, and year thirty — long after the last contractor has driven out of the gate and long after you have retired. Somebody in the meeting called that part “not really the project”, quite reasonably. Write down how you would plan this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question most closure plans skip

• “Deliver the demolition and hand the site over.” Hand it to whom? The obligation does not transfer because the project closed; it simply becomes nobody's, which is how it will be discovered — by somebody in fifteen years with no budget and no context.

• “Budget for the monitoring in the annual operating cost.” Sensible for year one and defenceless thereafter. An annual line with no protection is exactly what a future budget round reallocates in a difficult year, and nobody will be there to argue for it.

• “Dig the liner out and remove the problem.” If it were feasible it would be the right answer. It is not, and a plan that quietly assumes an ending it cannot achieve is worse than one that admits the obligation is permanent.

• “Write a thirty-year maintenance schedule.” Necessary and insufficient. A schedule with no named owner and no protected funding is a document, and documents do not survive three decades of staff turnover on their own.

• “Deal with it nearer the time.” The decision is being made now regardless: what gets capped, how, and to what standard determines what the next thirty years cost and whether walking away is ever possible.

• Everything downstream depends on a question most closure plans skip: what is this place for afterwards? The honest answers differ enormously. Returned to a natural landform, aiming at eventually handing the land back and ending the obligation. Redeveloped for another use, where a legacy may be tolerable under controls but constrains what can ever be built there. Or retained in place under permanent control — which is the honest answer for containment that cannot be removed, and it is your lagoon. Note you have two different end states on one site, and they generate completely different plans. State each as a testable condition rather than an aspiration: not that the site will be safe and rehabilitated, but the landform, the limits, the use it must support, and the criteria that would let the works walk away — and say plainly where walking away is not available. Then the two things that decide whether any of this survives: a named accountability that persists across decades of turnover, and funding in a form a future budget round cannot quietly reallocate.

Here is the end-of-life plan.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the plan - the end state, the disposal, the rehabilitation, the care regime nobody expects, and the ownership that has to outlive everyone who wrote it.

1. State the closure objective and the end state - what is this land and these structures FOR afterwards?

EVERY DECISION DOWNSTREAM OF THIS BOX DEPENDS ON IT, AND MOST CLOSURE PLANS SKIP IT. WHAT IS THIS SITE FOR AFTERWARDS? The honest answers differ enormously in cost, duration and risk. RETURNED TO A NATURAL OR AGRICULTURAL LANDFORM, with the intention of eventual RELINQUISHMENT - handing the land back and ending the obligation. REDEVELOPED for another industrial or municipal use, where a contaminated legacy may be tolerable under controls but constrains what can ever be built. RETAINED IN PLACE UNDER PERMANENT CONTROL, which is the honest answer for containment that cannot be removed. AT CORATHYL: the filter house is to be demolished and the platform reused for the new dosing plant; THE SLUDGE LAGOONS ARE TO BE CAPPED AND RETAINED, because the liner and its contents are not going anywhere. TWO DIFFERENT END STATES ON ONE SITE, AND THEY GENERATE COMPLETELY DIFFERENT PLANS. STATE THE END STATE AS A TESTABLE CONDITION rather than an aspiration: not 'the site will be safe and rehabilitated' but the landform, the contamination limits, the land use it must support, and the criteria that would let the organisation walk away. AND BE HONEST WHERE WALKING AWAY IS NOT AVAILABLE - because a plan that quietly assumes relinquishment when the contamination makes it impossible has mis-stated the organisation's obligation by decades. THE END STATE IS ALSO WHERE THE PEOPLE BELONG. Closure ends jobs. A just transition for the workforce and the host community is a CLOSURE OBJECTIVE, stated here alongside the landform, not appended later.

2. The disposal plan - sequence, isolation, waste routes and what is deliberately left

THIS IS THE PART THAT LOOKS LIKE A PROJECT, AND IT IS THE PART EVERYONE PLANS WELL. FOUR THINGS. THE SEQUENCE AND THE ISOLATIONS: the order of shutdown, drain-down, de-energisation and dismantling, and THE POINT AT WHICH EACH ISOLATION BECOMES PERMANENT rather than operational - because a temporary isolation left in place for thirty years is not an isolation, it is a hope. THE WASTE ROUTES: every stream named, with its classification, its lawful destination and the duty that attaches to it - and note that a duty of care on hazardous waste typically follows the producer beyond the gate, so 'removed from site' is not the end of the obligation. VALUE RECOVERY: what is worth selling, reusing or scrapping, and what recovering it costs - because value recovery is often assumed and rarely nets what the plan promised once handling and certification are counted. AND FOURTH, THE THING MOST OFTEN OMITTED: WHAT IS DELIBERATELY LEFT IN PLACE, AND WHY. Buried pipework, foundations, a lined lagoon, a capped cell. LEAVING SOMETHING IS A LEGITIMATE ENGINEERING DECISION AND AN UNDOCUMENTED ONE IS A TRAP FOR WHOEVER DIGS THERE IN 2050. Record what remains, where, and what it means for the site's future use - that record is a deliverable of this plan, not a by-product.

3. The rehabilitation plan - landform, contamination, and the people

REHABILITATION IS WHERE THE END STATE IS ACTUALLY BUILT. THE LANDFORM: profiles, drainage, cover depths and stability, designed for the land use named in box 1 - and drainage is the one that fails quietly, because a landform that ponds will defeat a cap that was otherwise sound. THE CONTAMINATION: what is treated, what is removed, what is contained, and the verification that proves it, against limits stated as numbers. AND THE DISCIPLINE THAT SAVES THE MOST MONEY AND IS ALMOST ALWAYS SKIPPED: PROGRESSIVE REHABILITATION. Closure plans incorporate progressive rehabilitation to reduce the impact on disturbed areas AND TO SUPPORT EVENTUAL RELINQUISHMENT. Rehabilitating an area as it goes out of use, DURING operation, is cheaper because the plant and people are already on site, and it is more provable because you accumulate a record of vegetation established and limits met over years rather than presenting an assertion at the end. Deferring everything to closure means doing it all at once, with no operating budget, at the moment the organisation has least appetite to spend. NOW THE PEOPLE, AND THIS IS NOT A COURTESY PARAGRAPH. CLOSURE ENDS JOBS, and a plan that ignores that is not comprehensive. You will not write the socio-economic transition plan yourself - it needs expertise you do not have - BUT IT IS YOUR DUTY TO ENSURE IT IS COMMISSIONED, INTEGRATED AND RESOURCED as a formal part of the closure programme, on the same schedule and in the same document as the landform. Closure planning covers land rehabilitation AND socio-economic transition, planned as early as feasible - and as early as feasible means while there is still time for retraining to mean something.

4. The post-closure care and maintenance plan - a safe inert state, not function

A DECOMMISSIONED ASSET STILL NEEDS MAINTENANCE, AND THIS IS THE PLAN NOBODY EXPECTS TO WRITE. AFTER THE ACTIVE EXECUTION PHASE - the high-intensity work of reshaping landforms and rehabilitating land - THE SITE ENTERS A POST-CLOSURE PHASE WHICH IS A MONITORING AND MAINTENANCE PHASE. POST-CLOSURE CARE CONSISTS OF MONITORING AND MAINTAINING THE CONTAINMENT SYSTEMS AND MONITORING GROUNDWATER TO ENSURE MATERIAL IS NOT ESCAPING. HOW LONG? In waste-containment regulation, POST-CLOSURE CARE MUST CONTINUE FOR AT LEAST 30 YEARS AFTER CLOSURE, extendable or shortenable on site-specific conditions. That figure comes from waste containment and is not a universal legal rule - but TAKE IT AS THE CONSERVATIVE BENCHMARK for any closure involving containment integrity or long-term contamination potential, because the principle it carries is the one that matters: THE OBLIGATION IS MULTI-GENERATIONAL, NOT A THREE-YEAR TAIL. NOW THE PART THAT REQUIRES YOU TO UNLEARN SOMETHING. EVERY MAINTENANCE INSTINCT YOU HAVE IS THE WRONG ONE HERE. You are not maintaining availability, reliability or production - THERE IS NO PRODUCTION. YOU ARE MAINTAINING A SAFE INERT STATE, and the failure modes that matter are the slow ones nobody is watching: cap erosion and settlement, drainage blockage, vegetation roots breaching a liner, burrowing animals, fence and signage decay, monitoring wells silting up, and the isolation that was never made permanent. Write the regime accordingly - inspection frequencies keyed to those modes, trigger levels that require action, and monitoring with a stated method and limit. AND STATE THIS PLAINLY IN THE PLAN: AN UNMAINTAINED DECOMMISSIONED ASSET IS NOT A BENIGN RUIN; IT IS AN UNMONITORED LIABILITY AND A POTENTIAL PUBLIC HAZARD. THE FAILURE TO MAINTAIN A SAFE INERT STATE CONSTITUTES NEGLIGENCE.

5. Ownership, funding and handover across decades - who holds this when everyone has gone?

THIRTY YEARS IS LONGER THAN ANY CAREER IN THE ROOM, SO THE PLAN MUST SURVIVE ITS AUTHORS. FOUR THINGS, AND THE FIRST IS THE ONE THAT DECIDES WHETHER ANY OF THIS HAPPENS. THE OWNER: A NAMED ROLE, NOT A NAMED PERSON AND NOT A DEPARTMENT THAT MAY BE REORGANISED. Over thirty years the obligation will change hands roughly four times, and each handover is where it gets lost - so specify what transfers with it: the plan, the as-left record from box 2, the monitoring history and the trigger levels. THE FUNDING, AND HERE IS WHY THIS BOX EXISTS. Corathyl's closure execution is R28.4 MILLION over eighteen months. The post-closure care is R1.35 MILLION A YEAR FOR 30 YEARS - R40.5 million undiscounted, a PRESENT VALUE OF R18.58 MILLION at 6 per cent. THE TOTAL OBLIGATION IS R46.98 MILLION, OF WHICH THE CARE PHASE IS 39.6 PER CENT. A provision that covers only the visible execution work therefore UNDERSTATES THE OBLIGATION BY R18.58 MILLION - and it is invisible precisely because it is not a project: it has no start date anyone remembers, no completion, and nobody presenting it at a capital meeting. THE REVIEW: when the plan is revisited and on what trigger, because a thirty-year plan written once is a thirty-year plan written wrongly. AND THE RELINQUISHMENT CONDITIONS: THE CRITERIA THAT WOULD LET THIS OBLIGATION END OR TRANSFER - stated as measurable conditions, since once final monitoring and maintenance is complete the cleared site and remaining infrastructure may be handed to external parties to finalise the post-closure programme. WITHOUT WRITTEN EXIT CRITERIA THE LIABILITY NEVER ENDS, because nobody is ever willing to be the person who declared it finished.

Box 4 is the one that requires you to unlearn something, and box 5 is where the money actually is. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Planning for closure means planning for a liability that will outlive your career, governed by a maintenance regime that is the opposite of the one you use today. Five questions:

  • What is this site for afterwards? The end state, as a testable condition - and an honest answer where walking away is not available. Two end states on one site generate two different plans.
  • How does it come apart? The disposal plan - sequence, permanent isolations, waste routes with the duty that follows them, value recovery, and what is deliberately left in place.
  • How is the end state built? The rehabilitation plan - landform, contamination, progressive rather than deferred, and a just transition for the people whose work ends.
  • What does a dead asset still need? A safe inert state, not function - and the slow failure modes nobody is watching.
  • Who holds this in 2050? Named owner, funding, review, and written relinquishment conditions.

The arithmetic that reframes the whole job. Thirty-four years of operation, eighteen months of decommissioning, thirty years of post-closure care: a 65.5-year obligation of which the care phase is 45.8 per cent and earns nothing. The care period is twenty times the execution period and 0.88 times the entire operating life. In money: execution R28.4m, care R1.35m a year for thirty years - R40.5m undiscounted, a present value of R18.58m. Total obligation R46.98m, of which care is 39.6 per cent. A provision covering only the visible execution work understates the obligation by R18.58 million, and it is invisible precisely because it is not a project - no start date anyone remembers, no completion, nobody presenting it at a capital meeting.

The part you have to unlearn. Every maintenance instinct you have been trained with is the wrong one here. You are not maintaining availability, reliability or production - there is no production. You are maintaining a safe inert state, and the failure modes that matter are the slow ones nobody watches: cap erosion and settlement, blocked drainage, roots breaching a liner, burrowing animals, decayed fencing and signage, silted monitoring wells, and the isolation that was only ever temporary. Inspection frequencies key to those, not to a production calendar.

And the sentence that belongs in the plan itself. An unmaintained decommissioned asset is not a benign ruin; it is an unmonitored liability and a potential public hazard. The failure to maintain a safe inert state constitutes negligence. The thirty-year benchmark comes from waste-containment regulation rather than from a universal legal rule - take it as the conservative anchor for any closure involving containment integrity, because the principle it carries is the one that matters: the obligation is multi-generational, not a three-year tail.

One honest boundary. You plan the end of life. You do not decide to close the asset, you do not sign the environmental authorisation, and you do not write the socio-economic transition plan yourself - but it is your duty to ensure that one is commissioned, integrated and resourced on the same schedule as the landform.

Does it scale? A demolished building is a project. A closed landfill is a promise. The difference is not size or cost - it is that one ends when the work stops and the other only ends when somebody with authority is willing to say, in writing, that it is safe to stop watching.

T4: Your Turn

An old inner-city gasworks site is coming out of use. The end state is contested, and you are not being asked to choose it.

PartyWhat they want
The asset ownerThe cheapest path to regulatory relinquishment
The city councilThe land remediated to support high-density residential development
A community heritage groupThe retort house and gasholder frame preserved within a public park
Site factsDetail
ContaminationCoal tar and heavy metals through the made ground, depth uncertain
StructuresRetort house and gasholder frame, both deteriorating, both unlisted
EmploymentEleven staff, most with more than twenty years of service
AdjacentHomes on two boundaries; a watercourse on the third
  1. Three end states. State each as a testable condition, not an aspiration.
  2. Three plans in outline. For each end state, sketch the disposal, rehabilitation and care implications.
  3. The care divergence. Which end state carries the longest obligation, which the shortest, and why?
  4. The heritage structures. What does preserving a deteriorating structure do to box 4 that demolishing it does not?
  5. The board paper. One page. What must the board see to choose honestly?

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the temptation is to rank the three options and recommend one. Resist it. Your task is to make the consequences of each visible so the people with the authority can choose - and a planner who quietly recommends the cheapest has made a decision about a community's land that was not theirs to make.

Second, the eleven staff are a primary consideration, not a footnote. The board paper must show how the choice of end state affects the viability of a just transition. A rapid demolition and exit offers little time for retraining and redeployment; a longer, phased remediation or preservation project creates different opportunities. The socio-economic transition plan is not an appendix; its feasibility and cost are part of the choice.

Third, step 3 usually surprises. The residential end state looks most expensive and it is - but it may carry the shortest ongoing obligation, because remediating to a residential standard can remove the contamination rather than contain it, which is what makes relinquishment possible. The cheapest option, containment in place, buys a low execution cost and a permanent care obligation. The cheapest path to relinquishment may be the one that never relinquishes anything, and putting that in front of a board is the most valuable thing you will do here.

Fourth, step 4 is the one groups skip. Preserving the structures converts a demolition into an indefinite structural maintenance obligation on deteriorating fabric next to housing - inspection regime, condition monitoring, a repair budget, and eventually a decision nobody wants to make about a structure the community now loves. That is a legitimate choice and it must be costed as a permanent care line in box 4, not as a one-off preservation project. A heritage outcome the organisation cannot fund in year twenty is not a heritage outcome; it is a slower demolition with a public argument attached.

T5: The Guardrails

Closure plans fail by stopping where the project stops.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Planning the demolition and not the decades. Eighteen months is the visible part. Thirty years is the obligation, and it is 39.6 per cent of the money.

• Assuming decommissioned means finished. An unmaintained decommissioned asset is not a benign ruin; it is an unmonitored liability and a potential public hazard. The failure to maintain a safe inert state constitutes negligence.

• Applying an operating maintenance regime to a shut asset. There is no availability to protect. Key the inspections to cap erosion, drainage, root intrusion and silted wells.

• A temporary isolation left permanently. Thirty years on, it is not an isolation, it is a hope.

• Deferring all rehabilitation to the end. Progressive rehabilitation is cheaper while plant and people are on site, and it accumulates the evidence that limits were met.

• No record of what was left in place. Buried pipework and foundations are a legitimate decision and an undocumented trap for whoever digs there in 2050.

• An end state stated as an aspiration. "Safe and rehabilitated" cannot be tested, so nobody can ever say the obligation is discharged.

• No relinquishment conditions. Without written exit criteria the liability never ends, because nobody will volunteer to be the person who declared it finished.

• An obligation owned by a department. Departments get reorganised. Name the role, and specify what transfers with it at each handover.

• Assuming relinquishment is available. Where contamination must stay, it is not - and a plan that assumes otherwise mis-states the organisation's obligation by decades.

• Treating the residual liability as a finance problem. The provision is finance's. The failure modes, the trigger levels and the monitoring are yours.

• Leaving the people to the end. Closure ends jobs. As early as feasible means while retraining still has time to mean something.

Onalenna planned eighteen months and owned thirty years. State the closure objective and the end state as a testable condition, including an honest answer where walking away is not available; write the disposal plan with its permanent isolations, its waste duties and a record of what is deliberately left behind; build the end state through progressive rehabilitation rather than deferring it all, and make sure the transition for the people whose work ends is commissioned and resourced on the same schedule as the landform; write the care and maintenance plan for a safe inert state, keyed to the slow failure modes nobody watches; and name the role that owns the obligation, the funding that carries it, and the measurable conditions that would let it end. Decommissioning is not the end of an asset's life. It is the start of a liability with no revenue attached - and the plan you write is the only thing that will still be speaking for the site when everyone who wrote it has gone. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, find out who in your organisation owns the obligation for the last thing it shut down.
