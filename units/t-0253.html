The front door and the hub

T1: The Ache

Information in your organisation does not move. Faults are reported from the field and logged - and then nothing happens, so the same problem is reported again next month from a different site, and no one ever connects the dots. Reality goes in and disappears.

And the knowledge that could fix it is stuck in one person's head. Your best expert on the high-lift pumps is at the main works, and when he is on leave, or the day he retires, everyone else is lost. Your organisation cannot hear the reality at its edges, and it cannot answer with what it already knows. Every site fails alone, and fails the same way, again and again.

Field reality vanishes into a black hole and expertise stays trapped in one head - so how should information actually flow through an asset organisation, and what mechanisms carry it?

Before you read on, decide. Three weeks ago the operator at the Kloof outstation told somebody about a new noise on the transfer pump. He is not sure who — the area supervisor, possibly, or the fitter who was passing. It was never written down anywhere, nobody came, and the pump failed on Tuesday. When you asked him about it he shrugged; he did not complain, and you could see he will not mention the next one. Meanwhile Johan at the main works is the only person who understands these pumps, and Kloof has just spent two days on a fault he diagnosed at another station in 2021 — which nobody else knew about. Write down how information should flow through this organisation — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two flows

• “Tell people to report faults properly.” He did report it — to somebody, out loud, which is what reporting looks like when there is nowhere for it to go. Asking harder without giving it a destination produces the same shrug.

• “Put Johan's knowledge in a manual.” Captures some of it and leaves the second flow with no mechanism: a manual answers the question somebody knows to ask, and Kloof did not know that 2021 fault existed.

• “Have the outstations phone me directly.” Works while you are available and makes you the single point of failure — and when you are on leave, the reports go back to being mentioned to whoever is passing.

• “Buy a maintenance system.” Software gives you somewhere to record things and does not decide who receives them, who routes them, or who closes them out. A system nobody owns collects what people remember to enter.

• “We are too small for departments and centres.” Fair, and it confuses a function with a box on a chart. In a works your size the hub might be one person for a few hours a week — what matters is that somebody owns the flow.

• The system lives or dies on information moving through it, and it runs in two directions. Upward: the reality at the edge — a new noise, a fault, a complaint, a reading — has to be captured and turned into a record the organisation can act on, or the whole thing is flying blind. Outward: the scarce expertise and hard-won standards at the centre have to reach every site and technician, or the best practice stays trapped and every site re-learns the same lesson the hard way — which is precisely what Kloof just spent two days doing. Two flows, and each needs its own mechanism. The upward one needs a single, known front door: one place where every fault, request and reading is captured, logged, routed and tracked to closure — because information with several possible destinations reliably arrives at none, and a report that is made and not recorded teaches the reporter not to bother, which you watched happen. The outward one needs a hub that carries expertise and standards out, so what Johan knows becomes what the organisation knows before he retires. And note that both are functions rather than boxes — the question is not whether you can afford a department, but who owns each flow.

A healthy asset management system listens to its edges through its service desk, and answers with shared capability through its centre of excellence. Let us see how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how information should move through an asset organisation - both ways at once - and the two mechanisms that carry it: the service desk (once called the call centre) that brings reality up from the field, and the centre of excellence that carries expertise and standards back out to it.

1. Information flows both ways

The first thing to understand is that the asset management system LIVES OR DIES on the information moving through it, and that information runs in TWO DIRECTIONS. UP: the reality at the edge - a pump making a new noise, a fault, a community complaint, a reading - has to be captured and turned into a record the system can act on, OR THE WHOLE SYSTEM IS FLYING BLIND. OUT: the scarce expertise and the hard-won standards held at the centre have to reach every site and every technician, OR THE BEST PRACTICE STAYS TRAPPED and every site re-learns the same lesson the hard way. These are two flows, and each needs a mechanism. Note that both are FUNCTIONS, NOT FIXED BOXES on a chart: a small organisation might have one person doing the hub part-time; a large one a whole department.

2. The service desk - the front door (reality up)

The SERVICE DESK (the call centre, by an older name) is the SINGLE, KNOWN POINT OF CONTACT where information from the field and the users flows IN. Every fault, request, complaint and reading goes to ONE PLACE where it is captured, logged, routed to the right hands, and tracked to closure. Its role is to turn the organisation's scattered reality into STRUCTURED RECORDS IT CAN ACT ON - and, just as importantly, IT IS HOW THE ORGANISATION LISTENS. When an operator reports a recurring problem or a community reports a water fault, the desk is the ear that hears them and honours what they said by turning it into something the system will act on. It is the front door through which the organisation finally hears its assets and its people.

3. The centre of excellence - the hub (expertise out)

The CENTRE OF EXCELLENCE is the other mechanism, and it carries the OPPOSITE FLOW. It is a concentrated home for the organisation's scarce specialist capability and its standards and best practice - the two or three people who know the reliability craft deepest, and the agreed way of doing things. From that hub, expertise and consistency flow OUT to the whole organisation: it answers the hard questions from every site, sets the common standards, and spreads what one site learns to all. Its role is to turn RARE KNOWLEDGE, which would otherwise sit in one head, INTO A SHARED STANDARD everyone can use - so the organisation SHARES its deepest knowledge with everyone who needs it, rather than letting it be hoarded or lost.

4. Each fails its own way - and it is the system's, not a person's

A service desk fails when it becomes a BLACK HOLE: a place complaints go to die, logged and never acted on, so people stop reporting and the system goes DEAF. THAT IS A BROKEN PROCESS, NOT THE FAULT OF THE PEOPLE STAFFING IT. A centre of excellence fails when it becomes an IVORY TOWER: hoarding expertise, issuing standards no one on the ground believes in or can use, disconnected from the reality of the work. THAT IS A FAILURE OF ORGANISATIONAL DESIGN, NOT THE ARROGANCE OF THE EXPERTS. Naming the failure honestly as the SYSTEM'S is what lets you fix it, rather than blame someone.

5. Make it a loop - learn once, improve everywhere

The two mechanisms are only FULLY REAL WHEN THEY ARE JOINED INTO A LOOP. The service desk carries the field's reality UP to where the expertise sits: the recurring problems it captures are exactly what the centre of excellence needs to hear to set the right standard. And the centre of excellence carries the expertise and the standard back DOWN to the field, often through the desk itself: a fix discovered at one plant becomes the answer given to every plant. AN ORGANISATION WITH BOTH, WORKING AND JOINED, LEARNS ONCE AND IMPROVES EVERYWHERE - instead of every site failing alone and repeatedly, deaf to its own edges and mute with its own knowledge.

T3: The Blueprint

The service desk is how the organisation hears its assets and its people; the centre of excellence is how it answers them with a single, expert voice. One without the other is a system that is either deaf or mute. A healthy asset management system listens to its edges and answers with shared capability. Five questions:

  • Which way does information flow? Both ways. Reality up from the field, and expertise and standards out to it. The asset management system is only as good as the information moving through it.
  • What brings reality up? The service desk - the front door. A single, known point of contact where field and user information is captured, logged, routed, and closed. It turns scattered reality into records the system can act on.
  • What carries expertise out? The centre of excellence - the hub. A concentrated home for scarce expertise and the organisation's standards, from which best practice flows to every site. It turns rare knowledge into a shared standard.
  • How does each fail? The black hole and the ivory tower. A desk that logs and forgets; a hub that hoards and dictates. Each is a system failure, not a person's fault.
  • What makes them work? Close the loop. The desk feeds the centre what it hears; the centre answers back through the desk - so the organisation learns once and improves everywhere.

Does it scale? This is how information should move in any group that must learn - a team, a network of clinics, a family business with several branches. Give it a front door where reality is genuinely heard and acted on, and a hub where hard-won knowledge is shared rather than hoarded, and join the two so what is learned in one place is known everywhere. An organisation that can hear its edges and answer with what it knows is one that gets better; one that can do neither repeats its mistakes forever.

T4: Your Turn

You are the asset manager for a growing utility that has just taken on its third water-treatment plant. The new sites report faults to their local supervisors, but the information goes no further. And the one real expert on high-lift pump maintenance is based at the main Corathyl works, and the new sites cannot get his time. Your job is to make the information flow.

  1. The service desk. Describe the role of the service desk you would establish. What makes it a front door for information, not a black hole?
  2. The centre of excellence. Describe the role of the centre-of-excellence function you would create. What makes it a hub for sharing expertise, not an ivory tower?
  3. The loop. Explain how the two must form a loop - what specific information flows from the desk to the centre, and what flows back.
  4. The cost. What does it cost the utility if this loop is never closed?

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the service desk: a single, known point where every site sends its faults, readings and requests, and where each one is logged, routed to the right team, tracked, and closed with the site told what happened. What keeps it from being a black hole is that everything captured is acted on and fed back - people keep reporting only because reporting visibly leads to something. It makes the organisation able to hear all three plants at once.

Second, the centre of excellence: the high-lift pump expert (and a peer or two) becomes a recognised hub whose job is not only to fix pumps at Corathyl but to answer the other sites' questions, set the common maintenance standard for those pumps, and spread what he knows. What keeps it from being an ivory tower is that his time is deliberately given to the field and his standards are built from the real problems the sites report - so they are usable, and believed.

Third, the loop: from the desk to the centre flows the pattern - "high-lift pump faults are recurring across all three sites"; from the centre back to the desk and the field flows the answer - a standard maintenance procedure, and expert guidance the desk can pass to whoever raises the next pump fault. The desk tells the centre what is really happening; the centre tells the field what to do about it.

Fourth, the cost of never closing it: the same pump failures recur at every site, unconnected and unlearned; the expert stays a single point of failure whose knowledge retires with him; and the new plants keep re-learning, expensively and sometimes dangerously, lessons the organisation already knew. The utility pays, again and again, for being deaf at its edges and mute with its own knowledge.

T5: The Guardrails

The service desk is how the organisation hears its assets and its people; the centre of excellence is how it answers them at scale - and each is only real if the information actually moves.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• A service desk that is a black hole. Logging without acting kills reporting and deafens the organisation. What comes in must be routed, closed, and fed back - or people stop telling you the truth. This is a process failure to fix, not a person to blame.

• A centre of excellence that is an ivory tower. Expertise hoarded and standards no one can use help no one. The hub must give its time to the field and build its standards from the field's real problems. This is a design failure to fix, not an expert to resent.

• The two operating in isolation. A desk that never feeds the centre, and a centre that never answers through the desk, are two half-mechanisms. Join them into a loop, or neither works fully.

• Expertise treated as personal power. Knowledge hoarded to stay indispensable makes the holder a single point of failure and the organisation fragile. Shared knowledge multiplies; hoarded knowledge retires with one person.

• A box on the chart mistaken for a working flow. Having a "service desk" on the org chart is not the same as information actually moving. The function is real only when the information genuinely flows and is acted on.

• Forgetting to close the loop with the field. Reality that rises but is never answered, or an answer that never reaches the person who reported the problem, breaks the circuit. Always close back to the edge that raised it.

Your organisation could not hear its own edges, and could not answer with what it already knew - reality vanished into a black hole and expertise stayed locked in one head. Now you can make it flow. Build a front door where the field is genuinely heard and every report is acted on and closed; build a hub where scarce expertise sets usable standards and reaches every site; and join them into a loop so what one place learns, all places know.
