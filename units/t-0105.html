Step 1 of 5

The storm you have not seen yet

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. Years ago, your predecessor built the embankment that protects the works from the river. He was sensible about it: he found the highest flood in thirty years of records - 6.1 metres - and built the wall a clear metre above it. For decades, it held. Every flood season, the water rose, and the wall was more than enough.

Then, one season, a single freak storm - the kind the old-timers call "once in a lifetime" - sends a wall of water over the top. The works floods. The pumps drown. The town is without clean water for weeks, and the repair bill runs into millions.

And here is what haunts you: the embankment did not fail. It performed exactly as it was built to. The problem was the design itself - it was built for the worst flood ever seen, not the worst flood that was actually possible. Thirty years of calm records had hidden a simple, brutal truth: a bigger one was always coming.

I built my defences a margin above the worst I had ever seen - and a once-in-a-lifetime storm destroyed them anyway. How do I design for the rare, violent extreme that has not happened yet, instead of just the worst in my short records?

Before you read on, decide. The river's average annual high-water mark is 4.5 m. In thirty years of records the worst flood reached 6.1 m. The embankment was built to 6.8 m — comfortably above the worst ever seen — and it did exactly what it was built to do. The flood went over it. The works supplies the town's water and the rebuilt embankment will stand for fifty years. Write down how high you would build it now, and how you would justify that height — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what thirty years of records actually tells you

• “Rebuild higher — above this flood, with a bigger margin.” The natural answer, and it is the same method that just failed, applied to a longer record. Next time the record will include this flood and the design will be a margin above that — always one event behind.

• “Get more historical data — go back further than thirty years.” Worth doing and it does not solve it. However long the record, the dangerous part is the rare event that has not yet occurred in it — and by definition it is under-represented in any window.

• “Build as high as the budget allows.” This makes the height a function of this year's money rather than of the risk, which means the wall's adequacy is decided by something with no relationship to floods.

• “Accept it — a once-in-a-lifetime storm is bad luck.” Comfortable and it will not survive the arithmetic below.

• “Move the works.” Sometimes genuinely correct, and it is a different and much larger decision that still requires knowing what it is being protected from.

• Notice what the thirty-year record actually is. It is not the range of what the river can do — it is the largest sample from a short window, and the part of the river's behaviour that matters most is precisely the part least likely to appear in it. So the sizing has to run the other way round: first decide, as a matter of consequence and economics rather than engineering, how rare a failure you are prepared to accept; then estimate how large an event of that rarity would be, from the shape of the extremes rather than from what happened to occur. And then do the arithmetic that usually changes people's minds: over the fifty years this thing will stand, the chance of meeting at least one event you called a once-in-two-hundred-year flood is about 22% — and for a once-in-a-hundred-year flood, about 40%. Rare in any one year is not rare across the life of the thing, and that is the sentence the last embankment was built without.

There is a discipline for sizing things against the extreme. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the design basis Onalenna drew up for the rebuilt embankment - the page that decides how high to build, not from the worst flood seen, but from the worst flood that could come.

1. The average (what NOT to design for)

The river's average annual high-water mark: 4.5 m. The worst flood in 30 years of records: 6.1 m. (You never build a flood wall for the average - and the 30-year worst is still not the worst possible.)

2. The return period (how rare must failure be?)

The works is high-consequence - losing it cuts the town's water for weeks - so we design against a 1-in-200-year flood: a 0.5% chance in any single year. (A choice of economics and safety, not just engineering.)

3. The return level (how big is that rare event?)

An extreme-value analysis of the annual flood maxima puts the 1-in-200-year level - the return level - at 8.2 m. Far above the 4.5 m average, and well past the 6.1 m we have ever recorded. The danger lives in the tail.

4. The over-its-life risk (rare is not rare over decades)

Over the embankment's 50-year life, the chance of meeting at least one 1-in-200-year flood = 1 - (1 - 1/200)^50 = 22%. (And a '1-in-100-year' flood? 1 - (0.99)^50 = 40% over the same 50 years - not rare at all.)

5. The design = return level + margin

Design flood level = the 8.2 m return level + 1.0 m freeboard (a margin for waves and uncertainty) = build the crest to 9.2 m. Sized for the extreme that is coming, not the worst we happen to have seen.

Read down it and feel the shift. The old wall guarded against 6.1 metres - the worst on record. This one is built to 9.2 metres - because the rare 1-in-200-year flood is an 8.2-metre monster, and over the wall's life it is far from impossible. The wall is no longer sized for the past; it is sized for the storm that has not arrived yet. Now let us walk through the thinking.

T3: The Blueprint

This is Extreme-Value Theory: the discipline of designing not for the common event in the middle of your data, but for the rare, dangerous event far out in its tail. Most things in life cluster around an average - but you do not build a flood wall for the river's average depth. That would be madness. You build it for the extreme. Each part of the decision answers a question:

  • Why not the average? Because the average never threatens you - the rare extreme does, and it is far, far larger. Designing for the middle of the data is designing for failure.
  • How rare must failure be? You choose a return period - here, 1-in-200 years - matched to the consequence. Losing the town's water for weeks is severe, so you pick a long return period. This is a decision of economics and safety, not just engineering.
  • How big is that rare event? Its size is the return level, estimated from the tail of the annual maxima - 8.2 metres here, far above anything everyday.
  • Is "1-in-200-year" really rare? Here is the part that stops people cold. A 1-in-100-year flood has a 1% chance each year - so over a 50-year design life, the chance of meeting at least one is 1 - (0.99)50 = 39.5%. Nearly half. Over 100 years it is 63% - more likely than not. A "1-in-100-year" event is not a problem for your grandchildren; it is a coin-flip across a career.
  • So how high do we build? The return level (8.2 m), plus a safety margin - the freeboard (1.0 m) - for waves and for what the maths cannot perfectly know.

Does it scale? Just as Onalenna sizes this wall for a 1-in-200-year flood, a coastal engineer sizes a sea wall for a 1-in-500-year storm surge, and a dam engineer sizes a spillway for a flood rarer still. The greater the consequence of failure, the longer the return period you design for.

T4: Your Turn

Now size your own defences for the rare bad day, not the average one. Think of something that is usually a minor nuisance but occasionally a disaster: your phone dying, transport failing, getting caught in a downpour, running out of cash far from home.

  1. The average day - what is the everyday version (a 10-minute delay, a slightly low battery)? You are not designing for this.
  2. Your return period - how rare a disaster will you guard against? "Once in 50 journeys, I get truly stranded"?
  3. The return level - how bad is that rare event (stranded for two hours; a dead phone when you urgently need it)?
  4. Your design + margin - what buffer sizes you for it? A small emergency cash reserve, a power bank, a packable raincoat. You are building freeboard into your own life.

T5: The Guardrails

Designing for the extreme is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust your design.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Designing for the average. The deadliest trap of all. The average never tests your defences; only the extreme does. Build for the middle of the data and you have built to fail.

• Designing for the recent worst. "The worst in our 30 years of records" feels safe - and it is the exact trap that drowned Corathyl. Short records simply have not yet shown you the big one. The future holds a value larger than your past.

• Misreading the name. Hearing "1-in-100-year" and thinking "not in my time". It is about a 40% chance over a 50-year career and more likely than not over 100 years. Rare per year is common over a lifetime.

• Assuming the past still holds (stationarity). Extreme-value maths assumes the climate that made your records is the climate of the future. With a changing climate, that is breaking down - today's 1-in-200-year flood may be a 1-in-100-year flood by mid-century. Design with that shift in mind, not against a frozen past.

• Over-designing blindly. Choosing an absurd return period the consequence does not justify wastes money that could protect something else. Match the rarity to the real cost of failure - neither reckless nor ruinous.

The wall that failed Onalenna was not badly built - it was built for the wrong storm: the worst that had happened, instead of the worst that could. Now you hold the discipline that gets it right: ignore the average, look hard at the tail, choose a return period worthy of the consequence, and build to that rare event plus a margin. Do that, and your defences will be standing on the day the storm you have never seen finally arrives.
