Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Dimakatso, project director on a new solar farm, and behind you sits 180 hectares of cleared land where 75 megawatts of solar panels are meant to rise. It is a Tuesday morning and you are standing on the gravel access road that is supposed to lead to it. Today, nothing is rising. Three flatbed trucks loaded with steel mounting frames are parked nose-to-tail, engines off, because a line of about forty people from the town stands across the road. Some hold cardboard signs. One reads, in thick black marker, "LOCAL JOBS FIRST." In your hand is the construction schedule you printed last night - the first column says panel installation begins today - and it is going soft and damp where your fingers grip it, because the sun is already hard at 9 a.m. and there is no shade on this road.

Mama Phindile walks toward you. She chairs the community trust and sits on the ward council, and she is not shouting. That is what worries you. She says, quietly, "Dimakatso, your contractor brought in a crew of sixty men from outside this district last week. You promised this town work. Where is it?" You open your mouth to say the project will create jobs - and then you stop, because you remember the number. Of the sixty workers on site, four are from the town. Four.

There is a second number you have been trying not to think about. Your power-purchase agreement with the national grid carries a penalty of R85,000 for every day past the connection deadline, which is now eleven months away. And buried in the licence conditions that let you build at all is a local-content requirement: at least 40% of your project's spend must go to local suppliers and labour, verified at commissioning, or the licence can be pulled. Your procurement manager bought the cheapest mounting frames he could find - they came from a factory overseas. You have not added up what that does to your local-content percentage. You have a feeling it is bad.

Your phone buzzes. It is Vusumuzi, your investor-relations contact, and the message is three words: "Are we delayed?" The trucks are not moving. The crowd is not moving. The penalty clock is running at R85,000 a day, the local-content clock is running toward commissioning, and Mama Phindile is still standing in front of you, waiting for an answer that is not just words.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The penalty clock is loud and the trucks are loaded. "Get the trucks past the crowd - call security, or promise anything, and start installing today." But that treats the community as an obstacle, when they are holding you to a promise the project made and dropped; force the road open and you confirm the broken faith AND still fail the 40% test at commissioning. "It's two separate fires - handle the blockade with PR, handle the licence with procurement." But they are one fire: the money spent outside the district is BOTH why the town is angry and why the local-content test will fail. "Trust the gut feeling that 'it's bad' and just start buying local from here on." But a feeling is not a finding; you do not yet know if you are 5% short or structurally short, and the cure is different for each. You have been treating the blockade and the local-content rule as two problems. They are one. The town is angry BECAUSE the project spent its money outside the district - and that same outside spend is exactly what will fail the 40% local-content test at commissioning. The blockade is not an interruption to the plan; it is the plan's hidden requirement arriving late and loud. So stop asking 'how do I get the trucks past these people?' and ask 'what did this project OWE, to whom, and where is that written down?' Then build the smallest instrument that makes the invisible visible: set a success line first ('if we are on track, when I lay every binding commitment beside what we have actually spent, the local-spend figure will be near 40% and each promise will have an owner who has seen it'), then pull every binding commitment onto one table - the licence conditions (40% local content), the power-purchase agreement (the R85,000/day penalty), and the community benefit agreement your predecessor signed (a 30% local-labour target + a preferred-supplier list). No one person has ever seen all three at once: the procurement manager who bought the cheap frames never saw the community agreement; the contractor who bused in sixty outsiders had a deadline and a budget and nothing about local labour. Each optimised their own line and broke a project-level promise nobody was holding. The real question is not 'how do I move the trucks today?' but 'what did we promise, who owns each promise, and is it built into the plan or sitting in a folder waiting to be discovered at the worst time?'
Five moves get the trucks moving and keep them moving. (1) THE TWO CLOCKS ARE ONE PROBLEM: the blockade and the local-content licence rule share a single root - money spent outside the district - so the blockade IS the licence's hidden requirement arriving late; reframe from 'get the trucks past these people' to 'what did we owe, to whom?'. (2) LAY EVERY BINDING COMMITMENT ON ONE TABLE, success line set first: the licence (40% local content), the PPA (R85,000/day), and the community benefit agreement (30% local labour + a local preferred-supplier list) - three documents, three folders, three owners, never seen together. (3) READ THE REAL NUMBER AND LET IT REDIRECT YOU: you braced for ~35%, but the reconciliation says local spend is R210 million of a R1.1 billion project - about 19%, structurally short, with the big panel and inverter contracts already committed offshore and unrecoverable; that kills 'just buy local from here and we'll pass' and replaces it with recover-what-you-can PLUS an early remediation conversation with the regulator. (4) TREAT OBLIGATIONS AS BINDING BASELINE REQUIREMENTS, as hard as the steel's load rating: local content and community benefit are not soft PR issues - designed into procurement, scheduling and contracts on day one they cost nothing; discovered at the end they cost a blockade, a penalty and a licence at risk. (5) GO EARLY, WITH THE AGREEMENT IN HAND AND A MARKED-UP PLAN, NOT WORDS: take the community benefit agreement back to Mama Phindile with a local-supplier switch for the remaining frames, a local-hire-first rule for the next phases with names and start dates, and a monthly meeting where the trust can SEE the local-spend percentage climb; the trust lifts the blockade in exchange for a verified, written schedule, and the road opens the next morning. The honest numbers: the R6m saved on cheap overseas frames set up an R85,000/day blockade, and the ~R5.9m fix almost exactly erases it - because the obligations were discovered, not designed in.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Dimakatso did not try to win the argument on the road. She did three things, in order, and the order mattered. Five moves:

  • The two clocks are one problem - the blockade is the plan's hidden requirement, arriving late: Dimakatso did not try to win the argument on the road. The first thing she did was stop pretending the two clocks were separate. Standing there, she had been treating the community blockade as one problem and the licence's local-content rule as another. They were the same problem. The town was angry BECAUSE the project had spent its money outside the district - and that same outside spending was exactly what would fail the 40% local-content test at commissioning. The blockade was not an interruption to the plan; it was the plan's hidden requirement arriving late and loud. So she reframed the question. Not 'how do I get the trucks past these people?' - which treats the community as an obstacle - but 'what did this project owe, to whom, and where is that written down?' The people on the road were not in the way of the plan; they were holding the project to a promise the plan had quietly dropped, and the penalty clock and the angry crowd were two faces of the single thing she had failed to build in.
  • Lay every binding commitment on one table, with a success line set first: second, she built the smallest instrument that would make the invisible visible - and set a success line before she read it, because she did not trust her gut feeling that 'it is bad' (a feeling is not a finding). She said it out loud in the site office: 'If we are genuinely on track, then when I lay every binding commitment beside what we have actually spent, the local-spend figure will be near 40% and each promise will have an owner who has seen it. If we are not, the number will be low and the promises will be orphans.' That was her yardstick, written before she looked. Then she pulled three documents already in the building - from three different folders, owned by three different people - onto one table: the GENERATION LICENCE CONDITIONS (40% local content, verified at commissioning, licence at risk); the POWER-PURCHASE AGREEMENT (R85,000 a day, deadline in eleven months); and the COMMUNITY BENEFIT AGREEMENT her predecessor had signed with the town's trust two years earlier (a local-labour target of 30% of construction jobs, and a preferred-supplier list drawn from the district). No single person on the project had ever seen all three at once. The procurement manager who bought the cheap frames had never been shown the community agreement; the contractor who bused in sixty outsiders had a deadline and a budget and nothing about local labour. Each person had optimised their own line - cheapest frames, fastest crew - and every one of those local choices broke a project-level promise nobody was holding.
  • Read the real number, and let it redirect you: she had braced for the instrument to confirm a near-miss - something like 35%, a gap she could close by switching the rest of the frames. The reconciliation said something worse, and it forced a real course-correction. Her actual local spend was R210 million against a R1.1 billion project - about 19%, not 35% - and most of the big-ticket procurement (panels, inverters) was ALREADY committed offshore and could not be unwound. That was the meaningful deviation: the gap was not a near-miss she could trade her way out of, it was STRUCTURAL. Switching every remaining line item still could not reach 40%. The number on the table killed her first plan ('just buy local from here on and we will pass') and replaced it with a harder, honest one: recover everything still recoverable, AND open an early remediation conversation with the regulator rather than gamble on hitting 40% at commissioning. Reading the real number, not her hopeful one, is what redirected her - and the lesson is that a yardstick set in advance only protects you if you let the true figure, however unwelcome, overrule the plan you walked in wanting.
  • Treat obligations as binding baseline requirements, as hard as the steel's load rating: what Dimakatso was really doing has a name. She was treating local-content rules and community obligations as BINDING REQUIREMENTS that must be designed into the project from the start - engineered into procurement, scheduling and contracts - not bolted on at the end. In project work this is building regulatory and social obligations into the project BASELINE, and verifying compliance continuously rather than at the finish line. The licence condition, the penalty clause, and the community agreement were never 'soft' issues for the public-relations team to smooth over later; they were hard requirements, exactly like the grid voltage or the load rating of the steel - and like any requirement, if you do not plan for it, you pay for it later, at the worst possible time. And the honest numbers prove it. To reach 40% she needed R440 million spent locally; she stood at R210 million, about 19%. The recovery came in three pieces. The smallest and most visible: a local supplier could take over the remaining undelivered frames, recovering about R18 million of local spend at an extra cost of about R2.4 million over the cheap overseas price - and on its own that lifts her barely two points, from 19% to about 21%. The painful, larger piece was a top-to-bottom review of every contract NOT yet committed - the civil works, the electrical balance-of-plant, the installation labour - and a rule that all of it now goes to district suppliers and a local-hire-first crew: roughly another R190 million of local spend, which is what actually drags the figure up toward 38%. And it stops there, short of 40%, because the single biggest tranche - the panels and inverters, already signed offshore - cannot be unwound. So the 'cheap' frame decision was never cheap: the R6 million it saved had set up an R85,000-a-day blockade and a licence she could lose, the R2.4 million premium to switch the rest plus about R3.5 million on local-hire training erased that saving outright, and the far larger premium on redirecting R190 million of other work locally, late and under pressure, ate the project's margin. Designed into the baseline on day one - district suppliers in the procurement rules, a local-hire-first crew in the contractor's scope - the same 38-40% would have cost almost nothing extra; obligations are cheapest engineered in and most expensive discovered.
  • Go early, with the agreement in hand and a marked-up plan, not words: third, a marked-up plan on paper is only a promise until the other side accepts it, so before she committed money Dimakatso tested it against the one party who could move the trucks. She went back to Mama Phindile not with words but with the community benefit agreement in her hand and the marked-up plan: a local-supplier switch for the remaining frames; a local-hire-first rule for the next phases with names and start dates; and a standing monthly meeting where the trust could SEE the local-spend percentage climb. Her success line was set in advance: 'If this is real to them, the trust agrees to lift the blockade in exchange for a verified, written schedule - not words.' The trust did. The road opened the next morning - the cheapest possible test of whether the plan was credible, and it passed. The regulator conversation was the second test, and there the result was only partial: she could not promise 40%, so she had to ask for a remediation path instead, going early and honest rather than hoping the gap would pass unnoticed at commissioning. It was not a clean win - the offshore panel and inverter contracts could not be unwound, the project would land near 38-39%, just short, and the margin was gone. But the trucks moved, the promise was being kept in the open where the town could watch it, and the bill was paid in daylight instead of exploding at the finish line. (Capturing the asset's technical scope before you build is a sibling discipline; each team optimising its own line until the whole loses is its own problem.)

See where it lands. The road opens the next morning, the trust watches the local-spend percentage climb at a monthly meeting, and the regulator hears the truth early enough to allow a remediation path instead of pulling the licence at commissioning. A project's regulatory and community obligations - local content, community benefit, a grant's spending split - are not soft issues to be smoothed over at the end. They are binding requirements, as hard as the load rating of the steel, and they are cheapest when engineered into the baseline on day one and most expensive when discovered at the finish line. Gather every commitment onto one table, name who owns each and whose daily target could quietly break it, build it into the budget and schedule and scope, verify it continuously - and if a promise has already slipped, go early, with the agreement in hand and a marked-up plan the other side can watch, not words. The people holding you to a promise are not in the way of the work; they are the work's whole point.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Let's take one step together before you walk on your own. Here is the same deep problem - but it does not look like it. No blockade, no penalty clock, no steel frames. Watch how the disguise works.

See the youth-skills grant that "spent on target" and still failed

Sithembile runs a small youth-skills programme for an NGO, funded by a two-year grant. It is month nine. Her finance officer, Tinyiko, is doing exactly what he was hired to do: keep the spend on track so the money is not sitting unused at year-end. He is proud - the burn rate is healthy, almost bang on target. Then a letter arrives from the funder asking for the mid-term report, and one line stops Sithembile cold: the grant requires that at least 60% of spend goes directly to beneficiary stipends and training, with the rest capped for admin and salaries. She has never looked at the spend that way. When she splits it out, beneficiary spend is sitting at about 41%. The money was used. It was just used on the wrong things - and the funder can claw back the difference.

On the surface this looks like an ordinary money problem - an overspend, a budgeting slip. Test that. First, look for the two clocks. Sithembile has one thing that is loud and soon (the mid-term report, due now) and one thing that is quiet and later (the clawback risk at the final audit). Where do both come from? The same root - money going to the wrong category. The healthy burn rate Tinyiko is so proud of is the EXACT thing that is failing the 60% rule. Just like the cheap overseas frames, the local win (spend on target) is the project-level promise breaking: two clocks, one cause. Now find the person optimising their own line. What is Tinyiko measured on, and has he ever seen the rule he is breaking? He is measured on burn rate, and he has almost certainly never seen the 60%-to-beneficiaries clause - it lives in the grant agreement in Sithembile's drawer, not in his spreadsheet. He is hitting his target AND breaking a promise nobody handed him: Dimakatso's procurement manager, wearing different clothes. And was the promise ever in the plan? If the 60% rule had been a line in Tinyiko's monthly tracker from month one, it would have been impossible to drift to 41% unnoticed. The promise was real but invisible - held in a folder, not built into the monthly numbers - so it was only 'discovered' at the report, late and all at once. Designed in, it would have cost nothing; discovered, it costs a clawback and a hard conversation with the funder. What made this harder than the road case was the disguise: no crowd, no penalty clock ticking in front of her - it FELT like a simple finance question. But strip away the surface and it is the identical structure: a binding obligation, scattered away from the person doing the work, never built into the plan, quietly broken by someone optimising their own line, with the bill deferred to the worst possible moment. The trap does not announce itself. It hides inside a number that looks healthy. Your job is to learn to recognise the shape, however it is dressed.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The trap Dimakatso fell into was not a solar-farm trap. It is this: a project carries obligations to the people and rules around it - written in licences, contracts and agreements - but those obligations are scattered, owned by different people, and never built into the plan, so each team optimises its own line and quietly breaks a promise nobody is holding, and the bill arrives late, all at once, as conflict and penalty. Find it where you are:

  1. Lay your obligations on one table. Think of a project, contract or initiative you are part of that has rules or promises attached from outside - a regulator's conditions, a client's terms, a community or staff agreement, a grant's spending rules, a local-procurement target. Where do those obligations physically live, and has anyone ever laid them all side by side?
  2. Find the people optimising their own line. Who is being measured on cost, or speed, or volume - and could be hitting their target while quietly breaking a promise made somewhere else? What would they need to SEE to stop?
  3. Identify your two clocks. An urgent one (a deadline, a penalty, an angry stakeholder) and an important one (a check, an audit, a verification that comes later). Are they secretly the same problem, the way the blockade and the local-content test were?
  4. Check whether the promise lives in the plan - and if it has slipped, go early. Pick one obligation: is it built into the budget, the schedule, the contract scope - or sitting in a folder, waiting to be 'discovered' at the worst time? If a promise is already broken, what would an honest, early conversation look like - going to the affected party with the agreement in hand and a marked-up plan, rather than with words?
