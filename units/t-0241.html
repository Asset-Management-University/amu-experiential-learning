No single best way

T1: The Ache

You announced the new culture. You wrote the plan - a good one. You held the kickoff meeting, put the posters up, sent the memo. And nothing changed. People nodded, complied where you were watching, and went back to exactly how they always did things the moment you turned around.

Here is what stings: your goal was right, and your plan was sound. What broke it was the method. You reached for the wrong approach - you decreed a change that people had to genuinely believe in, and got public compliance without private belief; or, another time, you tried to gently involve everyone in a crisis that needed a firm and immediate hand, and it drifted. The failure was not in the what. It was in the how you tried to make it happen.

I know what culture we need and I can write the plan - but the moment I try to make it real it stalls, because I keep choosing the wrong way to bring it about. How do I choose the right approach to changing a culture?

Before you read on, decide. You know exactly what has to change and the plan is sound — nobody disputes the diagnosis. It stalls every time. Look at the two attempts behind you. The reporting culture needed people to genuinely believe that raising a problem was safe, and you announced it: from Monday, all near-misses to be reported. You got compliance, and not one person told you anything that mattered. Then the effluent breach needed a firm hand within a week, and you consulted everybody — three workshops, wide input, and it drifted for two months. Both times you were competent and well-meaning. This change is different again in what it asks of people. Write down how you would choose your approach — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two axes underneath

• “Find the method that actually works and use it.” The search that will keep failing. A method that transforms one organisation fails in the next — not because it is bad, but because it was the wrong fit, which is precisely what your own two attempts demonstrate.

• “Be firmer — I was too soft the second time.” Half the lesson, taken as the whole. Applied to the reporting culture, more firmness would have produced more compliance and even less truth.

• “Involve people more — that is what went wrong the first time.” The opposite half, equally partial. Applied to the effluent breach it is what already drifted for two months while a real problem continued.

• “Get better at communicating it.” Both attempts were communicated clearly. People understood exactly what was being asked in each case; what differed was whether the way of asking suited what was being asked.

• “Bring in someone who does this professionally.” They will ask you which approach fits here — and if they do not, they will apply their favourite one, which is the same error with an invoice attached.

• The thing to un-learn is the search for the one correct method. This is not a discipline with a single true path; it is a set of choices, each right in some situations and wrong in others. So stop asking what the best way is and ask what the right way is here — for this change, this urgency, these people. The choice runs on two independent axes. First, who drives it: imposed from the top by authority, which is fast and takes no account of the views of those affected — so it misses what they know and buys compliance rather than belief, which is exactly what your near-miss announcement bought; or worked out with the people who must live it, which is slower and produces genuine ownership. Second, how it unfolds: laid out in advance as a defined sequence, or allowed to develop as understanding grows. Neither end of either axis is correct, and the effluent breach shows the cost of choosing wrongly in the other direction. And in practice the answer is usually a blend — some things held firm because they are genuinely not negotiable, and much left open because the people doing the work know things your plan cannot.

There is no single best way - only the right fit. Let us learn to choose it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you compare the approaches to culture change and choose the right one - reading the two axes of choice, diagnosing your situation, and blending them to fit the change and the people who must live it.

1. No single best way - the approach matters as much as the change

The first thing to un-learn is the search for the one correct method. CHANGE MANAGEMENT IS NOT A RELIGION WITH A SINGLE TRUE PATH; it is a set of choices, each right in some situations and wrong in others. A method that transforms one organisation FAILS in the next - not because it was a bad method, but because it was the wrong FIT. So stop asking 'what is the best way to change a culture?' and start asking 'WHAT IS THE RIGHT WAY HERE - for this change, this urgency, these people?' THE APPROACH YOU CHOOSE WILL MAKE OR BREAK A PLAN THAT IS OTHERWISE SOUND. There is no single best way; there is only the right fit for the situation and for the people who must live the change - and the craft is not having the best plan, but choosing the right approach.

2. Axis one - directive or participative (who drives it)

The first choice is WHO OWNS THE CHANGE. A DIRECTIVE approach imposes it from the top by authority - 'this is how it will be from Monday.' Its strength is SPEED: change can be pushed through quickly. Its costs are heavy and predictable - it takes no account of the views of the people affected, so it MISSES THE KNOWLEDGE AND IDEAS THEY HOLD, and it commonly breeds RESENTMENT, because change done TO people rather than WITH them is resisted. A PARTICIPATIVE approach involves the people who are affected in shaping the change. It is SLOWER and less controlled from the top, but it earns genuine BUY-IN and surfaces information the people on the ground can see and the managers cannot. The trade-off on this axis is stark and simple: SPEED VERSUS BUY-IN.

3. Axis two - planned or emergent (how it unfolds)

The second choice is the SHAPE of the journey. A PLANNED approach treats change as a top-down sequence of DEFINED STEPS, laid out in advance - predictable, orderly, and well suited to a STABLE environment where you can see the path. An EMERGENT approach treats change as a continuous, bottom-up process of EXPERIMENT, LEARN, AND ADAPT - less tidy, but far better suited to a TURBULENT environment where the path keeps shifting and a rigid plan would shatter on contact with reality. The trade-off here is PREDICTABILITY VERSUS ADAPTABILITY. And these two axes are NOT four rival doctrines to pick between - they are TWO DIALS you set, independently, to match your situation: who drives it (directive/participative) and how it unfolds (planned/emergent).

4. Choose by diagnosing the situation - a culture cannot be commanded

You set the dials by reading THREE honest things. URGENCY: a genuine crisis or a hard, non-negotiable deadline pushes you more directive and more planned - there is no time for slow consensus when the plant is unsafe today. THE DEPTH OF BUY-IN THE CHANGE NEEDS: and this is decisive for culture, because A CULTURE IS WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY DO WHEN NO ONE IS WATCHING, and that cannot be ordered into existence; a change people must believe in and live daily leans strongly participative. THE STABILITY OF THE ENVIRONMENT: stable favours planned, turbulent favours emergent. And hold this at the centre: THE CHOICE OF APPROACH IS AN ACT OF ACCEPTANCE. A PURELY DIRECTIVE APPROACH THAT TREATS PEOPLE AS INSTRUMENTS TO BE COMMANDED, OR AS OBSTACLES TO BE OVERCOME, VIOLATES THE STANDARD OF ACCEPTING PEOPLE AS THEY ARE. A PARTICIPATIVE APPROACH IS NOT SOFTNESS; IT IS THE RECOGNITION THAT THE PEOPLE WHO MUST LIVE THE CHANGE ARE WHOLE AND WELCOME, THE HOLDERS OF THE CURRENT CULTURE AND THE ONLY SOURCE OF THE NEW ONE. YOU CANNOT COMMAND A CULTURE INTO BEING BECAUSE A CULTURE IS WHAT PEOPLE CHOOSE TO DO WHEN NO ONE IS WATCHING. YOUR ROLE IS TO CREATE THE CONDITIONS IN WHICH THEY MAKE THE RIGHT CHOICE.

5. Blend loose-tight - and match the approach to the change

In practice the best change is rarely all one thing. The seasoned answer is LOOSE-TIGHT: be TIGHT - firm, directive, non-negotiable - on the FEW THINGS THAT MUST BE TRUE (the safety-critical standard, the direction of travel, the outcomes that matter), and LOOSE - participative, adaptive, emergent - on HOW PEOPLE GET THERE. Firm on the WHAT; open on the HOW. This gives people a clear and steady direction while letting them OWN THE PATH, which is exactly the combination that earns belief without losing control. And it names the classic, expensive failure for what it is: USING A FAST DIRECTIVE APPROACH TO FORCE A DEEP CULTURE CHANGE THAT COULD ONLY EVER HAVE BEEN EARNED - the change is announced, complied with in public, quietly ignored in private, and the gap between what people say and what they do only widens. Match the approach to the change: the deepest changes are never installed, they are chosen by the people who live them.

T3: The Blueprint

T4: Your Turn

Here are two changes at Corathyl. Your job is to set the dials differently for each - proving the craft is diagnosis, not one favourite method.

Scenario A - the crisis. A serious safety near-miss has revealed that a specific isolation procedure is being done wrong. It must change immediately, exactly, and without exception - lives depend on it.

Scenario B - the improvement. Onalenna wants to shift the plant's culture from "run to failure and fix it heroically" to "catch problems early and prevent them" - a change people must believe in and live every day.

  1. Place each change on the two axes. For A and for B, where do you set directive-vs-participative and planned-vs-emergent?
  2. Justify from the diagnosis. For each, say which of urgency, depth-of-buy-in, and environment drove your setting.
  3. Name tight and loose. For the change you would blend, say what you would hold tight (non-negotiable) and what you would leave loose (theirs to shape).
  4. Spot the failure. For Scenario B, describe what would happen if you ran it the way you should run Scenario A.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the placements: Scenario A is strongly directive and planned - the procedure is fixed, mandated, and rolled out as defined steps; there is no negotiating a safety-critical isolation. Scenario B is strongly participative and blended toward emergent - a prevention culture only exists if the technicians choose it, and how it takes root will be learned area by area.

Second, the diagnosis: A is driven by urgency and non-negotiable safety (no time, no discretion). B is driven by the depth of buy-in a culture requires (belief cannot be commanded) and a relatively stable environment that still needs room to adapt.

Third, tight and loose for B: tight on the non-negotiables (safety-critical checks are mandatory; the condition data will be looked at and acted on; prevention is the direction, full stop), and loose on the how (each team designs its own rounds, chooses its own early-warning signs, adapts as it learns). Firm on the what, open on the how.

Fourth, the failure: if B were run like A - imposed by memo, "from Monday everyone does preventive rounds, no discussion" - you would get ticked boxes and no belief. People would perform prevention when watched and revert the moment they were not, the say-do gap would widen, and the culture would not move, because you tried to command what could only be earned.

T5: The Guardrails

There is no single best way to change a culture; there is only the right fit for the situation and for the people who must live it - and you cannot command a culture into being.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Believing one approach is always best. The method that transformed one organisation fails in the next. Ask not "what is the best way?" but "what is the right way here?"

• Imposing a change that needs belief. A culture is what people do unwatched; it cannot be decreed. For deep change, lean participative - you earn a culture, you do not order it.

• Endless participation in a crisis. When the plant is unsafe today, a firm directive hand is not disrespect - it is responsibility. Urgency earns the right to be directive.

• A rigid plan in a turbulent world. A fixed sequence shatters on shifting ground. When the path keeps moving, go emergent - experiment, learn, adapt.

• Drifting emergently against an entrenched habit. Pure bottom-up drift never overcomes a deep old habit. Be tight on the direction so the drift has something to gather around.

• Confusing compliance with change. Public nodding is not private belief. If people revert the moment you look away, you chose the wrong approach, not the wrong people.

• Treating people as obstacles. The holders of the current culture are the only source of the new one. Respect for them is not softness; it is how culture actually changes.

• Being loose on the non-negotiables. Openness is not the absence of direction. Hold the few things that must be true firmly tight, and leave the rest genuinely loose.

Your plan was sound and your goal was right; what stalled it was reaching for the wrong way to bring it about. Now you can choose. Read the two axes - who drives it, and how it unfolds - and set them from the real urgency, the real need for belief, and the real stability of the ground; hold tight on the few things that must be true and leave the rest loose for the people to shape; and never try to command a culture, because a culture is only ever chosen by the people who live it. There is no single best way to change a culture - only the right fit for the situation and the people - and your craft is not the best plan, but the right approach for those who must live the change.
