Three suspects, three weeks, and a board that will act on whatever I write - how do I choose a way of answering that will still be believed when the answer is unwelcome?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a Tuesday and you are standing under Conveyor CV-204 at the Westbridge Cement works, an inland plant. The belt carries crushed limestone from the crusher to the kiln feed. It is the third time this month it has stopped, and the same part has failed each time: the tail-end idler bearing.

In your hand is a printed maintenance log. The numbers are not vague: 7 unplanned stops in 90 days, each costing the plant about R1.8 million in lost output and overtime. That is roughly R12.6 million gone in one quarter from this one bearing.

The plant manager, Mr Zwane, walks up while you are still reading. He does not raise his voice. "The supplier says their bearing is fine. Maintenance says it is the alignment. Procurement wants to switch to the cheaper bearing. Everyone has an opinion." He taps the log. "I have a board meeting in three weeks. I need to tell them what is actually wrong and what fix actually works - not who shouted loudest."

You can smell the hot grease where the last bearing seized. Behind you a fitter is already saying, loudly, that he knew it was the alignment all along. And there it is - the pull you have to resist. The easy move is to nod, write "alignment fault" in the report, and order the realignment. It feels decisive; it would make the noise stop today. But you have been here before: you fixed the "obvious" cause once, and the failures came back six weeks later, because the obvious cause was not the real one.

Three named suspects and a board that will act on whatever I write. Before I gather anything - how do I choose a way of answering that will still be believed when the answer is unwelcome?

Before you read on, decide how you will go about it. Three suspects, three weeks, R12.6 million already gone, a fitter who is certain, and a board that will act on your page. Write down what you would actually do first - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five ways this investigation usually starts, and what each one is choosing without noticing

• "Start measuring - get the data." The instinct of anyone serious, and it has already decided something: that the answer is a number. If part of what is happening is that the night shift aligns the belt differently because the standard procedure is unworkable in the dark, no amount of measurement will surface it, and the study will produce a confident number about the wrong thing.

• "Interview the fitters - they know this machine." They do, and this has also already decided something: that the answer is a reason people can give. Nobody on that floor can tell you whether the cheaper bearing lasts fewer hours under identical load, because nobody has ever run it under identical load.

• "Do both - it cannot hurt." Closer, and doing both without saying which answers what produces two piles of material and an argument about which one wins when they disagree. Mixed is a decision to be made and defended, not a way of avoiding the decision.

• "Look for published work on this bearing first." Sensible, and it is the second question. What is already known is worth finding, and it will not tell you what counts as an answer here - the board will act on your investigation, not on somebody's paper.

• "Just realign it and see." Fast and it is the option that already failed once on this conveyor, six weeks later.

• What every one of these does is choose what counts as evidence, silently, in the first ten minutes - and then spend three weeks and R12.6 million of credibility on that unexamined choice. A study is not made trustworthy by being careful. It is made trustworthy by having decided, out loud and in writing, what would count as an answer, what method that requires, and what it owes the people it involves - before anything is gathered.

T2: The Tool in Action

Before gathering anything: write the question, and say plainly what would count as an answer to it. Then choose the method that suits that answer, with the reason recorded. Then settle what you owe the people the study touches. Watch Lebohang do it on the CV-204 problem.

Step 1 - Set your stance (what counts as good evidence?)

Lebohang writes her question first: 'which factor - bearing brand, shaft alignment, or belt load - drives the early failure of the CV-204 idler bearing, and what setting fixes it?' Most of it is measurable (failure hours, alignment in millimetres, load in tonnes per hour), but she suspects the lubrication routine is being skipped - a people factor. So she chooses a PRAGMATIST stance: mostly quantitative, with a small qualitative strand on the lubrication habit.

Step 2 - Choose your tools with a reason

Quantitative (primary): a designed test measuring bearing life in running hours under controlled factors - justified because the core question is a measurable comparison. Qualitative (secondary): six short interviews with the night-shift fitters on the lubrication routine - justified because the literature flagged lubrication as an unmeasured human factor. A mixed-methods design, chosen on purpose, not by accident.

Step 3 - Get permission, protect people

Each fitter signs informed consent - voluntary, with no effect on their work record. Under POPIA the interview notes are anonymised (fitter 1-6), stored encrypted, used only for this study, and deleted after the report is approved. The supplier's failure-rate data is under NDA, so she publishes the pattern (alignment dominates) but seals the confidential pricing in an annex. The board hears: do not switch brands; realign to 0.8 mm or below and check alignment after every re-tension - saving most of the R12.6 million a quarter, proven by 32 runs and a literature base anyone can re-check.

See what the method did. It walked into a room full of certainty - the supplier sure, maintenance sure, procurement sure, a fitter loudest of all - and walked out with something none of them had: proof. And see the mission in it, because this is honesty built into a method. The deepest service an engineer can do is to refuse to send people the wrong way on a confident guess. A board acting on the loudest voice wastes scarce money and leaves the real fault to keep biting; this method makes the engineer earn the answer instead - an honest literature count anyone can re-run, a test sized so a real effect cannot hide in the noise, a conclusion that survives a sceptic. The loud fitter, here, was right by luck - and the method's gift is not that it flatters him, but that it can now prove what was true and disprove what was not, so the R12.6 million is spent on the realignment that works and not the brand switch that would not. Money is the measure of getting the truth right; the aim is an answer that protects the plant, the budget, and the people - not a feeling dressed as a fact.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the study as a chain of five moves.

  • 1. Set your stance. What counts as good evidence here - a measured number, a human reason, or both?
  • 2. Choose your tools with a reason. Quantitative, qualitative, or mixed - and why.
  • 3. Get permission, protect people. Consent and data safety, before you gather anything.

The ideas underneath. Your stance - the word for it is a paradigm - is what you are treating as good evidence: positivist, that the answer is something to be measured; interpretivist, that it is something to be understood from the people involved; or pragmatist, that it is both, fitted to the question. Naming it matters because it is chosen either way, and choosing it silently is how a study spends three weeks answering a question nobody asked. What follows from the stance is the method - quantitative, qualitative or mixed - and the discipline is not which you pick but that the reason is written down, so that somebody can disagree with the reason rather than with your conclusion. And the third move is not administration: informed consent and the duties under data-protection law are what you owe people for the fact that your study touches them, and they are settled before anything is gathered because afterwards is too late to ask.

Two things this move deliberately does not do, and both are separate work. Finding what is already known, in a form somebody else could re-run and check, is its own method. Designing the test itself, so that several suspects can be separated at once and a real difference is not lost in noise, is another. What this unit settles is the thing that has to be settled first: what would count as an answer here, which method that requires, and what the study owes the people in it. Choose that badly and both of the others will be executed impeccably on the wrong question.

T4: Your Turn

Take an argued-over, expensive problem of your own - a recurring failure, a disputed fix, a "we have always done it this way" - and run the five moves. Set your stance; find what is known with an honest count; choose your tools with a reason; design a fair test sized to be heard; and sort out consent and data protection before you gather a thing.

Two disciplines keep the choice honest. First, write down what would count as an answer before you choose how to look - one sentence, in the report, dated. It feels like paperwork and it is the only thing that stops the method being chosen by habit or by whichever tool you happen to be good with. A study whose stance was never written was still made; it was just made invisibly, and it cannot be defended when the answer is unwelcome. Second, settle what you owe the people involved before you gather anything, and treat that as part of the method rather than a form to sign. Consent obtained after the fact is not consent, anonymity promised and not designed for is a promise you will break by accident, and a fitter who suspects his answers will reach his supervisor will tell you what is safe rather than what is true - which corrupts the evidence as surely as a broken instrument would.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your study true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The stance was chosen by habit, not by the question. Whoever is comfortable with numbers reaches for measurement and whoever is comfortable with people reaches for interviews, and both will produce a competent study of whichever half of the problem their instrument can see. Write the question first, then choose.

• Mixed methods used to avoid deciding. Doing both is a legitimate choice and it requires saying in advance which question each half answers, and what you will do if they disagree. Without that, "mixed" means two studies and an argument.

• Ethics treated as a form rather than a method. Consent after the fact is not consent, and a promise of anonymity that the study design cannot keep will be broken accidentally. It also affects the evidence: people who fear the answer travelling tell you what is safe.

You came in under a stopped conveyor with three suspects, a loud floor, and three weeks. You can leave with something none of the shouting could give you: a named stance, an honest count of 9 studies from 142, a mixed-methods design, a 32-run test sized so the truth could not hide, an ANOVA that pins the cause on alignment and clears the brand, and consent and data protection in order. A finding strong enough that no one can wave it away - the real cause, proven, not the loudest voice. That is how an engineer protects the plant, the budget, and the people: not by being the most certain person in the room, but by being the one who can show their working when the hard question comes.
