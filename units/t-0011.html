Step 1 of 5

Not "very reliable" - a number, and how sure we are about it: how do I give a plant-level availability figure that shows how bad a bad year could be?

T1: The Ache

You are standing in the site office at Kgosi Ridge Manganese, a new ore-processing plant outside Hotazel in the Northern Cape. It is 7 a.m. The morning is cold, and through the wall you can hear the test run of the primary crusher - a deep, steady rumble that drops to silence every so often, then starts again.

In your hand is a one-page performance guarantee from the off-take contract. One line is highlighted in yellow: "The plant shall deliver a production availability of at least 95%." Fall below that and the penalty is R1.8 million for every percentage point, every year.

Across the desk sits Naledi, the project sponsor. She is not angry, but she is tense - the bank wants the design signed off today so the long-lead equipment can be ordered. She slides a single sheet toward you: the proposed line is a crusher, then a conveyor, then a screen, in series. One stops, the line stops.

"Engineering tells me each machine is 'very reliable'," she says. "But the crusher already tripped three times during the test this week. I need a number I can put my name next to. Not 'very reliable' - a number. And I need to know how sure we are about it."

You feel the pull to do the easy thing: multiply three availability figures from the supplier brochures, get one tidy percentage, and hand it over. But you know the truth - those three numbers are averages, the crusher is still settling in, and a single point estimate hides how wide the real answer could be. If you guess high and the plant under-delivers, the penalty lands on Naledi's desk with your name on the calculation. The smell of fresh concrete and machine oil drifts in from the plant. The crusher goes quiet again.

Not "very reliable" - a number, and how sure we are about it. How do I turn component reliability and repair times into a plant-level figure that shows how bad a bad year could be?

Before you read on, give Naledi a number. Three machines in series, supplier availability figures for each, a 95 per cent contract floor, R1.8 million a point, and the bank wants the design signed today. Write down what you would hand her and how you would work it out - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four numbers you could hand over, and what each one hides

• Multiply the three brochure availabilities. Fast, defensible on its face, and it gives one number - 97.2 per cent as it happens, which is right. What it cannot tell her is the only thing the contract cares about: how bad a bad year gets. A single figure has no bad year in it.

• Multiply them, then subtract a margin for safety. The instinct once you notice the first answer is thin, and the margin is invented. Whatever you subtract, somebody will ask where it came from, and the honest answer will be that you chose it.

• Quote the supplier figures and note they are averages. Honest, and it hands the problem back to Naledi in the same shape she gave it to you. She needs a number she can sign, and a caveat is not a number.

• Refuse until there is operating history. Correct in principle and the long-lead equipment order will not wait a year. Refusing to answer is also a decision, and it lands on somebody.

• What all four share is that they produce, at best, one number - the average year. But no year is the average year. Failures arrive when they arrive and repairs take as long as they take, and the plant's output is what falls out of that particular sequence. What Naledi actually needs is the range of years this design produces, and specifically its bad end - which is not something you can reach by adjusting a point estimate.

T2: The Tool in Action

Instead of trusting one average, simulate the plant living a whole year - drawing a random time to failure and a random repair duration for every machine as it goes - and then do it ten thousand times. What comes back is not a figure but a distribution of years. Watch it run on the Kgosi Ridge line.

The first is a Monte Carlo RAM model - a simulation that runs the plant's whole life, 10,000 times over, drawing random failures and repairs each time instead of trusting one average number. Run on the crusher-conveyor-screen line (8,760 simulated hours, 10,000 times), it gives a spread of production availability, not one figure:

  • P10 (a bad year): 96.1% - only 1 year in 10 is worse.
  • P50 (the best estimate): 97.2% - the middle outcome.
  • P90 (a good year): 98.1% - only 1 year in 10 is better.

The line is rated at 500 t/h, so ideal annual output (500 x 8,760) is 4,380,000 t. At P50 that is 4,257,360 t/yr; at P10, the worst-credible case, 4,209,180 t/yr. The contract floor is 95%. Even in a bad year you clear it by 1.1 percentage points.

See what ten thousand simulated years gave that the multiplication could not. The hand calculation and the simulation agree exactly on the middle - 97.2 per cent - which is the check that the model is sound. The number Naledi actually needed was the other one: 96.1 per cent in a bad year, which clears the 95 per cent floor by 1.1 points. That margin is the whole answer. Had the P10 come out at 94.6 the middle figure would have been identical and the design would have been unsignable, and no amount of care with a single average would ever have shown it. What she can put her name next to is not a percentage - it is a percentage with a bad year attached and a stated distance from the penalty.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the two analyses in eleven moves.

  • 1. Draw the reliability block diagram. Sketch each machine in the order it sits, and mark series (any stop stops the line) or parallel/standby. Kgosi Ridge is Crusher -> Conveyor -> Screen, all in series - no standby, yet.
  • 2. Gather MTBF and MTTR for each block. From supplier data and the commissioning log: Crusher 1,200 h MTBF / 18 h MTTR; Conveyor 800 h / 6 h; Screen 2,000 h / 12 h.
  • 3. Choose the failure and repair distributions. Time-to-failure follows an exponential distribution at that MTBF (the standard, honest starting choice for random failures); repair time follows a lognormal distribution at that MTTR.
  • 4. Run the simulation. Simulate one full year (8,760 h), 10,000 times: each run draws random failure and repair times for every block in turn, and tallies the uptime.
  • 5. Read the availability distribution. The 10,000 simulated years give you P10, P50 and P90 - the spread you saw in T2.
  • 6. Sanity-check against a hand calculation. Availability per block = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR): Crusher 1,200/1,218 = 0.9852; Conveyor 800/806 = 0.9926; Screen 2,000/2,012 = 0.9940. Series = 0.9852 x 0.9926 x 0.9940 = 0.9721 -> 97.2% - matching the simulation's P50 exactly. The hand calculation only ever gives you that one number; the simulation also gives you the P10, which is the number the contract actually cares about.
  • 7. Translate to production and to the contract. Ideal annual output = rated throughput x 8,760 h. Compare expected and worst-credible tonnes against the contract floor.

The ideas underneath. RAM stands for Reliability, Availability and Maintainability - how often a machine breaks, how fast you fix it, and the result: how much of the time it is ready to work. A Monte Carlo model answers "what is the full range of what could happen?" by rolling the dice thousands of times instead of trusting one average. P10/P50/P90 answer "how bad could a bad year get, and how good could a good one be?" - not just "what is likely?". And note what this model does not answer. It takes each machine's reliability as a given and asks what the plant does with it; it cannot tell you whether a machine is itself getting better or worse as you fix it, which is the question those three crusher trips raise, and a separate piece of work with its own arithmetic. Running this model on numbers from a machine that is still settling in is exactly why step seven insists on saying which assumption the answer rests on.

T4: Your Turn

Lift these two blank pro-formas onto a sheet of paper or a spreadsheet and run them on your own line and your own failure log. The headers ARE the steps - fill each one in, in order.

Pro-forma 1 - availability of a line. First sketch your reliability block diagram: each machine in order, marked series or parallel/standby. Then, one row per block, fill in: Block, Series or parallel?, MTBF (h), MTTR (h), Block availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR). Then roll up: series availability (multiply the block availabilities together); the worst block (your bottleneck); ideal annual output (rate x 8,760 h); expected output (ideal x series availability); worst-credible output (ideal x your P10 - run a Monte Carlo if you have buffers or standby); your contract or target floor; and whether you clear it, expected and worst-credible both. If you have any parallel or standby blocks or buffers, the hand calculation is a sanity check only - run the Monte Carlo for the real P10/P50/P90.

Your one-line answer: "Expected production availability ___%, worst-credible (P10) ___% against a floor of ___% - a margin of ___ points. The figure assumes ___, and the sanity check against the hand calculation agrees to ___."

Now attempt these four on your own. Try each one before you open its reveal - that is where the skill is built.

Task 1 (warm-up). A single pump has MTBF = 500 h and MTTR = 8 h. What is its availability? Then: if a second, identical pump is added so the line needs only one of the two running (parallel redundancy), would availability go up or down, and roughly why?

What good looks like

A = 500 ÷ (500 + 8) = 0.9843 -> 98.4%. With a parallel spare the line only stops when both pumps are down at once, which is rare, so availability goes up sharply, toward roughly 99.97%. Redundancy means a single failure no longer stops the line.

Task 2. A line is Feeder -> Mill -> Pump -> Filter in series, rated 300 t/h, running 8,760 h/yr: Feeder 1,000 h MTBF / 5 h MTTR; Mill 600 h / 20 h; Pump 1,500 h / 8 h; Filter 900 h / 4 h. Compute each block's availability, the series availability, the expected annual tonnes, and the tonnes lost to downtime.

What good looks like

Feeder 1,000/1,005 = 0.9950; Mill 600/620 = 0.9677; Pump 1,500/1,508 = 0.9947; Filter 900/904 = 0.9956. Series = 0.9950 x 0.9677 x 0.9947 x 0.9956 = 0.9536 -> 95.4%. Ideal = 300 x 8,760 = 2,628,000 t. Expected ≈ 2,506,000 t. Lost ≈ 122,000 t/yr. The Mill, at 96.8%, is the bottleneck - it is where a buffer or redundancy would pay off, and where a full Monte Carlo model would earn its keep.

Task 3 - the number the contract cares about. A four-machine line in series simulates out at P50 96.4 per cent and P10 94.8 per cent. The contract floor is 95 per cent, with a penalty of R2.4 million per point per year. Your colleague reports "96.4 per cent, comfortably above the floor". What is wrong with that report, and what would you write instead?

What good looks like

The report quotes the middle year and the contract is exposed in the bad one. At P10 the line is 0.2 points below the floor, so roughly one year in ten carries a penalty of about R480,000 - and the sentence "comfortably above the floor" is describing a year that will often not happen. What to write instead: "Expected 96.4 per cent; worst-credible (P10) 94.8 per cent, which is 0.2 points below the 95 per cent floor and implies an expected penalty exposure of roughly R480,000 in about one year in ten. The design meets the floor on average and not at the bad end." A complete answer also names the fix worth pricing - a buffer or a standby on whichever machine has the lowest availability, because that is what pulls the bad end up.

Task 4 (stretch) - when the simulation and the hand calculation disagree. You run the model and get P50 = 91.7 per cent. The hand calculation of the same line - each machine's MTBF divided by MTBF plus MTTR, multiplied together - gives 95.8 per cent. Both were done carefully. What do you do, and what are the two most likely explanations?

What good looks like

Stop and reconcile before reporting either. The hand calculation is the check on the simulation and a four-point disagreement means one of them is wrong about the plant. The two likeliest explanations: (1) the model contains something the hand calculation cannot represent - a repair crew that can only attend one machine at a time, a spare that takes days to arrive, a start-up sequence - in which case the simulation is right and lower, and you should say which of those effects is doing it; or (2) an input has been entered in the wrong units or the wrong place, most often an MTTR in days against MTBF in hours, in which case the simulation is simply wrong. A complete answer says which way to test it: strip the model back to the plain series case and see whether it reproduces 95.8 per cent. If it does, the difference is real and explainable. If it does not, the model has a fault and no result from it should leave your desk.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your model true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• You used the wrong failure distribution. This model assumes failures arrive at random, which holds for many complex systems in their useful life and does not hold for something dominated by wear-out - a liner, a belt, a tyre - or by infant mortality in a new machine. Run it on a wear-out problem and it will return a confident, wrong spread. If you know the failures come from something wearing out, this is not the tool.

• The inputs came from brochures for a machine still settling in. The model faithfully propagates whatever reliability you feed it. Say in the report which assumption the answer rests on, and whether the machine has yet demonstrated it.

• Your Monte Carlo P50 lands far from the hand-calculated series availability. Likely a modelling error - the wrong block connection (parallel coded as series or vice versa), wrong units (minutes vs hours), or too few simulation runs. Always do the hand sanity check: for a simple series line, the P50 must land near MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR) multiplied across blocks. If it does not, the model is wrong, not reality.

• Availability looks suspiciously high. A buffer or standby may be modelled with infinite capacity, or repairs may start instantly with no crew or spares delay. Add a realistic logistic delay (waiting for crew, spares, permits) to MTTR - "time to repair" on site is rarely just the wrench time.

• The single availability number feels fragile in the meeting. You quoted only the mean and lost the spread. Always report P10, P50 and P90, not one number - the contract risk lives in the P10, not the average.

You stood in that site office with a 95 per cent floor, R1.8 million a point, a bank waiting, and three supplier averages that multiply to a tidy number with no bad year in it. You can hand Naledi something she can sign: expected production availability 97.2 per cent, worst-credible 96.1, clearing the floor by 1.1 points - checked against the hand calculation to the decimal, with the assumption it rests on written beside it. A single number tells you about the average year, and nobody operates an average year. The figure that decides whether that contract is safe to sign is the one at the bad end, and you only get it by simulating the years rather than averaging the machines.
