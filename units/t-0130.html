A field of grey

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. A repair weld on the high-pressure raw-water main has been radiographed by a contractor, and the main cannot be backfilled and pressurised until somebody signs. That somebody is you.

The report says: no significant indications - accept. You open the image. It is a field of grey. There is a darker line down near the root, and you have no idea whether that is porosity you could live with, slag, or a lack of penetration you absolutely could not.

Then you notice something worse. The report never says what the shot was capable of resolving. No demonstrated sensitivity, anywhere on it. So "no significant indications" might mean the weld is sound - or it might mean the image was too blurred to have shown a flaw of the size that would matter, and nobody checked.

And the main runs under a town.

I am being asked to stake public safety on a measurement I cannot verify. How do I check whether this image can be believed at all - and then read it well enough to sentence what is in it?

Before you read on, decide. The report says “no significant indications”, and it is honest — that is genuinely what was seen. What it never says is what the shot was capable of resolving. There is no demonstrated sensitivity anywhere on it. So “nothing found” might mean the weld is sound, or it might mean the image was too blurred to have shown a flaw of the size that would matter, and nobody checked which. The source was a few millimetres across, the part is 25 mm thick, the source sat about 600 mm away. And the main runs under a town. Write down how you would decide whether this image can be believed at all — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question that comes before reading

• “Accept it — the contractor is qualified and the report is honest.” Both true, and neither is the point. A truthful account of what was seen tells you nothing about what could have been seen, and it is the second that your decision rests on.

• “Look at the image myself and judge it.” Reading an image you have not qualified is how a blurred shot gets read confidently. You would be interpreting grey without knowing what size of feature that grey was able to contain.

• “Have it re-shot by somebody else.” Money spent to obtain a second unqualified image. Unless you specify what it must demonstrate, you will get another honest report with the same silence in it.

• “Use a different method as well.” Sometimes right, and it is the answer to a question you have not yet asked. Adding methods before you know what this one achieved is buying coverage blind.

• “It is probably fine — flaws that size are rare.” A probability standing in for a measurement, on a main beneath a town. If that reasoning were acceptable, the inspection was never needed.

• The discipline starts before any reading takes place: prove the image can support a claim, then read it, then sentence it. Proving it means checking the things that set its resolving power against what the job demanded, so the record can state the size of feature it demonstrably would have shown — and until that exists, interpretation is decoration. Then read it for what it physically is: not a photograph of the weld but a map of how much material the beam passed through, so less material means more radiation through and a darker mark, mechanically rather than visually. From there the shape is the diagnosis — rounded, angular, straight, branching, and where it sits — each corresponding to a different physical defect, so identification is a reading rather than an impression. And close it by naming what this image could never have shown, because some flaws are invisible to this method by orientation alone, however good the shot. Only then do you sentence anything.

There is a sheet for this, and it is only five questions long. Here it is, filled in.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the interpretation and sentencing sheet for that weld, worked all the way to a decision.

1. Can I believe this image? - unsharpness and demonstrated sensitivity

Geometric unsharpness: Ug = F x t / d = 3 mm x 25 mm / 600 mm = 0.125 mm. The procedure limits it to one-hundredth of the 25 mm thickness, so 0.25 mm. PASS. Sensitivity: 2-2T on a 25 mm part means an IQI 0.5 mm thick with its 2T hole - 1.0 mm diameter - visible on the image. It IS visible on this shot. VERDICT: this image has proved it could show a 1.0 mm feature. Now, and only now, I may read it.

2. What am I actually looking at? - a thickness map, not a photograph

Attenuation: I = I0 x exp(-mu x x). Where there is LESS material in the beam path, more radiation gets through, so the region images DARKER. A void, a crack or a gas pore is dark. A denser inclusion is lighter. This is a map of radiographic thickness along the beam path - it is not a photograph of the weld.

3. What is the indication? - the shape is the diagnosis

The indication is a straight, sharp, CONTINUOUS dark line running along the weld root centreline. Rounded dark spots would be porosity; irregular angular dark shapes would be slag; a jagged, branching, irregular line would be a crack; a straight line offset to one side along a fusion face would be lack of fusion. A straight sharp line ON the root centreline is LACK OF PENETRATION.

4. What could this image never have shown me? - the orientation blind spot

Radiography sees a planar flaw only when the beam runs roughly ALONG its plane, so there is a length of missing material to cast a shadow. A tight crack lying across the beam can be invisible. So a clean radiograph does not mean 'no cracks' - it means 'no cracks lay in a direction this shot could see'. If planar flaws of unknown orientation are the risk, specify ultrasonic testing alongside.

5. The sentence - against a named standard, by type or by size

Acceptance standard: the fabrication code named in the specification (here, the process-piping code). Lack of penetration is a planar, sharp-tipped flaw, and codes reject those BY TYPE - the argument 'it is only short' is not available. Rounded porosity would instead be sentenced by size and distribution against stated limits. SENTENCE: REJECT. Cut out, repair, and re-radiograph to the same demonstrated sensitivity. Recorded: standard, clause, indication, decision, and my name.

Look at the order, because the order is the whole discipline. Nothing is read until box 1 has proved the image was capable of showing what she was hunting for. Then the physics, then the shape, then - before any decision - an honest statement of what this image could never have contained. Only then a sentence, tied to a clause, with her name on it. She walked in unable to say anything and walked out with REJECT she could defend to anyone. Let us take it apart.

T3: The Blueprint

What you felt in T1 was not ignorance about welding. It was paralysis in front of an unverified image. The sheet cures it by naming five questions, in order:

  • Can I believe this image? Two numbers answer it. Geometric unsharpness is the blur every radiograph has, because the source is not a point: Ug = F x t / d, where F is the focal-spot size, t the distance from the flaw to the detector and d from source to object. Here 3 x 25 / 600 = 0.125 mm, inside the 0.25 mm the procedure allows. Notice how it behaves: bring the source in to 300 mm and the blur doubles to 0.25 mm, exactly at the limit; at 200 mm it is 0.375 mm and the shot is invalid. Then the image quality indicator - a small stepped plate with holes of 1, 2 and 4 times its own thickness, laid on the part and shot with it. "2-2T" means an IQI 2% of the part thickness with its 2T hole visible: on 25 mm steel, a 0.5 mm plate whose 1.0 mm hole you must actually see. The IQI is the radiograph's own exam paper - it has to pass its test, in front of your eyes, before it is allowed to test your weld. Without it, "no indications found" is the emptiest sentence in inspection.
  • Why is it dark? Radiation is absorbed as it passes through material - I = I0 x exp(-mu x x) - so where there is less material in the beam path, more gets through and the image is darker. That is all. A void, a pore, a crack: dark. A denser inclusion: lighter. What you are holding is a map of thickness and density along the beam path, not a photograph of a weld.
  • What shape is it? The shape is the diagnosis. Rounded dark spots, single or clustered, are porosity. Irregular, angular, often elongated dark shapes are slag inclusions. A straight, sharp, continuous dark line on the root centreline is lack of penetration. A straight line offset to one side, along a fusion face, is lack of fusion. A fine, jagged, irregular, sometimes branching line is a crack.
  • What could this image never have shown me? This is the question people skip, and it is the one that kills. Radiography sees a planar flaw only when the beam runs roughly along its plane, so there is a length of missing material to cast a shadow. A crack is like a playing card: face-on to the beam it throws a shadow you cannot miss; edge-on it very nearly vanishes. So a clean radiograph does not mean "there are no cracks" - it means "no crack lay in a direction this shot could see". Where planar flaws of unknown orientation are the risk, ultrasonic testing is specified alongside, and each method guards the other's blind spot.
  • What does the standard command? Characterising is not sentencing. You sentence against a named acceptance standard and a cited clause. The principle underneath the clauses is worth carrying: codes do not tolerate flaws that behave as sharp-tipped crack starters, which is why the planar family - cracks, lack of fusion, lack of penetration - is almost universally rejectable by type, regardless of length. Rounded porosity is treated as a stress raiser and sentenced on size and distribution against stated limits. Codes differ in their numbers, so always cite the clause you actually applied.

One honest boundary, so you know exactly what you now hold. This unit teaches you to read and question a radiographic report as a responsible engineer. It does not certify you to perform the interpretation - that is a formally qualified role with its own training and examination. Your job is to make sure the test was valid and the sentence is right against the standard you specified: the intelligent customer and the last guardian of quality, not the operator.

Does it scale? A radiograph report with no stated sensitivity is like a medical scan reported without saying whether the machine could even resolve something the size of what you were looking for - a reassuring sentence built on an unknown.

T4: Your Turn

A 40 mm thick pressure-vessel weld. Focal spot F = 2 mm, source-to-object d = 800 mm, flaw-to-detector t = 40 mm. The procedure limits unsharpness to one-hundredth of the thickness and requires 2-1T sensitivity. Work the sheet.

  1. Can it be believed? Compute Ug. What is the limit here, and does the technique pass?
  2. What must be visible? State the IQI thickness for 2% of 40 mm, and the diameter of the 1T hole that has to be seen.
  3. Characterise. The image shows a fine, irregular, jagged dark line that changes direction. Name it - and say whether it is sentenced by type or by size.
  4. Name the blind spot. What flaw could this shot have missed entirely, and which method would you add to cover it?
  5. Sentence it. Write the one line you would sign, naming what you accepted or rejected and on what basis.

The test of a real answer: you did step 1 before step 3. If you characterised the indication first and only then checked whether the image was valid, you read an image you had not yet earned the right to read.

T5: The Guardrails

Radiographic interpretation goes wrong in a short list of very specific ways, and most of them end with something unsound being accepted.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The empty sentence. Accepting "no indications found" from a report with no stated, demonstrated sensitivity. You have been told nothing at all: an image too blurred to show a 3 mm flaw will honestly report no indications. Ask for the IQI level and the unsharpness every single time.

• The blind spot. Reading a clean radiograph as proof there are no cracks. It is proof that no crack was oriented to be seen. If planar flaws matter, the inspection plan needs a second method, not a second radiograph from the same angle.

• The sizing fallacy. Arguing that a lack of penetration or a lack of fusion is "only a short one". Planar flaws are rejected by type because of the sharp tip, not the length - and that argument tends to be made loudest when the repair is expensive.

• The digital trap. Winding the contrast and brightness of a digital image up and down until something appears. Adjust far enough and noise becomes an indication, or a real flaw is flattened into the background. Interpret within the stated display conditions, and record what you used.

• The projection error. Trying to read through-wall depth off a radiograph. It is a two-dimensional projection: it gives you position, length and area honestly, and depth only by inference. If depth governs the decision, that is a different measurement.

• Shooting for a pretty picture. An image with pleasing contrast that was taken outside the required density and geometry is not a compliant image. Compliance is not the same thing as clarity, and only one of them can be defended afterwards.

That is not a formality. It is the difference between signing a document and taking responsibility for one.
