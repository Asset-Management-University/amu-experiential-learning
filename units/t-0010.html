Step 1 of 5

A 32-year-old truss fails the safety-factor rule on paper (1.35 against 1.5), but the board will ask 'what is the chance it actually fails?' - and I cannot answer that with one number. How do I turn the doubt into a real probability?

T1: The Ache

You are on the walkway above the reservoir at Ridgepoint Bulk Water, looking up at roof truss T-6. It is 32 years old. Your colleague Boipelo, the structures lead, stands next to you with her arms folded. The board meets on Thursday: it will either approve R4.2 million to strengthen T-6 now, or leave it for the next budget cycle in three years.

You have done the normal sum. You worked out the worst load the truss could ever carry - full roof weight, a soaked concrete deck after heavy rain, plus the heaviest maintenance trolley - and divided the truss's strength by it. You got a safety factor of 1.35. The drawing-office rule says 1.5. So on paper, T-6 fails the rule. But you do not believe the worst case happens often: the deck is almost never fully soaked, the trolley is rarely at mid-span.

Boipelo says it plainly: "A safety factor of 1.35 doesn't tell the board anything. They will ask me: what is the chance it actually fails? And I can't answer that with one number." She is right. The single factor hides everything - how much the load varies, how unsure you are of the steel's true strength after 32 years, how those two kinds of doubt stack up. There is a faint smell of chlorine and wet concrete rising from the reservoir below.

If you go to the board with "safety factor 1.35, rule says 1.5," they will spend R4.2 million out of fear, or refuse it out of stubbornness - and neither of you will actually know if T-6 is safe.

A single safety factor hides everything and answers nothing. How do I turn the scatter in the load and the doubt in the steel into a real, defensible probability that T-6 fails - and an honest account of how much I trust that number?

Before you read on, decide. Truss T-6 carries the roof. Three coupon tests put the critical chord’s strength at a mean of 820 kN, scattering by about 12%. Five years of deck-moisture and trolley logs put the load on it at a mean of 560 kN, with a spread of about 70 kN. The old worst-case sum gives a safety factor of 1.35 against a rule that says 1.5. Strengthening costs R4.2 million. Write down what you would tell the board, and how you would show it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what a single safety factor throws away

• “1.35 is below 1.5, so it fails the rule. Strengthen it.” Defensible on paper and it spends R4.2 million without anybody learning whether T-6 was ever likely to fail. If the board asks “what was the chance?”, there is no answer in the file.

• “The worst case almost never happens. On mean load it is 820 over 560, about 1.46 — near enough.” A better number, honestly reasoned, and still a single ratio. It says nothing about how often the load might cross the strength, which is the only thing anybody actually wants to know.

• “Cut more coupons and test them.” This reduces one doubt — how much you know about the steel. It does nothing at all to the other, which is that the load itself genuinely varies. More tests on the wrong doubt buy nothing.

• “Apply a bigger factor to be safe.” Conservatism piled on conservatism, with no idea which of the two doubts is driving the risk — so you cannot tell whether you have bought safety or only cost.

• “Instrument it and watch for cracks.” Worth doing alongside, and no answer to the question in front of you, which is whether it is safe now.

• Notice what every one of them does: collapses two spread-out ranges into one number, and then argues about the number. Keep them as ranges instead and the question becomes answerable. The margin between strength and load has a mean of 820 − 560 = 260 kN, and the two spreads combine to about 120.8 kN — so the margin is roughly 2.15 spreads wide, which puts the chance of failure at about 1.6%, or 1 in 63, over the period those load records cover. That is a number a board can act on. And the method does something a safety factor never can: it tells you which doubt is driving the risk — so you can see whether better load data would buy more certainty than R4.2 million of steel. The two doubts are not even the same kind of thing: one is real scatter that will always be there, the other is ignorance that measurement would reduce.

You need a method that turns doubt into a number. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is structural reliability analysis, and it answers one question: what is the real chance this thing will fail? It treats strength and load not as single numbers but as they really are - spread-out ranges of possibility - and asks how often, across all the ways those ranges combine, the load crosses over the strength. That crossover chance is the probability of failure. The main tool, the First-Order Reliability Method, also tells you which of your doubts is driving the risk. Watch it run on T-6.

Step 1 - Frame the limit state, describe inputs as distributions

For T-6's critical bottom chord, g = R - S, where R is the chord's tensile resistance and S the demand from the roof loads. Resistance R (from 3 coupon tests, modelled lognormal): mean 820 kN, CoV 0.12, so sigma_R = 820 x 0.12 = 98.4 kN. Load effect S (from 5 years of deck-moisture and trolley logs, modelled normal): mean 560 kN, std dev 70 kN. (The old worst-case sum gave a safety factor of just 1.35, below the rule of 1.5 - but the worst case almost never happens; on mean load the ratio is 820/560 = 1.46, a number that still tells the board almost nothing.)

Step 2 - Compute the reliability index (beta) and Pf (FORM)

Mean of g = 820 - 560 = 260 kN; std dev of g = sqrt(sigma_R^2 + sigma_S^2) = sqrt(98.4^2 + 70^2) = sqrt(14,584) = 120.8 kN. So beta = 260 / 120.8 = 2.15, and Pf = Phi(-2.15) = 0.0158 - about 1.6%, or roughly 1 in 63, over the period the load data represents. Already this says far more than 'safety factor 1.35': the board now has a number it can weigh.

Step 3 - Find what drives the risk (the gift of FORM)

The variance shares: resistance sigma_R^2 / (std dev of g)^2 = 9,684 / 14,584 = 66%; load = 4,900 / 14,584 = 34%. Two-thirds of the risk comes from uncertainty about the truss's STRENGTH, not the load - the single most useful line in the whole analysis, and you only see it because reliability analysis breaks the problem open.

Step 4 - Check the curvature (SORM) and split the doubt (UQ)

SORM nudges Pf from 1.6% to about 1.6% (beta 2.15 to about 2.12) - a change under 10%, which confirms FORM was trustworthy here; report Pf about 1.6%. The split: the load spread is mostly aleatoric (design for it). The strength CoV of 0.12 rests on just 3 coupon tests on a 32-year-old truss - largely epistemic. If the true CoV is 0.08 (steel healthy), beta rises to about 2.7 and Pf drops to about 0.3%; if 0.16 (hidden corrosion), beta falls to about 1.75 and Pf climbs to about 4.0%. So the honest answer is Pf about 1.6%, credibly anywhere from 0.3% to 4.0% - and that whole spread is driven by how little we know about the steel, which we can fix.

Step 5 - Turn the split into the decision, report honestly

Two-thirds of the risk and nearly all the reducible doubt live in the steel strength, resting on 3 tests. So before spending R4.2 million, propose a R30,000 inspection and 12 more coupon tests. If the steel comes back healthy (Pf about 0.3%), defer the R4.2m for three years with confidence; if poor (Pf about 4.0%), the board approves the spend without fear. The board paper: Pf about 1.6% (range 0.3% to 4.0%); the dominant driver is epistemic uncertainty in steel strength; the recommended action is the R30,000 inspection first. Boipelo can now answer the board's real question - what is the chance it fails, and how sure are we? - with a defensible number and a cheaper next step.

See what the analysis did. It turned "1.35 against a rule of 1.5" - a number that spreads fear without information - into "a 1.6% chance of failure, and here is exactly how sure we are, and here is the cheap test that would tell us more." And see the mission in it, because this is honesty and stewardship in the same method. The single safety factor is a comfortable lie: it sounds precise and answers nothing, and it pushes a board either to spend four million rand out of fear or to refuse it out of stubbornness, with no one any the wiser about whether people are safe under that roof. Reliability analysis refuses that. It says plainly how likely failure is, and - more honestly still - it splits the doubt into the part that is real randomness you must design around and the part that is simply not having measured enough, which a thirty-thousand-rand inspection can clear. So the four-million-rand decision is no longer made in the dark: you buy down the cheap doubt first, and then you either defer the spend with confidence or justify it with evidence. The money - R4.2 million against R30,000 - is the measure of what an honest number is worth; the aim is a truss you actually know to be safe, and scarce public funds spent on knowledge instead of fear.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the analysis in five moves.

  • 1. Frame the limit state, describe inputs as distributions. g = Strength - Load; each input a mean and a spread.
  • 2. Compute the reliability index and Pf. FORM: beta = margin over its own scatter; Pf from beta.
  • 3. Find what drives the risk. Which doubt - strength or load - owns most of the failure chance.
  • 4. Check the curvature, split the doubt. SORM; then aleatoric (irreducible) versus epistemic (reducible).
  • 5. Turn the split into the decision. Buy down the reducible doubt cheaply; report honestly.

The ideas underneath. The limit-state function g = R - S is the safety check: failure is g below zero, and the method computes the chance of that. Each input is a distribution - a mean and a coefficient of variation (CoV), its spread relative to the mean - because strength is not exactly 820, it is around 820. The First-Order Reliability Method (FORM) finds the reliability index, beta - how many standard deviations the safety margin sits above failure - and the probability of failure, Pf, is the normal-curve probability of landing below minus beta (written Pf = Phi(-beta)). FORM's gift is the sensitivity: each input's share of the variance, so you see which doubt drives the risk. The Second-Order method (SORM) corrects for a curved failure boundary when an input is lognormal. And uncertainty quantification (UQ) splits doubt into aleatoric (genuine randomness - you measure it, design for it, but cannot remove it) and epistemic (not knowing enough - more measurement shrinks it). It fits high-stakes, hard-to-reverse calls where a single safety factor hides too much - critical structures, renewal or life-extension of ageing assets; it is overkill for routine low-consequence parts or early sketch design with no distribution data, where a code safety factor is faster and fine.

Two disciplines keep the analysis honest. First, use the true mean load, never the worst case, and a real spread - the worst-case-divided-by-strength habit is exactly what produced the useless 1.35; reliability works on how the load and the strength actually vary, so a single conservative number smuggled into a probabilistic method poisons the answer in both directions. Second, split the doubt before you spend, and chase the reducible part first - the most expensive mistake in this whole field is to treat all uncertainty as irreducible and design (or spend) around it, when a large slice is simply "we have not measured enough"; find where the reducible doubt lives, buy it down with the cheapest test that will move it, and let the big, hard-to-reverse spend wait for that evidence.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your reliability analysis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A safety factor is one number hiding many. It cannot say how the load varies or how sure you are of the strength. When the decision is big, compute the probability, not the ratio.

• Distributions, not worst cases. Use the true mean and the real spread of each input. A worst-case number forced into a probabilistic method gives a false answer.

• Read the sensitivity - it tells you where to look. The input that owns most of the variance is where the risk lives, and where a measurement will move the answer most.

• Split the doubt: design for one kind, measure away the other. Aleatoric randomness you design around; epistemic doubt you can shrink with cheap tests. Confusing them wastes money.

• Buy down the cheap doubt before the dear spend. A R30,000 test that could defer or justify a R4.2m decision is the most valuable line in the plan.

You came onto the walkway with a failed rule of thumb and a board paper you could not defend. You can leave with a real probability and the honest spread behind it: T-6 fails about 1.7 times in a hundred, credibly somewhere between 0.3% and 4.0%, and almost all of that uncertainty is not the steel truly varying but our not having measured it - which a R30,000 inspection can clear before anyone spends R4.2 million. Turn one hiding safety factor into a probability of failure and the honest spread behind it - the difference between guessing and knowing, and the cheap test that buys down the doubt before the big spend. That is how a high-stakes call on an ageing asset is made well: not with a comfortable ratio that answers nothing, but with a number you can defend, a spread you can name, and the cheapest possible step toward certainty.
