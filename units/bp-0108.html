The job that belonged to no one

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and a phone call has just ruined your morning: one of your bakkies has been pulled over and impounded. Its licence expired weeks ago. A fine, a day off the road, lost deliveries, a scramble to free it - all over a renewal nobody did.

So you gather everyone, expecting to find who dropped the ball. And here is the strange, sinking part: nobody did. You ask Sipho - he says, "I run operations; I assumed the office tracked the licence dates." You ask the office - "We renew what we're told to; we assumed the drivers flagged it." You ask the driver - "I drive the bakkie; I didn't know checking the licence was on me." Every single answer is true. No one was lazy. No one lied. The renewal simply wasn't anyone's actual job.

It belonged to all of them, which meant it belonged to no one - sitting silent on a calendar nobody owned, until the day it cost everyone. And you realise this is not the only such thing in your growing business; it is just the first one to bite.

Everyone honestly assumed someone else had it, so it fell through the gap and we all paid. How do I make sure the things that matter actually have an owner?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The tempting answer is to call a meeting and say "everyone take more responsibility, be more careful." But stop - that is exactly what you had. Everyone WAS being responsible, for their own clear jobs. The licence wasn't anyone's clear job, so more care changes nothing; the gap is still there. And doing it all yourself just makes you the bottleneck again.

Before you read on: if the problem isn't lazy people but an unowned job, what would actually close the gap? What has to become true for that renewal - and the next silent thing like it?

Everyone gave a true answer - the driver drives, Sipho runs operations, the office does what it's told. No one was lazy. The renewal just wasn't anyone's actual job. The gap is the problem, not the people.
Telling everyone to 'be more careful' won't help - that's what we had, and it failed. A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. It needs ONE name against it.
So I name one person who ANSWERS for it - who decides and carries the final responsibility - then say who does it, who pays, who's told. And it's not real until that person says out loud: 'yes, that one is mine.'

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The fix is not more effort; it is making responsibility visible and singular, so nothing important can hide in the gap between people:

  • The gap: a job that belongs to no one: when an important thing sits between people, each assumes - in good faith - that someone else has it. It belongs to everyone, so it belongs to no one - and the risk grows silently until it bites all of you.
  • Name ONE owner who answers for it: the single person whose name is on it - who DECIDES and answers for it. Not necessarily the one who does the work or pays; the one who carries the final responsibility. One name per thing.
  • Then spell out the rest, in plain words: who DOES the work, who PAYS, and who must be kept INFORMED. Four simple roles, written where everyone can see them.
  • It isn't real until someone ACCEPTS it: a duty handed over in silence is still an orphan. It only sticks when the owner says it aloud: 'yes - that one is mine.' A small promise that closes the gap.
  • This is a gift, not a punishment: the owner is trusted and empowered to look after something that matters - a champion, not a scapegoat. You're not hunting who failed; you're making sure good people can't be set up to fail by a gap.

The heart of it is that last pair of ideas. A duty only becomes real when one person's name is on it AND that person has said, out loud, "that one is mine." A responsibility handed over in silence - assumed, implied, hinted - is still an orphan, and orphans are exactly what fall through the gap.

And hold tight to the spirit of it: this is a gift, not a hunt for someone to blame. You are not asking "who failed?" - everyone answered truthfully, the SYSTEM failed. You are giving each thing that matters a trusted guardian, so good people are never again set up to fail by a hole nobody could see. Clear ownership is one of the kindest things a leader can give a team.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So instead of a telling-off, Muhammad starts a simple list on the wall - two columns, "Thing" and "Who answers for it" - and they fill it in together.

The thing: keeping every bakkie's licence and permits current.

Who ANSWERS for it (the owner): Sipho. One name. He decides, and he carries the final responsibility for it.

Who DOES the work: the office, doing the actual renewals.

Who PAYS: Ibraaheem releases the fee from the books.

Who is kept INFORMED: the drivers, told well before any date.

The moment that makes it real: Muhammad looks at Sipho. "Compliance for the whole fleet - that's yours now. You good with that?" Sipho nods: "Yes. It's mine." That sentence is the whole fix. The orphan job now has a guardian, and the silent calendar now has a watcher. The problem never recurs - and they work down the list, giving every other silent thing an owner too.

For the engineers: a steam line runs across the boundary between two departments. A leak starts; Department A thinks it's B's side, B thinks it's A's fluid - so it worsens for months, wasting a fortune in energy, until the pipe fails. The fix isn't a better pipe; it's the plant manager saying: "Department A owns this whole line, vessel to vessel. The buck stops with you." One owner, end to end.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Nothing was wrong with your people - only with the empty space between them. One visible list and one spoken "that's mine" turned an orphan job into an owned one. This closes gaps anywhere two or more people share something that matters:

  1. Find the orphans. What important thing does everyone assume someone else is handling? (The recurring small problem nobody ever quite closes is the clue.)
  2. Give it one owner, in the open. Name the single person who answers for it; then say who does the work, who pays, who's kept informed. Write it where all can see.
  3. Make it real out loud. It isn't owned until that person says "that one is mine." Frame it as trust, not blame.
R

Name one orphan in your world this week, and give it an owner who accepts it aloud. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

A growing team doesn't usually fail at the big, obvious jobs - those always have a name on them. It fails in the quiet gaps between people, where good intentions are no substitute for a clear owner. The leader who leaves no orphans builds something that doesn't drop the things that matter.
