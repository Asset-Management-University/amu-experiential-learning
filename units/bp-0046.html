Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Kabelo, a skilled electrician, and you decided to do things the right way: registered the business, got proper insurance, keep your trade licences perfectly up to date. You're proud to be a formal, trustworthy business. But the paperwork never stops - the tax return, the van's licence renewal, the annual fee for your trade certification - each taking time you don't have and money that must be paid, no matter what.

Last month was slow: a handful of jobs, a thin profit. Then the public-liability insurance renewal arrived, costing exactly the same as last year when you were busy - and it landed like a hammer, swallowing nearly all the money you'd fought so hard to make. It feels deeply unfair, and a voice whispers: "If I'd stayed informal, taking cash jobs, I wouldn't have this cost. Maybe I was a fool to register. Maybe I should shrink back under the radar."

These costs are the same no matter how little I sell, and they're crushing me - do I have to shrink back into hiding, or is there a way to carry them that doesn't bleed me dry?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to listen to that whisper and shrink back - go informal again to escape the cost. It feels safer. But it's a trap: staying small and under-the-radar cuts you off from bigger clients who need proper invoices, stops you ever growing, leaves you one unlucky moment from serious trouble - and smallness is the very thing that keeps the cost heavy on each job you do. Your other move is to just carry on and absorb each one in a panic: every renewal letter, you drop everything, scramble for the money, rush the forms - paying the "panic price" again and again in stress, lost work, and hurried mistakes (and when you delay and hope, you get fines and worse).

The costs stay the same whatever you sell. So before you read on: what could actually change how heavy they feel on each job - and what would stop you paying that panic price every single time?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

A cost that stays the same whatever you sell is not beaten by hiding from it - it's beaten by growing into it, and by paying it calmly on a system instead of in a panic every time. (The enemy isn't the rules; many exist to protect your customers, proving you're safe and qualified. The enemy is the panic, and the belief that staying small is safe.) Five moves:

  • See the cost for what it is - fixed: these costs are like rent: you pay the same whether you make one chair or a hundred. They barely change with your sales - so at low volume they're brutal on each job, and at higher volume they're small. This isn't bad luck; it's just maths - and because it's maths, you can use maths to beat it.
  • Don't flee into smallness - dilute by growing: because the cost is fixed, the only way to make it smaller PER sale is to spread it over MORE sales. R12,000 a year of licences and returns is an extra R120 on every job if you do 100 a year - but only R12 a job if you grow to 1,000. The cost didn't vanish; its weight on each sale shrank. Growth is the cure; staying small is the trap that keeps the weight heavy (and cuts you off from clients who need a formal supplier).
  • Stop paying the panic price - map it once: take one afternoon and list every recurring obligation - tax, insurance, licences, permits - and for each: what is it, how often, how much, and what does it need (which documents)? That single afternoon turns a year of nasty surprises into one predictable, manageable list - you can't plan for what you haven't named.
  • Batch, standardise, and schedule ahead: now work the map: do obligations that fall in the same period together in one organised morning (batch); make a simple checklist for each so you never re-invent the process (standardise); and set a reminder a month BEFORE each deadline, not on the day (schedule). You stop paying in a rushed, error-prone panic and start paying calmly, on purpose.
  • Outsource only the sharp parts: your time is valuable - you're a brilliant tradesperson, not an accountant. If a tax return takes you 10 hours you could have spent earning R4,000 on jobs, paying a bookkeeper R1,500 to do it isn't an expense, it's a profit. Outsource the specific hard tasks where an expert is genuinely cheaper than your lost time; keep the simple things (renewing a licence online) in-house. (Respect the rules - most protect your customers; the enemy is the panic and the hiding, never meeting your obligations.)

See the maths turn the hammer into a feather. Say your licences, renewals and returns total R12,000 a year. Do 100 jobs and that's an extra R120 on every job - heavy. Grow to 1,000 jobs and the same R12,000 spreads to just R12 a job. The cost didn't vanish; its weight on each sale shrank - so growing is the cure and hiding is the trap. Then you take one afternoon to map every obligation (what, when, how much, what it needs), batch and schedule them ahead instead of scrambling, and hand the one sharp part to an expert only where they're cheaper than your time (a R1,500 bookkeeper beats 10 of your hours worth R4,000 in jobs). A fixed cost is best diluted by growing into it, not fled by staying small; and paid once, on a calm system, not again and again in a panic. The paperwork stops being a punishment for doing right, and becomes a predictable, shrinking part of a business that serves more people every year.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

The same fixed-cost squeeze hits any small operator who's gone properly set up. Watch a freelancer.

See the same move with a freelance designer

Devi is a freelance graphic designer. Her fixed costs are her annual tax return, an expensive design-software subscription (the same price whether she has one client or twenty), and her professional-body membership. With only a few clients, that software cost feels huge on every project, and she's tempted to drop to cheaper, less professional tools - which would only limit the clients she can attract.

Instead she runs the pattern: she sees the cost as fixed, and works to dilute it by winning more clients; she maps her renewal dates and tax deadline; she sets aside time each month to sort invoices and receipts (batching her bookkeeping so the return is simple); and she pays an accountant for the final submission (outsourcing the sharp part), freeing her for design work worth far more than the fee.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own work - the costs and obligations that stay the same whether your month was good or bad:

  1. Identify your fixed obligations, and resist the trap of hiding. List the costs that don't move with your sales - licences, permits, insurance, subscriptions, returns - and see that staying small is what keeps them feeling heavy. Your mission is to serve people with your skill; growing lets you serve more and lightens this load on each sale.
  2. Take one afternoon and map them. For each: what, when, how much, what it needs - turning a year of nasty surprises into one known list.
  3. Systemise it, and value your time. Put reminders in your calendar now, make a checklist for each, decide what to batch - and for each task ask "is my time better spent doing this, or earning at what I do best?", outsourcing only where the answer is clear.

Going formal - doing the right thing - should never feel like a punishment, and the unfairness you feel is real: a cost that ignores how hard your month was lands cruelly on the small. But the answer was never to hide and stay small, shutting yourself off from the people a trustworthy, growing business could serve. Carry the cost the way you'd carry any weight that won't shrink: spread it across more good work, and lift it calmly on a system rather than snatching at it in a panic. Do that, and the very paperwork that felt like a hammer becomes quiet proof that you are exactly what your customers can trust.
