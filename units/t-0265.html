Eleven in the first line, none in the second

T1: The Ache

You are Ntsika, and somebody senior has finally asked the right question.

After the external assessor found eleven things the internal audit had not, the works manager asked, reasonably: "How did nobody notice for three years?"

It is a fair question and you cannot answer it, because on the face of it plenty of people should have. The operators check their equipment daily. The supervisors sign off every work order. There is a monthly performance report. There is an internal audit programme. There is a management review. By any account this is a plant with a great deal of checking in it.

And a criticality register sat three years out of date, touching every job plan, and not one of those activities was ever going to find it.

Not because anybody was careless. Because of where they all sit.

This organisation checks itself constantly and a three-year gap went unseen. How do I map who is actually assuring what, and find the place where the answer is nobody?

Before you read on, decide. This plant checks itself constantly. The operators do a daily round. The supervisors sign off every work order. There is a monthly performance report, an internal audit programme, and a management review. There is also the fact that the maintenance manager reads every breakdown report personally, that the shift supervisor walks the plant before handover, and that the reliability engineer looks at the vibration trends because he finds them interesting — none of which is written down anywhere. And a criticality register three years out of date, touching every job plan in the works, sat underneath all of it and not one of those activities was ever going to find it. Nobody was careless. Write down how you would map who is actually assuring what — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why position beats diligence

• “Add another check.” A sixteenth activity in the same position as the other fifteen. If the ones you have cannot see this, another of the same kind will not either.

• “Make the internal audit more thorough.” Worth doing and it is one activity among many, and thoroughness aimed at the same scope finds more within that scope. The register was outside what any of them was looking at.

• “People should have noticed.” Every one of them did what they were meant to do. The gap is structural rather than a matter of attention, and blaming attention guarantees it recurs somewhere else.

• “List all the assurance activities we have.” The right first step and, stopped there, it produces a reassuring list of fifteen. A list shows abundance; it cannot show where they all sit.

• “Bring in external auditors annually.” Adds a genuinely independent view once a year, and buying it before you know what your own arrangement cannot see means paying somebody to look wherever they choose.

• Arrange the activities by independence rather than by importance. Those who do the work check their own; those who oversee it check on their behalf; and those genuinely independent of both check whichever way the answer falls. All three are legitimate and none substitutes for another, because each sees only what its position permits — and an activity performed by the people whose work it examines cannot discover that the whole basis of that work is out of date. Counting by position is what makes the hole visible: many activities clustered at one level and none at another is not an abundance of assurance, it is a structural blind spot, and it is invisible while they are merely listed. Two more disciplines. Write against each what it is meant to catch — anything you cannot say that of is not assurance, however diligently it is done. And include the informal ones and mark them: the manager who reads every report, the walk before handover, the engineer who finds the trends interesting. Those are real assurance today and gone the morning that person leaves, and the map is the only place that fact becomes visible. Then follow each to whoever receives its answer, because one that reports to nobody has produced information that changes nothing.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the map - every activity somebody relies on, placed in a line by independence, counted, followed to whoever receives the answer, and the gaps written as structure.

1. List every activity somebody relies on to know the system is working - including the informal ones

<strong>Start by listing every activity that somebody, somewhere, is relying on to know this system is working. Not the ones in the procedure - the ones people actually rely on.</strong> <strong>Mine came to sixteen.</strong> The obvious ones: the operator's daily check, the planner's schedule review, the supervisor's work-order sign-off, the monthly performance report, the internal audit programme, the management review. <strong>And then the ones nobody had written down and everybody depended on:</strong> the fact that the maintenance manager reads every breakdown report personally; the informal walk the shift supervisor does before handover; the reliability engineer who happens to look at the vibration trends because they find it interesting. <strong>Write those down too, and mark them.</strong> <strong>An assurance activity that depends on one person's habit is real assurance today and is gone the morning they leave</strong>, and the map is the only place that fact becomes visible. <strong>Then write, against each one, what it is meant to catch.</strong> <strong>If you cannot say what an activity would detect, you have found something before you have even started mapping</strong> - an activity that consumes real time and protects against nothing in particular, which is more common in mature organisations than in new ones.

2. Place each activity in a line - who does the work, who oversees it, who independently checks

<strong>Three lines, and the only thing that separates them is independence from the work being assured.</strong> <strong>The first line owns and does the work, and checks its own output</strong> - operators, planners, supervisors. This is where most assurance genuinely happens and it is not a lesser line; it is the fastest and cheapest place to catch anything. <strong>The second line sets the rules and oversees the first from outside the doing</strong> - the people who own the standard, monitor compliance and challenge, without owning the work itself. <strong>The third line is independent of both and reports elsewhere</strong> - internal audit, reporting to a level above management, whose value is precisely that it does not depend on the goodwill of the people it examines. <strong>Now be strict, because the commonest error is generous.</strong> A supervisor checking their own team's work orders is <strong>first line</strong>, however senior they are and however conscientious. A monthly report compiled by the department about itself and sent upward is <strong>first line</strong>, not second - <strong>reporting your own performance is not oversight of it.</strong> <strong>The test is one question: could this activity produce a finding that is uncomfortable for the person doing the activity?</strong> If not, it is the first line, and calling it anything else is how organisations come to believe they have three lines when they have one.

3. Count what fell in each line - and look hardest at the empty one

<strong>Now count, because the shape is the finding and it is almost never what the organisation believes it is.</strong> <strong>Of my sixteen: eleven in the first line, none in the second, three in the third, and two performed by nobody at all.</strong> <strong>Read that shape.</strong> <strong>Eleven first-line activities is not a weakness - it is a plant where people check their own work carefully, which is worth having.</strong> <strong>Three in the third line is a functioning internal audit programme, which many organisations lack.</strong> <strong>And the middle is empty.</strong> <strong>Nobody sets the standard for how the first line checks itself, nobody looks at whether those eleven activities are still doing anything, and nobody challenges from outside the doing but inside the organisation.</strong> <strong>Which is exactly how a plant arrives at the position this module opened with.</strong> The first line was working and honest. The third line audited three clauses in two years, which is all a third line can do. <strong>And the gap between - the continuous oversight that would have noticed a criticality register going three years without an update - did not exist, so nothing found it until an external assessor arrived.</strong> <strong>The two activities in no line at all get their own row and their own finding.</strong> Somebody believes those are happening.

4. For each activity, record who receives the result - and follow it to a decision

<strong>An assurance activity that produces a result nobody receives is not assurance. It is work.</strong> <strong>So add a column: who gets the answer, and what happens then.</strong> <strong>Mine produced two findings I had not been looking for.</strong> <strong>First, the internal audit reports go to the maintenance manager - who is the person responsible for most of what is audited.</strong> That is not a third line. Independence of execution is only half; <strong>a third line that reports to the manager of the audited area is structurally unable to produce an uncomfortable finding, however independent the auditor personally is, and the auditor should not be asked to carry that alone.</strong> <strong>Second, the monthly performance report goes to a meeting, and I traced twelve months of minutes: not one references it.</strong> The report is produced, distributed, filed - <strong>and evaluated by nobody, which means the clause requiring evaluation is not met however good the reporting is.</strong> <strong>Follow every line to a decision, and where the trail ends, say so.</strong> <strong>The question is never 'is this activity performed'. It is 'has anything ever been different because of it'</strong>, and that question is answerable from minutes and records rather than from opinion.

5. Produce the map, and write the gaps as structure rather than as effort

<strong>The output is one page: activities down, lines across, recipient and last-decision beside each.</strong> <strong>Then the gaps, written as structure.</strong> <strong>Mine.</strong> <strong>There is no second line</strong> - so nominate one, name what it owns (the standard for first-line checks, and a periodic review of whether those checks still detect anything), and give it to a role that does not own the work. <strong>Internal audit reports into the area it audits</strong> - change the reporting line so it goes above the audited function, which costs nothing and is the single highest-value change on the page. <strong>Two activities are performed by nobody</strong> - assign them, or decide deliberately that they are not needed and remove them from everybody's assumptions, which is a legitimate answer and is better than a phantom control. <strong>Three activities depend on one person's habit</strong> - either write them into a role or accept the exposure knowingly. <strong>Now the test.</strong> <strong>Read every gap and ask whether it could be closed by somebody trying harder.</strong> <strong>None of these can, and that is what makes them structural.</strong> <strong>A responsibilities map is not a diagram of who is to blame when something is missed. It is a diagram of who was ever going to catch it</strong> - and its most useful finding is nearly always a place where the honest answer is nobody.

T3: The Blueprint

An assurance map is not a diagram of who is to blame when something is missed. It is a diagram of who was ever going to catch it - and its most useful finding is nearly always a place where the honest answer is nobody. Five moves:

  • List every activity somebody relies on. Including the informal ones - and mark the ones that depend on a single person's habit.
  • Place each in a line by independence. Be strict. Doing the work and checking your own output is one line, not two.
  • Count, and look hardest at the empty line. The shape is the finding.
  • Follow each result to a recipient and a decision. An activity nobody receives is work, not assurance.
  • Write the gaps as structure. None should be closeable by trying harder.

The three lines, and the only thing that separates them. Independence from the work being assured. The first line owns and does the work and checks its own output - operators, planners, supervisors. This is not a lesser line; it is the fastest and cheapest place to catch anything, and a plant with a strong first line is a well-run plant. The second line sets the rules and oversees the first from outside the doing. The third line is independent of both and reports elsewhere.

The test that keeps you strict. Could this activity produce a finding that is uncomfortable for the person doing the activity? A supervisor checking their own team's work orders - no, however senior and however conscientious. A monthly report the department compiles about itself - no. Reporting your own performance is not oversight of it. Being generous with this test is exactly how an organisation comes to believe it has three lines when it has one.

The shape. Sixteen activities: eleven in the first line, none in the second, three in the third, and two performed by nobody at all. Read it. Eleven is a plant where people check their own work carefully. Three is a functioning internal audit programme, which many organisations lack. And the middle is empty - nobody sets the standard for how the first line checks itself, and nobody looks at whether those eleven activities still detect anything. Which is precisely how a three-year-old register survives. The first line was working and honest; the third line audited three clauses in two years, which is all a third line can do; and the continuous oversight in between did not exist.

The two findings the recipient column produced. Internal audit reports to the maintenance manager - the person responsible for most of what is audited. That is not a third line: independence of execution is only half of it, and an auditor asked to carry structural independence personally has been given somebody else's problem. And the monthly performance report goes to a meeting whose twelve months of minutes never once reference it - produced, distributed, filed, and evaluated by nobody.

An honest boundary. This maps assurance and finds structural gaps. It does not create a second line - that is an organisational decision with a cost, and somebody senior has to want it. It does not fit every organisation; a small works may reasonably combine lines, and the point is then to know and state that rather than to pretend. And a map is of today: it goes stale exactly like the register in the ache, which is itself an argument for the second line it usually finds missing.

  1. Re-place every activity using the independence test. Say which move and why.
  2. The regulator is listed as line 3. Is that right? Be careful.
  3. Count the corrected map. What is the shape?
  4. The engineering manager appears at the centre of nearly everything. Say what the structural problem is - without suggesting anything about them personally.

Work it before you turn over.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. Re-placed. Daily checks and work-order sign-off: line 1, correctly. Monthly compliance report to the board: the compliance officer does not own operations, so this is genuine line 2 in function - but they report to the engineering manager, which compromises it; keep it as line 2 and flag the reporting line. Quarterly process-safety review, chaired and presented by the manager who owns the works: line 1. He is reviewing his own area and presenting his own account of it. Annual internal audit by a contracted firm reporting to the engineering manager: this is the hard one. The auditors are externally independent and their reporting line is not - they report to the owner of what they audit. Call it line 3 in execution, line 1 in governance, and say so plainly, because the distinction is the finding.

2. The regulator. Not line 3. All three lines are inside the organisation. The regulator is external oversight and it is genuinely valuable, but counting it as your own assurance is how a company outsources its conscience - and a regulator inspects to their scope on their schedule, not to yours. Move it off the map into a separate row marked external.

3. The corrected shape. Line 1: four (daily checks, sign-off, the safety review, and the leak review that is not happening). Line 2: one, compromised. Line 3: none, once the audit's reporting line is honestly described and the regulator is removed. A company that believed it had a balanced three-line model has, in structure, four first-line activities and one compromised second.

4. The engineering manager. They own the works, the network and maintenance; they chair the safety review of their own area; the compliance officer reports to them; and the internal auditors report to them. Nothing here is about this person. They may be excellent, and the concentration probably arose from a small company growing without anybody redrawing the lines. The structural problem is that there is no route by which an uncomfortable finding about this manager's area can reach the board without passing through them. That is a property of the diagram, and it would be equally true if the post were vacant.

5. The leak review. It is on the slide, it is in nobody's job, and it has not happened for eight months. This is worse than an activity that never existed, because the organisation is relying on it: somebody, somewhere, is not worrying about leak trends on the understanding that this is covered. A phantom control does not merely fail to protect - it displaces the attention that would otherwise have gone there. The finding is that assurance activities have no owner-of-record and no check that they are still occurring.

6. Two realistic recommendations. First: change the internal audit reporting line so the auditors report to the board or a non-executive director rather than to the engineering manager. This costs nothing - the same firm, the same days, the same fee - and it is the single highest-value change available to them. Second: do not create a compliance department. Give the second line to somebody who already exists and does not own operations - the finance manager, the company secretary, whoever - with a narrow, real remit: maintain the list of assurance activities, confirm quarterly that each is still occurring, and report exceptions to the board. That is perhaps two hours a quarter, and it would have caught both the leak review and the register.

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Counting a supervisor checking their own team's work as oversight. Doing the work and checking it is one line, however senior or conscientious the person is.

• Counting an informal habit as an assurance activity. If it depends on one person remembering to do it, mark it as such - it is not structural assurance, and it will not survive that person leaving.

• Missing that an activity has no recipient. Work that nobody receives and acts on is not assurance, whatever it is called on the org chart.

• Being generous with the independence test. Ask whether the activity could produce a finding uncomfortable for the person doing it. If not, it is not oversight - however independent the person feels.

What you can do now that you could not before. List what an organisation actually relies on, including what is not written down. Place activities by independence rather than by seniority. Read the shape and find the empty line. Follow every result to a recipient and a decision. And write gaps as structure, so that none can be closed by somebody trying harder.

The works manager's question was the right one and it had a real answer. Nobody noticed for three years because eleven activities were looking at the work, three were looking at the system once every eight months, and nothing at all was looking at whether the eleven still worked.

Every one of those people was doing their job. That is what makes it a structural finding rather than a human one.
