T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a cold morning at a crude-oil refinery, and you are the reliability engineer on the crude unit; your coffee is still too hot to drink. On the control-room screen for the crude distillation tower - the tall column that boils crude oil and splits it into fuels - is the overhead line, the big pipe carrying hot vapour off the top. The number you are watching is the temperature at the top: 118 degrees C. Two days ago it sat at 132. The night-shift operator, Khanyisile, points at it with the end of her pen and says, flatly, "It has been creeping down all week. Is that a problem? Nobody told me a number to watch."

You do not have a good answer, and that is the part that stings. You know - somewhere in the back of your mind - that the top of this tower is where acidic water can form and start eating the steel from the inside, and that there is a "safe zone" for that temperature. But you cannot remember it, it is not written on the screen, and it is not in the operator's procedure. So right now, the only thing standing between a slow leak and a safe plant is whether someone happens to feel a number "looks wrong".

Down the corridor the plant manager's footsteps are getting closer; the monthly reliability meeting is in twenty minutes, and corrosion is item one - because this unit had an unplanned shutdown eight months ago that cost the refinery R6 million. The tempting move is to tell Khanyisile "keep it above 120, I think", write it on a sticky note, and walk into the meeting. It would feel like an answer. But you would be guessing - and a guessed limit that is wrong is more dangerous than no limit at all, because people will trust it.

Write down what you would tell her, and what you would take into that meeting. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one leaves her holding

• “Tell her to keep it above 120 — I’m fairly sure that’s right.” Thirty seconds, and it is the most dangerous line on this page. A guessed limit that is wrong is worse than no limit at all, because people will trust it — she will hold the plant at your remembered number for years, and nobody will ever check it again.

• “Tell her to call me whenever it moves.” Safe for you and useless at three in the morning when you do not answer. It also leaves the judgement with the one person who cannot make it: she needs a number and an action, not access to your opinion.

• “Set one hard limit and trip the unit if it’s crossed.” Clear, defensible, and it treats a slow drift and a sudden excursion as the same event. A number you may sit past for an hour and a number that does damage in minutes are different things, and giving them one response means you will either trip the plant needlessly or respond far too slowly.

• “Go and find the corrosion report first.” Exactly right, and it is a starting point rather than an answer — the report gives you the mechanism and the condition that drives it, and turning that into limits an operator can act on at three in the morning is the work that still has to be done.

• “Put it on the agenda — we’ll agree limits properly next month.” Professionally impeccable and it leaves the plant running all month with the same protection it has now, which is whether somebody happens to feel a number looks wrong.

The uncomfortable thing to keep hold of: right now the only thing standing between a slow leak and a safe plant is whether somebody notices a number and happens to be worried by it.

What you need is not a guess but a method: a repeatable way to set the exact temperatures, pressures and flows equipment must stay inside to avoid damage, and a clear rule for what each operator does when a number drifts out. Here is that tool, worked on this tower.

T2: The Tool in Action

An Integrity Operating Window (IOW) is a set of limits on a process variable that keeps equipment from degrading faster than planned - a "health fence" around a number. Stay inside and the equipment ages at the slow, expected rate; step outside and you spend its life faster than you planned. The recognised recipe is API RP 584 (the American Petroleum Institute's Recommended Practice - a guide, not a law). Watch it turn this morning's worry into a precise answer.

Step 1 - The variable and the damage mechanism

The variable: tower-top overhead temperature, tag TI-118 on the crude tower C-101 (carbon steel). The slow damage it guards against: acid dew-point corrosion - at the cold top, hydrochloric acid and water condense together and eat the steel. The enemy is LOW temperature, not high - so this is a low-side window.

Step 2 - The basis and the cliff

The cliff comes from the corrosion report (the basis you can defend): the acid dew point is 110 C - below it, acidic liquid forms on the steel. The rates: metal loss jumps to about 3 mm/year below the dew point, versus about 0.2 mm/year run safely above; with a 6 mm corrosion allowance, that is the spare steel gone in about 2 years instead of the planned thirty.

Step 3 - Set the three nested fences, with margin

Set the fences inside the 110 C cliff, with margin: CRITICAL 115 C (a 5 C margin - below it damage can begin within hours); STANDARD 120 C (a working buffer - correct it on shift); INFORMATIONAL 125 C (normal running is 130-135, so dropping below 125 is the first sign of a downward trend). The fences step out from the cliff: 115 -> 120 -> 125.

Step 4 - The action, the owner, and where it lives

Informational (below 125): the operator logs it, the engineer reviews the trend. Standard (below 120): the operator raises the temperature back toward 130 and tells the engineer the same shift to find the cause. Critical (below 115): the operator restores above 120 at once and calls the supervisor, reducing rate if it cannot be restored. Review trigger: re-check if the crude feed changes, and at least yearly. All of it on the screen, not buried.

Now run this morning's 118 C against the fences: it is below the informational limit (125) and below the standard limit (120), but still above the critical limit (115). So this is a standard breach - not an emergency, but a real "fix it this shift": raise the temperature back toward 130, log it, and tell the engineer to find why it has crept down all week. You now have a precise answer for Khanyisile and for the plant manager - and it is not a guess; it traces straight back to the dew point in the corrosion report. The most useful idea in the whole method is that not every limit is equally urgent: a limit you can drift past for an hour is a very different thing from one that does damage in minutes, so each variable gets up to three nested fences keyed to how fast the harm comes.

T3: The Blueprint

You build one IOW in four steps - the heart of API RP 584's seven.

  • 1. The variable and the damage mechanism. Name the exact variable and its tag, the equipment it protects, and the slow damage mechanism it guards against (corrosion, cracking, creep, fouling, embrittlement) - and which way the enemy lies, too high or too low. An IOW with no real mechanism behind it is just noise that trains people to ignore alarms.
  • 2. The basis and the cliff. Find the value at which damage truly begins - the cliff (a dew point, a creep temperature) - from a basis document you can defend, plus the fast-vs-safe damage rate and the remaining allowance. Every limit must trace to a real number someone signed.
  • 3. Set the three nested fences, with margin. Place limits inside the cliff, never on its edge: critical (act now - damage starts fast), standard (fix it this shift), informational (watch it - it is trending). Check the order steps the right way for your enemy.
  • 4. The action, the owner, and where it lives. For each fence write the exact action and the named person who does it; put the numbers on the operator's screen and procedure, not just a register; and set a review trigger.

The three fences, by urgency: a critical limit (cross it and damage or a safety event can begin within minutes - an immediate operator response), a standard limit (damage builds over days or weeks - a planned, same-shift response), and an informational limit (not urgent, but the number is trending the wrong way - worth tracking). To be usable, each IOW must carry the variable and where it is measured, the limit values and their severity, the degradation mechanism (the reason), the required action and who does it, and the basis. IOWs fit best where a variable drives a known slow damage mechanism and the equipment fails slowly and expensively. They are not a replacement for safety trip systems: those guard against sudden dangerous events and act automatically; an IOW guards against slow damage and relies on people responding.

Two rules keep the fence honest. First, set every limit inside the safe zone, never on the cliff edge - a margin is what gives a person time to react before real damage starts; a critical limit set at the damage point protects no one. Second, apply the one-line sanity test before you publish: could you defend every number to a plant manager by pointing at the basis document? If yes, the fence is real; if any number traces only to a hunch, it is a sticky note - go back to the basis. And make sure the three fences step the right way for your enemy: cold or hot, the most urgent fence sits closest to the cliff.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your IOWs honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A guessed limit is worse than none. A wrong number that people trust gives false comfort and may even point them the wrong way. Every limit must trace to a basis document - if you cannot defend it, do not publish it.

• Leave a margin - never fence on the cliff edge. The critical limit sits inside the damage point, with enough gap that a person has time to act before harm begins. A limit set at the cliff is a limit that fires too late.

• No mechanism, no fence. Set limits only where a variable drives a real, known damage mechanism. A limit "just because" is noise - and noise trains operators to ignore the alarms that matter. Fewer, well-reasoned fences beat many guessed ones.

• An IOW is not a trip system. It guards against slow damage and relies on people; it does not replace the automatic safety systems that catch sudden events. Know which danger you are managing, and do not let one stand in for the other.

• It is not set-and-forget. A changed feed can move the cliff; metal thins over the years. Re-check each window when conditions change and at least yearly, with a named owner - a fence nobody maintains quietly rots.

Khanyisile was never failing - she was doing exactly what she should, watching a number nobody had given her a meaning for. The fix was never to ask people to feel harder; it was to write the safe zone down, in numbers, where they work, with an action against each fence. An Integrity Operating Window is the engineered health-fence that replaces a guessed number on a sticky note. Build it from a basis you can defend, put it on the screen, and the slow leak that used to hide in "it looks a bit low this week" becomes a clear, owned, this-shift action - caught years before it ever costs R6 million.
