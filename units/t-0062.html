T1: The Ache

It is a cold Tuesday morning in the control room of a wind farm, high on an inland escarpment. You are the asset performance lead. Three screens glow in the dark room, showing live readings from all 92 turbines: wind speed, blade pitch, gearbox temperature, vibration, power output. Every turbine sends a new reading every ten seconds, around the clock, all year.

In your hand is a printed email from the Chief Financial Officer, one line underlined in red: "We are sitting on eight years of turbine data. Everyone keeps saying it is gold. Show me the money by month end."

You feel a small jump of pride - you know this data is good; your team has used it to cut downtime and plan repairs, saving the company about R4.1 million last quarter. But that saving is inside your own walls. The CFO is asking something harder: can this data earn money from outside the company, as a product someone else will pay for? You feel the pull to do the obvious thing - open a spreadsheet, dump every field, and offer the whole lot to anyone who will buy it. Just sell the data, a voice says. But some of it could expose how your turbines really perform - the kind of thing a competitor or a maintenance contractor could use against you at the next contract talk. Sell the wrong field and you hand away your edge for a small cheque.

We are sitting on eight years of turbine data - everyone says it is gold, and the CFO wants money from it by month end. How do I find the data worth selling, shape it for a real buyer, and price it without giving away the farm?

Before you read on, decide. Eight years of data from 92 turbines, R4.1 million of value already taken from it inside your own walls, and a CFO asking whether it can earn money from outside by month end. And a voice saying: open a spreadsheet, dump every field, sell the lot. Write down what you would actually do. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one gives away

• “Export everything and offer it to whoever will buy.” Fast, and it is the one answer that can genuinely damage the company. Some of those fields show how your turbines really perform — and a maintenance contractor or a competitor reading that across the table at the next contract talk has been handed your position for a small cheque.

• “Anonymise it and then sell everything.” Better, and it treats the risk as a single switch. Different buyers expose you in different ways: stripping names protects individual turbines and does nothing about a site-by-site map that tells a rival exactly where the wind is good.

• “Price it by what it cost us to collect.” The intuitive basis and the wrong one. Your collection cost is a fact about you; what a buyer will pay is a fact about the decision they can now make. Price from your cost and you will either leave most of the value behind or ask for something nobody wants.

• “Build a platform and sell subscriptions.” A real business, and it is a year of work and a product nobody has asked for yet. You do not know who the buyer is, and a platform built before that question is answered is the most expensive way to find out.

• “It’s too risky — keep using it internally.” Safe, defensible, and it answers a different question from the one the CFO asked. The internal R4.1 million does not stop being real if some of the data also earns outside.

The sentence to hold on to before you turn the page: nobody buys data. They buy a decision they could not make before — which means the first thing to find is not a field, but somebody with a job they cannot finish.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to find the data worth selling, turn it into something a buyer can actually use, and price it without hurting yourself. Here is that method, worked on these 92 turbines.

T2: The Tool in Action

You are building a data-monetisation strategy - a plain plan answering four questions: what data do we have that is valuable, who would pay for it, what shape must it be in so they can use it, and how do we sell it without hurting ourselves. This is external monetisation: a product another organisation pays you for, on top of the value you already get using the data yourself. It runs in five steps. Watch it on the wind farm.

Step 1 - Build a data inventory

Rate each data type quality x uniqueness (1-5). Gearbox temperature + vibration scores high on both - clean AND rare. The 8-year wind-speed record is clean and fairly rare. The maintenance logs are rare (5) but messy (3) - free text written by technicians over the years. The high-on-both rows are the candidates; the maintenance logs are a candidate too, but flagged for cleaning first.

Step 2 - Find the buyer and their job-to-be-done

Two buyers fit. The turbine maker (OEM - the original equipment manufacturer): their job is 'improve our next gearbox design so it fails less' - which fits the gearbox + maintenance history exactly. A wind-resource consultancy: their job is 'tell our clients how much power a site will make' - which fits the 8-year wind record. Design a product for each fitting job.

Step 3 - Choose a product form

The OEM has engineers who read raw numbers and want to know WHY gearboxes fail - so a raw data feed (clean, anonymised gearbox data with matched maintenance outcomes; the messy logs must be cleaned first - real work). The consultancy does not want 8 years of 10-second readings, it wants the answer - so an insight report (a tidy long-term wind-resource summary). Different jobs, different forms - deliberately not the same thing to both.

Step 4 - Screen for risk (the step you must not skip)

OEM feed: legal PASS (no personal data); contractual PASS (the purchase contract allows sharing back to the maker); competitive FAIL (raw outcomes could reveal one model underperforms) -> FIX: anonymise + aggregate (remove turbine IDs and the site name) -> PASS. Resource report: competitive FAIL (could help a rival build a farm next door) -> FIX: sell the HISTORICAL summary only, to consultancies not site-scouting developers -> PASS. A gate that cannot be fixed means decline the buyer.

Step 5 - Price it to the value, and pick a model

OEM: a better gearbox saves them millions in warranty claims -> a yearly subscription of R900,000 for refreshed, anonymised data (one-off cleaning cost R250,000, so year-1 net R650,000). Consultancy: a solid local reference saves weeks of modelling -> a pay-per-report licence of R120,000; expect 3 buyers = R360,000 (cost R40,000, net R320,000). Year-1 total: R1,260,000 revenue, R290,000 cost, R970,000 net - earned from data you already owned, without giving away your edge.

See what the method did. It refused "dump it all and sell" and instead found two real buyers, each with a job worth paying to finish, shaped a different product for each, screened every product through three gates, and priced to the buyer's gain - landing about R0.97 million in year one from data the company already owned. You do not sell data; you sell a better decision someone outside can make with it - and the price is the measure of how much that decision is worth to them, never your cost of collecting it. And the step that protects everyone is the risk screen: the OEM gets anonymised, aggregated data so no single turbine is exposed; the consultancy gets only the historical summary, not a map for a rival to build next door. Value created, edge kept, no one harmed.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the strategy in five moves.

  • 1. Build a data inventory. List every data type and rate it quality (complete and clean?) x uniqueness (does anyone else have it?). The rows high on both are your candidates; flag the unique-but-messy ones for cleaning.
  • 2. Find the buyer and their job-to-be-done. Who outside would make a better decision with this? People buy help finishing a job, not data - match your data to a job someone pays to finish.
  • 3. Choose a product form. Match the form to the job: a raw feed, an insight report, or a benchmark / service. Do not default two buyers to the same thing.
  • 4. Screen for risk. Three gates before anything leaves: legal (law/privacy), contractual (agreements), competitive (your edge). Fail a gate? Apply a fix (anonymise, aggregate, historical-only) or decline.
  • 5. Price it to the value. Tie the price to the buyer's gain, not your cost; pick a model (subscription / pay-per-use / value-share).

The distinction worth keeping: internal monetisation is using your own data to save or make money inside the company (better repairs, less downtime - you already do this); external monetisation, this unit's target, is a product an outside organisation pays for. It fits when three things are all true: your assets produce a steady stream of data, someone outside would make a better decision with it, and you can share it without breaking a law, a contract, or your competitive edge. It does not fit when the data is thin or unreliable, when no outside party actually needs it, or when sharing would break a privacy law or a customer contract - there, stop; a weak data product earns little and damages trust. Note the scope: this is data from industrial assets (turbines, pumps, plant), not personal data - and the legal gate in step 4 is exactly where any privacy law is checked first.

Two disciplines keep this honest. First, never let anything out before the risk screen - the rushed self wants to skip straight to the cheque, and that is exactly how a privacy breach, a broken contract, or a handed-away edge happens; run all three gates, fix or decline, every time. Second, price to the value, not the cost: what you spent collecting the data is irrelevant to the buyer - what their better decision is worth to them is the whole story, and that is usually many times your cost. Sell the insight, not the raw dump; help a real decision; and let the revenue be the measure that you served someone well.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your data strategy honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Never skip the risk screen. Legal, contractual, competitive - run all three before a single field leaves the building. The fastest way to turn an asset into a liability is to sell data you were not allowed to, or that gives away your edge.

• Sell the decision, not the dump. A buyer does not want your raw exhaust; they want a job finished. Shape the data into the form that finishes their job - a feed, a report, or a service - and you sell something far more valuable than a spreadsheet.

• Price to the buyer's gain, not your cost. What it cost you to collect the data is your business and irrelevant to them. What their better decision is worth is the price - usually many times your cost.

• Anonymise and aggregate by default. If exposing a single asset, site or person could harm someone or hand away an edge, strip the identifiers and roll the numbers up. Most of the value survives; most of the risk does not.

• If it does not help a real decision, do not sell it. A weak or pointless data product earns little and erodes trust. Monetise only where someone genuinely makes a better decision because of you.

You started the morning with three glowing screens and a one-line demand, and the temptation to throw eight years of data over the wall for whatever you could get. You can walk into the CFO's office with one page instead: two buyers, two products, every risk screened and fixed, and a year-one net of about R0.97 million - earned from data you already owned, without handing away your edge or exposing a soul. Sell valuable insight, not just data; price to the buyer's gain, not your cost; and never skip the risk screen. Do that and the data your assets quietly produce every ten seconds becomes a second, honest income stream - one that helps a real buyer make a real decision, and measures its own worth by how well it served them.
