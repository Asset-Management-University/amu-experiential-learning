The document you trust

T1: The Ache

A maintenance crew is confidently overhauling a pump, working carefully to the procedure in the workshop file - a procedure printed two years ago, whose torque values were revised last spring. They do everything right, to the wrong numbers. The drawing beside it was superseded months back, but nobody withdrew the old print, so it is still there, still trusted.

The same week, an auditor asks Onalenna for last year's failure history on that pump line. She goes to pull the completed work orders - and they are gone, thrown out in a workshop clean-up as old clutter. There is no history to show, and no history to compute the pump's reliability from either.

Two quiet disasters, and neither announced itself. The crew were not careless - they were handed a wrong document that looked exactly as authoritative as the right one. And the history was not stolen - it was simply never kept. Nothing here went wrong loudly. It went wrong silently, which is worse.

A lost document I go and look for - but a wrong document I trust will hurt me without warning, and a history I never kept is gone for good. How do I make sure the right version is always in hand, and the record I will need is always there?

Before you read on, decide. Two things surfaced this week. The crew rebuilt the gearbox carefully, methodically, exactly to the torque values on the procedure in their hands — a copy printed two years ago, superseded last spring when the values changed. Nothing about it looked wrong; it looked exactly as authoritative as the current one. And separately, you were asked for the pump's failure history so its reliability could be established, and there isn't one — not lost, never kept. Nobody was careless in either case, and neither failure announced itself. Write down how you would make sure the right version is always in hand and the record you need is always there — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and which document is actually dangerous

• “Tell people to check they have the latest version.” Check against what? They had no reason to suspect it, because a superseded document carries no warning label — and asking everybody to verify every document before every job puts the burden at the point where there is least time.

• “Put everything on the shared drive.” Helps, and the printed copy in the crew's hands came off that drive two years ago. Central storage does nothing about the copies already in circulation, which is where the harm happened.

• “Keep better records from now on.” Right about the second failure and it will drift within a year unless somebody has decided which history must be kept and for how long — “better” is not a retention decision.

• “Discipline is slipping — audit compliance.” An audit against a system that does not exist. The crew complied fully; they complied with the wrong thing.

• “It is only paperwork — the real work is the engineering.” This week it produced a gearbox built to wrong numbers and a pump whose reliability cannot be established. Both are engineering outcomes.

• Start by inverting what most people assume the problem is. A document you cannot find is only an inconvenience — you know it is missing, you go and look, and until you have it you do not act. A document that is quietly wrong is a hazard, because you do not know it is wrong, and you will act on it with complete confidence. So the purpose of controlling documents is not tidiness: it has exactly one job — to make sure the person at the point of use is working from the current, correct, approved version, and knows it. Every mechanism — revision status, a master list, controlled copies, withdrawing superseded ones — is machinery in service of that single outcome. Then the second discipline, which is a different problem in similar clothes. A document guides future action and must be current; a record is evidence of what happened and must be preserved unchanged. They need opposite treatment — one superseded and withdrawn, the other never altered — and they fail in opposite ways: a stale document misleads you, while a record never kept is simply gone, and no diligence afterwards recovers it.

Here are the two disciplines that do it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you keep the current version in hand and the history safe - seeing the real danger, controlling the copy, telling a document from a record, setting the retention period, and knowing why none of it is bureaucracy.

1. See the real danger - a wrong document you trust, not a lost one

START BY INVERTING WHAT MOST PEOPLE ASSUME THE PROBLEM IS. A DOCUMENT YOU CANNOT FIND IS ONLY AN INCONVENIENCE: you KNOW it is missing, so you go and look for it, and until you have it you do not act on it. A DOCUMENT THAT IS QUIETLY WRONG IS A HAZARD, because you do NOT know it is wrong - a two-year-old procedure carries no warning label, a superseded drawing looks identical to the current one, and you will act on it with complete confidence, walking straight into the mistake it contains. That is why the crew working carefully to last spring's revised torque values, off a procedure printed two years ago, is the real nightmare: they did everything right, to the wrong numbers, and nothing warned them. SO THE PURPOSE OF DOCUMENTATION CONTROL IS NOT TIDINESS, AND IT IS NOT PAPERWORK FOR ITS OWN SAKE. IT HAS EXACTLY ONE JOB: TO MAKE SURE THE PERSON AT THE POINT OF USE IS ALWAYS WORKING FROM THE CURRENT, CORRECT, APPROVED VERSION - AND KNOWS IT. Every mechanism you are about to meet - the revision status, the master list, the controlled copy, the withdrawal of old versions - is machinery in service of that single outcome. Hold the outcome in mind and the mechanics make sense; forget it and they feel like bureaucracy. THE SINGLE HARDEST TRUTH OF THE UNIT: A DOCUMENT YOU TRUST THAT IS QUIETLY WRONG IS MORE DANGEROUS THAN A DOCUMENT YOU CANNOT FIND.

2. Controlled vs uncontrolled - the master and its revisions

NOW THE MACHINERY THAT DELIVERS THAT OUTCOME. EVERY CONTROLLED DOCUMENT HAS: a UNIQUE IDENTITY (so it can be referred to without confusion), an OWNER (someone responsible for keeping it right), a REVISION STATUS (which version is this, and is it the current one?), an APPROVAL before it may be used, and a SINGLE MASTER that is the one true source. There is a MASTER LIST - a register that names each document and states which revision is current - so anyone, anywhere, can check what they are holding against the truth. AND HERE IS THE DISTINCTION THAT MATTERS MOST IN DAILY WORK, THE ONE THAT DECIDES WHETHER THE PERSON AT THE BENCH IS SAFE: A CONTROLLED COPY IS ONE THE SYSTEM KEEPS CURRENT - when the master is revised, this copy is replaced, so what is in your hand tracks the truth automatically. AN UNCONTROLLED COPY IS A SNAPSHOT - correct at the instant it was printed, and silently wrong the moment the master moves on. That is exactly why a print taken off the system is marked plainly - UNCONTROLLED COPY, CHECK THE SYSTEM FOR THE CURRENT VERSION - so the next person who finds it on a bench is warned not to trust it blindly. AND THE STEP PEOPLE SKIP AND PAY FOR: WHEN A DOCUMENT IS REVISED, THE OLD VERSION MUST BE ACTIVELY WITHDRAWN FROM EVERYWHERE IT LIVES - the workshop file, the pinboard, the drawer. A superseded print left in circulation is a trap already set, and it will catch someone eventually, because it looks exactly like the document they are supposed to use.

3. Document or record? - the two mirror-image disciplines

THIS IS THE HINGE THE WHOLE UNIT TURNS ON, SO GET IT EXACTLY RIGHT. A DOCUMENT TELLS YOU WHAT TO DO - the procedure, the drawing, the blank work-order template, the standard. A RECORD PROVES WHAT WAS DONE - the completed work order, the test result, the signed permit, the calibration certificate. THEY ARE OPPOSITE DISCIPLINES, AND THAT IS THE POINT. FOR A DOCUMENT YOU WANT EXACTLY ONE VERSION, ALWAYS CURRENT, WITH THE OLD ONES GONE - because a second, older copy is a hazard (see box 2). FOR A RECORD YOU WANT TO KEEP IT, UNCHANGED, FOR AS LONG AS IT HAS VALUE - because a lost record is history you can never get back, and an altered record is evidence you have destroyed. THE NEAT TEST THAT TIES THEM TOGETHER AND MAKES IT UNFORGETTABLE: THE SAME BLANK FORM IS A DOCUMENT; THE INSTANT IT IS FILLED IN AND SIGNED, IT BECOMES A RECORD. The blank permit template is a controlled document - one current version, revised and approved; the completed permit is a record - kept, unaltered, retained. Control the first so only the newest survives; retain the second so none is ever lost. AND CONFUSING THE TWO IS EXACTLY HOW THE TWO DISASTERS HAPPEN: treat a record like a document and you overwrite or bin the history (the work orders in the skip); treat a document like a record and you hoard old versions until someone works off the wrong one (the superseded drawing in the file). One current truth; one permanent memory. Keep them straight and the rest is easy.

4. Set the retention period on purpose, not by habit

HOW LONG DO WE KEEP THIS? IS A DECISION, NOT A DEFAULT, AND YOU MAKE IT BY ASKING WHAT THE RECORD IS FOR AND WHO WILL NEED IT AND WHEN. FOUR HONEST REASONS SET THE FLOOR. THE STATUTORY OR LEGAL MINIMUM: some records the law simply requires you to keep for a set number of years, and that is not negotiable. THE WARRANTY PERIOD: keep the evidence while a claim is still possible, or you cannot make the claim. THE ASSET'S OWN LIFE: a pressure vessel's inspection records must OUTLIVE THE VESSEL ITSELF, because the next inspector, years from now, needs the whole history to judge it safe - a record tied to a long-lived asset inherits that long life. AND AUDIT AND LIABILITY: you may have to prove, years later, that the work was done correctly, by whom, to what result. THEN HOLD BOTH ERRORS IN VIEW AT ONCE. KEEP RECORDS TOO SHORT AND YOU DESTROY THE VERY FAILURE HISTORY THAT PLANNING RUNS ON - last year's completed work orders are NOT clutter, they are the times-between-failure, the recurring-fault patterns, the evidence for repair-versus-replace - and you may quietly breach the law at the same time. KEEP EVERYTHING FOREVER AND YOU DROWN THE SIGNAL IN COST AND NOISE, so that when you finally need one record it is lost among a million you never needed. THE ANSWER IS A WRITTEN RETENTION SCHEDULE - A PERIOD SET DELIBERATELY FOR EACH TYPE OF RECORD - not a default of keep-it-till-the-cabinet-is-full-then-throw-out-whatever-is-oldest, which is precisely how a plant's failure history ends up in a skip during a tidy-up.

5. Why it is not bureaucracy - the point-of-use person, and the plant's memory

END ON WHY NONE OF THIS IS BUREAUCRACY, BECAUSE IF THE LEARNER FILES IT UNDER RED TAPE THEY WILL NOT DO IT, AND IF THEY FILE IT UNDER BLAME THEY WILL RESENT IT. BOTH ARE WRONG. FIRST, THE PERSON AT THE POINT OF USE: DOCUMENT CONTROL IS NOT BUREAUCRACY. IT EXISTS TO PROTECT THE PERSON AT THE POINT OF USE FROM BEING SET UP TO FAIL. USING A SUPERSEDED DOCUMENT HANDED TO YOU IS A FAILURE OF THE SYSTEM, NOT THE PERSON. THE SYSTEM'S JOB IS TO MAKE THE RIGHT VERSION THE EASIEST ONE TO FIND AND USE. The crew who worked to the wrong torque values did nothing wrong - the control that should have withdrawn the old procedure did, and blaming the crew both wounds a person and hides the real fault, so the same thing happens again next month. SECOND, THE RECORDS: A RECORDS SYSTEM IS NOT A LIBRARY OF BLAME. IT IS THE PLANT'S MEMORY. IT ALLOWS THE ORGANISATION TO LEARN FROM ITS HISTORY INSTEAD OF BEING FORCED TO REPEAT IT. You keep the records not to catch someone out three years later, but so the plant can remember what it has already learned - which pump keeps failing, which repair held and which did not, what the machine's real story is. For a planner this is not abstract: THAT MEMORY IS THE RAW MATERIAL OF EVERY PLAN YOU WILL EVER MAKE, and the day it is thrown out is the day you start guessing. Control the documents to protect the person doing the work today; retain the records to protect the plant's ability to learn tomorrow. That is the whole discipline, and it is the opposite of pointless paperwork.

T3: The Blueprint

A document you trust that is quietly wrong is more dangerous than a document you cannot find. Control documents so only the current version survives; retain records so the history is never lost. Five questions:

  • What is the real danger? Not the lost document - the wrong one you trust. A superseded procedure looks exactly as authoritative as the right one, and it will hurt you without warning.
  • Controlled or uncontrolled? A controlled copy is kept current; an uncontrolled copy is a dated snapshot. When the master is revised, the old version is withdrawn so it cannot be picked up by mistake.

  • How long do I keep it? Set the retention period by purpose - legal minimum, warranty, the asset's own life, audit and liability - and write it down as a schedule, not a habit.

T4: Your Turn

Here is a real pile off a planner's desk. Sort it out.

#Item
1The current pump-overhaul procedure (revision 4)
2A blank permit-to-work template
3Three older prints of a pump drawing (revisions A, B and C) in the workshop file
4A stack of last year's completed and signed work orders
5A calibration certificate for a pressure gauge, signed and dated
  1. Sort each as a document (tells you what to do) or a record (proves what was done).
  2. The uncontrolled print. You decide to keep one older drawing print at a bench as a reference. Write the exact words you would stamp on it to protect the next person who finds it.
  3. Control the document. For the pump-overhaul procedure (item 1), name what "controlled" requires - and what must happen to revisions A-C of the drawing now that a newer one exists.
  4. Set retention. Choose a retention period for the completed work orders (4) and the calibration certificate (5), and justify each by its purpose - not a number, a reason.
  5. The cost of loss. Name the specific maintenance-planning cost of having binned last year's work orders.

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the sort. Documents (tell you what to do): the current procedure (1) and the blank permit template (2) - and the drawing prints (3) are documents too, but superseded ones. Records (prove what was done): the completed work orders (4) and the signed calibration certificate (5). The tell: could you fill it in or work from it (document), or does it already record a thing that happened (record)?

Second, the stamp. Something unmistakable, such as: "UNCONTROLLED COPY - FOR REFERENCE ONLY - CHECK THE MASTER LIST FOR THE CURRENT REVISION BEFORE USE." The point is that the next person is warned, in the document's own words, that it is a dated snapshot and not to be trusted for real work.

Third, control. The procedure being "controlled" requires: a unique identity, an owner, a clear revision status (it is revision 4), approval before use, a single master on the system, and availability at the point of use - and, critically, that when it was revised the earlier revisions were withdrawn. For the drawing, revisions A, B and C must now be actively withdrawn from the workshop file (removed or clearly marked superseded), because three obsolete prints sitting next to the current one is three traps waiting to be picked up.

Fourth, retention with reasons. The completed work orders: keep them long enough to serve the asset's life and the planning history - several years at least, justified by the failure history and repair-versus-replace evidence they carry, and by any statutory/audit minimum. The calibration certificate: keep it at least to the asset's next calibration and audit cycle, justified by proving the gauge was fit when used and by liability if a reading is later questioned. The mark of a real answer is that each number comes with a because, not a guess.

Fifth, the cost of loss. Binning last year's work orders destroyed the failure history - the times between failures, the recurring-fault patterns, the basis for computing reliability and for the repair-versus-replace decision. Next year's plan is now blind: you cannot see what has been failing, how often, or whether to keep fixing it - you are planning from scratch on a machine that had a story you threw away.

T5: The Guardrails

Control documents so only the current version survives. Retain records so the history is never lost. A document tells you what to do; a record proves what was done.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Trusting a document without checking its revision. A wrong version carries no warning and looks exactly as authoritative as the right one. Check the revision against the master list before you act on it.

• Using an uncontrolled print as if it were current. A print is a snapshot, correct only when it came off the system. Mark it uncontrolled, and check the master for the current version before real work.

• Not withdrawing the old version on revision. A superseded print left in the file is a trap already set. When a document changes, actively remove the old one from everywhere it lives.

• Confusing a document with a record. Overwrite a record and you lose the proof; hoard old versions of a document and you breed the wrong-version hazard. One you keep current; the other you keep, unchanged.

• Setting retention by habit, not purpose. "Keep it till the cabinet is full" is how the failure history ends up in a skip. Set a period per record type from legal, warranty, asset-life and audit needs, and write it down.

• Binning the work-order history planning needs. Completed work orders are the failure patterns and the repair-versus-replace evidence. Throw them out and next year's plan is blind.

• Keeping everything forever. Retain without limit and the signal drowns in noise and cost, and nothing can be found when it matters. Retention has a floor and a ceiling.

• Blaming the person handed a wrong version. Using a superseded document you were given is a failure of control, not of the person. Fix the system that let the old version survive.

A crew worked to a two-year-old procedure and a superseded drawing, and an audit found last year's history in a skip - two silent disasters, because a wrong document you trust hurts you without warning and a record never kept is gone for good. So control your documents: give each a unique identity, an owner, an approved revision and a single master, keep the copy at the point of use current, mark every uncontrolled print for what it is, and withdraw the old version the moment it is superseded. And retain your records: tell a document from a record, keep every record unchanged for as long as it has value, and set that period on purpose - from the law, the warranty, the asset's own life and the audit - written as a schedule, never a habit. Control documents so only the current version survives; retain records so the history is never lost - because document control protects the person at the sharp end, and the records are the plant's memory it plans and learns from. The next time a document is handed to you, check its revision before you trust it; and the next time a stack of old work orders looks like clutter, remember it is the story of the machine, and the day you bin it is the day you start planning blind.
