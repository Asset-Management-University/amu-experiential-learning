The vibration report says this pump motor is healthy - bearings smooth, alignment good - but it keeps tripping on overload in the early hours and nobody can find why. How do I look inside a running motor for a broken rotor, without opening it or shutting it down?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday at the Ironside Pump Station, the works that lifts raw river water up to the town's water treatment plant. You are the reliability technician on shift. In front of you sits Raw-Water Pump 3 - a big motor the size of a beer keg, bolted to its pump, humming under a thin film of dew.

In your hand is a vibration report from last week. Pump 3 looks fine on it: bearings smooth, alignment good, every trace where it should be. On paper, this machine is healthy. But it is not. Twice in the last month, Pump 3 tripped on overload in the early hours, restarted on its own, and nobody could find why.

The plant manager, Bokamoso, stands next to you, arms folded. "We have a delivery promise to the town. If Pump 3 drops out during the morning demand peak, the reservoir runs low and we get phone calls from the municipality by 09:00. I need to know what is wrong with this motor. Not a guess. A reason." You crouch and put your hand near the frame - warmer than Pump 1 beside it, which carries the same load, and the faint hot-varnish smell of a motor working hard.

Here is the trap you can feel pulling at you: the easy move is to call it a bearing problem and swap the bearings, because that is the fault everyone knows how to chase. But the vibration trace already told you the bearings are fine. The problem is inside the motor - somewhere the vibration probe on the frame cannot see: a crack in the rotor buried deep in the middle, or the rotor sitting slightly off-centre. You cannot open the motor on a live shift, and you cannot keep waiting for the next trip.

How do I look inside a running motor and tell, with evidence, whether the rotor is broken or off-centre - without taking it apart, and without shutting it down?

Before you read on, decide. The motor keeps tripping. The vibration trace says the bearings are fine, so the problem is inside — a cracked rotor bar buried in the middle, or a rotor sitting slightly off-centre. You cannot open it on a live shift and you cannot wait for the next trip. The only thing leaving the motor that you can measure without touching it is the electricity going in. Write down how you would tell which fault it is — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the frame cannot feel

• “Put more vibration sensors on it, in more places.” More of the measurement that has already told you what it can. A crack in the middle of a rotor and an off-centre rotor are both faults the frame barely feels, which is why the trace looks fine.

• “Wait for the next trip and inspect it then.” That is the plan you already have, and it costs a production stop to buy information you could have had while it ran.

• “Thermal-image it. A cracked bar will run hot.” It might, eventually, at the surface, through a housing — a slow, blurred version of something happening deep inside.

• “Measure the current and see whether it is high.” The right place to look, and the wrong question of it. A level tells you the motor is working hard; it cannot tell you which of two faults is making it do so.

• “Take it out and megger it during the next shutdown.” That tests the insulation, which is not what is being asked, and it tests it when the motor is stopped and unloaded — which is when a rotor fault hides best.

• The current is not just a level. It is a signal, and the rotor writes on it: a fault inside the rotor disturbs the field in a rhythm tied to how fast the rotor is slipping behind, and that rhythm appears as small peaks arranged around the mains frequency, in a pattern that differs depending on whether the rotor is cracked or off-centre. So the same instrument answers both parts of the question — which fault, and how far gone, from how tall those peaks are relative to the main one — on a motor that is running, under load, without anybody opening anything. Under load matters: it is the load that makes the rotor work hard enough to speak at all.

The motor is already telling you, every second, through its electricity. Here is how to read it.

T2: The Tool in Action

Motor Current Signature Analysis (MCSA) reads the fingerprint a fault leaves in the current a motor draws. Instead of listening to the motor's vibration from the frame, you listen to its electricity - and faults deep inside the rotor, which the frame barely feels, leave small, regular peaks (sidebands) around the big mains-frequency peak. Their position names the fault; their height grades it. Watch it run on Pump 3.

Step 1 - Know why, then set up safely

Pump 3's nameplate: an AC induction motor, 4 poles, 400 V, 50 Hz, rated 48 A, synchronous speed 1500 rpm (the field speed for a 4-pole 50 Hz motor). You clamp a current probe around one phase lead feeding the motor and connect it to a current analyser, with arc-rated PPE on, the permit-to-work in place, and a second person present. You do not open the motor or touch bare conductors.

Step 2 - Record the current under a steady, real load

You let the pump settle at its normal duty and record about 60 seconds of steady running, noting the load holds at 44 A the whole time - close to the rated 48 A, a good real load for MCSA. The current analyser builds the spectrum, with the big 1x peak at 50 Hz dominating it.

Step 3 - Measure the true speed, work out the slip

The tachometer reads a running speed of 1480 rpm; the synchronous speed is 1500 rpm. So slip = (1500 - 1480) / 1500 = 20 / 1500 = 0.0133, about 1.33%. That slip is what fixes exactly where a rotor fault's sidebands will sit.

Step 4 - Predict where the fault shows, then read it

Sideband spacing = 2 x slip x line frequency = 2 x 0.0133 x 50 = 1.33 Hz. So a broken bar would put a peak at the lower sideband, 50 - 1.33 = 48.67 Hz (and the upper at 51.33 Hz). You zoom in around the 50 Hz peak and there it is - a small, clear peak at 48.67 Hz, exactly where the maths said. You measure its depth: 38 dB below the 1x peak (the 1x peak set as the 0 dB reference).

Step 5 - Judge the severity, decide with evidence

The guide for the lower-sideband depth: more than 50 dB down = healthy, 45-50 = early stage, 40-45 = likely cracked, less than 40 = severe. Pump 3's 38 dB is less than 40 - the severe band, a broken rotor bar. You cross-check eccentricity (an off-centre rotor would show sidebands at the running-speed rhythm, about 24.67 Hz either side) and find nothing, so the rotor is centred. The report to Bokamoso: 'Pump 3 has a broken rotor bar - a current sideband at 48.67 Hz, exactly the position for this motor's 1.33% slip, sitting only 38 dB below the line peak, in the severe band; no eccentricity. This explains the early-morning overload trips. Take it out at the next planned window and send the rotor for repair.' A reason, not a guess.

See what MCSA did. The vibration report said "healthy", and on its own terms it was right - the bearings were fine. But the machine kept tripping, because the fault was somewhere the frame probe simply cannot see, and the easy, familiar move - swap the bearings and hope - would have cost money, a shift, and another 2 a.m. failure, while the real fault sat untouched inside the rotor. And see the mission in it, because this technique is the refusal of two comfortable answers at once. The first is the false all-clear: a clean report on the wrong sense is not health, it is a blind spot, and the discipline is to go and look where the fault actually lives rather than where the light is good. The second is the blind swap: replacing the part everyone knows how to replace, because doing something feels better than admitting you cannot yet see the cause. MCSA refuses both by doing something quietly rigorous - it predicts, from the motor's own slip, the exact frequency a broken bar would sing at, and then goes to that precise spot on the spectrum to see whether the note is there. Found exactly where the physics said, graded against a guide, with the alternative fault ruled out, it turns "I think it might be the bearings" into "it is a broken rotor bar, here is the evidence, here is how severe, here is what to do" - a reason the manager can defend to the municipality and act on with confidence. And it is done without ever gambling with a life: clamped around the cable, never opened, never improvised alone around live power. The aim is the town that keeps its water through the morning peak, because someone read the electricity instead of guessing at the steel.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the diagnosis in five steps.

  • 1. Know why, then set up safely. The frame cannot see the rotor; clamp the current, work live-safe.
  • 2. Record under a steady, real load. MCSA needs the motor working, not idling.
  • 3. Measure the true speed, work out the slip. The sidebands' position depends on it.
  • 4. Predict where the fault shows, then read it. 2 x slip x line frequency; measure the depth in dB.
  • 5. Judge the severity, decide with evidence. Grade it, rule out the alternative, give a reason.

The ideas underneath. A motor turns electrical energy into turning force, and a fault inside leaves a tiny regular fingerprint in the current it draws. Recording that current and asking "what rhythms hide inside it?" gives a current spectrum (the analyser does the maths, an FFT, for you). The mains rhythm - the line frequency, 50 Hz here - shows as one tall 1x peak, the motor doing its job. A faulty rotor grows sidebands just left and right of it: their position tells the fault type, their height the severity. Position depends on slip - the small amount an induction motor lags its magnetic field. A broken rotor bar (the rotor is a cage of bars) puts sidebands at a spacing of 2 x slip x line frequency; air-gap eccentricity (the rotor off-centre) leaves its own pattern at the running-speed rhythm. You read the depth in decibels (dB) below the 1x peak - deeper is healthier. It fits an AC induction motor you can safely clamp under a steady real load, and it is the electrical complement to vibration analysis; it is the wrong tool for an idling motor (sidebands hide at low load), or for a bearing, balance or alignment fault.

T4: Your Turn

Take an AC induction motor of your own that is misbehaving in a way vibration cannot explain. Read its nameplate; set up live-safe with a clamp-on probe; record a steady, loaded window; tachometer the true speed and work out the slip; predict the broken-bar sideband at 2 x slip x line frequency and measure its depth; then grade it, rule out eccentricity, and write the verdict with a reason.

Two disciplines keep the diagnosis trustworthy. First, predict the frequency before you look, and read the right load - MCSA is powerful precisely because the physics tells you exactly where a fault must appear, so you go to that spot and confirm, rather than hunting the spectrum for anything odd and talking yourself into it; and you do it under a real, steady load, because a broken bar goes quiet on an idling motor and you will miss it. Second, never trade safety for a reading, and never let a clean report on one sense pass for health - work the permit, the PPE, the clamp-on probe and the second person every time, because no diagnosis is worth a life around live power; and remember that a healthy vibration trace only clears what vibration can see, so a machine that misbehaves with a clean report is telling you to look with a different sense, not to relax.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your diagnosis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A clean report on the wrong sense is a blind spot, not health. Vibration clears only what the frame can feel. A motor that misbehaves with a clean trace is telling you to look inside, not to relax.

• Predict the frequency, then go and look. The slip tells you exactly where a broken bar must sing. Confirm at that spot - do not hunt the spectrum for anything odd and convince yourself.

• Read a real, steady load. Rotor-bar sidebands shrink and hide on an idling or wobbling motor. No load, no signal - you will miss the fault.

• Grade it, and rule out the alternative. Measure the sideband depth against the guide, and check the eccentricity rhythm too - so your verdict names one fault with evidence, not a hunch.

• Never trade safety for a reading. Permit, arc-rated PPE, clamp-on probe, second person - every time. No diagnosis is worth a life around live power.

You came to the pump with a clean vibration report, two unexplained trips, and a manager who needed a reason by the morning peak. You can leave with one: Pump 3 has a broken rotor bar - a current sideband at exactly 48.67 Hz, where this motor's 1.33% slip says a broken bar must sit, 38 dB below the line peak in the severe band, with no eccentricity signature, so take it out at the next planned window and re-bar the rotor. When the frame probe says healthy but the machine is not, look where the fault lives - read the electricity, predict the fault's fingerprint, find it exactly there, and give a reason, not a guess. That is what keeps a town's water flowing through the morning peak: not a brave bearing swap, but the quiet rigour of predicting where a fault must show and going to that exact spot to find it - safely, around live power, with the evidence in hand.
