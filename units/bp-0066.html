Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Reabetswe, and you have a brilliant, simple plan: a small recycling depot in your neighbourhood. You've poured your heart into it - found an unused yard, worked out the budget, the materials to collect, who'd buy the recycled bales. The plan is technically perfect. You're already R20,000 in - R15,000 on second-hand baling machines, R5,000 deposit on the lease - and proud.

Then, a week before you open, a letter arrives from the council. Someone you never spoke to - a neighbour down the street - has complained about the noise and the trucks. The officer tells you the yard isn't zoned for this; you needed a special permit you never applied for. Everything stops. The money is spent, the machines sit silent, and you feel furious, stuck, and a little foolish - but the plan was so good. And it isn't the first time a great idea has hit a wall made of a person you didn't see coming.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to make your next plan even more perfect - better spreadsheets, tighter budget, more technical research. But you're only polishing the what and the how; the wall that stopped you wasn't a number, it was a person with a say you never asked, and a perfect plan still dies if someone with power objects. Or you try to push through - march to the council, "but it's good for the community, and I've already spent R20,000!" - but announcing your fixed plan at people who can block you just turns a quiet concern into a public fight, and now they have a reason to use the power they hold. Or you learn to deal with each ambush as it comes - sort out this permit, then move on - but you only ever solve the last surprise, never asking who the next unseen blocker is (the landlord? the buyer? the health inspector?), so you're set up to be blindsided all over again.

The wall is always a person you didn't ask. So before you read on: what question should your plan answer that it never does - and when must you ask it?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Your plan answered what to do and how - it was a good plan - but it had a fatal blind spot: it never asked who else has a say in whether this happens at all? And the people who can stop you don't wait politely; they surface the moment a decision is hard to reverse. The fix is to find the wall while it's still just a few bricks. Five moves:

  • See the trap - the missing question: a plan that only covers what and how has a hole in it shaped like a person. It never asked who could block it or who it affects - so the blocker arrives by surprise, late in the day, after your time and money are already sunk. The plan wasn't weak; it was incomplete in one specific way: it answered everything except who else gets a say in whether it happens at all.
  • The blockers don't wait politely: silence early is not consent. The people with a say surface on THEIR schedule, not yours - and that is almost always the moment the decision becomes hardest and most expensive to undo (the money spent, the lease signed, the scaffolding up). So don't hope they stay quiet; assume the wall is coming, and go looking for it while it's still just a few bricks and cheap to move.
  • Make a wide list of everyone who could help or block: before you spend a single rand, sit down and cast the net wider than feels necessary - every party the plan could touch or who could touch it: neighbours, the permit office, the health department, the landlord, the buyer of what you make, the wider community, even your own people. The ones you forget are precisely the ones who ambush you, so a list that feels slightly paranoid is about right.
  • Sort by power and by care - find the dangerous few: for each name, ask two plain questions: how much POWER do they have to help or block this, and how much do they CARE about it? The neighbour: high power (they can petition) and high care (it's their street). The permit office: high power (they can shut you) but low care (you're one file of hundreds - still dangerous, because their power is absolute). The few who are HIGH on both are the ones who blindside you if left to last - they are your first conversation, not your afterthought.
  • Engage them early by listening, not announcing: go to the dangerous few BEFORE the plan is locked - not with a finished plan to announce, but with a question: 'I'm thinking of doing this here; I want it to be good for everyone - what would your worries be, what would make it work for you?'. This is not a trick to push your plan through: you must be willing to let their answers genuinely change it. Listening ('don't run before 9am', 'keep the trucks off the road') doesn't weaken the plan - it makes it real, and turns a blocker into a partner. A few hours of honest conversation, and a small permit fee, beats a sunk fortune and a stalled project.

See it rescue Reabetswe's next project. Before spending a rand, she lists everyone the plan touches, marks each by power and care, and sees the neighbours (high power, high care) and the permit office (high power) are the ones to reach first. She knocks on doors with a question, not an announcement - "what would your worries be?" - hears "noise before 9am, and the trucks", adjusts the hours and the route, and lodges the R500 permit early. The depot opens on time, with the street on its side. A few hours of asking, and a small fee, in place of a R20,000 ambush. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a depot - any plan that touches other people hits the same wall. Watch a family planning a loft extension.

See the same blind spot with a home extension

Their plan is perfect, drawn up by an architect; the builders arrive and put up the scaffolding. The next day, a legal letter from the next-door neighbour: the extension will block the light to their kitchen window, and an old legal right lets them halt the whole project. Work stops, lawyers get involved, costs spiral.

Now run the blueprint. Before even hiring the architect, they make their wide list - the neighbour, the council, others on the street affected by parking - and sorting it, they see the neighbour is high power (a legal right) and high care (it's their home). So the neighbour becomes their first conversation: "we're thinking about an extension - can we show you a rough sketch and make sure it doesn't cause you problems?" That one respectful talk surfaces the light issue while the plan is still a cheap drawing, not an expensive, half-built reality.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at a plan you're making right now - a business, a community project, even a change at home. Stop polishing the what and the how, and ask the missing question: who else has a say?

  1. Make your wide list. Write down every person, group, or department that could be affected or could have an opinion - don't censor it.
  2. Sort it, and find the vital few. Mark how much power each holds and how much they care; circle the few who are high on both - they are your priority.
  3. Go and listen, before the plan is set in stone. Go to them now, with an open mind, ready to let what you hear make the plan better - not to ask permission, but to show respect and gain knowledge.
R

Name the plan, list who could help or block it, and mark who's high on both power and care. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build the list and the power-and-care sort together.)

Pouring your care into a solid plan and then being ambushed was never naivety or poor planning - we're all taught to perfect the what and the how, and the who is a genuine blind spot almost everyone shares; the fix is a learnable habit, not a flaw in you. And this is not about managing people or charming them into a fixed plan: it's honest listening that can truly reshape what you build - because people you engage openly become allies, while people you try to neutralise become enemies who simply wait. Ask the missing question early, listen as if the answer matters (it does), and you spot the people who could stop you in time to meet them as partners - and the plan you build with them is not only un-blindsided, it's better, and kinder to everyone it touches.
