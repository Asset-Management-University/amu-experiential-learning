Ordered, received, billed

T1: The Ache

You have sent the report. A week later, the question comes back: "Are you sure about this R45,000 work-order cost?" And you are not sure. You know the number came straight out of the system, that it was booked to the work order and flowed into your maintenance-cost report - but you never actually checked it against the invoice, the delivery note, or the purchase order. You trusted the system, and the system trusted whoever typed the number in.

That is the quiet dread of reporting costs you have not verified: if even one of them is wrong, it does not stay wrong in one place. It flows into the budget, the cost-per-asset, the reliability-per-rand, the next business case - every decision that trusts your report inherits your unchecked number. An unverified number is not a fact; it is a risk you have just passed on to everyone downstream.

The costs in my report come straight from the work orders, but I never checked them against what was actually ordered and received - and if one is wrong, it corrupts everything built on it. How do I verify cost data before I report it?

Before you read on, decide. The report is ready and the costs came straight out of the work orders. Then the finance clerk asks, in passing, whether anybody checked that R84,000 bearing order against what actually arrived — and you realise nobody ever has. Not this one, not any of them. You also remember the storeman mentioning that a delivery came short and the rest followed a fortnight later, and you have no idea whether that was this order. Meanwhile these figures are already in the maintenance budget, in the cost-per-asset table, and in the business case you submitted last month. Write down how you would verify cost data before reporting it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why an error does not stay in one place

• “The system is reliable — the figures are what they are.” The system faithfully records what it was told. It has no way of knowing whether the goods arrived, or arrived in full, and it will report a wrong figure with exactly the confidence of a right one.

• “Check the big ones and let the small ones go.” A reasonable-sounding sampling rule, and it is a decision about materiality made without evidence. It also misses the pattern kind of error, where many small ones share a cause.

• “Fix it at year-end when finance reconciles.” By then the number has already shaped a budget and a business case, and you cannot unwind a decision that has been taken on it.

• “Ask the supplier to confirm their invoices are correct.” Asks one of the three parties to audit itself, and the likeliest error here is not a supplier's dishonesty but a delivery nobody counted.

• “Add a note that the figures are unaudited.” A caveat beside a precise number, which will be read and ignored — the figure travels and the note does not.

• The principle underneath: a number you have not checked against the reality it claims to describe is not a fact, it is an assumption — and once it is in the records it never becomes more true. It just travels. Your wrong figure does not stay wrong in one place: it flows into the budget, the cost per asset, the next business case, so every decision that trusts your report inherits your unchecked number. So verify at the source, once, before it is paid and before it is reported — the only point at which correcting it is cheap. The check itself is simple: three documents must tell the same story. What you agreed to buy — item, quantity, price. What was actually delivered and received. What the supplier is billing. Where all three agree the cost is evidenced; where they disagree, the disagreement is the finding, and it gets resolved before the number goes anywhere. Two honesties keep it real. The receipt must record what genuinely arrived, not what was expected — a receipt signed off the order defeats the entire control, and that short delivery is exactly the case. And a mismatch is investigated rather than adjusted, because making the figures agree is how an error becomes a misstatement.

You verify at the source, with a simple, powerful control: the three-way match. Let us learn it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you verify financial data before you report it - matching what was ordered, received and billed, reconciling it to the work order, and handling the mismatches honestly.

1. Why verify before you report

Start with the principle the whole technique rests on: A NUMBER YOU HAVE NOT CHECKED AGAINST THE REALITY IT CLAIMS TO DESCRIBE IS NOT A FACT, IT IS AN ASSUMPTION - and once an unverified cost enters your records, it never becomes more true. IT JUST TRAVELS. So you verify it at the SOURCE, once, before it is paid and before it is reported, rather than discovering it was wrong three reports later when it has already shaped a budget and a business case. A wrong work-order cost does not stay wrong in one place: it flows into the maintenance budget, the cost-per-asset, the reliability-per-rand, and the next business case - every decision that trusts your report inherits your unchecked number. AN UNVERIFIED NUMBER IS NOT A FACT; VERIFY AT THE SOURCE, ONCE, SO EVERY DECISION THAT FOLLOWS IS TRUE.

2. The three-way match: ordered, received, billed must agree

The classic control for verifying a cost is the THREE-WAY MATCH, and it is exactly what it sounds like: before a supplier's invoice is paid, THREE DOCUMENTS MUST TELL THE SAME STORY. The PURCHASE ORDER - what you agreed to buy: the item, the quantity, the price. The GOODS RECEIPT - what was actually delivered and received. And the INVOICE - what the supplier is billing you. You match all three on QUANTITY, PRICE AND TERMS, and you PAY ONLY WHAT ALL THREE AGREE ON. ORDERED, RECEIVED, BILLED: if those three agree, the cost is real; if they disagree, something is wrong and it must be resolved before a single rand moves. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake - IT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN PAYING FOR WHAT YOU GOT AND PAYING FOR WHAT SOMEONE TYPED.

3. Reconcile the work-order cost

In the asset-management world the match extends to the WORK ORDER. The cost booked to a maintenance work order - its LABOUR, its PARTS, its CONTRACTOR CHARGES - must trace back to and reconcile with those same procurement documents: the parts on the work order to the purchase order, goods receipt and invoice for those parts; the contractor charge to the contractor's order and invoice. When you do this BEFORE the work-order cost rolls up into your monthly report, the maintenance cost you report is THE COST THAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED, evidenced by documents, rather than a figure the system inherited from an unverified invoice. This is precisely what 'verify financial data before it is reported' means in practice: you do not report the work order's number and hope, you report the number the documents confirm.

4. A control against error and fraud - needs segregation of duties

The three-way match earns its keep TWO WAYS. It catches the HONEST ERRORS that are constant and costly: the duplicate invoice paid twice, the quantity billed that exceeds what was delivered, the price that crept above the agreed order, the invoice for goods that never arrived. And it DETERS AND DETECTS FRAUD: a fabricated cost CANNOT PRODUCE A GENUINE GOODS RECEIPT TO MATCH, so billing for undelivered goods or a phantom supplier is exposed the moment you insist all three documents agree. For this to work, one condition is essential - SEGREGATION OF DUTIES: the person who orders must not be the same person who confirms receipt and approves the invoice, because one person controlling all three steps can make the documents 'agree' on a lie. And hold this clearly, because it is the heart of how AMU teaches controls: A CONTROL IS A GUARDRAIL FOR THE SYSTEM, NOT A CAGE FOR THE PERSON. IT PROTECTS THE HONEST FROM ERROR, THE ORGANISATION FROM LOSS, AND THE PROCESS FROM DOUBT. WE BUILD STRONG CONTROLS NOT BECAUSE WE ASSUME PEOPLE ARE DISHONEST, BUT SO THAT EVERYONE CAN OPERATE WITH CONFIDENCE, KNOWING THE SYSTEM ITSELF ENSURES INTEGRITY. A GOOD CONTROL PROTECTS HONEST PEOPLE FROM SUSPICION.

5. Handle exceptions honestly - a mismatch is a question

When the three documents do not agree, you have an EXCEPTION, and how you treat it matters as much as catching it. The invoice is HELD - payment does not go through - until the discrepancy is resolved, with small differences within an agreed TOLERANCE allowed to pass so trivial roundings do not stall everything. But RESOLVING IS NOT ACCUSING: A MISMATCH IS A SIGNAL TO INVESTIGATE, NOT A VERDICT OF FAULT. WE APPROACH IT WITH CURIOSITY TO FIND THE CAUSE, NOT WITH AN ASSUMPTION OF BLAME. MOST DISCREPANCIES ARE HONEST ERRORS - A TYPO, A SHORT DELIVERY, A PRICE UPDATE NOT YET REFLECTED. THE PROCESS IS TO RESOLVE THE DISCREPANCY, NOT TO ACCUSE THE PERSON. You find the cause, correct the record to the verified figure, and pay and report only that - in the spirit of two parties fixing an error together, not one catching the other out. That is what keeps the control an instrument of trust rather than a source of fear.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. A Corathyl work order for a pump overhaul has three parts line items from one supplier. You hold the purchase order (PO), the goods receipt (GRN), and the invoice. Verify before you pay and report.

ItemPurchase orderGoods receiptInvoice
Mechanical seal1 at R2,0001 received1 at R2,000
Bearings2 at R1,5002 received3 at R1,500
Coupling1 at R4,000none received1 at R4,000
  1. Find the discrepancies. Run the three-way match on each item and name what is wrong with items 2 and 3.
  2. Calculate the verified cost. What total should be approved for payment and reported?
  3. Item 2 - the action. What must happen before this invoice is paid?
  4. Item 3 - why the goods receipt matters. Explain why matching only the PO to the invoice would have missed this, and how the goods receipt protects the organisation.
  5. Segregation of duties. Explain how having one person order and a different person receive makes this control effective.

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the discrepancies: Item 1 (seal) is a perfect three-way match - ordered, received and billed all say 1 at R2,000. Item 2 (bearings): the invoice bills for 3 but only 2 were ordered and 2 received - an over-quantity billing of one bearing. Item 3 (coupling): it is on the PO and the invoice, but the goods receipt shows none received - billed for something not delivered.

Second, the verified cost: pay and report only what all three agree on - seal R2,000 + bearings (the 2 actually ordered and received) R3,000 + coupling (not received) R0 = R5,000. The invoice total was R2,000 + R4,500 + R4,000 = R10,500, so R5,500 would have been wrongly paid and reported without the match.

Third, item 2 action: hold the invoice as an exception, investigate the cause (a keying error, or an over-bill), correct it to 2 bearings = R3,000, and pay and report only the verified figure - approached as resolving a discrepancy, not accusing the supplier.

Fourth, item 3: matching only the PO to the invoice would have passed the coupling, because the PO and invoice agree - it was ordered and it was billed. Only the goods receipt reveals it was never delivered. The goods receipt is the independent evidence of reality, and skipping it is the exact gap that billing-for-undelivered-goods relies on.

Fifth, segregation of duties: if the person who ordered were also the one confirming receipt and approving payment, they could sign off a delivery that never came. Splitting order and receipt between two people means the receipt is independent evidence, so no one person can make the documents "agree" on a lie - which protects the honest and blocks the dishonest.

T5: The Guardrails

Ordered, received, billed must agree before you pay and before you report - because an unverified number is not a fact you can report, it is an assumption you are about to spread.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Reporting unverified work-order costs. A number in the system is not a verified fact. Trace it to the order, the receipt and the invoice before it enters your report.

• Matching only two documents. Checking the PO against the invoice while skipping the goods receipt is the exact gap that billing-for-undelivered-goods exploits. Always match all three.

• One person controlling all three steps. If the same person orders, receives and pays, the documents can be made to "agree" on a lie. Separate the duties.

• Treating every mismatch as fraud. Most discrepancies are honest errors. Investigate with curiosity to find the cause, not with an accusation.

• Waving a mismatch through to hit a deadline. A held invoice is the control working. Resolve the exception before you pay; the deadline is not worth a fiction in the report.

• No tolerance policy. With no agreed tolerance, trivial roundings stall everything and people start skipping the check. Set a sensible tolerance so effort goes where it matters.

• Verifying in the report instead of at the source. By the time a wrong cost is in the report it has already spread. Verify once, at the source, where the documents are.

• Assuming the number is right because it is in the system. The system trusts whoever typed it in. The match is what makes the number trustworthy.

The dreaded question - "are you sure about this cost?" - only has a good answer if you verified before you reported. So verify at the source: make the purchase order, the goods receipt and the invoice agree on what was ordered, received and billed, pay only what all three confirm, and reconcile the work-order cost to those documents before it ever reaches your report. Keep the duties separated so the evidence stays independent, and treat every mismatch as a question to resolve rather than a person to accuse. Ordered, received, billed must agree before you pay and before you report - because an unverified number is not a fact, it is an assumption, and the whole job is to make sure that what you report is what truly happened.
