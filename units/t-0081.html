I have to commit four billion rand to a 25-year water plan built on one guessed future - the mine stays, demand climbs, the rain holds. How do I build a strategy that survives even if the mine leaves and the droughts come?

T1: The Ache

You are Kefilwe, Head of Asset Strategy at the Marematlou Water Board (MWB), a regional bulk-water utility. MWB runs a 25-year-old dam, two treatment works, and 340 km of steel pipeline carrying clean water to four towns and a mine - about 480,000 people in all. In your hands is a single sheet: the draft 25-year capital plan, with R4.2 billion of pipeline replacement penned in for years 9 to 17.

Refiloe, the Finance Director, slides it back across the table. Her pen taps the page twice. "This plan assumes the mine is still buying 40 Ml/day in 2050. It assumes demand keeps climbing. It assumes the rain holds. What if the mine closes in 2032? What if we get a run of drought years? What if a new industrial park doubles demand instead?"

Your stomach tightens, because she is right. The plan is a straight line drawn from today. But you lived through 2015, when the dam dropped to 28% and the towns nearly ran dry; you have read rainfall records that swing wildly; you know the mine's owners are nervous about the ore price. The next 25 years could go several very different ways - and the pipeline takes 8 years just to build, so you cannot wait and see. You catch yourself reaching for the easy move: pick the "most likely" future, add a 10% safety margin, call it prudent. But a single padded forecast is still a single guess - and a 10% margin will not save you if the mine walks away and takes 22% of your revenue with it.

You have built me one future - the mine stays, demand climbs, the rain holds - and I am being asked to spend four billion rand on it. How do I build a strategy that survives even if the mine leaves and the droughts come?

Before you read on, decide. Four billion rand, a pipeline that takes eight years just to build, twenty-five years of life, and one future on the page: the mine stays, demand climbs, the rain holds. You have watched the dam fall to 28%, and the mine is 22% of your revenue. Write down what you would take to the board. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and where each one breaks

• “Take the most likely future and add a ten per cent margin.” It sounds prudent and it is the trap you already caught yourself reaching for. A padded forecast is still a single guess — and ten per cent does not survive the mine walking away with 22 per cent of your revenue.

• “Get a better forecast — hire someone who models this properly.” Worth having and it answers the wrong question. Better modelling narrows the error around a prediction; it cannot tell you whether a mine’s owners will sell in year nine, and eight years of construction means you must commit before anybody could know.

• “Wait and see — decide when things are clearer.” The pipeline takes eight years to build. Waiting until the future is visible means the water arrives eight years after it was needed, and the towns that nearly ran dry in 2015 are the ones that pay for the delay.

• “Build it smaller to be safe.” Closer than the others, and on its own it is another single bet — this time on the low future. If demand does climb, a small pipeline is a permanent ceiling on 480,000 people’s water, chosen by an engineer hedging.

• “Tell them we cannot responsibly decide with this much uncertainty.” Honest, and it hands the decision to the calendar. Not deciding is a decision, and it is the one that guarantees the eight years start late.

The thing to hold on to before you turn the page: you are not being asked to know which future is coming. You are being asked to spend four billion rand of other people’s money in a way that is not a disaster in any of them.

You need a way to build a long-term strategy that does not bet everything on one guessed future - a method that lays out several plausible futures on purpose, then tests your strategy against each to see where it breaks. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is scenario planning - the version Shell made famous in the 1970s, when its planners imagined an oil shock years before it came and were ready. The idea is simple and humble: you stop trying to predict the future. Instead you describe several different futures that could each realistically happen, and you make sure your strategy can cope with all of them. Watch Kefilwe run it on the MWB pipeline.

Step 1 - Frame the focal question (one sharp decision)

Kefilwe writes hers on the board: 'Over the next 25 years, what water-supply strategy should MWB commit to - and can it survive a future where the mine leaves, the rains fail, or demand surges?' The decision in the middle is how much, and what kind of, supply capacity to build. The R4.2 billion pipeline takes 8 years to build, so she cannot wait and see which future arrives.

Step 2 - Find the two critical uncertainties (the axes)

From a long list - rainfall, the mine, population, a possible industrial park, electricity, grants, recycling, pipeline ageing - two stand out. Pipeline ageing is high-impact but predictable (steel corrodes on a schedule); population growth is steady at 1.5% a year. But the MINE'S SURVIVAL (40 Ml/day, 22% of revenue, hostage to ore prices) and RAINFALL (it sets how much water there even is, and swings wildly year to year) are huge AND genuinely unpredictable. Those two are the axes.

Step 3 - Build the four scenarios (the 2x2 grid)

Demand (mine stays plus growth vs mine leaves) crossed with water (wet vs dry): 'Boom Town' (mine stays, park opens, demand 240 Ml/day, but the 220 Ml/day dam cannot keep up - a shortage); 'Thirsty Boom' (demand 240 AND drought cuts the dam to 150 in bad years - the worst squeeze); 'Easy Street' (mine closes in 2032, demand falls to 150, rains hold - spare capacity, the worry is wasted money); 'Lean and Dry' (mine gone, demand 150, drought, dam 150 - tight but roughly in balance).

Step 4 - Test the plan, then find the robust strategy

The draft 'build the full R4.2bn pipeline for 240 Ml/day' survives Boom Town but FAILS in Easy Street and Lean and Dry (R4.2 billion moving water nobody buys - a stranded asset) - a bet on one future, broken in two of the four. The robust answer: PHASE the pipeline (build phase 1 now for R1.5 billion, not R4.2 billion up front, with a decision gate before phase 2); add R600 million of water recycling (it helps in every dry box); sign a flexible 5-year mine contract, not a 25-year assumption. This holds up acceptably in all four.

Step 5 - Set the signposts (what to watch)

Kefilwe lists the signals: the mine signing or refusing a supply renewal (Boom vs Lean); three consecutive below-average rain years (Wet vs Dry); industrial-park planning approval (the demand surge confirmed). She brings it back to Refiloe: no four-billion-rand bet on a single guess, but a R1.5 billion first step, a recycling hedge, and a clear list of exactly what would make MWB build more. Refiloe signs.

See what the five steps did. They replaced one four-billion-rand bet on a single guessed future with a strategy that survives all four plausible ones: a smaller first step, a recycling hedge, a flexible contract, and a list of what to watch. And see the mission in it: a water board does not get to gamble 480,000 people's taps on a forecast, nor to strand four billion rand of public money on assets nobody ends up needing. The honest posture in the face of deep uncertainty is not confident prediction - it is humble preparation. A robust strategy is never the cheapest answer for any one future, but it is never a disaster in any of them - and that trade, safety bought with a little lost best-case, is exactly what stewardship of other people's water and money asks of you. You cannot know which future is coming; you can refuse to be caught out by it.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the foresight in five moves.

  • 1. Frame the focal question. One sharp, hard-to-reverse decision over a long horizon - not "what will the world be like?".
  • 2. Find the two critical uncertainties. Score every driving force on impact and unpredictability; the two high on both are your axes.
  • 3. Build the four scenarios. Cross the axes; name, story and number each plausible future.
  • 4. Test, then find the robust strategy. See where your plan breaks; choose the moves that survive all four.
  • 5. Set the signposts. The early-warning signals of which future is arriving.

The ideas underneath: a scenario is a short, believable story about how the world might look years from now, written clearly enough to plan against; a driving force is something outside your control that pushes the future (rainfall, the mine, town growth); a critical uncertainty is a driving force that is both very important AND genuinely hard to predict - those are what you build scenarios around, while a high-impact but predictable force (steady population growth) you simply plan for. A robust strategy works out acceptably in every scenario, even if it is the perfect choice for none; robustness is the prize. One warning matters above all: scenarios are not forecasts and not probabilities - you are never saying "this future is 60% likely", only "this future is plausible, so my strategy had better survive it"; the moment you treat one scenario as the bet, you are back to single-guess planning with extra steps. Scenario planning fits big, slow, hard-to-reverse decisions under deep uncertainty (10-plus-year horizons, a few genuinely swinging uncertainties). It does not fit short predictable decisions (just forecast next year's chemical order), situations with little real uncertainty (scenarios would be theatre), or when you need one precise number rather than robustness.

Two disciplines keep the foresight honest. First, never let a scenario become a forecast - the whole power of the method is holding several plausible futures at once; the instant you decide which one is "really" going to happen and plan for that, you have thrown the tool away and gone back to a single padded guess. Second, judge a strategy by its worst box, not its best - the question is never "how good is this plan if Boom Town arrives?" but "how bad is it if Lean and Dry does?"; robustness means raising the floor across all four futures, not the ceiling in your favourite one.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your scenario planning true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A scenario is not a forecast. You are not predicting; you are stress-testing. Never attach a probability and never call one future "the plan" - that is single-guess planning in disguise.

• Build on the uncertainties, not the certainties. A force that is high-impact but predictable (steel ageing, steady growth) you just plan for. Only forces that are important AND genuinely unpredictable earn an axis.

• Make all four plausible and sharply different. If one scenario is a fantasy or two are near-twins, the grid teaches you nothing. Each box must be a future a reasonable person fears or hopes for.

• Robust beats optimal. The winning strategy is rarely the best in any single future. Choose the one that is never a disaster, not the one that is perfect for the future you happen to like.

• Set the signposts, then keep watching. Scenario planning is not decide-once. Name the real signals of which future is arriving, and revisit when they trip - or the plan quietly rots into a forecast.

You came into that boardroom with one future drawn on a sheet and four billion rand resting on it. You can leave with something far stronger: four plausible futures named and faced, a strategy that survives all of them, and a clear list of the signals that say which one is coming. A single forecast is a bet; a robust strategy is an insurance policy. You cannot predict the next 25 years - but you can build a plan that survives them. That is what it means to steward a community's water and its money with humility instead of hubris: not to guess the future right, but to make sure no plausible future can fail the people who depend on you.
