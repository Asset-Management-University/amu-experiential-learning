Step 1 of 5

The fully designed theatre upgrade lost the budget to a paragraph with a round number on it - how do I stop maturity deciding which project wins?

T1: The Ache

The capital committee at Erasmus Regional Hospital meets once a year and allocates about R70 million. There are six requests on the table this morning totalling R148 million.

You are the infrastructure manager, and you have watched this meeting go wrong three years running.

Two of the six matter to this story. The theatre ventilation upgrade has a completed design, a bill of quantities, two contractor prices and a programme: R42.6 million. Theatre 3 has been running on a failing air-handling unit for four years and the infection-control reports say so, every quarter, in the same words.

The other is patient-flow digitisation. It is a page and a half of intent - genuine intent, written by somebody who cares - and a figure at the bottom: R18 million.

You already know how the morning goes, because you have seen it. R18 million against R42.6 million reads as less than half the cost for something that sounds transformative, and it will get funded. The theatre will not. That will be the fourth year.

And here is what makes it hard rather than merely annoying: nobody in that room is doing anything wrong. The committee is comparing two numbers, which is exactly what a committee is for. The person who wrote the R18 million was not being dishonest - they had nothing to base a better figure on, so they wrote a round number, as anybody would. And the theatre's R42.6 million is genuinely more precise, which is genuinely why it looks worse.

Two years ago a R12 million request approved off a similar page and a half closed out at R31 million, and took the theatre's slot for three years running. Everybody in the room remembers it. It changed nothing, because nobody could say what should have been done differently.

The better-worked-out project keeps losing to the vaguer one, because being worked out is what makes a cost look big. How do I stop maturity deciding which project wins?

Before you read on, decide what you would take into that room. Six requests, R70 million, a theatre that has failed infection control for four years, and a page and a half with R18 million on it that will beat it. Write down what you would actually do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five things that get tried, and why each one loses

• "Make the case for the theatre harder - lead with the infection-control reports." The right instinct and it has been tried three times. It turns the meeting into a contest of urgency, and every one of the six requests has somebody who can make it sound urgent, because they are all real needs.

• "Point out that the R18 million is a guess." True, and it lands as an attack on a colleague who is not guilty of anything - they had nothing to cost. It also invites the reply that the theatre's figure is only a tender and tenders come in high, which is not entirely wrong.

• "Ask for both." R60.6 million of a R70 million budget for two of six requests. It may even work once, and it does nothing about next year, when the same thing happens with different projects.

• "Score all six against weighted criteria." More rigorous, and it puts a defined project and a paragraph into the same scoring matrix - so the paragraph scores well on everything, because there is nothing in it yet to score badly. Rigour applied to incomparable things produces confident nonsense.

• "Bring up what happened two years ago." Everybody already remembers, which is precisely the point: the memory has changed nothing three years running, because a cautionary tale is not a procedure.

• What all five accept is that the six requests belong in one comparison. They do not. One is a priced design and one is an intention, and putting them side by side does not compare their merit - it compares how far each has been worked out, and reliably rewards whichever has been worked out less. The move is not a better argument inside the comparison. It is to refuse the comparison until the things in it are comparable.

T2: The Tool in Action

Score how well each request is defined before looking at any cost. Put each cost's possible accuracy beside it. Then refuse to rank across definition levels - sort into those ready to fund and those ready to define - and price the defining as its own small line. Watch it run on the six requests.

Step 1 - Score how well each request is actually defined, before looking at any number

Boitumelo Matlala goes through the six requests with the costs covered over. The theatre ventilation upgrade has a completed design, a bill of quantities, two contractor prices and a programme - fully defined. The oxygen plant replacement has a scope, a layout and a supplier budget quote - a concept, not a design. Patient-flow digitisation is a page and a half of intent with no scope of what is being digitised. Two more sit in between and one is a straight equipment replacement with a catalogue price. She writes the level against each and only then uncovers the costs.

Step 2 - Put the cost estimate's accuracy beside the cost, and see what that does to the comparison

Theatre ventilation: R42.6 million, plus or minus 10 per cent, from two priced tenders - so somewhere between R38 and R47 million. Patient-flow digitisation: R18 million, and Boitumelo writes what the accuracy of that figure actually is - unknown, because nothing has been scoped, and industry experience on comparably defined work is minus 20 to plus 100 per cent, which puts it between R14 and R36 million. Put like that, the two ranges very nearly meet - R36 million against R38 million. The R18 million that looked less than half the theatre's cost might be most of it, and nobody in the room had been shown that, because the accuracy column had never existed.

Step 3 - Refuse to rank across definition levels, and gate instead

Boitumelo brings two lists instead of one. Ready to fund: theatre ventilation and the equipment replacement, both fully defined and priced. Ready to define: the oxygen plant and two others, which need scoping studies. And the digitisation request, which needs somebody to say what is being digitised before it can be costed at all. The committee's instinct is that this is a delaying tactic - and the answer is the July board paper from two years ago, where a R12 million request approved as a paragraph closed out at R31 million and took the theatre's slot for three years running.

Step 4 - Price the work of getting the immature ones ready, and fund that separately

The scoping work for the oxygen plant is about R900,000 against a project of roughly R38 million - a little over 2 per cent. For the digitisation request it is R400,000 to establish what would actually be digitised and what it would save. Boitumelo asks for R1.8 million across four studies, which is one per cent of the capital list, and says the uncomfortable part out loud: some of these studies will conclude that the project should not be done, and that is the cheapest possible outcome rather than a failure of the study.

Step 5 - Say what happens when somebody insists on funding an undefined project anyway

The digitisation request is approved anyway - the provincial office has asked for it by name. Boitumelo does not argue further and does not quietly accept it either. The minute records that it is approved at concept level, that the estimate carries a minus 20 to plus 100 per cent range against R18 million, that R400,000 of the approval is a definition study reporting in four months, and that the committee will see a revised figure before the balance is committed. When it comes back at R29 million, nobody has to litigate whose fault it was - the range had said so, in writing, and the committee had accepted it with its eyes open.

See what covering the costs for ten minutes did. Scored on definition first, the R18 million turns out to carry a range of R14 to R36 million against the theatre's R38 to R47 - and the top of the one very nearly reaches the bottom of the other. The figure that read as less than half the cost might be most of it, and the committee had never been shown the accuracy column because it had never existed. Then the move that actually changes next year as well as this one: two lists instead of one. Ready to fund competes for the capital; ready to define competes for R1.8 million of scoping studies, one per cent of the list. And notice what happens when the gate is overridden - because it is, immediately, on the provincial office's instruction. Boitumelo neither argues further nor quietly accepts it. The minute records the definition level, the range, and the date a revised figure comes back. When it returns at R29 million, nobody has to litigate whose fault that was. The gate's value is not that it holds. It is that an override is visible and its cost is predicted rather than discovered.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the first one has to happen before any cost is visible.

  • 1. Score how well each request is defined - idea, concept, or priced design - with the costs covered over.
  • 2. Put each cost's possible accuracy beside it, and read the two columns together as ranges.
  • 3. Sort into groups by definition level; do not rank across them. Only the defined compete for capital.
  • 4. Price the definition work as its own line - a small percentage - and say what it buys.
  • 5. When the gate is overridden, record the level, the range and the review date rather than pretending it held.

What you have applied is a stage-gate discipline, and in capital projects the sequence of definition levels is often called front-end loading - the deliberate work of defining a project before committing to it, with a gate between each level that a project must pass rather than argue past. The accuracy band that goes with each level is the part people skip and the part that does the work: an order-of-magnitude estimate from a concept genuinely cannot be held tighter than roughly minus twenty to plus one hundred per cent, and a definitive estimate from a priced design can be held to about ten. Reading a cost without its band is what makes an undefined project look cheap. And the reason to gate rather than to score is a bias with a name - optimism bias - which is not dishonesty but the ordinary fact that an unexamined project has had no chance to reveal its difficulties, so the less examined it is, the better it looks.

It fits any set of competing requests for a fixed pot where they are at different stages of development - capital lists, research portfolios, grant rounds, IT roadmaps, bid selection. Two limits are worth being straight about. It slows things down deliberately, so on a small or genuinely urgent decision the gate can cost more than it saves - match it to the size of the commitment. And it says nothing whatever about whether a project is worth doing: gating is a comparison discipline, not a merit test, and a fully defined bad project will pass every gate and should still be refused on its merits by somebody paying attention.

Two disciplines decide whether the gate does anything. First, score the definition level before you look at the costs, and do it in that order visibly. Once the numbers are in your head the scoring becomes an argument about the project you already favour, and you will not be able to tell that it has - the reasoning will feel exactly as sound. Covering the costs for ten minutes is a trivial act that protects the whole exercise, and doing it in front of the committee is also the best explanation of why the exercise is not a delaying tactic. Second, when somebody overrides the gate, write the override down without arguing further. The temptation is to fight, and losing that fight is how the discipline gets discredited as obstruction. A recorded override - this level, this range, this review date - costs you nothing, keeps the relationship, and does the one thing the cautionary tale never did: it makes the consequence predicted rather than discovered, so the next override is argued about with evidence instead of memory.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the gate honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A cost quoted without its accuracy band. This is the whole mechanism. A number from a paragraph and a number from a priced design are not the same kind of object, and putting them in one column makes the vaguer one look cheaper.

• Definition scored after the costs are known. The scoring will drift toward the project you favour and it will feel entirely principled. Cover the costs, score, then uncover - in that order, in front of people.

• One ranked list across definition levels. A ranking of incomparable things is not rigour. Group first, rank inside the groups.

• Definition work not funded. If nothing pays for scoping, immature requests stay immature and keep winning. It is a small percentage and it needs its own line in the budget.

• A definition study treated as a failure when it says no. A study that concludes the project should not be done has returned many times its cost. Say so when you ask for the money, so the answer is allowed to be no.

• An override argued rather than recorded. Fighting it gets the discipline labelled obstruction. Record the level, the range and the review date, and let the revised figure make the argument for you.

• The gate mistaken for a merit test. It only makes things comparable. A well-defined bad project passes every gate, and refusing it on its merits is still somebody's job.

• The person who wrote the vague request treated as the problem. They had nothing to cost with. The fault is a process that asked them for a number before anybody funded them to find one - and saying that plainly is what gets the gate adopted rather than resented.

You went into that room knowing how the morning would go, with four years of infection-control reports and no argument that had ever worked. You can bring something different: six requests scored for definition before anyone saw a cost, an accuracy band beside every figure, two lists rather than one, and R1.8 million - one per cent of the list - to turn the intentions into things that can actually be compared next year. The vaguer project was never cheaper. It was only less examined, and being less examined is what made it look cheap. And when the gate is overridden, as it is, the range goes in the minute - so that the R29 million arrives as something the committee predicted rather than something that happened to it.
