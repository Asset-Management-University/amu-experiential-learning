Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Andiswa, the manager of a small foundry. It is pouring day. The furnace holds R150,000 of molten bronze for a set of critical pump impellers, and everything depends on pouring at the right temperature - about 1,150 degrees. Too cold and the metal will not fill the mould, leaving a useless casting; too hot and it damages the sand mould and changes the metal itself.

The furnace has two temperature gauges, a primary and a backup. Today they disagree. The primary reads 1,155 - ready to pour. The backup reads 1,120 - dangerously cold. Your foreman, twenty years on the floor, squints at the colour of the melt and taps the primary gauge. "Looks about right to me. That backup one has been acting up for weeks." You have a schedule to keep and a customer waiting, so you make the call: "Trust the primary. Let's pour."

The result is a disaster. A cold pour - the metal freezes halfway into the mould, leaving a deformed, useless lump of bronze. The mould is destroyed. The entire R150,000 batch is scrap, and the client's order will be late. The foreman just shrugs: "The gauge said it was hot enough." You stare at the two silent gauges on the panel. One of them lied, and you listened.

Two gauges on the same furnace disagree and I have no idea which to believe - how do I run a plant when I can't trust my own numbers?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fix everything. You get a quote to have all 480 instruments in the plant professionally calibrated: R250,000 and three weeks of disruption. Impossible - and even if you did it, they would start drifting out again the very next day. Your second thought is simpler: just replace the faulty gauges. You buy two brand-new temperature probes for the furnace - and out of the box they still read four degrees apart. New does not mean accurate; it just means new, and a slightly smaller bank balance. Your third attempt is to rely on people: "use your gut, keep an eye on things." This breeds arguments - one operator trusts the colour of the metal, another the sound of the furnace, and when a batch goes wrong everyone blames someone else's gut. That is opinions, not a system; you have not solved the problem, only made it personal.

Calibrating everything is impossible. New gauges are not accurate. Guts just argue. So before you read on: the real question is not 'how do we fix every gauge?' - it is 'how do we know WHICH numbers to believe?'

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop trying to trust the instruments and start building a system to trust the measurements. An instrument you have not checked is just a rumour with a number on it - and a confidently wrong instrument is the most dangerous thing in your plant, because you believe it and act on it. Five moves:

  • See the danger - an uncalibrated instrument is worse than none: the breakthrough is to stop trying to trust the INSTRUMENTS and start building a system to trust the MEASUREMENTS. An instrument you have not checked against a known truth is not giving you data - it is giving you a rumour with a number on it. And a confidently WRONG instrument is the most dangerous thing on the plant, far worse than one that is obviously broken: a dead gauge you distrust and work around, but a lying gauge you BELIEVE, and you pour, you isolate, you sign off - acting on its lie. So the first move is simply to name the danger out loud: a reading you cannot trust is not a small inconvenience, it is false confidence steering real and sometimes unsafe decisions.
  • Focus on the few that drive a decision or protect a person: you cannot calibrate everything - 480 instruments, drifting constantly, would swallow the whole budget and still be out of date tomorrow. You do not need to. Most readings are just rough comfort; only a few actually DECIDE something or PROTECT someone. So ask of every instrument: does this number drive a critical decision (the temperature that says when to pour) or guard a person (a pressure alarm, a crane load cell)? Find that handful - perhaps fifteen of the four hundred and eighty - and concentrate all your calibration effort there. The other parts can wait. This is the same focusing discipline as bp-0158: spend your rigour where a wrong number does real harm.
  • Calibrate the critical against a known, traceable truth: to know if a reading is true you must compare it to something you KNOW is true. So buy or borrow one high-grade, certified reference standard - a traceable thermometer, a known test weight, a calibrated pressure source - and make it your 'known truth'. Then check the critical few against it on a RISK-BASED interval, not a blanket 'once a year for everything': how fast the instrument drifts, multiplied by how much its error would cost, sets the frequency - the furnace probes weekly, the crane load cell monthly. Traceable means the standard itself can be traced back to a national reference, so your 'truth' is real, not just another opinion.
  • Make trust visible: a checked instrument is only useful if the person reading it KNOWS it has been checked. So make trust visible at a glance: a simple dated tag on each critical instrument - 'calibrated', the date, who did it - so anyone on the floor can see in a second whether to believe the number or treat it as a rough guide. Now the operator about to pour points to the green tag, and the whole team knows that reading is real. An untagged gauge is openly just a guide; a tagged one is verified truth. Visibility turns calibration from a back-office record into a live signal everyone uses.
  • Treat 'no trust' as a fault: the discipline only holds if an out-of-calibration CRITICAL instrument is treated like a broken safety guard - because that is what it is. If a furnace probe is found off by more than its allowed limit, the process stops: you switch to the backup, or read the certified reference directly, until it is fixed - no guessing, no 'it's probably fine'. You would never knowingly run a plant on a guard you know is broken; running it on a reading you know is untrustworthy is the same gamble with a friendlier face. Make the rule explicit and hold it, and the false-confidence disasters simply stop happening.

See it transform Andiswa's plant. And a furnace probe found off by more than its limit now stops the process - switch to the reference, no guessing - exactly as a broken guard would. The arguments stop; the scrap rate from bad pours falls by ninety percent in three months, saving hundreds of thousands of rand. The plant runs on verified truth, not guesswork. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This is not only about furnaces - you meet the same trap in your own kitchen. The oven dial says 180 degrees, you bake for the usual time, and the cake comes out burnt outside and raw within. The number on the dial lied.

See the same five moves on a kitchen oven

• See the danger: false confidence in the dial wastes your time and ingredients and ruins the meal.
• Focus on the few: you do not care if the oven is right at 70 degrees - only at the temperatures you actually bake and roast at, like 180 and 200.
• Check against a known truth: a cheap oven thermometer (about R100) is your 'known truth' - set the dial to 180, wait, and it might read 210, so now you KNOW the oven runs 30 degrees hot.
• Make trust visible: the 'label' is the new rule you share with everyone who uses it - "to get 180, set the dial to 150".
• Treat 'no trust' as a fault: you would not bake an important birthday cake on 'hope for the best' - you act on the truth you found, every time.

You stopped trusting the rumour on the dial and started acting on a checked measurement - the same five moves, a different plant.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look around your workplace - the digital displays, the pressure gauges, the dials, the dashboard numbers. Which of those do you use to make decisions? Which do you trust without thinking? Now ask: when was that number last checked against a known, proven truth? If the answer is "I don't know" or "never", you are running on rumours.

  1. List the deciding few. With your team, name the top five measurements that decide whether you have a good day or a bad one.
  2. Ask of each: how do we know this is true? If there is no answer, you have found a rumour you have been treating as a fact.
  3. Check the most critical one. Find a way to compare it to a reliable, traceable source - a certified device, or at least two independent gauges with every disagreement investigated.
  4. Make trust visible. Propose a simple tag, sticker or board mark that shows, at a glance, that a reading has been checked - and treat any critical unverified reading as a question, not a fact.

This was never about a careless foreman or a bad operator - they were doing their honest best with the only thing they had, a number on a dial, and the system never told them whether to trust it. You cannot manage what you cannot measure - but you cannot measure with an instrument that lies, and false confidence costs far more than the calibration ever would. Build a workplace that runs on truth: a few numbers you have genuinely checked beat a plant full of confident rumours.
