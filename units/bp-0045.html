Step 1 of 5

A giant moved in who will always be cheaper than me - how do I survive?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and one morning you see them: big, bright banners on a new depot that's just opened a few streets away - a national discount chain, promising the lowest gas price in the country. Your stomach drops. A quick check on your phone makes it worse: their advertised price for a 9kg cylinder is less than you pay your own supplier. They're selling it for cheaper than you can buy it.

A few days later the mentions start. A customer, handing over an empty cylinder, says, "Hey Muhammad, seen the price at that new place? Crazy cheap." They still buy from you - but the question hangs in the air. Then a regular quietly cancels their standing delivery. They don't say why. You know why. The panic sits cold in your gut, and you can see only two roads, both to ruin: match their price and lose money on every sale until you bleed out, or ignore them and watch your customers peel away one by one.

How can a small operator possibly survive when a giant moves in who will always be cheaper than me?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your instinct is to fight - but every move is just the giant's game, on the giant's turf. The race to zero: you drop your price to match. The phone rings a little more, but your heart sinks at every sale; a week later you've worked harder, burned more fuel, and have almost nothing - you've become an unpaid delivery agent for your supplier, and your one bakkie runs out of fuel long before their fleet does. Starving your strengths: to afford even a small cut, you think about skipping a safety check or stretching the bakkie's service - and feel sick, because you'd be killing the very things that make you good. The slow bleed: you freeze and hope it's a fad - and lose a familiar name every week anyway.

Matching, cutting corners, freezing - all losing moves on the same ground. So: if you can't win the price fight, what fight can you win?

A big discount operator opened nearby, selling 9kg cylinders cheaper than I can BUY them wholesale. Match the price and I bleed to death; ignore it and customers peel away. It feels like a fight I've already lost.
If I can't win the price fight, what fight CAN I win? What if I stopped fighting on their ground and asked my loyal customers why they actually stay?
Refuse the price-fight: ask your people why they choose you; your strength is exactly where the giant is weak (closeness, speed, trust, reliability); be the best for a defined few, not the cheapest for all; let the price-shoppers go on purpose; stand with peers.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The price-fight is a losing frame - so refuse it, and move the battle to ground where you're strong and the giant is weak. Five moves:

  • Refuse the frame - and ask your people why they stay: stop staring at the giant's banners and turn to the customers still with you. Go and ask, honestly: 'I know there's a cheaper place now - I'm grateful you stayed. Can I ask why you choose Tank You?' You can't defend a strength you haven't named, and only your customers can tell you what it really is.
  • Find your strength where the giant is weak: listen, and they won't say 'your price'. They'll say: 'when I message you, I know it's you'; 'you deliver in twenty minutes, right before I cook'; 'you know my gate code'; 'I trust your cylinders are safe and full'. A giant is built for low cost and high volume - which makes it slow, faceless, impersonal. Everything it CAN'T do is your home ground.
  • Be the best for a defined few, not the cheapest for all: your job was never to be the cheapest gas for everyone - it's to be the most reliable, convenient, trusted supplier for the people who VALUE that. Double down on it; make it your public promise. You're not selling cheap gas - you're selling the guarantee they never run out mid-meal, and a problem solved in minutes by one message.
  • Let the price-shoppers go - on purpose: the hardest, bravest step. Some customers will always chase the lowest price, whatever you do; chasing them bleeds you dry and steals energy from your loyal core. Let them go with grace: 'I understand completely - thank you for your business; if you ever need a fast, reliable delivery, you know my number.' That's not failure, it's focus - you choose your customers as much as they choose you.
  • Stand with your peers, not against them: other small operators face the same giant - they're not your real enemy. Talk to them: a group buy for a better supplier price, covering each other's areas when one is sick, referring out-of-area calls. A cluster of small boats sailing together is far harder for a big wave to swamp than one boat alone.

Here's the heart of it: a giant's great strength - its size - is also its weakness. Built for low cost and high volume, it cannot be close, fast, personal, or trusted the way you can; the very things it can't do are your home ground. So you stop trying to be a smaller, cheaper version of them and become the best you for the people who value it. You win not by being the cheapest for all, but the best for a defined few - and that's a fight a giant can never win for you, because it can't be your neighbour.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This is the survival strategy for any small player against a giant - watch it at a corner shop.

See it when a big supermarket opens down the road

A giant supermarket opens nearby, selling milk, bread and sugar cheaper than the corner shop can buy them. You can't win that price fight - so you refuse it.

• Ask your people: why do they still pop in? "Because you're two minutes away when I just need milk." "Because you let me pay at month-end." "Because you know my name." "Because you're open when they're closed."

• Your strength where they're weak: they're built for the big weekly shop; you're built for the quick last-minute dash. They're a faceless destination; you're part of the neighbourhood.

• Best for a defined few: not the trolley-fillers - the person out of teabags, the parent needing bread for tomorrow's lunch, the elder who enjoys the daily chat. Double down: stock fresh local bread, deliver to elderly neighbours, stay open just after the supermarket closes.

• Let the price-shoppers go + stand with peers: accept you'll lose the bulk-buyers; don't fight over 24-packs. Team up with the local butchery and bakery - swap referrals, build a small local web that wins on service, not bulk.

You survive - and thrive - not by being a smaller supermarket, but by being the best corner shop imaginable.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This is survival for any small player in any field. Look at your own venture:

  1. Name the giant and refuse their frame. Who is the bigger or cheaper competitor - and what's the price-fight they want you to have? Don't take it.
  2. Ask your people why they stay. Go to the customers who choose you for a reason other than price, and listen - the uncopiable strength they describe (speed, personal touch, deep know-how, reliability, trust) is your battleground.
  3. Focus, let go, and find peers. Be the best for the defined few who value that; let the price-only shoppers go with grace; and find the other small players you could stand with instead of fighting.

A giant will always be bigger and cheaper - that was never the fight to win. Its size buys it scale and steals its soul: it cannot know your name, reach your door in minutes, or earn your trust. Plant yourself exactly where its model makes it weak, serve your people better than anyone on earth, and you become something no price can undercut - not the cheapest, but the one they'd miss if you were gone.
