My best fitter, after 300 clean repetitions, skipped a safety check and a 900 kg anode nearly came down on him - how do I find why the task let him fail, instead of blaming a careful man and retraining him to no effect?

T1: The Ache

It is 04:40 on a Tuesday at the Bluegum Aluminium Smelter, in an inland industrial town. You are the maintenance reliability engineer for Potline 2. The pre-dawn air fogs the control-room glass, and under it you can hear the low hum of the reduction cells drawing current.

In your hand is an incident report from last night. A fitter, Lungile, was changing the carbon anode on Cell 47 - a 17-step job. On step 9 he was meant to confirm the crane hook was fully seated in the anode rod's lifting eye before signalling the operator to take the load. He did not. The hook slipped, a 900 kg anode dropped 1.2 metres onto the cell crust, and molten bath splashed. Nobody was burned - this time. Lungile has done this job more than 300 times without a slip.

The plant manager, Mrs Letlhare, is at your desk in her coat, car keys still in her hand. She is not shouting; that is somehow worse. "Lungile is one of my best fitters," she says quietly. "If he can miss step 9, anyone can. I do not want to hear that he was careless. I want to know why the task let him fail - and I want it before the safety board meets on Friday."

You feel the pull to do the easy thing: write "operator error - retrain Lungile," close the file, move on. Everybody would accept it. But you know that retraining a careful man who has done the job 300 times will change nothing. The same trap is still sitting in step 9, waiting for the next tired fitter at 04:00.

Lungile is one of my best. If he can miss step 9, anyone can. I do not want to hear he was careless - I want to know why the task let him fail, and how to make sure it never lets the next person fail either.

Before you read on, decide. Lungile has done this job three hundred times and missed step 9 at four in the morning. His supervisor is not shouting, which is worse, and she has asked for something specific by Friday: not that he was careless, but why the task let him fail. Write down how you would find that out. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one does about the next fitter

• “Operator error — retrain him and close the file.” Everybody would accept it, and that is what makes it dangerous. Retraining a careful man who has done the job three hundred times will change nothing, and the trap stays in step 9 waiting for the next tired person at four in the morning.

• “Add a second signature to step 9.” Feels like control, and it is a remedy chosen before anybody has looked at what step 9 actually asks a person to do. If the step already merges a check and a signal, another signature adds a name to the same trap.

• “Talk to Lungile — he’ll tell us what happened.” Essential, and it is one step and one night. He can tell you what he did; he cannot tell you which of the other sixteen steps are waiting to do the same thing to somebody else, because those have not caught anyone yet.

• “Nobody should be doing this at four in the morning.” Possibly true and outside your gift by Friday. It also concedes the point you should be arguing: a task that only works on a well-rested person in daylight is a task with a fault in it, not a task with a staffing problem.

• “Rewrite the procedure to be clearer.” The right family of answer and it is still a guess about which steps need it. Seventeen steps, and only some of them invite a mistake — rewriting all of them uniformly means the dangerous ones get the same attention as the safe ones.

Her question is more exact than it first sounds, and it is the whole brief: not what Lungile did, but where in this task a normal person is likely to fail, and how bad it would be — found before the failure, not after.

You have the seventeen-step procedure in front of you. What you do not have is a reliable, repeatable way to walk a task and find every place where it quietly invites a person to make a mistake - before the mistake happens. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is SHERPA - the Systematic Human Error Reduction and Prediction Approach. It answers one plain question: where in this task is a normal person likely to make a mistake, and how bad would it be? - and it answers it not by blaming the worker but by examining the task itself for the conditions that invite error. Watch it run on the anode change.

Step 1 - Build the task breakdown (HTA: the REAL steps)

The anode change is already 17 numbered steps. You walk it with the fitter and find steps 6 and 7 are done in the opposite order to the sheet (the tool trolley is on the left). You note it at once: the real task differs from the written one, and SHERPA must analyse the real one.

Step 2 - Walk the five error types, score each

Most steps yield one or two low-likelihood, low-consequence errors. Step 9 (confirm the hook is seated, then signal the lift) stands out: a CHECKING error - skip the confirm, signal anyway - judged HIGH likelihood and SEVERE consequence (a 900 kg anode drops 1.2 m, molten bath splashes). That is the exact slip that happened.

Step 3 - Find the error-provoking conditions (in the task, not the person)

Step 9 has three conditions, all in the task: NO FEEDBACK (a seated hook and a nearly-seated hook look the same - no click, no light); the CHECK and the SIGNAL are one merged motion (look up at the hook while raising the arm to signal, so the check gets swallowed); and TIME PRESSURE (each cell off-line costs about R18,000 an hour and night shift is measured on cells returned, so a check with no visible payoff is the first thing shaved). Any fitter, on a cold night, after 300 clean repetitions, is set up to fail step 9.

Step 4 - Design remedies that change the task, not the worker

For step 9, ranked: BEST - a spring-loaded latch that gives an audible click and shows a green band only when fully seated (about R2,400 a hook x 6 = R14,400), so 'nearly' can no longer pass for 'seated'. GOOD - split the check from the signal into 9a ('hand on the seated hook, say seated aloud') and 9b ('step back, then signal'), forcing the check to finish first. BACKSTOP - a second pair of eyes on night shift, kept only until the latch is fitted, because it relies on a person being free and attentive.

Step 5 - Record and act (the one-page table, with owners and dates)

The latch goes to the workshop with a two-week date; the 9a/9b rewrite goes live tonight as the interim control; the written step 6/7 order is corrected; two identical isolation valves get colour-coded tags. On Friday the board hears not 'operator error - retrain him' but 'step 9 was a designed trap - no feedback, a merged check-and-signal, time pressure; here are three remedies, two already in place'. The trap is gone, for him and for whoever changes the next anode at 04:00.

See what the five steps did. They turned "operator error - retrain him" into something true and useful: step 9 was a designed trap - no feedback, a merged check-and-signal, and time pressure - and three remedies that take the trap out of the task. This is the heart of it, and it is the AMU principle made into engineering: people are reliable but not perfect, so a good task makes the right action easy and the wrong action hard. When a careful person fails, the honest question is never "what is wrong with them?" but "what in this task set them up?" You protect Lungile's dignity and you protect the next fitter at 04:00 - by fixing the work, not by blaming the worker.

T3: The Blueprint

You run SHERPA in five moves.

  • 1. Build the task breakdown (HTA). The job as numbered steps, as actually done, walked with the people who do it - not the version on the sheet.
  • 2. Walk the five error types. For each step ask: could an action, checking, retrieval, communication or selection error happen? Score each credible error by likelihood and consequence.
  • 3. Find the error-provoking conditions. For the worst steps, ask what in the task makes the slip easy and natural.
  • 4. Design task remedies. Change the design, not the effort - feedback, a forcing step, a guard, a second pair of eyes - ranked by how little they rely on the person.
  • 5. Record and act. One page: step, error, type, likelihood, consequence, remedy, owner, date.

The ideas underneath: a Hierarchical Task Analysis is just a tidy numbered list of what the job really involves, and SHERPA needs the real task, because the gap between the written and the actual is where slips hide; the five error types save you from imagining every mistake from scratch (action, checking, retrieval, communication, selection); an error-provoking condition is a feature of the task - not the person - that makes a mistake likely (no feedback, look-alike controls, time pressure); and a remedy is ranked by reliance - a physical change that makes the wrong action impossible beats a procedure, which beats "be more careful", which barely counts at all. SHERPA fits any task done by people where a slip could hurt someone, damage an asset or stop production, and which has clear ordered steps - maintenance, start-ups, isolations, changeovers. It does not fit open-ended diagnostic or creative work with no fixed sequence, or a fast emergency with no procedure to analyse.

Two disciplines keep the analysis honest. First, never write "be more careful" as a remedy - it is the blame reflex in disguise, and it changes nothing; if your best fix is the worker trying harder, you have not finished the analysis. Look until you find a change to the task itself. Second, treat a production metric that pressures a check away as part of the trap - if the count of cells returned, or the clock, is what made the fitter shave step 9, then the metric is an error-provoking condition like any other; name it, and the fix may be a target as much as a latch. The point is always the same: hold the worker's dignity, and put the fault where it truly lives - in the design of the work.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your human-factors analysis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• "Operator error - retrain" fixes nothing. Retraining a careful person leaves the trap in the task for the next one. If the answer blames the worker, you have stopped one step too soon.

• Analyse the real task, not the written one. People adapt procedures to reality; the slip almost always hides in the gap between the sheet and what is actually done. Walk it with the doers.

• Rank remedies by how little they rely on the person. A physical guard or feedback beats a procedure, which beats a reminder, which beats "try harder". Reach for the design change first.

• A pressuring metric is an error-provoking condition. If the clock or the count makes a person shave a safety check, the metric is part of the trap - name it alongside the missing latch, do not pretend it is the worker's weakness.

• Hold the worker's dignity throughout. The person is the most reliable part of the system, not the weakest. Speak of the task's flaws, never the person's character - that is both kinder and more true.

You came in at dawn with an incident report and a manager who refused the easy lie. You can give her something far better than "retrain Lungile": the seventeen-step task walked end to end, the designed trap in step 9 named for what it is, and three remedies - two already in place tonight - so that no fitter, however tired, however rushed, is set up to fail there again. Find the trap in the task, not the flaw in the worker - and you protect both the good person in front of you and every person who will do this job after them. That is what it means to put people first: to fix the work so the work stops failing the people. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one real task and walk its five error types together.)
