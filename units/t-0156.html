Eleven on the shelf, and none in the system

T1: The Ache

It is 2am and a critical pump at Corathyl Water Treatment Works is down. The crew needs one bearing. You are Onalenna, and the call comes to you: the storeroom system says there are none in stock. So you authorise the emergency - a courier across the province, a technician on overtime - R8,500 to get the works running again by morning.

The next morning, the storeroom finds eleven of that exact bearing on the shelf.

They were there all night. They were catalogued under three different descriptions - "brg for pump", "bearing 123-A", and an obsolete manufacturer number nobody had linked to the current one - so the system held them as three unrelated things, and none of the three matched what the crew searched for. Eleven units at R1,800 each: R19,800 of the works' capital, sitting in an item the system could not see as one item. And one of those three codes had shown no movement for three years, so it was on the list to be written off as obsolete stock - the current part, about to be scrapped.

Nothing was missing. Nothing was stolen. The data was all there, and it was useless, because it was free text - and free text cannot be searched, counted or trusted.

My storeroom holds parts my system cannot find, so I buy what I already own and nearly scrap what I still need. How do I structure a parts catalogue so that an item can actually be found, counted and re-sourced?

Before you read on, decide. You paid a premium to have a bearing couriered in overnight, and it was on the shelf the whole time — under “brg for pump”. There are two other records for the same bearing: one as “bearing 123-A”, one under a supplier's old part number. Quantities of four, five and two. That last record has not moved in three years, so it is on the list to be written off as obsolete — the current part, about to be scrapped. Nothing is missing. Nothing is stolen. Every bit of the information is there. Write down how you would structure a catalogue so an item can be found — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why free text cannot be searched

• “Tell people to search harder before ordering.” Asks everyone to guess which of several prose descriptions somebody used years ago. The storekeeper who wrote “brg for pump” was not careless; free text simply admits no discipline to be careless about.

• “Do a stock count and clean up the duplicates.” Necessary, and a one-off against a continuous process. Without changing how items are described, new duplicates start forming the week after the count finishes.

• “Use the manufacturer's part number as the identifier.” The trap. A part number is something the item has, not something it is — manufacturers renumber whenever they like, and the day they do, all your history is orphaned. One of your three records is already an old supplier number.

• “Buy a better stores system.” Software will search whatever you gave it. Import three prose descriptions of one bearing into an excellent system and it will find you three items, faster.

• “Stop the write-off — that part is current.” Correct today, and it is a rescue rather than a fix. Somewhere in that storeroom are other current parts sitting under stale records nobody has happened to look at.

• The whole difficulty is that one physical thing exists under several descriptions, so it is several things to every question anybody asks — and no amount of diligence in the store repairs a defect that lives in how items are described. Three parts to the fix. Give each distinct item one permanent identity that belongs to you, so your history hangs off something the manufacturer cannot renumber. Move descriptions from prose to a controlled form — a fixed noun, a modifier, and defined attributes recorded the same way by everyone, so a search on any attribute finds it and the ambiguity that breeds duplicates has nowhere to live. And classify items into a recognised scheme, so like sits with like — which is what lets you ask a question about a whole category rather than only find an item you already know exists. Then those three records collapse into one, and the eleven units you actually own — around R19,800 of capital — finally appear as eleven.

There is a way to turn a storeroom of noise into a catalogue that answers. Here it is, for the pump spares.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the structured MRO catalogue - the same storeroom, described so that it can be searched.

1. How do we refer to this item, permanently? (one item, one code)

Every distinct item gets ONE INTERNAL STOCK CODE. That code is YOURS, it is permanent, and all your history hangs off it. The MANUFACTURER PART NUMBER is theirs - they renumber parts whenever they like, so a part number is something the item HAS, not something the item IS. Identify an item by the maker's number and the day they renumber it, your history is orphaned. Here the same bearing was sitting under THREE codes - quantities 4, 5 and 2 - so the storeroom held 11 units of one item at R1,800 each (R19,800 of capital) and the system could not see it as one thing. One item, one code: the three collapse into one, and 11 units finally appear as 11.

2. How do we describe it so a search finds it? (noun-modifier, not free text)

Free text is where duplicates are born: 'brg for pump', 'bearing 123-A' and an obsolete number are three descriptions of one part, and no search can join them. Replace it with the NOUN-MODIFIER convention - a controlled noun, a modifier, then defined ATTRIBUTES: BEARING, BALL, DEEP GROOVE; BORE 50 MM; OD 90 MM; WIDTH 20 MM; SEALED. Now the item is described the same way every time, by anyone, and a search on any attribute finds it. Then CLASSIFY it under a recognised taxonomy (UNSPSC, eCl@ss or ETIM) so it sits with the other bearings, and use ISO 8000 - the master-data quality standard - to hold the descriptions to a consistent form.

3. What replaced it, and what did it replace? (the supersession chain)

Manufacturers replace part numbers. SUPERSESSION records that: a PREDECESSOR (the old number, replaced) linked to its SUCCESSOR (the new number that replaces it). Link the chain and a search on the obsolete number still lands on the current part. Leave it broken and two things go wrong. The obsolete number becomes a dead end, so someone re-orders a part already on the shelf. And worse, CONSUMPTION HISTORY SPLITS: one of the three codes here showed ZERO movement for three years - only because every issue had been booked against the other two - so it looked obsolete and was nearly written off, when it is in fact the current part. A broken chain does not just hide a part; it FAKES ITS DEATH.

4. What else fits, and who else makes it? (interchangeability and alternates)

Record what else will actually do the job. INTERCHANGEABILITY is whether parts can be used in each other's place; TWO-WAY INTERCHANGEABLE means the parts are identical for practical purposes and either serves for the other, while one-way means the substitution runs in ONE direction only - and getting that backwards fits the wrong part. Equivalence is judged on FORM, FIT AND FUNCTION, never on a similar description. The standard source is the SPIR (Spare Parts and Interchangeability Record), the structured template the buyer gives the equipment supplier to complete at procurement - so the best time to get this data is at handover, not at 2am. Filling this column also exposes the items where you have only ONE possible supplier, which is a risk you could not previously see.

5. Does the catalogue actually work? (the populated deliverable and its test)

The deliverable is the POPULATED CATALOGUE for a class of assets - a row per item carrying stock code, structured description, classification, manufacturer part number, supersession links and verified alternates. Then PROVE it works: search for the bearing by its old number, its new number and its attributes, and land on ONE record each time. Count what changed - three codes collapsed into one; correct holding for the item is 4, so of the 11 units, 7 are surplus and R12,600 of capital is released; and the R8,500 emergency call-out for a part already in the building does not happen again. Last, set the RULE for adding new items, because a one-off cleanup rots: if new parts can still be created as free text, the duplicates come straight back.

Notice what box 3 rescues. A part that looked dead for three years was not dead at all - its history had simply been split across codes nobody had linked. The catalogue did not just find the bearing; it stopped the works from scrapping the one it needs. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

The problem has a name: findability. A part you cannot find is a part you do not have. Five questions build the catalogue that fixes it:

  • How do we refer to this item, permanently? With one internal stock code per distinct item - yours, permanent, the thing all your history hangs off. The manufacturer part number is theirs and changes when they choose; it is something the item has, not what the item is. One item, one code.
  • How do we describe it so a search finds it? With the noun-modifier convention - a controlled noun, a modifier, then defined attributes - instead of free text: BEARING, BALL, DEEP GROOVE; BORE 50 MM; OD 90 MM; WIDTH 20 MM; SEALED. Then classify it under a recognised taxonomy (UNSPSC, eCl@ss, ETIM) so like sits with like, and hold the descriptions to a consistent form using ISO 8000, the master-data quality standard.
  • What replaced it, and what did it replace? The supersession chain - a predecessor linked to its successor - so an obsolete number still resolves to the current part. Leave it broken and consumption history splits across codes, which fakes obsolescence: a live part shows no movement and gets written off.
  • What else fits, and who else makes it? The interchangeability record. Two-way interchangeable parts are identical for practical purposes and either serves for the other; a one-way substitution runs in one direction only, and reversing it fits the wrong part. Equivalence is judged on form, fit and function, never on a similar description. The standard source is the SPIR - the Spare Parts and Interchangeability Record the buyer gives the equipment supplier to complete at procurement.
  • Does the catalogue actually work? Test the populated deliverable: search by old number, new number and attributes, and land on one record each time. Count what changed - duplicates collapsed, capital released. Then set the rule for adding new items, because a one-off cleanup rots if free text can still get in.

One honest boundary. You are building the dictionary - the reference data that says what each item is, what it is called, what replaced it and what else fits. You are not setting stock levels or reorder points, not forecasting demand or designing the supply chain, and not deciding which critical spares are worth holding - those are separate units. A neighbouring technique cleans a messy work-order history so that an analysis of the past comes out right; this one structures the catalogue so that an action in the present is possible. One fixes the logbook. This one builds the dictionary the logbook writes in.

Does it scale? A book nobody catalogued is just paper on a shelf - it may be the best book in the building, and no borrower will ever find it. A spare part nobody catalogued is just metal in a bin, and the same is true at 2am. The principle does not change from one bearing to two hundred thousand line items; only the cost of ignoring it does.

T4: Your Turn

Four storeroom records for electric motors, exactly as they were typed:

Free-text descriptionMfr part no.Qty
motor 5.5kw 4-pole foot mountM55-9001-F2
5.5kW motor for conveyor 12M55-9001-F3
Electric Motor 5.5kW 4P Flange MntM55-9001-G1
Motor, conveyor, 5.5kW (see M55-9001)M55-44712

The supplier confirms that part number M55-4471 was superseded by M55-9001-F.

  1. Describe. Write the structured noun-modifier description for each distinct item.
  2. Code and collapse. Assign an internal stock code, say which rows collapse into one record, and give the total quantity that record should hold.
  3. The one that does not. Say which row does not collapse, and justify it on form, fit and function.
  4. Supersede. Record the supersession link, naming predecessor and successor.
  5. Source. Name one thing you would need from the supplier's SPIR to complete the alternative-sourcing column.

The test of a real answer: three of these four rows are the same item and one is not, and the free text will not tell you which. The only way to see it is to translate every row into structured attributes first - at which point one of them reads MOUNTING: FLANGE against the others' MOUNTING: FOOT. That is a form-fit-function difference, not a wording difference. Merge it and you have put a motor that cannot be bolted down into the slot where a foot-mounted one is needed. Cataloguing is not blind tidying; it is a technical judgement about what is genuinely the same part.

T5: The Guardrails

Catalogues fail in a small number of predictable ways, and every one of them is expensive.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Identifying an item by the manufacturer's part number. It is their number, and they will change it. Use a permanent internal stock code and keep the manufacturer part number as an attribute, or the next renumbering orphans your history.

• Leaving free text in place. This is where duplicates are born. Structured descriptions must be enforced for new items, not just applied once to old ones.

• Over-merging. The opposite failure, and the more dangerous one: collapsing items that differ on a form-fit-function attribute. A flange-mounted motor is not a wording variant of a foot-mounted one. A wrong merge is a breakdown waiting to happen.

• Leaving supersession chains broken. It splits consumption history and fakes obsolescence - a part with no recorded movement may be your most-used item, with every issue booked against its successor. Parts get scrapped this way.

• Treating every alternate as two-way. Many substitutions run in one direction only. Record the direction, or someone will fit the equivalence backwards.

• Recording alternates you have never verified. A similar description is not equivalence. Form, fit and function, confirmed - ideally from the SPIR at handover, when the supplier is still contractually obliged to answer.

• Letting the catalogue rot. A once-off cleanup with no rule for creating new items simply rebuilds the mess over a few years. The governance rule is part of the deliverable.

• Confusing cataloguing with stocking. This technique says what an item is. It does not say how many to hold, when to reorder, or whether the spare is worth having at all.

Onalenna paid R8,500 at 2am for a bearing that was already in her building, and came within a signature of scrapping the very part her pumps run on. Not because the storeroom was empty or the records were missing, but because the catalogue was written in a language nothing could search. Give every item one permanent code, describe it by noun, modifier and attributes, classify it, link what replaced it, record what else genuinely fits and where it comes from - and then prove the thing works by searching it. A part you cannot find is a part you do not have.
