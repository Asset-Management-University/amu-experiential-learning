My cousin put everything into one company she believed in - and lost it all when it failed. How do I make sure that never happens to me?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tshegofatso, and you watch this happen to someone you love. Your older cousin Mologadi has just landed a great job at a company everyone is talking about. She is proud, and she is sharp. As a staff perk she can buy the company's shares at a discount - and she decides to go all in. Every rand she has saved, she puts into that one company's stock.

"It's a sure thing," she tells you, her eyes bright. "I work there. I know how good we are. This is how you get properly wealthy, Tshego - you back a winner."

It feels so safe, so certain. And for a year it works beautifully - the share price climbs, and Mologadi feels like a genius. Then the news breaks. A scandal, a failed product, a sudden shift in the market - it happens frighteningly fast. The company's value evaporates. The share price falls and falls, and within a week the company files for bankruptcy. Mologadi does not just lose her job. Every single rand of her life savings, tied up in those shares, is gone too. Wiped out, in days.

You sit with her in the quiet afterwards, the shock still in the room. And the part that frightens you most is this: she did not gamble on something dodgy. She backed a real, solid company she knew inside out and believed in completely. It was not a stupid decision - it felt like the smartest, safest one she could make. And still it ended in total ruin.

How is that even possible - to lose absolutely everything, just like that, from one single thing failing? And how do I make sure it never, ever happens to me?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Trying to protect yourself from Mologadi's fate, three answers tempt you - and each one misses the real lesson. "I'll just put it all in the one company or coin I believe in MOST." But believing in something, even knowing it inside out, does not stop it failing - Mologadi believed completely, and it still collapsed. "I'll pick a few different winners myself to spread it a bit." But no one - not even the professionals - can reliably pick winners, and a handful is still not nearly enough spread to save you if they stumble together. "I'll keep everything in cash, then nothing can fail." But now all your eggs are in the 'cash' basket, where inflation and a zero growth-gap quietly bleed your money's power away year after year. The real lesson is not about WHICH thing to pick at all.

So before you read on: Mologadi did not pick a bad company - so what was it about HOW she invested, not WHAT she invested in, that let one failure take everything?

Notice what Mologadi did NOT do wrong. She did not gamble on something dodgy - she backed a real, solid company she knew well and believed in. So the ruin did not come from a bad choice of company. Where did it come from? What was the ONE thing about HOW she invested - not WHAT she invested in - that turned one company's failure into total ruin?
Imagine if Mologadi's money had been split across not one company but a hundred. That same company fails - what happens to her now? Almost nothing: one out of a hundred sinking is a scratch, not a death. The danger was never the one company; it was that it was the ONLY thing she owned. So what is the fix?
Never put all your eggs in one basket. Spreading your money across MANY holdings means no single failure can wipe you out - and the magic is it does this WITHOUT lowering what you can expect to earn. The easy everyman tool is an ETF: ONE purchase buys you a tiny slice of hundreds of companies at once (a REIT does the same for property), and professionals run it for you. This is called diversification.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The company was not the mistake. Owning ONLY the company was. Five moves:

  • See the one-basket ruin: The deadliest - and most avoidable - risk in all of investing is putting everything in one place. One company, one crypto coin, one property: if it is ALL you own, then that one thing failing takes EVERYTHING. Mologadi's mistake was not choosing a bad company; it was that the company was the only basket she had. When it dropped, there was nothing else to catch her.
  • The fix - spread your holdings: The cure is simple: spread your money across MANY different holdings. If you own a tiny piece of hundreds of things, then any ONE of them failing is barely a scratch - the other hundreds carry you. You stop trying to pick the one winner that will never fail (no one can), and instead make sure that no single failure can ever ruin you.
  • The 'free lunch' of spreading: Here is the part that feels almost too good to be true: spreading your risk this way does NOT lower what you can expect to earn over time. You cut the risk of being wiped out by a single failure, and you give up nothing in return for it. That is why it is often called the only 'free lunch' in investing - a real gain with no real cost.
  • The everyman tool - ETFs and REITs: You do not have to buy hundreds of shares one by one. A single purchase of a broad ETF (an exchange-traded fund) buys you a tiny slice of hundreds of companies at once; a REIT does the same for many properties. In one easy step you get the full spread - and professionals run the fund for you, so it costs you almost none of your time (the kind of asset that funds your life without eating it).
  • Name it - and know its honest limit: This is DIVERSIFICATION, and the rule is one line: never let any one thing have the power to ruin you. But be honest about what it can and cannot do. Spreading protects you from a SINGLE holding sinking you. It does NOT protect you from a whole-market storm, where almost everything falls together for a while - for that, you need the 'don't sell in a crash' rule from the last unit. Diversification stops one company drowning you; staying the course gets you through the storm.

The whole thing lives in one line: never let any single thing have the power to ruin you - spread your money across many holdings, so no one failure can take more than a scratch. What destroyed Mologadi was not a bad choice of company; it was that the company was her ONLY basket. Spread that same money across hundreds of holdings - which a single ETF does for you in one easy purchase - and the very same collapse becomes a number you would barely notice. Best of all, spreading costs you nothing in expected return; it is as close to a free gift as investing offers. Just hold the honest limit in mind: spreading saves you from one company sinking you, but not from a whole-market storm - and for the storm, you already have your crash rule. Refuse the all-in bet, spread wide, hold your nerve, and no single failure will ever be able to do to you what it did to your cousin.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own life, watch the very same company collapse hit two investors who arranged their money differently.

See two people, the same R20,000, the same company failing

Two people each start with R20,000 to invest, and - by chance - the same promising tech company ends up failing on both of them.

The all-in investor does what Mologadi did: puts the whole R20,000 into that single company's shares. For a while it climbs. Then the company hits a crisis and goes bankrupt, and the shares become worthless. Their R20,000 becomes R0. Everything, gone, because everything was in one place.

Nthabiseng puts her R20,000 into one broad ETF - a single fund that holds shares in 500 different companies at once. By coincidence, that exact same tech company is one of the 500. When it goes bankrupt, its slice of her fund drops to zero too - but it was only one five-hundredth of what she owned. Her R20,000 dips by about R40, to roughly R19,960, and keeps right on growing with the other 499 companies. She felt the very same failure Mologadi did. For her, it was a rounding error.

Same money, same company, same collapse. One was wiped out; the other barely noticed. The only difference was the spread.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Now make sure no single failure could ever do to you what it did to Mologadi:

  1. Run the ruin test on any plan. Whenever you are tempted to put money into something - a hyped share, one crypto coin, a single business - ask the one question that matters: 'if this ONE thing failed completely tomorrow, would I be wiped out?' If the answer is yes, your money is too concentrated, no matter how much you believe in it.
  2. Spread, in one easy step. You do not have to buy hundreds of shares yourself. Learn how a single broad ETF gives you a slice of hundreds of companies at once (a REIT does the same for property) - instant spread, professionally run, so no one failure can sink you and it barely costs your time.
  3. Hold the honest limit in mind. Spreading protects you from one thing failing - not from a whole-market storm where everything dips together. So pair it with your crash rule from the last unit: spread wide so no single failure ruins you, and stay the course so a storm cannot scare you out.

What happened to Mologadi was real, and it was avoidable - not by picking a better company, but by never letting one company be her whole world. You cannot make any single thing safe from failing; what you CAN do is arrange your money so that no single failure has the power to ruin you. The all-in bet will always feel exciting, and it will always be sold to you hard - but you can honour that pull, and still choose the quiet, unshakeable power of the spread. That is you, being the change - making sure the story you just watched can never become your own.
