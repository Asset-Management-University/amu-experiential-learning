T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, three weeks into your first real job: asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, the plant that sends clean water to every tap in the town.

You start to notice something. On the early shift, an operator skips a filter backwash to clock off on time. On nights, someone runs the main pump past its red line to catch up on output. When money is tight, the service on the standby pump gets pushed to "next month" - again. A new operator, unsure what matters most, makes a careful guess. Not one of them is lazy or careless; each is doing their honest best. But every person is pulling by their own idea of what counts - speed, output, saving money, just getting through the shift - because nobody has ever written down what Corathyl stands for.

Then it catches up. The over-run main pump fails on a Friday night. The standby - the one whose service kept getting pushed - will not start. For three days, the town has no water. The clinic rations. Schools close. You stand in front of an angry community with no good answer.

And when you trace it back, there is no villain to point at. The failure was not one bad decision. It was the absence of one shared thing: a clear promise, from the top, about what the works values and protects in its assets - something every shift could pull toward instead of away from.

Everyone here is trying their best, yet their choices pull the works apart - because we never agreed, on one page, what we stand for with our assets. How do I give a whole organisation a single true north?

Before you read on, decide. Nobody here did anything indefensible. The night shift kept the pump running through a warning because stopping it would have cut supply to the town. The day shift deferred an overhaul because the money was needed for something more urgent. The fitter fitted the part that was available rather than the one specified, because waiting would have meant three days down. Each of them can explain their reasoning, and each was protecting something worth protecting. The pump failed anyway, and there is nobody to point at. Write down what you would do about it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what is actually missing

• “Find out who was ultimately responsible and address it with them.” The most natural move, and it will fail on the evidence: every person acted defensibly, so the search either finds nobody or lands on whoever is least able to argue. It also teaches the whole works that the risk of a judgement call is personal, after which nobody makes one.

• “Write a procedure covering this situation.” Useful for this situation, and there are a hundred others. A procedure tells somebody what to do; it cannot tell them what to prefer when two rules both apply and point opposite ways, which is exactly what happened here.

• “Get the shifts together and talk it through.” Genuinely worth doing, and it settles this argument in this room with these people. Nothing about it reaches the shift that comes on at ten tonight, or the fitter who joins in March.

• “Escalate every trade-off like this to me.” That works until you are asleep, on leave, or one of forty such decisions in a week. It also makes the organisation slower at precisely the moments that are time-critical.

• “More training, so everybody understands the equipment better.” They understood the equipment. Not one of these decisions came from ignorance about pumps.

• Look at what the three decisions actually had in common. Each person faced two good things that could not both be had — supply against protecting the asset, this year's budget against next year's failure, uptime against doing it properly — and each resolved it in a different direction, correctly, according to a priority they had never been told. So the missing thing is not a rule, a meeting or a person. It is a ranking: a short, signed, public statement of what this place values in order, so that when two goods collide at three in the morning the answer is already decided and everybody knows it. It does not tell anybody what to do. It tells them what comes first — which is the only thing that could have made those three decisions agree.

There is a humble, powerful tool for exactly this: one page that sets the direction for every asset decision an organisation makes. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Before we name it or take it apart, here is what you are working toward: the whole of Corathyl's intent for its assets, on one page. Read it slowly. Notice how it would settle the very arguments that broke the pump - it tells every shift, plainly, what comes first.

1. Our Purpose (why we manage our assets)

Corathyl exists to deliver safe, clean, reliable water to the homes, clinics and schools of our community, every day. We look after our assets - the pumps, filters, pipes and reservoirs - so they can keep that promise for the long term: safely, and at a fair cost.

2. The Frame for Our Goals

This Policy sets the frame for the goals we choose and review each year - for example our targets for water quality, for hours of uninterrupted supply, and for safe working. Every goal we set must serve this Policy.

3. How It Serves Our Plan

This Policy carries out the Corathyl Service Plan - our promise to the community and the municipality to supply safe water now, and to grow as the town grows. Looking after our assets well is how we deliver that plan.

4. Our Commitments

We commit to: the health and safety of the public and our staff above all else; meeting every legal and regulatory requirement for treating and supplying water; managing each asset across its whole life to deliver real value, not just the lowest cost today; and improving our performance year on year.

5. Who Owns It, and Keeps It Alive

Approved and signed by the Works Manager and the Lead Engineer together - our top management. Shared with every member of staff, and available to the municipality, the auditors and the community on request. Reviewed every year, and again whenever the works changes in a big way.

Five short boxes. No jargon. But now the works has a true north. A new operator, the Works Manager, an auditor, or you at 2am could read this and know what Corathyl stands for - and what to protect when two good choices pull against each other. That clarity is the prize. Now let us walk through it, so you can build one yourself.

T3: The Blueprint

This one page has a formal name: it is an Asset Management Policy. Its shape is not an accident - it follows the international standard for managing assets, ISO 55001 (clause 5.2). The standard does not hand you the words; it names the few things a real policy must do. Here is each box, as the plain question it answers - with Corathyl's page still in front of you.

  • 1. Our Purpose - Why do we manage our assets at all? Lead with the people the asset serves (safe water for the community), never "to fix pumps". A policy that forgets its purpose ends up protecting the wrong things.
  • 2. The Frame for Our Goals - What will steer the goals we set each year? The policy does not list the goals - those change. It is the frame they must fit. This is the part most real policies get wrong: the frame for objectives lives inside the policy, as one of its own jobs.
  • 3. How It Serves Our Plan - How does looking after assets carry out the bigger plan? A policy floating free of the organisation's plan has no authority. Anchor it: Corathyl's policy exists to deliver the Service Plan.
  • 4. Our Commitments - What do we swear to, no matter what? The few non-negotiables: safety first, obey the law, care for each asset across its whole life (not just today's cheapest choice), and improve each year. These are what would have saved the pump - they tell the night shift, in writing, that safety and whole-life care beat tonight's output.
  • 5. Who Owns It, and Keeps It Alive - Who stands behind it, and how does it stay real? A policy is a promise from the top - and "top management" is a role, not one person's job title. At Corathyl it is the Works Manager and Lead Engineer together; at a refinery it might be the Site Director and their leadership team. Either way it must be signed at the top, shared with everyone, open to stakeholders, and reviewed - or it dies in a drawer.

You might think: this is just a small-town water works - does it really share anything with a giant refinery or a national grid? Look at the two policies side by side. The water works commits to "the health and safety of the public above all else"; the refinery commits to "process safety and environmental integrity". They are the same instrument: a one-page, top-management declaration of what is non-negotiable for their assets, written in the language of their own world. The scale changes; the tool does not. Master it on Corathyl's one page, and you can read - or write - the policy of any organisation on earth.

T4: Your Turn

Now you write one. Choose an organisation or shared asset you actually know - one with more than one person and real stakeholders, where its failure would let others down: a student society's equipment (the minibus, the sound gear), a community project (a borehole, a food garden, a clinic's standby generator), or a small business or workshop you know well. (Not your own phone or laptop - a policy is a promise between people, so it needs more than one.)

Five boxes feels like a lot? Start with just two - Our Purpose and Our Commitments. Get those honest and the rest follow. Keep each box to a sentence or two: a policy is short on purpose.

T5: The Guardrails

A one-page policy is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then go back and strengthen any box that needs it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The Policy-vs-Plan mix-up (the big one). A Policy is your constitution: short, stable, a statement of what you stand for. A Plan is the detailed programme: schedules, budgets, who-does-what-by-when. If your page has dates, maintenance schedules, or rand amounts on it, you have written a Plan, not a Policy. Keep the policy to direction and commitments; the detailed plans come later, and must answer to it.

• Vague platitudes. "We value quality and excellence" commits you to nothing. A real commitment can be checked: "the safety of the public above all else", "meet every legal requirement". If it could never be broken, it is not a commitment.

• A wish-list with no order. A policy that does not say what comes first cannot settle a hard choice. The whole point is to tell the night shift that safety beats output - so say so, plainly.

• Left in a drawer. A policy no one has read steers nothing. If your team cannot say what it commits to, it does not exist yet. Sign it, share it, put it on the wall.

• Never reviewed. The works changes; an un-reviewed policy slowly stops being true. Set a date to look at it every year, and whenever something big changes.

The three days Corathyl stood dry did not come from one bad decision - they came from never having agreed, on one page, what the works stood for. Now you hold the tool that gives any organisation its true north: a short, honest promise that lets every hand pull the same way. Master it here, and you can write - or read - the policy that keeps the water on, the plant safe, the lights burning, anywhere.
