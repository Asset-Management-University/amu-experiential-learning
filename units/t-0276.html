Step 1 of 5

Seventy-one per cent of our failure records say 'Other' - but twelve thousand typed notes underneath them say exactly what is going wrong. How do I count what nobody wrote in a countable field?

T1: The Ache

The depot office at Ekuthuleni Water Services has one working air conditioner and a wall of lever-arch files. Outside, two crews are loading a pump onto a bakkie. Six hundred and forty pumps and valves push water to about ninety thousand people, and the northern suburbs have been on tankers for nine days.

Councillor Nkabinde was in the depot on Tuesday and she asked one question, without heat, which made it worse: "You have been fixing these pumps for seven years. What is actually breaking?"

You are Refilwe Mosiane, the asset engineer, and you know the answer is in the system, because every job the department has ever done has a job card. You pull the report. There is a field called Failure Cause, a tidy dropdown with fourteen options, and it is the field every analysis in this department has ever been built on.

Seventy-one per cent of twelve thousand eight hundred records say "Other".

You already know why, and it is not carelessness. A fitter closes a job card at half past four, at a borehole, on a phone, with a crew waiting. The dropdown does not contain the thing that broke. "Other" is the honest option. And then - because they are conscientious, and because they want the next person to know - they type what actually happened into the notes box underneath. Twelve thousand eight hundred times over seven years. "glnd packing repacked, sand in casing again". "impellor worn thin, 3rd time this yr".

The truth is all there. It is simply in a form that cannot be counted, and no report in this building has ever looked at it.

The one field I can count is empty of meaning, and the twelve thousand notes that hold the answer cannot be counted. How do I get a number out of what my own people have already written?

Before you read on, say how you would answer the councillor by Friday. Twelve thousand eight hundred free-text notes, seven years, one of you. Write down what you would actually do this week - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four answers, and where each one stops

• "Fix the dropdown and start capturing it properly from now on." Right, and worth doing, and it answers nothing until 2033. It also leaves seven years of the department's own observation sitting unread while you wait for new data that says the same thing.

• "Read them all." At fifteen seconds a note that is fifty-three hours of reading. Some people would genuinely do it once. Nobody does it twice, which means you can never re-run it, never check yourself, and never split the answer a different way when the next question comes.

• "Sample a hundred and scale it up." Much better - and hold on to this one, because it is half of the right answer. On its own it gives you a rough proportion and nothing else: no way to cross the finding against which borehole, which season, which pump.

• "Ask the fitters what breaks most." Their judgement is real and you should ask them. It is also a memory of seven years, weighted toward last month and toward the jobs that were miserable - and if it disagrees with the councillor's impression, you are back to two opinions.

• What all four have in common is that they treat writing as something only a person can read. The move that changes everything is to make the words countable - to decide, in advance and in the open, what handful of things the notes are actually describing, and then sort every one of the twelve thousand eight hundred into those buckets by a rule you could show the councillor. Not to guess what the notes say. To count what they say.

T2: The Tool in Action

Read a hundred by hand first and let the categories come out of the fitters' own words. Make the same thing look the same, so a count can see it. Sort every note by a rule you can read aloud. Then measure how often your sorting is wrong - and where. Watch it run on Ekuthuleni's seven years of job cards.

Step 1 - Read a hundred of them yourself, before you automate anything

Refilwe Mosiane reads a hundred job cards with a pen in her hand. What she finds is not the dropdown's list. Over and over: gland packing repacked, seal weeping, sand in the casing, impeller worn thin, starter contactor burnt, rag blockage. She also finds three notes that are just 'attended, no fault found' and two that are somebody's phone number. By note sixty she has stopped adding new categories, which is how she knows a hundred was enough. Six buckets, written on paper, in the fitters' own words - not 'mechanical failure' but 'gland packing'.

Step 2 - Clean the text so that the same thing looks the same

The same repair is written 'gland pkng', 'gland packing', 'glnd packing repacked' and 'repacked gland'. Refilwe builds a small mapping file - forty lines, ten minutes - that sends all of them to one spelling. She drops words that appear in almost every note and separate nothing: attended, site, pump, done, ok. She does not touch the originals; the cleaning happens on a copy, because the fitters' actual words are the record and she may need to go back to them.

Step 3 - Sort every note into one of your buckets with rules you can read

Rule by rule: any note containing gland or packing goes to gland packing, unless it also contains impeller, in which case impeller wins because that is the bigger job. Sand, grit or silt goes to abrasive ingress. Contactor, starter, overload or tripped goes to electrical. Rag, blockage or choked goes to blockage. Twelve thousand eight hundred notes sort in about four seconds. Nine per cent land in unsorted, and Refilwe reads forty of those too - most are genuinely 'no fault found', which is itself a finding worth reporting.

Step 4 - Measure how often your sorting is wrong, and say so out loud

Two hundred fresh notes, read by hand against the rules: 182 agree, 18 do not - 91 per cent. And the disagreements are not scattered. Fourteen of the eighteen are gland-packing adjustments that the rules filed under seal replacement, because fitters use the two words loosely. So the honest statement is not 'the classification is 91 per cent accurate'. It is: '91 per cent, and the error is almost entirely one boundary - gland packing against seal replacement - which means the combined sealing figure is solid and the split between those two is not.' That sentence is worth more than a bigger percentage.

Step 5 - Turn the count into the answer somebody was actually asking for

Sealing work is 31 per cent of everything - the largest single thing the department does. But when Refilwe puts it against the boreholes each pump draws from, it is not spread evenly: it is more than half the work on the eleven pumps drawing from the sandy northern field and ordinary everywhere else. So the answer to the councillor is not 'we should spend more on seals'. It is: 'the sealing work that dominates our whole maintenance load is concentrated on eleven pumps drawing sandy water, and a strainer upstream costs a fraction of what we currently spend replacing what the sand destroys.' Nobody could have reached that from a field that said Other.

See where the answer actually came from. Not from the tool - the sorting itself took four seconds - but from the hundred notes read by hand at the start, which is what stopped the categories being invented at a desk, and from the two hundred read at the end, which is what turned "91 per cent accurate" into something far more useful: 91 per cent, with the error almost entirely on one boundary. And notice whose finding this is. Twelve thousand eight hundred notes, typed at half past four at a borehole by people who could have left the box empty and did not, became the sentence that gets a strainer installed on eleven pumps. The method did not create that knowledge. It was always in the building, written down, by the people doing the work - it just could not be counted, and so it had never once been used.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the first and fourth are the ones that make it honest.

  • 1. Read a hundred by hand and let the categories come from the words people actually used. Stop when new ones stop appearing.
  • 2. Clean on a copy so the same thing looks the same - one spelling per idea, and drop the words that separate nothing.
  • 3. Sort by rules you can read aloud, most specific first, with an unsorted pile you keep visible.
  • 4. Take a fresh sample, read it yourself, and count the disagreements - then find where they cluster.
  • 5. Cross the biggest category against something else you know, and look for where it concentrates.

What you have just done is the working core of text classification - one of the everyday uses of what gets called natural-language processing - and its vocabulary is only names for the moves. The words you dropped because they appeared everywhere and separated nothing are stop words, and they are always specific to your world: "pump" is a stop word in a pump depot and a vital signal in a report about a whole municipality. Deciding categories by reading first, rather than choosing them in advance, is working bottom-up from the data - and it is why the buckets came out as "gland packing" rather than "mechanical failure". Sorting by rules you can read aloud is a rule-based classifier; the alternative is to train a model on examples you have labelled, which is more powerful, needs far more labelled examples, and gives up the thing that matters most here - that a councillor can be shown exactly why any single note landed where it did. The fresh sample you read at the end is a held-out check, and the disagreements you found are the honest statement of what your count is worth.

It fits any place where an organisation has been writing things down for years in a box nobody counts - job cards, incident reports, complaints, clinical notes, delivery exceptions, call logs. It is the wrong instrument when there are only a few hundred notes, which you should simply read; and it can only ever tell you what people wrote down, so what nobody ever recorded will not appear no matter how good the sorting is.

Find the box in your organisation that people type into and nobody counts - job cards, complaints, incident reports, delivery notes, referrals. Print a hundred at random and read them with a pen. Write down the categories the words actually fall into. Then clean a copy, write your rules, sort the lot, and take a fresh two hundred to check yourself against.

Two disciplines separate a finding from a fabrication. First, let the categories come out of the reading, and never from a scheme designed before you looked - a list invented at a desk will silently absorb everything it did not anticipate into whichever bucket is nearest, and you will never know, because the sorting will look complete. The hundred notes you read by hand are not preparation for the work; they are the part of it that decides whether the number means anything. Second, publish the accuracy and the shape of the error in the same breath as the finding, before anyone asks. "Thirty-one per cent" said alone will be quoted for years and hardened into fact. "Thirty-one per cent, from a sort that agrees with my own reading nine times in ten, with almost all the disagreement on one boundary" is a number a decision can safely be built on - and stating it yourself is what makes you the person who can be believed the next time.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your count true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Never design the categories before you read. Everything the scheme did not anticipate will land quietly in the nearest bucket, and the result will look complete while being wrong in a way nobody can see.

• Keep the unsorted pile, and report its size. Forcing every note somewhere makes a tidier chart and destroys your best quality signal. A count with 9 per cent unsorted and honest beats one with 0 per cent and hidden.

• Clean on a copy, never on the record. The original words are the evidence, and you will need to go back to them the first time somebody challenges a finding.

• A high accuracy figure can still hide a broken answer. Where the errors sit matters more than how many there are: errors spread evenly are noise, errors on one boundary mean one specific number in your report is not safe to quote.

• It can only count what somebody wrote. Failures that nobody records - because they are fixed in five minutes, or because reporting them causes trouble - are invisible here, and their absence is not evidence that they are rare.

• These are your colleagues' words, not raw material. Say whose observation the finding came from when you present it. Seven years of people typing at half past four, when they did not have to, is what made the answer possible.

• The purpose is to fix the system, not to assess the person. This method gives you an unusually direct view of what your colleagues see and say. Its only legitimate use is to find and fix what they are reporting - the sand in the water, the fault that keeps coming back, the tool nobody has. Turning it on individuals - whose notes are shortest, who reports the most problems, whose spelling is worst - betrays the trust that produced the data, and it is the surest way to make the next seven years of notes empty. If you cannot say out loud, to the people who wrote them, what you are counting their words for, do not count them.

You came into that office with one countable field that said "Other" seven thousand times, and twelve thousand notes that held the answer in a form no report could reach. You can go to the councillor with something else entirely: a third of everything the department does is sealing work, it is concentrated on eleven pumps drawing sandy water rather than spread across the network, a strainer upstream costs a fraction of what is currently spent replacing what the sand destroys - and here is how often that count disagrees with a careful human reading, and exactly where. This is how seven years of your own people's writing stops being an archive and becomes the evidence that changes what gets funded. The northern suburbs do not need a better dropdown. They need the water back on. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring a hundred real notes from your own system and read them together.)
