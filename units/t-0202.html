The fourth move

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna. A critical pump is down, and every day it stays down costs Corathyl R45,000 in lost production. One local shop can rebuild it fast. The owner names his price: R850,000.

The number lands like a punch - and, strangely, you cannot immediately argue with it. You can see three moves and you dislike all of them. Pay it, and you have been had. Fight it down a bit, and you are still inside his number. Send the pump 400km away, and you lose three weeks.

You sense there is a fourth move. You cannot yet see it.

He has one price and I have three bad answers. How do I stop haggling over a single number and find the move that leaves us both better off?

Before you read on, decide. The pump is down and costing R45,000 a day. One local shop can rebuild it in about five days; the alternative is shipping it 400km, which means roughly 21 days plus R180,000 in transport and expediting. The owner names his price: R850,000. It lands like a punch and you cannot immediately argue with it. He is not a shark — he has a real business, a full workshop and his own constraints, and he has told you he is quiet in November and that his people hate rush jobs. You can see three moves and dislike all of them: pay it, push it down a little, or send it away. Write down what you would do — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the homework nobody does

• “Pay it — the downtime costs more.” Probably right and taken blind. You have not established what the alternative actually costs, so you do not know whether R850,000 is a bargain or R200,000 too much — and you will feel had either way.

• “Push him down — offer R600,000 and meet in the middle.” Haggling inside his frame. The best case is a smaller number on the one dimension he chose, and the likely case is a worse relationship with the only shop that can help you quickly.

• “Tell him we will go elsewhere.” A bluff you have not costed. If he calls it you must either follow through at three weeks or retreat, and having retreated once you will never be believed again.

• “Escalate — get the manager to lean on him.” Pressure applied to a supplier you will need repeatedly, on a job where his goodwill determines whether it takes five days or eight.

• “Send it away on principle.” Spends three weeks of production to avoid feeling taken advantage of, which is the most expensive emotion available here.

• Do the homework nobody does: work out precisely what you will do if there is no deal, and cost it. Twenty-one days at R45,000 is R945,000, plus R180,000, so your alternative is R1,125,000. Now compare properly — his five days cost you R225,000 in downtime, so the point at which walking away becomes genuinely better is a price of about R900,000. His R850,000 is already inside your walk-away by fifty thousand. That is what costing an alternative does: it turns an intimidating number into a comparable one, and it is where your power actually lives — not in your argument but in your alternative, because someone who can walk is calm, and calm is leverage. Then the fourth move: leave the single dimension. Behind his position sits an interest, and they are not the same — he named a price, but he has told you he is quiet in November and that his people hate rush jobs. Trade on what each of you values differently: timing, future work, payment terms, who does the strip-down. Those cost one side little and are worth much to the other, which creates value before anybody claims any of it. A negotiation on one number can only divide; one on several can enlarge.

Here is the fourth move.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you prepare and conduct a negotiation - the walk-away, the interests, the value you create before you claim it, and the close that holds.

1. Know your walk-away before you start - and never reveal it

A NEGOTIATION IS WON OR LOST BEFORE YOU SIT DOWN, IN A PIECE OF HOMEWORK ALMOST NOBODY DOES: WORKING OUT WHAT YOU WILL ACTUALLY DO IF THERE IS NO DEAL. That is your BEST ALTERNATIVE TO A NEGOTIATED AGREEMENT - your BATNA - and it is the single most important thing you bring to the table, more than any argument. YOUR POWER IN A NEGOTIATION IS NOT YOUR ARGUMENT. IT IS YOUR ALTERNATIVE. A person with a strong walk-away and a weak case beats a person with a strong case and no walk-away every time, because the one who can walk is calm, and calm is leverage. So do two things before the meeting. FIRST, MAKE YOUR ALTERNATIVE AS STRONG AS YOU CAN - a second supplier quoted, another buyer approached, a fallback plan costed - because improving your BATNA is the only way to gain real power without saying a word. SECOND, TURN IT INTO A NUMBER: YOUR RESERVATION POINT, the least favourable terms past which walking away is genuinely better than any deal. At Corathyl the critical pump is down at R45,000 a day of lost production, and only one local shop can rebuild it fast. Onalenna's ALTERNATIVE is to ship it 400km away: about 21 days down plus R180,000 to transport and expedite, an effective cost of R1,125,000. The local shop can turn it in 5 days, which is R225,000 of downtime, so the MOST she should rationally pay the local shop for the job itself is R1,125,000 minus R225,000 - A RESERVATION POINT OF R900,000. AND HERE IS THE RULE THAT PROTECTS IT: NEVER REVEAL YOUR RESERVATION POINT, and set it BEFORE you hear their number, because the first offer ANCHORS - it drags every later number toward it - and a walk-away you decided in advance is your only defence against being pulled into a bad deal by a big opening figure. YOUR BATNA IS YOUR SOURCE OF CALM, NOT A WEAPON TO BE USED AGAINST ANOTHER PERSON.

2. Find the interests behind the positions - on BOTH sides

MOST NEGOTIATIONS GO WRONG BECAUSE BOTH SIDES ARGUE OVER POSITIONS INSTEAD OF INTERESTS. A POSITION IS WHAT SOMEONE DEMANDS - 'R850,000'. AN INTEREST IS WHY THEY WANT IT - and behind every position there is always an interest, usually more than one. Two people fighting over a single number have nowhere to go but up, down, or split-the-difference. Two people who understand each other's interests can trade. So do the work of looking behind the numbers, on BOTH sides. WRITE DOWN YOUR OWN INTERESTS AS A LIST OF ISSUES, NOT JUST PRICE. Onalenna does not only want a low price; she wants SPEED (every day down is R45,000), RELIABILITY (a rebuild that lasts), and a supplier she can call again in a hurry. THEN ESTIMATE THE OTHER SIDE'S INTERESTS AND THEIR OWN ALTERNATIVE. The shop's cost to do the job is around R420,000; below about R500,000 they would rather not take it - that is their reservation point. But price is not all they care about: a small specialist shop most values a STEADY PIPELINE OF FUTURE WORK and PROMPT PAYMENT for cash flow, because feast-and-famine and slow-paying clients are what keep such businesses awake at night. ESTIMATING THE OTHER SIDE'S RESERVATION POINT AND INTERESTS IS ANALYSIS, NOT MANIPULATION - a negotiation is a problem to be solved, and you cannot solve a problem whose boundaries you refuse to see. The two reservation points define the ZONE OF POSSIBLE AGREEMENT - the ZOPA - which here runs from about R500,000 to R900,000: R400,000 of value sitting on the table waiting to be divided, and, more importantly, a set of DIFFERENT interests waiting to be traded. AND IF THE TWO RESERVATION POINTS DO NOT OVERLAP AT ALL, THERE IS NO ZOPA AND NO HONEST DEAL - the skill then is to recognise it early and walk, not to bully someone across their own floor.

3. Create value before you claim it - trade across issues

HERE IS THE FOURTH MOVE - THE ONE THAT IS NEITHER PAY, NOR FIGHT, NOR WALK. BEFORE YOU ARGUE ABOUT HOW TO DIVIDE THE PIE, MAKE THE PIE BIGGER. This is the difference between DISTRIBUTIVE bargaining, which splits a fixed amount so your gain is their loss, and INTEGRATIVE negotiation, which trades across several issues that the two sides value DIFFERENTLY, so both walk away better off. The method is simple to state and powerful in use: FIND WHAT IS CHEAP FOR YOU TO GIVE AND DEAR FOR THEM TO GET, AND SWAP IT FOR WHAT IS CHEAP FOR THEM AND DEAR FOR YOU. Lay the issues from box 2 side by side. Onalenna can offer two things that cost her almost nothing and are worth a great deal to the shop: a TWO-YEAR PREFERRED-SUPPLIER AGREEMENT (she needs a reliable rebuild partner anyway, so promising future work is nearly free to her and solves the shop's single biggest worry) and PAYMENT WITHIN 7 DAYS instead of the usual 60 (a timing change that costs her little but transforms a small shop's cash flow). In return she asks for what SHE values: a lower price on this job and a guaranteed 5-day turnaround. THE DEAL THAT EMERGES: R560,000 for the rebuild, a 5-day turnaround, a two-year agreement, payment in 7 days. Look at what each side got. The shop earns R140,000 margin AND gains a pipeline AND gets paid fast - far better than a squeezed R500,000 cash-in-60-days job. Onalenna pays R560,000 plus R225,000 downtime, R785,000 all in, against a R1,125,000 alternative - SHE SAVES R340,000 and locks in speed and reliability that a pure price haggle would never have reached. NEITHER OF THEM WON BY BEATING THE OTHER. They won by noticing that they wanted different things, and trading.

4. Claim your share without burning the relationship

CREATING VALUE AND CLAIMING VALUE ARE DIFFERENT SKILLS, AND YOU NEED BOTH: EXPAND THE PIE, THEN CLAIM A FAIR SHARE OF IT - HONOURABLY. Start with the anchor. THE SHOP OPENED AT R850,000, AND THAT NUMBER IS DOING WORK ON YOU WHETHER YOU LIKE IT OR NOT - the anchoring effect means the first figure quietly drags every later figure toward it. THE DEFENCE IS THE RESERVATION POINT YOU SET IN BOX 1, BEFORE YOU HEARD THEIR NUMBER. Do not react to the anchor - do not accept it, and do not just chip small amounts off it, because chipping off an anchor keeps you inside their frame. Set it aside and re-anchor to something real: 'the job's fair value, given a long-term relationship and fast payment, looks more like the mid-five-hundreds to me - let me show you why.' MAKE OFFERS THAT ARE TIED TO INTERESTS, NOT EGO - every number you name should point back to a reason (the value of the pipeline, the cost of the delay, the fair margin on the work), because a principled offer is hard to refuse and easy to say yes to, while a naked demand invites a naked counter-demand. AND KNOW THE ONE HONEST REASON TO WALK: THERE IS NO ZOPA. If their floor is above your ceiling - if the least they will take is more than the most you should pay - then no deal beats your alternative, and the skilled move is to thank them and go, not to bully or beg. A NEGOTIATION IS A SEARCH FOR A DEAL BOTH SIDES CAN LIVE WITH, NOT A CONTEST TO SEE WHO CAN WIN. And the sharpest warning of all: THE MOST DANGEROUS DEAL IS ONE YOUR COUNTERPARTY CANNOT AFFORD TO DELIVER. NEVER NEGOTIATE A PRICE SO LOW THAT IT GUARANTEES FAILURE - a shop squeezed below its cost will cut corners on YOUR pump, and their failure will become your problem the day it fails again.

5. Close it so it holds - a deal both can deliver, written down, relationship intact

A NEGOTIATION IS NOT FINISHED WHEN BOTH SIDES SAY YES. IT IS FINISHED WHEN THE DEAL IS DELIVERED, AND THAT STARTS WITH CLOSING IT WELL. THREE THINGS. FIRST, AGREE ONLY WHAT BOTH SIDES CAN ACTUALLY DELIVER. Test the deal against reality before you shake hands: can the shop really turn it in 5 days, or did they say yes under pressure to a promise they will break? A deal built on a commitment someone cannot keep is worse than no deal, because you have stopped looking for alternatives and built your plan on sand. HONESTY ABOUT YOUR INTERESTS IS A STRENGTH HERE - saying plainly 'the 5 days is the part I cannot flex on, tell me honestly if that is real for you' gets you a truer deal than a cleverer one. SECOND, WRITE IT DOWN - properly. The handshake captures the intent; the written agreement captures what happens on the bad day, and turning an agreed deal into an agreement that holds under stress is a discipline of its own (specify what it must achieve, test it from inside the failure it exists for, name the delegations and the records). A deal remembered differently by each side is a dispute waiting to mature. THIRD, KEEP THE RELATIONSHIP, BECAUSE YOU WILL MEET THIS PERSON AGAIN. You needed this shop this week and you will need them next year; the young entrepreneur who wins their first contract by grinding the buyer into a loss has won a job and lost a client. The aim was never to beat them - IN MANY NEGOTIATIONS ONE PARTY HAS MORE STRUCTURAL POWER, AND WE DO NOT PRETEND OTHERWISE, BUT A STRONG BATNA AND A CLEAR RESERVATION POINT ARE HOW A PERSON WITH LESS POWER BUILDS AGENCY, PROTECTS THEIR DIGNITY, AND TURNS DEPENDENCE INTO CHOICE. You leave with a deal you can both deliver, a relationship you can both use again, and the quiet knowledge that you found the fourth move.

Box 1 is where the power actually comes from. Box 3 is the fourth move. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

My real power in a negotiation is not my argument. It is the strength of my alternative. Five questions:

  • What will I do if there is no deal? Your BATNA, made as strong as you can, turned into a reservation point - the number past which walking beats any deal. Set it before you hear theirs. Never reveal it.
  • Why does each side want what it wants? Interests behind positions, on both sides. List your issues, not just price. Estimate their alternative and their needs.
  • How do we make the pie bigger? Trade across issues valued differently - what is cheap for you and dear for them, swapped for the reverse.
  • How do I claim a fair share? Handle the anchor, tie offers to interests, and walk if there is no overlap.
  • How do I make it hold? A deal both can deliver, written down, with the relationship intact.

The arithmetic of the fourth move. Onalenna's alternative to a deal is shipping the pump away: 21 days down at R45,000 plus R180,000 transport - an effective cost of R1,125,000. The local shop turns it in 5 days (R225,000 downtime), so the most she should pay for the job itself is 1,125,000 - 225,000 = R900,000: her reservation point, set before the meeting and never spoken aloud. The shop's cost is estimated at about R420,000 and its floor is therefore likely around R500,000. So the Zone of Possible Agreement runs from R500,000 to R900,000 - R400,000 of value to divide, and, more usefully, two sides that want different things. A single-number haggle just splits toward R700,000 and touches none of it. But Onalenna most values speed and reliability; the small shop most values a steady pipeline and prompt payment. So they trade: a two-year preferred-supplier agreement and payment in 7 days (cheap for her, dear for the shop) in exchange for R560,000, a guaranteed 5-day turnaround. The shop earns R140,000 margin and a pipeline and fast cash; Onalenna pays R785,000 all-in against an R1,125,000 alternative - she saves R340,000 and secures speed a price fight would never have reached. Neither won by beating the other. They won by noticing they wanted different things, and trading.

Why the alternative is the whole game. The number that opened the meeting - R850,000 - was anchoring you, dragging your sense of "reasonable" toward it whether you agreed or not. The only defence is a reservation point you fixed before you heard it. And that reservation point comes entirely from your BATNA: improve your alternative and your floor rises and your calm with it; have no alternative and you will take whatever you are given, however good your argument. A negotiator with a strong walk-away and a weak case beats a negotiator with a strong case and no walk-away, every time.

And the ethics, which are not a footnote. A negotiation is a search for a deal both sides can live with, not a contest to see who can win. Your BATNA is your source of calm, not a weapon to be used against another person. Honesty about your interests is a strength - you can keep your walk-away private without ever being dishonest; be open about what you need the deal to achieve, and private about the exact point at which you will walk away. And the most dangerous deal is one your counterparty cannot afford to deliver: never negotiate a price so low that it guarantees failure, because their failure will become your problem. A shop squeezed below cost cuts corners on your pump.

One honest boundary. You negotiate the terms; you do not decide the deal alone - your principal or board sets the mandate you work within - and you never agree what the other side cannot actually deliver. A deal that sinks your supplier sinks your asset next.

Does it scale? The bridge to a good deal is never a better argument - it is a better alternative. A person with two job offers negotiates a salary calmly; a person with one negotiates from need. Everything else in this unit is downstream of that one truth.

T4: Your Turn

Now a negotiation where the power is not on your side.

A young entrepreneur - a graduate of AMU's start-up course - is bidding to provide a small, regular maintenance service to a large, established local company. The company's procurement manager is experienced, unhurried, and has three other bidders. The entrepreneur has this one prospect and no others yet. It is the most unequal table they will ever sit at.

ElementDetail
The entrepreneur wantsThe contract, a fair price, and a reference that wins the next three clients
The buyer wantsA reliable service, a low price, and no risk of a small supplier failing mid-contract
The buyer's alternativeThree other bidders and the option to keep doing it in-house
The entrepreneur's alternativeRight now, almost nothing - which the buyer can sense
The openingThe procurement manager says the budget is "well below" the entrepreneur's expected price, and asks for their number first

Your task: prepare this entrepreneur for the meeting.

  1. The alternative. The entrepreneur's BATNA is weak. What can they do before the meeting to strengthen it - even a little?
  2. The reservation point. What is the lowest price they should accept, and what goes into it besides "what I hoped to earn"?
  3. The anchor and the trap. The buyer asked for their number first, having implied the budget is low. What is the buyer doing, and what should the entrepreneur do?
  4. The trade. Name three non-price issues the entrepreneur could trade to create value for both sides.
  5. The honest floor. When should this entrepreneur walk away - and why is being willing to the source of their only real power?

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the BATNA. It is weak, and pretending otherwise is the first mistake. But it is not fixed: before the meeting the entrepreneur can get one more prospect in the pipeline (even a small one changes everything), price out what else they would do with the same hours, and decide honestly what this contract is worth as a reference versus as income. A single second option converts begging into choosing - that is why box 1 says improving your alternative is the only way to gain real power without saying a word.

Second, the reservation point is not "what I hoped to earn". It is their true cost to deliver - materials, hours, travel, a fair wage for their own time - plus the smallest margin that keeps the business alive. Below that number the contract loses money and slowly kills the business, however prestigious the client. Winning it below cost is the trap, not the prize.

Third, the buyer asked for their number first, after signalling a low budget - a double move: an anchor ("budget is low") and a request to make the entrepreneur anchor themselves, low. The entrepreneur should not blurt a number into that frame. They can decline gracefully - "before I put a price on it, help me understand what 'reliable' is worth to you here, and what's gone wrong with suppliers before" - moving the conversation from position to interest, and only then naming a price tied to value, not to the buyer's implied ceiling.

Fourth, the trades. The entrepreneur has cheap-to-give, dear-to-get things a big buyer values highly: a longer contract term (certainty, in exchange for a lower monthly rate), flexible or off-hours scheduling (the buyer's convenience, at little cost to a one-person operation), and detailed reporting and a named single point of contact (which answers the buyer's real fear - that a small supplier is a risky supplier). Each trade lets the buyer say yes to a price the entrepreneur can live on, by getting something worth more to them than the discount.

Fifth, the walk. If the buyer's real ceiling sits below the entrepreneur's true cost, there is no ZOPA, and the honest, powerful move is to decline warmly and keep the door open - "at that number I'd have to cut corners, and I won't do that to you or to my name; if the budget changes, I'd love to talk." The willingness to walk is the entrepreneur's only real leverage against a far stronger party: a strong reservation point and a clear alternative are how a person with less structural power builds agency, protects their dignity, and turns dependence into choice. We do not pretend the table is equal. We teach the weaker party how to stand at it.

T5: The Guardrails

Most people lose a negotiation in the preparation they never did.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Negotiating one number. A single position has only three moves - up, down, split. Put more issues on the table and you can trade instead of fight.

• No alternative, so no floor and no calm. Without a BATNA you will take what you are given. Build and improve your alternative before the meeting - it is your only real power.

• Revealing your reservation point. The moment they know your walk-away, they anchor just above it. Keep it private - you can do that without lying.

• Reacting to the anchor. Chipping small amounts off their opening number keeps you inside their frame. Set it aside and re-anchor to real value.

• Confusing positions with interests. "R850,000" is a position. "I need steady work and fast payment" is an interest. You can only trade on interests.

• Leaving value on the table. If you only argued about price, you almost certainly missed a trade that would have made both sides better off.

• Winning a price the other side cannot deliver. A counterparty squeezed below cost cuts corners on your asset. Their failure becomes your problem.

• Treating it as a contest to beat a person. It is a problem to solve together. You will sit across from this person again.

• Staying when there is no overlap. If their floor is above your ceiling, there is no honest deal. Walk warmly, and keep the door open.

Onalenna had one price and three bad answers. Work out what you will do if there is no deal, make that alternative as strong as you can, and turn it into a walk-away number you set before you hear theirs and never reveal; look behind both sides' positions to the interests, listing every issue that matters and not just price; make the pie bigger before you divide it by trading what is cheap for you and dear for them against the reverse; claim a fair share by handling the anchor and tying every offer to a real reason; and close a deal both sides can actually deliver, written down, with the relationship whole. Your real power is never your argument - it is the strength of your alternative. Improve it, and you negotiate from calm; neglect it, and you negotiate from need. Take one thing you have to negotiate soon - a price, a contract, a wage - and before you talk to anyone, write down two things: what you will do if there is no deal, and what the other side actually needs behind the number they will name. That page is the fourth move.
