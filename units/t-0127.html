The risk nobody could see

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have found something that needs fixing. The chlorine dosing installation - the equipment that makes the town's water safe to drink - is well run, but there is one specific weakness in how the neighbouring community would be warned if anything ever went wrong. You have done the analysis properly. You know exactly what it would cost to close.

So you take it to the board, and you bring your evidence: the risk register, the likelihood ratings, the severity scores, the matrix, the integrity levels. It is rigorous work and every number in it is defensible.

The meeting collapses in two directions at once.

Half the room glazes over somewhere in the second column. It is dense, it is technical, and their honest conclusion is that the engineers clearly have this in hand. The other half hears the word chlorine and stops listening for a different reason - now there is alarm in the room, and questions you never intended to raise, and a member asking whether the plant is safe at all. You spend the rest of your time calming people rather than deciding anything.

You leave with no decision. And the part that stings is that you were not trying to frighten anyone or blind them with detail - you were trying to show them one specific, fixable gap in a system that is otherwise well controlled. Nobody in that room could see what you could see. And because they could not see it, the gap is still open tonight.

My analysis was rigorous and correct, and the room either panicked or switched off - so nothing was decided and the gap stayed open. How do I show a non-technical audience a real risk honestly, so they can actually act on it?

Before you read on, decide. Your analysis was rigorous and correct. You said the words “uncontrolled chlorine release” and the chair went pale and wanted the plant shut. You showed the analysis to steady them, and three people around the table visibly stopped listening somewhere in the second page. You were not trying to frighten anyone or bury them in detail — you were trying to show them one specific, fixable gap in a system that is otherwise well controlled. Nothing was decided. The gap is still open tonight. Write down how you would show them a real risk so they could act on it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why both halves of the room failed the same way

• “Simplify it — cut the technical detail.” That is what produced the panic. Strip the structure away and all that is left is the size of the words, and “chlorine release” without anything holding it back is an unbounded threat.

• “Give them the full analysis so they can see the evidence.” That is what produced the glazing. An undifferentiated mass of correct detail is exactly the form in which one urgent gap becomes indistinguishable from forty things already handled.

• “Use a risk rating — call it high and be done.” A number with its reasoning removed. It settles nothing, because the room's real question is not how bad it is but what to do, and a rating hides both the controls that work and the one that does not.

• “Bring in an expert to say it with more authority.” Borrowed authority moves a decision without the room understanding it, which works once and leaves them no better able to see the next one.

• “Just tell them what to approve.” Tempting, and it asks people accountable for the consequence to act on trust alone. When it costs money or someone questions it later, an unexplained instruction has nothing to stand on.

• Notice that both halves of that room failed for the same reason: each got either a conclusion with no structure or a structure with no shape, and neither could see where their attention belonged. What is missing is a picture whose shape carries the argument. Put at the centre the moment control is lost — precisely that moment, not the disaster that might follow, because everything is positioned relative to it and naming a catastrophe there throws the whole picture out. On one side, the credible causes and, against each, what stands in its way. On the other, the harms that would follow and what would limit them — a different job entirely, since those do not prevent anything, they only decide how bad it becomes. And then the detail that makes the picture honest rather than reassuring: what would quietly defeat a barrier — the detector out of calibration, the valve never function-tested, the warning plan nobody has drilled. That is where your gap lives, and it is the one thing a list of controls can never show.

There is one picture that does this, and it is the standard tool for exactly this job. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the same risk, same facts, nothing softened - drawn so that anyone in the room can see the whole story and the gap in it.

1. The top event - the moment control is lost

The knot at the centre: an uncontrolled release of chlorine gas from the dosing area. Note carefully - this is the moment control is LOST, not the harm itself. Getting this right is what makes the two sides line up; name a disaster here and the whole picture goes out of shape.

2. Threats and preventive barriers - the left side

Each credible cause, and what stands in its way: a failed connection - stopped by certified fittings and a torque procedure; corrosion of the dosing line - stopped by the inspection and replacement schedule; a cylinder dropped during delivery - stopped by proper lifting equipment and trained handlers. Cause, then the barrier holding it back.

3. Consequences and mitigative barriers - the right side

If control is lost anyway: harm to operators - limited by gas detection, alarm and breathing apparatus; harm to neighbours - limited by automatic isolation and a community warning; loss of supply - limited by standby dosing. These barriers do not prevent the event; they decide how bad it becomes.

4. Escalation factors - what quietly defeats a barrier

What would quietly defeat a barrier? A gas detector out of calibration defeats detection. An isolation valve never function-tested may not close when asked. And a warning plan nobody has ever drilled is not a barrier at all - it is a hope on paper. Each of these needs its own control, or the barrier above it cannot be counted.

5. The brief - the gap made visible, and the decision requested

In plain words, no jargon: 'The causes are well controlled - three solid barriers. But on the community side there is ONE barrier, and it has never been drilled. We are asking for R250,000 to install and test an automatic warning system. If it is not approved, that gap stays open and our neighbours depend on a plan no one has practised.' The picture shows the gap; the brief asks for the decision.

Look at what the picture does that the register could not. In one glance you can see the causes and that each one has something holding it back; you can see what would happen if control were lost; and you can see - immediately, without being told - that on the community side there is a single barrier and it has never been drilled. Nobody has to take that on trust. It is simply visible. Now let us name the shape.

T3: The Blueprint

That picture is a bow-tie, named for its shape, and it exists because a risk analysis that a decision-maker cannot read protects nobody. Each part answers a question:

  • What is the moment we lose control? That is the top event, the knot at the centre. It is not the disaster - it is the loss of control that could lead to one. "Uncontrolled release of chlorine gas" is a top event; "people harmed" is a consequence. Getting this distinction right is what makes both sides line up.
  • What could take us there, and what stands in the way? The left side: each credible threat, and between it and the knot, the preventive barriers that stop that cause producing a loss of control.
  • What follows, and what limits it? The right side: each consequence, and between the knot and it, the mitigative or recovery barriers that decide how bad it becomes. These do not prevent the event - they contain it.
  • What could quietly defeat a barrier? Those are escalation factors: the detector out of calibration, the valve never function-tested, the plan nobody has drilled. Each needs its own control - because a barrier you cannot rely on should not be counted as a barrier at all. A procedure that has never been trained is not a barrier; it is a hope on paper.
  • So what am I asking you to decide? The brief that travels with the picture: what is well controlled, exactly where the gap is, what you want approved, and what stays exposed if it is not.

Two disciplines make this honest rather than merely pretty. First, tune the detail to the audience - but tune the jargon, never the truth. You may drop the integrity levels and the likelihood tables for a community meeting; you may not drop an inconvenient consequence or quietly shrink a gap. Second - and this is the heart of it - the gaps are the point. There is a strong pull to present a bow-tie with a barrier under every threat, because a visible hole feels like an admission that you have not done your job. Resist it completely. An honest bow-tie with a visible gap is the work of a competent engineer; a bow-tie with no gaps at all is either a genuinely perfect system, which is rare, or it is not honest. The gap is not your failure - it is your case.

Does it scale? This is how a good doctor explains a serious diagnosis to a family: here is what can bring it on, here is what we do to prevent it, here is what happens if it occurs, and here is what we do to limit the harm - rather than handing them the raw pathology report and hoping they follow.

T4: Your Turn

Now draw one - and draw it to get a decision, not just to fill in boxes. Choose a real hazard in your own world: a gas cylinder in a workshop or kitchen, working at height on a ladder, a long night drive, a chemical store, a crowded event.

  1. Write the top event. The moment control is lost - not the injury. Check it: could several different things cause it, and could several different things follow from it? If not, you have probably written a consequence.
  2. Left side. Two or three credible threats, and the barrier standing between each and the top event.
  3. Right side. Two or three consequences, and what limits each one.
  4. Escalation factors. For your most important barrier - what would quietly stop it working?
  5. The brief, in two sentences. Name the weakest or missing barrier, and ask for the specific thing that would close it - money, time, training, a test.

The honesty check: if your finished picture has no gap anywhere, look again. Either you have found a genuinely well-defended hazard - which does happen - or you have quietly drawn the system you wish you had. Only one of those is any use to the person who has to decide.

T5: The Guardrails

The bow-tie is simple to draw and easy to draw dishonestly - usually by accident. Glance through these before you present one.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Putting the disaster in the middle. The top event is the loss of control, not the harm. Write "people injured" as your top event and the right-hand side has nothing left to say.

• Counting barriers that exist only on paper. An untested valve, an uncalibrated detector, a plan nobody has drilled - these are hopes, not barriers. If you cannot say when it was last proven to work, do not draw it as solid.

• Hiding the gaps to look competent. The single worst mistake, because it destroys the entire purpose of the picture. You are not being marked on how few holes you have; you are asking someone to help you close one.

• Drowning it in detail. Forty threats in six-point type is the risk register again, in a new shape. Tune the detail to the audience - the method is designed for exactly that.

• Frightening people instead of informing them. Fear produces panic or dismissal, and neither is a decision. Show the controls that are working as clearly as the one that is missing; that is what makes the ask credible.

• Leaving off the brief. A beautiful diagram with no request attached gets admired and filed. The picture shows the gap; the brief asks for the decision.

Onalenna's analysis was right all along - what failed was the journey from her screen to somebody else's understanding, and the gap stayed open because nobody could see it. Now you hold the picture that carries it across: name the moment control is lost, show what could cause it and what stands in the way, show what would follow and what would limit it, be honest about what could defeat a barrier, and put the gap and the ask in plain words underneath. Do that, and the risks you can see stop being private knowledge and start being decisions - which is, in the end, the only way a barrier ever actually gets built.
