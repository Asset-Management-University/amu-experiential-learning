Step 1 of 5

A water pipeline came in R130 million over, three years late, and the two poorest villages are still dry - and 'incompetence' explains none of it. How do I show who had the power, what the rules allowed, and where the money leaked?

T1: The Ache

You are standing in a site office on the edge of Motseng, a dusty town in the foothills of the escarpment. On the desk is a thick bound report: the Motseng Bulk Water Pipeline - Project Close-Out, Year 5. You are the new asset planner for the Motseng Regional Water Board, and your manager has asked you one question for the board meeting on Monday.

The pipeline was meant to bring clean water from the Motseng dam to 42,000 people in four villages. It was budgeted at R180 million and planned for three years. The report says it came in at R310 million and took six years. Worse: two of the four villages still have no water. The pipes reach them, but the booster pump station that was supposed to push water up the last hill was never built. The line item for it - R22 million - was quietly moved, in Year 3, to "additional contractor mobilisation".

You flip to the contractor list. The main civil contract went to a firm owned by the brother-in-law of the councillor who chaired the project steering committee. Your stomach tightens. A voice says: just write that the contractor was incompetent and the budget was poorly managed, and move on. That is the easy answer. It would fit on one slide.

But it is not the real answer. "Bad management" does not explain why the pump station - the one part that served the two poorest villages, the two with the fewest votes - was the exact thing that got cut. It does not explain why nobody objected. The pattern is too neat to be an accident. The money did not just disappear; it went somewhere, and the decisions that moved it were made by people who gained from making them.

The easy answer is 'incompetence', and it explains nothing. How do I take this project apart and show who had power, what they wanted, what the rules let them get away with, and where the money leaked - clearly enough to survive a hard question in the boardroom?

Before you read on, decide. The cut fell on the pump station — the one part serving the two poorest villages, the two with the fewest votes — and nobody objected. A voice says: write that the contractor was incompetent and the budget was poorly managed, and move on. It would fit on one slide. Write down what you would put in the report. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one protects

• “Poor management and an incompetent contractor.” It fits on a slide and it explains nothing. It cannot say why the cut landed on that specific component rather than anywhere else, and it cannot say why nobody objected. Notice also who it protects: incompetence blames the work, and everybody with power keeps their position.

• “Follow the money — find out who took what.” Sometimes there is theft, and sometimes there is none and the outcome is identical. Money moves lawfully towards people who gain from moving it, through decisions that are all individually defensible — and a report that only looks for theft finds nothing and concludes all is well.

• “Say the scope was cut for budget reasons — that’s factually true.” It is true and it is the most misleading sentence available. Every scope cut is for budget reasons. The question your report exists to answer is why this cut and not another, and the neutral phrasing quietly retires it.

• “Name the people responsible.” Brave, and it will not survive the boardroom without a rule and a number behind each claim. It also aims at individuals when the pattern may be produced by what the rules permitted — and if so, replacing the people changes nothing at all.

• “Recommend better oversight next time.” Safe, unobjectionable, and it will be adopted and change nothing, because it does not say which decision it would have caught.

The observation you started with is the finding, and it is the one you must not lose: the pattern is too neat to be an accident. The waste was not random, and where it landed is the evidence.

You need a reliable, repeatable method - one you can use on the next project before it goes the same way. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is political-economy analysis - often shortened to PEA. "Political economy" just means the study of how power and money interact. A pipeline does not choose where the money goes; people do, and people respond to incentives - votes, margin, a job for a relative. PEA sits next to the technical analysis and explains the choices the engineering view cannot, by asking four plain questions: who gains, who loses, and what rules let them? Watch it run on the Motseng close-out report.

Step 1 - Frame the gap (promise vs reality)

The Motseng Bulk Water Pipeline was to bring clean water to 42,000 people in four villages, budgeted at R180 million over three years. It came in at R310 million over six - a R130 million overrun - and two of the four villages are still dry, because the booster pump station for the last hill was never built. Its R22 million was quietly moved, in Year 3, to 'additional contractor mobilisation'. Something is wrong, and it is not just the money.

Step 2 - Map the players and their incentives

Councillor Mosela (chaired the committee): re-election - the two villages with water are his vote base, the two without are not - and a family contract keeps the family loyal. The contractor (his brother-in-law's firm): maximum revenue for minimum work. The Board officials: a quiet life and a signed-off project (objecting risks their careers). The two served villages: water now, no questions. The two unserved villages: water - but few votes, no committee seat, so their want carries no weight. The shape appears: the people who wanted the pump station built had the least power in the room.

Step 3 - Name the rules (institutions), strong or weak in practice

The budget-move rule: the committee could shift a line item under R25 million without board approval if labelled 'operational' - the R22m cut was relabelled 'contractor mobilisation', just under the line. Weak; the gate that let the money move. The conflict-of-interest rule: members must declare family ties to bidders, but Mosela declared nothing and no one checked bidder ownership - on paper, not enforced. The audit rule: close-out audit only at Year 5, after all the money was spent - weak timing. The rules that should have stopped this were too loose or never enforced.

Step 4 - Find the rents (value captured), with numbers

'Additional contractor mobilisation' appeared four times across Years 2-4, totalling R48 million (including the R22m pump station) - but mobilisation is a one-time start-of-job cost, so charging it four times to the same contractor on the same job is not a real cost: R48m is the clearest rent. Rate inflation: the pipe-laying rate was R6,200/m against a neighbouring district's R4,100/m the same year; over 38 km, (6,200 - 4,100) x 38,000 = R79.8 million of excess. Together about R128m of the R130m overrun is value captured, not engineering difficulty.

Step 5 - Synthesise into one story (and the fix)

'Councillor Mosela chaired a committee able to move R25m without sign-off (weak rule) with an undeclared family tie to the contractor (rule not enforced); his incentive was re-election and family, the contractor's was margin; together they used relabelled mobilisation (R48m) and an inflated R6,200/m rate (about R80m excess) to capture value; the cut fell on the pump station - the one part serving the two villages with the fewest votes and no seat; the result: R130m over, three years late, two villages still dry. This was not incompetence.' The fix follows: close the budget-move loophole, enforce the conflict check, audit at each payment, and give the unserved a seat.

See what the method did. It took a vague, unprovable feeling - something is wrong here - and turned it into a defensible account with a player, a rule, or a number behind every claim. And see the mission in it, because this technique is justice made into a method. The deepest wrong in public infrastructure is not that money is wasted; it is that the waste is not random. The cut fell on the pump station serving the two villages with the fewest votes and no seat at the table - the failure was aimed, by the shape of who held power, at exactly the people with the least of it. PEA refuses the comfortable lie of "incompetence" because that lie protects the powerful and blames the work; it names instead who gained, which weak rule let them, and where the public's money leaked - and then it points at the fix, including the most important repair of all: a seat for the unserved. The R130 million is the measure of the capture, but the aim is water in all four villages and a system that cannot be gamed the same way twice. Used with care - it warns plainly against seeing plots where there are only honest mistakes - it is how an asset planner serves the community over the connected.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the analysis in five moves.

  • 1. Frame the gap. The promise against what happened - budget, time, and who is still unserved.
  • 2. Map the players and incentives. Every stakeholder and what they actually gain or lose.
  • 3. Name the rules. The institutions that shaped behaviour - strong or weak in practice.
  • 4. Find the rents. Where value was captured for private gain, with the arithmetic.
  • 5. Synthesise into one story. Join incentives, rules and rents into the paragraph that forces the fix.

The ideas underneath. An incentive is the thing that actually pulls a player one way - their real gain or loss, never their job title. Institutions, in this sense, are the written and unwritten rules of the game: procurement law, who may sign off a budget move, whether anyone checks a declaration; you judge each on practice, not paper, because a rule that is never enforced is a weak rule. A rent is money or value someone gets without creating equal worth in return - a tollgate on a road they did not build; rent-seeking is arranging things so money flows to you through your position rather than your work, and finding the rents shows where the money leaked and why. The synthesis joins these into one chain of cause and effect: these incentives, allowed by these weak rules, created these rents, which produced this outcome. PEA fits when an outcome looks shaped by interests, not just engineering, and you are at Stage 1 trying to understand why before proposing a fix; it does not fit a purely technical failure (a bearing wore out) or an honest mistake with no incentive behind it - reach for it there and you see plots where there are none. Match the tool to the pattern.

Two disciplines keep the analysis honest. First, write the incentive, never the job title - "the manager wanted to manage well" explains nothing; push until you have the concrete personal gain (re-election, margin, a quiet career, a loyal family), because the incentive is the engine and the title is just the label on it. Second, put a number on every rent, or do not call it one - "corruption" is an accusation a board can dismiss, but "the same one-time mobilisation fee billed four times, R48 million" is a fact it cannot; the arithmetic is what turns a suspicion into a finding, and a finding into a fix. And hold the warning beside both: name capture only where the pattern and the numbers are real, never where an honest mistake will do.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your analysis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The incentive, not the title. Write what each player personally gains or loses, never their role. The gain is what moved the money; the title just sat in the room.

• Judge rules on practice, not paper. A conflict-of-interest rule nobody checks is a weak rule. Ask of each: did it actually bite here?

• Every rent carries a number. "Corruption" is dismissable; "R48m of repeated one-time fees" is not. Show the arithmetic or do not make the claim.

• Do not see plots in honest mistakes. PEA is for outcomes shaped by interests, not for a worn bearing or a single good-faith error. Match the tool to the pattern.

• End with the fix, and a seat. The point is not to expose rot but to repair the system - close the loophole, enforce the check, audit each payment, and give the unserved a place at the table.

You came in with a thick report and a feeling you could not defend. You can leave with one paragraph: the players and their incentives, the weak rules that let them, the rents with their numbers, and the outcome they produced - every claim anchored, the whole thing fitting on one slide, pointing straight at the loophole to close and the seat to give. This was not incompetence. It was the predictable outcome of who held power and which rules let them use it - named clearly enough to fix. That is how the public's money is defended and the unserved are finally counted: not by crying "corruption" into a room that can ignore it, but by laying down a chain of cause and effect that no hard question can break.
