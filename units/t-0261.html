Step 1 of 5

Four events, four different people

T1: The Ache

You are Ntsika, still on placement, and you have been asked to write up an incident.

A balance tank overflowed at two in the morning. Forty minutes, all of it into the containment bund, nobody hurt and nothing outside the site. The operator started the standby pump when the alarm came in, and the standby pump was running against a closed discharge valve.

Somebody in the meeting has already said what everybody is thinking: he should have checked the valve.

And it is true. He should have.

Then you go and look at the history, because you are new and you do not know that people usually do not, and you find that this is the fourth time in three years. Four events. Four write-ups. All four closed as operator error. All four followed by retraining.

Four different operators.

You sit with that for a while. Because if the cause were a person, it would be the same person.

The obvious answer is true and it has been the answer four times and the plant keeps doing this. How do I write a report that gets past the person and reaches the thing that keeps producing this?

Before you read on, decide. The balance tank overflowed at 02:14. The operator acknowledged the alarm, started the standby pump from the panel, and did not discover for seventeen minutes that its discharge valve was closed. Operator error, plainly, and the draft write-up says so. Then, because you are new enough not to know that people usually do not, you look at the history: fourth time in three years. Four events, four write-ups, all closed as operator error, all followed by retraining. Four different operators. You also notice, without making anything of it, that the panel shows the pump running but not whether it is delivering, and that the valve is routinely left shut after the monthly test. Write down how you would write this report — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the sentence you are circling

• “Report it as operator error — it is what happened.” It is true, and it has been true three times, and each time it was followed by an action that did not work. An explanation that is accurate and has failed three times is not the cause of anything.

• “Retrain the operator, and this time properly.” The fourth application of the intervention that failed after the first, second and third. It also teaches four capable people that the plant's answer to this is that they were the problem.

• “Write a stronger procedure for the monthly test.” Closer to something real, and it is still an instruction competing with a habit, on a night shift, against a panel that will show the same reassuring thing.

• “Say it was a training gap across the team.” Widens the blame from one person to four rather than moving it off people at all, and produces a team-wide session about something none of them did not know.

• “Note it as human error with contributing factors.” The form of words that lets everything close. “Contributing factors” is where the actual cause goes to be acknowledged and not acted on.

• Hold the sentence you are circling: if the cause were a person, it would be the same person. Then write the report in a way that can reach past them. Start with a timeline and nothing else — times and observable actions, no adjectives — because everything that goes wrong in these reports goes wrong in the first paragraph. Failed to, unfortunately, should have: each is a conclusion smuggled into the evidence, and once one is in the timeline the rest of the document is arguing rather than investigating. Check the history before you analyse the event, because one occurrence invites a story about a person and a pattern across four refutes it. Keep asking why past the point where a person appears: what made that action the reasonable thing at the time, given what could be seen from where they stood — a panel showing running but not delivering, and a valve routinely left shut. Treat the operator as the expert they are; they were there, and an investigation that positions them as the fault forfeits its only first-hand account. And write actions that change the plant rather than the people: an indication that cannot be misread, or a valve that cannot be left closed.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the report - the timeline without adjectives, the history checked first, the whys chained past the person, the operator treated as the expert they are, and actions that change the plant.

1. Write what happened, in order, with no adjectives and no blame - just the sequence and the times

Start with a timeline and nothing else, because everything that goes wrong in these reports goes wrong in the first paragraph. 02:14 - high-level alarm on the balance tank. 02:16 - operator acknowledges the alarm and starts the standby transfer pump from the panel. 02:19 - level continues to rise. 02:24 - operator goes out to the pump house. 02:31 - finds the standby pump running with the discharge valve closed. 02:33 - opens the valve; level begins to fall. 02:40 - level normal. Approximately 40 minutes of overflow to the containment bund. Now read what I have just written and notice what is not in it. No 'failed to check'. No 'unfortunately'. No 'should have'. Every one of those words is a conclusion smuggled into the evidence, and once one is in the timeline the rest of the report is arguing rather than investigating. Write times and actions and readings. Nothing else. If you do not know something, write that you do not know it - 'the discharge valve position before 02:31 is not recorded' is a real finding, and pretending you know is how a report becomes fiction that people rely on.

2. Before analysing anything, check whether this has happened before - and to whom

Do this before you analyse anything, because it will change what you are analysing. I searched three years of records for the same event. It has happened four times. Now the number that decides this report: the four events involved four different operators. If the cause were a person, it would be the same person. Four different, competent, separately trained people, doing the same thing on the same plant, and arriving at the same outcome - that is not four people making a mistake. That is a plant that produces this outcome, and it has now demonstrated it four times. And look at what was concluded each time. All four were closed as operator error. All four resulted in retraining: four sessions, two hours each, six people each time - 48 hours of training. Against 44 hours of lost production across the four events. We have now spent more time retraining people for this than the failures themselves have cost us, and it has happened four times, so the retraining is not working. That sentence is not an attack on anybody. It is a measurement, and it is the most useful thing in the report.

3. Ask why until you are past the person - and keep going while the answer is still about somebody's choice

Now the chain, and the rule is simple: while the answer is still about what a person chose, you have not finished. Why did the tank overflow? The standby pump was running against a closed discharge valve. Why was the valve closed? It is left closed after the monthly test, and has been for years. Why does nobody open it? The test procedure does not say to. Why did the operator not notice? - and stop here, because this is where the first investigation ended and it is not deep enough. Keep going. What would have shown them? The panel shows the pump as running; there is no flow indication and no valve position on the panel at all. Why not? The flow transmitter was removed in a modification in year two and never replaced. Why was it not replaced? It was on a list that was closed when the project closed. Now you are somewhere useful. The first analysis had three steps and ended at a person. This one has seven and ends at a missing instrument and a procedure that does not close a valve. The person is in the chain - they are the one who could not see - but they are the point at which the plant became unreadable, not the point at which it went wrong.

4. Treat the person who was there as your best source - not as your subject

The operator who was on shift knows more about this than anybody you will speak to, and how you ask decides whether you get it. Ask about the work, not about the incident: what normally happens when that alarm comes in, what you check first, what usually causes it. Not 'why did you not check the valve', which is a question with an accusation folded inside it, and which will get you a defensive answer from somebody who is now protecting themselves rather than helping you. What mine produced. That the high-level alarm on that tank comes in perhaps twice a week and is nearly always a float issue, so the reasonable first move is to start the standby and watch - which is what the plant has taught everybody to do, and which was the right move on the evidence available. That going out to the pump house takes eleven minutes each way at night. And that two of the other three operators had found the same valve closed on other occasions and mentioned it, and there was nowhere to put that. That last one is the finding of the entire investigation and it came from asking somebody about their job. Write it into the report as evidence, in their words, and attribute it to the role rather than the name.

5. Write actions that change the plant, each with one owner and a date - and none that say be more careful

An action that asks somebody to be more careful is not an action. It is a wish, and it is what all four previous reports produced. Test every action you write with one question: could this be satisfied by somebody trying harder? If yes, rewrite it. Mine. Amend the monthly test procedure to require the discharge valve to be reopened and the position confirmed - owner, the maintenance planner, by the end of next month. Reinstate flow indication on the standby pump at the panel - owner, the instrument engineer, with a business case if it exceeds his authority. Add valve position to the panel display for both transfer pumps - same owner, same route. Create a route for an operator observation that is not an incident - because two people saw this coming and had nowhere to put it - owner, the operations manager. Four actions, four named owners, four dates, and not one of them can be satisfied by a person trying harder. Compare the action taken four times before: retrain the operators. That one can only be satisfied by people trying harder, which is why it was available, and why it was chosen, and why we are here for the fifth time.

Box 2 is where this report becomes different from the previous four, and box 5 is where it either changes something or joins them. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A root cause report is not an account of what went wrong. It is an instrument for changing a plant - and it fails the moment it arrives at a person, because a person is where an investigation can comfortably stop. Five moves:

  • Timeline, no adjectives. Times, actions, readings. "Failed to" and "unfortunately" are conclusions smuggled into the evidence.
  • Check the history before you analyse. Four events, four different operators. If the cause were a person, it would be the same person.
  • Chain the whys past the person. Keep going while the answer is still somebody's choice. Three steps ends at an operator; seven ends at a missing instrument.
  • The operator is your best source. Ask about the work, not about the incident.
  • Actions that change conditions. One owner, one date, and none that a person could satisfy by trying harder.

The number that carries the report. Four events. Four different operators. That single comparison does more work than any analysis technique in this unit, and it takes twenty minutes in the maintenance history. Four separately trained, competent people, doing the same job on the same plant, arriving at the same outcome - that is not four mistakes. That is a plant that produces this outcome, and it has now demonstrated it four times.

The arithmetic of the response so far. Four retraining sessions, two hours each, six people each time: 48 hours of training. Against 44 hours of lost production across the four events. More time has now been spent retraining people for this than the failures have cost - and it has happened four times, so the retraining is not working. That is not an attack on anybody. It is a measurement, and it is the most persuasive sentence in the report precisely because it is arithmetic rather than opinion.

Where the person belongs in the chain. Not absent - that would be dishonest, and a report that pretends nobody was there is not trusted by anybody who was. The operator is in the chain. They are the point at which the plant became unreadable: the panel showed the pump running, there was no flow indication and no valve position, and eleven minutes of walking separated them from the only place the truth was visible. That is a description of a plant, not a verdict on a person, and it is also more accurate.

The test that kills a bad action. Could this be satisfied by somebody trying harder? "Retrain the operators" can only be satisfied that way - which is why it is always available, why it was chosen four times, and why nothing changed. "Reinstate flow indication at the panel" cannot be satisfied by anybody trying harder, and that is what makes it an action.

An honest boundary. This produces a report that reaches systemic conditions and proposes changes. It does not decide whether those changes get funded - that is the next document. It is not a formal incident investigation under a regulator's process, which has its own required form and people trained to run it; your report may feed one. And it does not settle questions of accountability, which are somebody else's to weigh and are not what this instrument is for.

T4: Your Turn

A different incident, and a harder one, because this time there is only one event.

ItemDetail
What happenedA lifting operation dropped a 300 kg valve about 1.5 m onto a walkway. Nobody was under it. Minor damage to the grating
Immediate findingThe sling was rated at 250 kg. The rigger selected it
The historySearched. No similar event in five years
The riggerFourteen years' experience. Certified. Has never had an incident
What he saysHe knew the valve was heavy. The 250 kg sling was the only one on the rack. The correct slings are kept in the stores cage, which closes at 16:00. The lift was at 17:20 during a breakdown
What the first draft says"Root cause: incorrect sling selection by the rigger. Action: retrain riggers on sling selection and rating."
  1. The history is empty. Your best evidence in the worked example was four events and four people. You do not have that. Say what you use instead, and why the empty history does not mean this is a one-off caused by one person.
  2. Chain the whys from "the valve was dropped" until you are past the person. Show each step.
  3. Test the draft's action. Apply the one question. What does it tell you?
  4. The rigger's account contains the whole investigation. Identify the three separate systemic conditions inside it.
  5. Write three actions with owners, none of which can be satisfied by somebody trying harder.
  6. The uncomfortable part. The rigger did use a sling below the rated load, knowingly. Say how the report handles that honestly - without either hiding it or making it the root cause.

Work it before you turn over. The point of this one is that you will usually be given one event and an obvious answer.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. Without a history. You use the conditions instead of the repetitions. Ask: would a different competent person, in this situation, have done something different? A rigger at 17:20 during a breakdown, with the correct slings locked away and one sling on the rack, faces the same three choices whoever they are - use what is there, stop the job, or find a key. An empty history does not prove the conditions are safe; it may only mean this is the first time all three lined up. You are not looking for a pattern in the past. You are testing whether the situation reliably produces the outcome.

2. The chain. Why was it dropped? The sling was below the load. Why was that sling used? It was the only one on the rack. Why was it the only one? The correctly rated slings are in the stores cage. Why were they not available? The cage closes at 16:00. Why was the lift after 16:00? It was a breakdown, and breakdowns do not observe stores hours. Why did the job proceed rather than stop? - and here is the real one - because stopping a breakdown repair at 17:20 has a visible cost that somebody will ask about, and using an available sling has no visible cost at all until it fails. Six steps, ending at an access arrangement and an incentive, not at a rigger.

3. The test. "Retrain riggers on sling selection." Could that be satisfied by somebody trying harder? Yes. So it is not an action. And note the second problem: he already knew. He knew the valve was heavy and he knew the rating. Training somebody in what they already know is not merely weak, it is evidence the analysis stopped early.

4. Three systemic conditions in his account: (a) rated lifting equipment is behind a door that closes before the shift that most needs it; (b) the rack holds an under-rated sling with nothing preventing its use for a job it cannot carry; (c) stopping a breakdown repair is costly and visible, while proceeding with what is to hand is neither. Every one of those existed before he arrived at work.

5. Three actions. Provide out-of-hours access to rated lifting equipment - owner, the stores manager, with a lockbox or an after-hours key held by the shift supervisor. Remove the 250 kg sling from that rack, or mark and segregate slings by rating so that reaching for the wrong one requires a deliberate act - owner, the lifting equipment inspector. Give the shift supervisor explicit authority to stop a breakdown job for equipment availability, in writing, and record when it is used - owner, the maintenance manager. None can be satisfied by anybody trying harder.

6. The uncomfortable part, handled honestly. Write it plainly and once: "The sling used was rated below the load, and the rigger was aware of the rating." It goes in the timeline, as fact, without adjectives. It is not the root cause and it is also not omitted - a report that leaves it out is not honest, and everybody who was there knows it happened, so leaving it out costs the report its credibility with exactly the people it needs. Then the analysis continues past it, and the report says why: a decision that four other competent people would have faced identically, under conditions that made it the reasonable-seeming option, is a condition rather than a choice. Naming what somebody did is not the same as blaming them for it. The difference is whether you stop there.

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Writing the timeline with adjectives and verdicts already built in. "Failed to" and "unfortunately" smuggle a conclusion into what should be plain evidence - times, actions, readings, nothing else.

• Stopping the whys at the first person mentioned. A person is where an investigation can comfortably stop. Keep chaining while the answer is still somebody's choice.

• Skipping the history check. If the same failure has happened to different people, the cause is not a person - check the history before you analyse anything.

• Writing an action a person could satisfy just by trying harder. "Retrain the operators" is always available, which is why it gets chosen. An action that does not change a condition does not change the outcome.

What you can do now that you could not before. Build a timeline with no verdicts in it. Check the history before you analyse. Chain the whys past the person to a condition somebody can change. Ask an operator about their work rather than about their mistake. And write actions that a plant has to satisfy rather than a person.

The operator on the night of the overflow did what the plant had taught everybody to do, on the evidence the panel gave him. Two of his colleagues had seen the same valve closed and had mentioned it, and there was nowhere for that to go.

Four events and four different people is not a coincidence you have to explain. It is the finding, and it was in the records the whole time.
