Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, founder of Tank You. A customer has just called, unhappy: they ordered a 9kg gas cylinder for their heater, but your driver delivered a big 19kg one that doesn't fit. A hot flash of anger and worry - this looks careless, unprofessional. Your first instinct is to find out who did this. You check the schedule: it was Sipho. Sipho - your most trusted, experienced driver. You feel let down, and you want to make an example of him so it never happens again.

You call the drivers together, ready to come down hard. But as they gather, something's wrong: the usual chat is gone, everyone's staring at their boots. Thabo, who last week cheerfully told you about a near-miss at a tricky gate, suddenly has nothing to say. The room is cold and silent. And a chilling thought lands: the harder I push to find someone to blame, the less anyone will tell me. You can find a culprit, or you can find the cause - but it's dawning on you that you can't have both.

I need to stop this happening again - but my own hunt for who to blame is making the truth disappear. How do I get the truth so I can actually fix it?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your mind races, and each path has a trap. You could come down hard on Sipho to "send a message" - and feel a moment of control. But the message the team actually learns is: honesty is dangerous here; if you slip, hide it; if you see a problem, keep quiet. Sipho, a good man, is crushed - and the real reason for the mistake stays hidden in the system, waiting for the next driver. You could swing the other way and let it go - "mistakes happen" - keeping everyone comfortable but learning nothing, so the customer's still stuck and the same slip recurs. Or do nothing and hope.

Each one fails your mission: one attacks the truth-teller, the other ignores the truth. So before you read on: what does "making an example of someone" actually do to the one thing you most need - the truth - and to the real cause underneath?

Something went wrong - the wrong cylinder delivered - and my instinct is to find WHO did it and make an example. Coming down hard teaches everyone that honesty is dangerous (so the truth dries up and the real cause survives); shrugging it off teaches nothing (so it recurs). You can find a culprit, or find the cause - but not both. What's the question that gets you the truth?
Ask 'why could this happen?', not 'who did it?'. Run the substitution test: would a normally careful person, in that exact situation, likely have done the same? If yes, the situation is the cause - find the built condition (tiny labels, a rushed schedule, bad info) and fix it. Make telling the truth safe, even rewarded. And hold the honest line: this is no blame for honest people in a broken system, NOT no consequences ever - reckless and deliberate acts still answer.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You take a breath and look at Sipho not as a culprit but as your best source of information. You change the question in your head from "who did this?" to "how could this happen?". Five moves:

  • See the pull to blame for what it is: finding one person to name feels like justice and closure - simple, satisfying, done. But it's a shortcut to a dead end: it leaves untouched every condition the business built around that person, so the same failure returns wearing a different name. Naming a culprit is not the same as removing a cause.
  • Blame burns the truth: how you respond teaches everyone watching what honesty costs. Punish the truth-teller and witnesses go quiet, near-misses stop being reported, evidence quietly vanishes - so the NEXT time something goes wrong you'll have even less to work with. Truth is the only raw material an investigation has, and blame is the fire that burns it.
  • Ask 'why could this happen?', not 'who did it?' - and run the substitution test: change the question. Walk through what really happened, then ask the test that matters: 'would another normally careful person, in that exact situation, likely have done the same thing?' If yes, the person isn't the cause - the situation is. Go find the condition the business built (the near-identical labels, the rushed schedule, the wrong list, the missing check).
  • Fix the conditions and make truth safe: separate the honest person from the broken conditions, and repair the conditions so the next person can't fall into the same trap. Then make honesty safe - even rewarded: thank people for reporting, and treat mistakes and near-misses as gifts that show you where to get stronger. Do this and future investigations get richer, not poorer (this is bp-0133's 'fix the system, not the worker', made into a way of responding).
  • Hold the honest line: make it crystal clear: this is no blame for an HONEST person caught in a broken system - it is NOT 'no consequences ever'. Deliberate sabotage and reckless rule-breaking still answer for it. The skill - and the fairness - is telling an honest error (fix the system) from a reckless choice (hold to account). Fairness cuts both ways.

Watch it work. You ask Sipho to walk you through his morning: the schedule was tight; at the depot the 9kg and 19kg cylinders stand side by side; their labels are nearly identical - same colour, same design, just a tiny "9" or "19" - and in the rush he grabbed the wrong one. You run the test: would another careful driver, rushed, with poor light and two near-identical cylinders stored together, likely have done the same? Plainly, yes - so the cause was the situation, not Sipho. Had you punished him, you'd never have learned about the labels or the storage, and next time no one would dare tell you anything. So you fix the conditions - bright yellow labels for 9kg, blue for 19kg, different sizes in different bays - and you thank Sipho in front of the team for the honesty that exposed a trap that could have caught anyone, with a new rule: "bring me your mistakes and near-misses - they're gifts; they show us where to get stronger." And you hold the honest line: this is mercy for honest error in a broken system, not "no consequences ever" - deliberate or reckless danger still answers. Ask why this could happen, not who did it; truth is the only raw material an investigation has, and blame burns it.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Blame burns the truth in any setting - not just a depot. Watch it on a hospital ward.

See the same choice on a hospital ward

A dedicated nurse, Zanele, is caring for a very sick patient when she realises a routine tablet for another, stable patient is ten minutes late. She gives it, and - following the rules - honestly reports the small delay.

• The blame response: her manager fixes only on the error and gives her a formal warning for a "medication timing error". Zanele feels punished for being honest; every nurse hears the story, and the unspoken rule becomes never report a mistake unless you must. Weeks later a more serious error happens - and the nurse stays silent, terrified. The hidden danger grows.

• The "why?" response: a better manager asks "why was the dose late?", and learns about the sicker patient who needed urgent care. The substitution test - would another good nurse, facing that exact choice, have prioritised the sicker patient? - says yes. The cause isn't Zanele; it's the workload. The manager fixes the real issue (staffing, patient assignments) and thanks Zanele for the honesty that keeps the whole ward safer.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of the last time something went wrong in your world - at work, in your family, in a team you're part of - and remember that hot, instinctive pull to find out who was responsible. Now rewind, and respond differently:

  1. Ask 'why could this happen?', not 'who did it?'. Talk to the person at the sharp end to understand, not to accuse - they're your best source of the truth, not your target.
  2. Run the substitution test, then fix the condition. "Would a careful, well-meaning person, in their exact shoes, have possibly done the same?" If yes, you haven't found a culprit to blame - you've found a broken condition to fix (a confusing instruction, a poor tool, too little time, bad information). Fix it so the next person can't fall in.
  3. Make truth safe - and hold the honest line. Thank people when they tell you hard things; that's how you earn the information you need. And keep the line clear: this finds causes, it doesn't excuse deliberate recklessness.

The pull to find a culprit is human - it feels like justice, and it's quick. But it buys a moment's satisfaction at the price of the one thing that could actually keep people safe: the truth. Every honest person you protect, and every broken condition you fix, makes the next person's work safer and the next truth easier to tell. Blame serves the ego; learning serves the mission. Choose to learn.
