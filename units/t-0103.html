The safety net you cannot see

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. It is 2am, and the main pump has just tripped. But you are calm - because Corathyl has a standby pump for exactly this moment. Its entire job is to take over in an emergency. You reach over and hit its start button.

Nothing.

The standby pump is dead. And not dead just now - it has been dead for months. It failed quietly, sitting there idle, and nobody knew, because nobody ever tested it. The one device whose whole purpose was to save you in an emergency had failed silently, and its failure stayed invisible until the precise moment you needed it - and now the town's water is draining away while you stare at a button that does nothing.

This is a hidden failure. It is the opposite of the main pump, whose failure was evident - it stopped, alarms rang, you knew instantly. A hidden failure gives no warning at all. You cannot wait for it to announce itself, because it never will. You have to go looking for it.

My safety net failed silently months ago, and I only found out the instant I fell. I cannot see a hidden failure coming - so how often must I test the thing, to be sure it will be there when I need it?

Before you read on, decide. The standby pump exists for one reason: to take over when the main pump stops. Last week the main pump stopped, and the standby did not start — it had been dead for months, and nothing had indicated it. From the records, a standby like this suffers a hidden failure roughly once every 50 months. Works Management has decided it must be available at least 99% of the times it is called on. Write down how often you would test it, and how you would defend that answer — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why this failure is a different species

• “Test it monthly — that feels about right.” It may well be about right, and you cannot defend it, cannot adjust it when the standby is replaced with a more reliable one, and cannot explain to anybody why not quarterly. A custom is not an answer; it is an answer somebody else worked out once and nobody has revisited.

• “Put a sensor on it so we know if it fails.” Sometimes possible and it moves the problem rather than solving it: now something else can fail silently, and you are back to asking how often to check that.

• “Service it on the same schedule as the main pump.” The main pump's interval is built from wear during operation. This one sits idle, and it fails from sitting — corrosion, seizure, a flat battery — which is a different mechanism at a different rate. Borrowing the number borrows the wrong number.

• “Test it more often — it is critical, so weekly to be safe.” Every test costs, and some tests carry their own risk. “More is safer” with no number attached is how testing regimes become unaffordable and then get quietly abandoned, which leaves you worse off than a defensible interval you can actually keep.

• “Fit a second standby.” Now you have two things that can fail silently, and no more idea about either.

• What makes this hard is that every instinct you have is trained on failures that announce themselves. The main pump stopping was loud and instant. This one gives nothing at all — its whole job is to do nothing until called, so failure and health look identical from outside, and no amount of attentiveness would ever have caught it. Since it will never tell you, the only question is how often you go and look, and that has an answer built from exactly two things: how often it fails while sitting idle — a number from your own records, and not the same number as its wear-out rate in service — and how much unavailability has been agreed as tolerable, which is not an engineering quantity at all but a decision somebody with authority has to make and own. Both are already in front of you. The interval falls out of them, it moves sensibly when either changes, and you can explain it to anybody who asks.

There is a simple, rigorous way to answer exactly that "how often". Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the one-page worksheet Onalenna built the next morning, so this never happens again - the calculation that turns "how often should we test it?" from a guess into a number.

1. The Hidden Function - a protective job you cannot see fail

Standby pump SBP-02: provides emergency water flow when the main pump goes down. It sits idle - so you cannot tell it has failed just by looking; only a real test, or the emergency itself, reveals it.

2. MTBF - how often does it fail, sitting on standby?

From the maintenance records: it suffers a hidden failure on average once every 50 months. (Use its STANDBY failure rate - corrosion, seizure, a flat battery - not its running wear-out rate; they are different numbers.)

3. Tolerable Unavailability (U) - how much risk can we accept?

Works Management decides the standby must be available at least 99% of the time it is called on. So the most we will tolerate finding it failed is 1% = 0.01.

4. The Calculation - FFI = 2 x MTBF x U

FFI = 2 x 50 months x 0.01 = 1.0 month. (Notice the logic: more failures - a smaller MTBF - or less tolerance for risk - a smaller U - makes the interval shorter; you must test more often.)

5. The Answer, and a sense-check

Test the standby pump functionally every 1 month. Sense-check: the average chance of finding it failed = FFI / (2 x MTBF) = 1 / 100 = 1% - exactly our target. Not a guess - a calculated, defensible interval.

Five steps, one multiplication, and the guesswork is gone. The standby's own failure history and the level of risk the works can accept together decide the test interval - here, once a month. Not "whenever someone remembers"; not "once a year because that felt about right" - a calculated, defensible every month. Now let us walk through why it works.

T3: The Blueprint

That number has a name: the Failure-Finding Interval, or FFI. It is the rigorous maths that proper Reliability-Centred Maintenance (the global standards SAE JA1011 / JA1012) brings to hidden functions - the standby pumps, fire pumps, safety valves and alarms that sit silent until a crisis calls on them.

Build the logic by feeling the need for each piece before you name it:

  • First: how reliable is this thing - how often does it tend to fail just sitting there, waiting? That is its Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) - here, once every 50 months.
  • Then: what risk can we live with - the absolute most we could tolerate the chance of finding it already dead when we need it? That is the Tolerable Unavailability (U) - here, 1 in 100, or 0.01.
  • Finally: knowing how often it fails and the risk we will accept, how often must we check it? That is the Failure-Finding Interval (FFI) itself.

And the formula that ties them together is just one line:

FFI = 2 x MTBF x U

Read the sense in it: if the device fails more often (a smaller MTBF), or you can tolerate less risk (a smaller U), the interval gets shorter - you must test more often. (One honest note on the maths: this trusted formula assumes the chance of failure stays steady over time and that a test puts the device back to good-as-new - which is the standard, correct model for almost all standby equipment.)

Does it scale? Just as Onalenna now tests the Corathyl standby pump every month to keep it 99% certain to start, an aviation engineer calculates the test interval for an aircraft's emergency power unit, and a hospital engineer for its backup generator - the same formula, guarding the same kind of silent, life-saving safety net.

T4: Your Turn

Now find a hidden safety net in your own life and work out how often you should be checking it. Pick one: a smoke-alarm battery, the spare tyre in a car, a fire extinguisher, the backup of your files.

  1. The hidden function - what is its job, and why can't you see it fail just by looking?
  2. Estimate its MTBF - honestly, how long before you'd expect it to have quietly failed (the battery flat, the tyre soft, the backup corrupted)? In months.
  3. Decide your tolerable risk (U) - what chance of finding it dead can you live with? 10% (0.1)? 5% (0.05)? 1% (0.01)?
  4. Calculate - FFI = 2 x MTBF x U.
  5. Your answer - that is how often you should test it. Does it feel right? (If it says "test the smoke alarm every two years", your U was too generous for something that saves lives.)

T5: The Guardrails

The failure-finding interval is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust your number.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• "It's not running, so it can't fail." The deadliest trap. If you cannot tell whether a device works just by looking at it, it has hidden failure modes - and it needs a failure-finding test. Standby and safety gear fail silently most of all.

• Using it on evident failures. The FFI is only for HIDDEN functions. A running pump that stops announces itself - it does not need finding. Do not waste the calculation where the failure is already obvious.

• The wrong MTBF. Use the failure rate for the device sitting on STANDBY (seizure, corrosion, a flat battery) - not its wear-out rate while running hard. They are different numbers, and mixing them gives a dangerous interval.

• The fake test. The test must truly prove the function. Pressing the "test" button on a smoke alarm only proves the beeper works - not that it can still smell smoke. A meaningless test makes the whole interval worthless.

• Guesswork in disguise. "We've always tested it yearly" is not a reason. The FFI gives you a simple, defensible number built from the real failure rate and the real risk - use it instead of habit.

The standby that failed Onalenna at 2am did not have to. Its silence was not bad luck - it was a test that was never calculated and never done. Now you hold the small, sharp piece of maths that keeps a safety net honest: ask how often it fails, decide how much risk you can bear, and let one multiplication tell you how often to look. Do that, and the net will be there the day you finally fall into it. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, bring a hidden safety net of your own to your next session.
