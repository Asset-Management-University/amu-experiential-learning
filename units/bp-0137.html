Why do good parts keep dying young?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and one number in Ibraaheem's books will not stop climbing: repairs. Clutches especially. They burn out across the fleet far faster than they should - so you do the obvious thing and start buying the better, pricier clutches. And those die young too. You are pouring money into a hole and the hole only gets deeper.

Then, riding along with one of the newer drivers, you see it. He pulls off with his foot resting on the clutch, rides it up every hill, slams into gear, races the engine before it is warm. He is not lazy or reckless - he is working hard, hurrying to make the deliveries. He simply has no idea that the way he drives is quietly killing a R8,000 part. Why would he? Nobody ever told him. And he never sees the repair bill - that lands on your desk, a world away from his seat.

The truth turns your whole diagnosis upside down. It was never the parts.

I keep buying better parts and they keep dying. What if it's not the parts at all - but how we use them? And how do I fix that WITH my drivers, not by turning on them?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Two easy answers are sitting right there, and both are wrong. The first - buy even better parts - you have already tried; better parts just die better. The second - call the drivers in and tell them off - feels tempting, but stop and ask: did they ever actually KNOW how to look after the clutch? If the answer is no, then the gap is not their carelessness. It is something you never gave them.

Before you read on, sit with that. If buying better parts won't fix it, and blaming the drivers isn't fair, where did this really go wrong - and whose job is it to put it right?

I keep buying better clutches and they keep burning out fast. If a better part dies just as quickly, the part was never the problem. Something is destroying them - and it must be how they're being used.
My drivers aren't careless - they were never TOLD how to look after a clutch, and they never see the repair bills. How could they respect a limit nobody ever gave them? This is on me, not on them.
So the fix isn't a better part or a telling-off. It's to set the few real limits, write them in the cab, explain the WHY to the drivers as partners, and coach gently - so the right way becomes the normal way.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The leak was never in the parts, and never in your people. It was in a gap you didn't know you had left open:

  • It's rarely the parts - it's how they're USED: an asset pushed past what it was built for wears out far faster. Buying a better part just gives the same rough use something new to destroy.
  • The trap that causes it (nobody's fault): the limits were never clearly DEFINED, so they were never EXPLAINED to the people using the asset, so they cannot be respected. Habit and time-pressure fill the gap. Nobody chose this - it just grew.
  • The hidden split: the user is not the fixer: the person USING the asset almost never sees the repair bill, so the damage is invisible to the very person causing it - and lands, again and again, on whoever pays and fixes.
  • The fix is cheap, upstream - and starts with YOU: as the owner: DEFINE the few real limits simply; WRITE them where the work happens; bring the users IN as partners (explain the why, never blame - they couldn't respect a limit nobody gave them); COACH the behaviour, don't just count the failures; make the right way the easy way.

Read that third point again, because it is the heart of it: the one using the asset and the one repairing it are different people. The driver feels the rush of pulling off fast; he never feels the R8,000 bill three weeks later. The damage is invisible to the very person doing it. You cannot scold that gap away - you can only close it with information and a shared reason to care.

And here is the part that takes a real leader, not a boss: this is yours to own. The honest words are "I never set the limits, so I never gave you a fair chance to meet them - let's fix it together," not "why are you breaking my bakkies?" One builds a team of proud caretakers. The other builds people who hide the damage. Bring them in as the experts on their own work, and the cheapest fix you will ever make - a conversation and a small card in the cab - will outlast the most expensive part you could buy.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad does not send an angry message. He gathers the drivers, and starts by taking the blame himself.

See how he closes the gap - with the drivers, not against them

He owns it first: "This is on me. I never showed anyone how to look after these bakkies, and they're costing us a fortune in repairs. That's my mistake, not yours. I need your help to fix it."

They define the limits together - the drivers actually know the roads, so they help write it: don't ride the clutch; let her warm a minute before you push; don't load past the axle line; ease into gear. Five plain things, no jargon.

It gets written where the work happens - a small card clipped in every cab. Not a rulebook to punish by; a reminder, in their own words.

He coaches, he doesn't police - one gentle ride-along each, showing the feel of it, explaining the R8,000 nobody had ever mentioned. The drivers are stunned - they genuinely never knew.

The result: clutch life more than doubles. Repairs fall. And something better - the drivers start treating the bakkies as theirs, pointing out small problems early, proud to be trusted. The cheapest fix in the whole business.

And it is not just bakkies on a dirt road - for the engineers, the story is identical on a plant floor. A pump fails early, again and again, and the instinct is to buy a tougher pump. But an operator is running it past its rated load because the limit was never posted at the controls. Define it, post it at the panel, bring the operator in - and the "weak" pump lasts for years.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You stopped buying better parts and started closing the gap - and discovered the cheapest repair of all is a clear limit, kindly shared. This works for anything that wears out faster than it should:

  1. Suspect the USE, not just the thing. If good replacements keep failing fast, the way it's being used is the real culprit - look there before you spend again.
  2. Define the few real limits, and write them where the work happens. Not a thick manual - a handful of plain do's and don'ts, where the hands are.
  3. Bring the users in - and own your part. Explain the why, never blame (they couldn't follow a limit nobody gave them), and make the right way the easy way. If you're the only user, the limit is for you too.
R

Pick one thing that keeps failing, find the limit nobody ever set, and share it kindly this week. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

Anyone can blame the tool or the worker and keep paying. The leader who looks upstream - who realises the damage was designed in by a gap they left open, and closes it with their people rather than against them - protects both the asset and the person. That is stewardship: of things, and of human beings.
