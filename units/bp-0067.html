Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tshiamo, and you run a small, trusted print shop. Last year was painful: a big new client placed a huge order and for six solid weeks your single press ran day and night. You were swamped, and you had to turn away your regulars - your bread and butter - apologising again and again. The feeling of letting people down, of failing to serve, was sharp. "Never again," you told yourself, so you took out a loan and bought a second, much bigger, faster press.

And now this year you have the opposite pain. The big client's project is finished, and the new press - the one that cost so much - sits silent and cold three days a week, a heavy weight on your books. The money sunk into that idle machine could be paying your staff more, or lowering prices for your community, or just letting you breathe. You feel like you're always fighting the last war: you size for the pain you just felt, and by the time the new kit arrives, the world has already changed.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Reacting to the last pain feels like decisive action. So you size to the last peak - enough to handle that busiest-ever week - but that week was a rare spike, and now for the other 51 weeks you're oversized and the idle asset bleeds you dry; you solved the crisis and created chronic waste. Or you trust the brochure number - "the new press does 10,000 flyers an hour, that'll cover everything" - but in the real world, with paper changes, ink refills, warm-ups and the odd jam, it truly delivers nearer 6,500; you sized your business on a number reality never matches. Or you just guess - a competitor expands so you do too, or you hope "if you build it, they will come" - which isn't a plan, it's a wish, and the moment the future differs even slightly, you're wrong again.

You keep getting it wrong because you're steering by the last pain or a brochure fantasy. So before you read on: what two honest numbers would you need, right now, to stop guessing and start choosing your size on purpose?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You keep getting the future wrong because you never honestly estimate the need AND never honestly check what your assets can truly deliver - you're solving a two-part problem with one part of the answer, and that part is a guess. Fix both, then size the gap on purpose. Five moves:

  • See the trap - reacting to the last pain: with no forward view of the real need, you're forced to steer by the rear-view mirror: you size UP right after a shortage and DOWN right after a glut, so you swing forever between too little and too much and always arrive a war too late. Reacting to fresh pain feels decisive, but it guarantees you're solving the crisis you just had, not the one that's coming.
  • Estimate the need ahead, as a range not a point: stop reacting and start anticipating: look at real signals - your order book, seasonal patterns, talks with regulars about their plans - and project what you'll need over the period ahead. Don't invent one confident number; build a RANGE - low, likely, high (say a likely 7,000-9,000 an hour, a quiet 6,000, a rare spike of 11,000). The range is your map: it tells you what you must cover and where your risks are. (Building this honest range is the cousin of bp-0085 - there you judge a single number someone hands you; here you build the range yourself.)
  • Check your true deliverable capacity, not the brochure: put the brochure away and measure what your assets ACTUALLY produce in your real workshop, with all its interruptions - time it over a few real days. The new press the salesperson said does 10,000 an hour truly delivers ~6,500 after setups, refills, warm-ups and jams; the old one ~4,000; so your real total is ~10,500, not the much higher number the labels and brochures promised. You cannot size to a capacity you don't actually have.
  • Size the gap with deliberate headroom: now compare the two honest numbers: a likely need of 7,000-9,000 against a true capacity of ~10,500. You can see at a glance you cover the likely case and a small surge, but NOT the rare 11,000 spike - so you choose, calmly, not in panic: keep the big press for the rare spike, or sell it and plan to outsource the overflow? The space between the top of likely need (9,000) and true capacity (10,500) is your headroom - and you set it ON PURPOSE, sized to the cost of being wrong (more if turning people away is a disaster, less if idle kit is the bigger crime).
  • The cost of guessing is paid twice - so revisit: too little capacity means turning people away, a service that fails them when they need you; too much means money sunk in idle things that could have served people elsewhere - both are real costs to weigh, not just 'play it safe big' or 'run it lean'. And because the future keeps moving, you revisit the need-estimate every few months and adjust, rather than betting once and living with the guess. Sizing honestly is how you serve the people who depend on you, without waste.

See it settle Tshiamo's swing. She estimates the likely need at 7,000-9,000 an hour (a quiet 6,000, a rare spike of 11,000), measures her presses at a true 4,000 and 6,500 (not the brochure's 10,000) for ~10,500 total, and sees at a glance she covers the likely case with a little headroom but not the rare spike - so she chooses, calmly, to keep the big press only if the spike is worth it, or sell it and outsource the overflow. No more buying in panic and regretting it; a deliberate size, chosen against the cost of being wrong. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about presses - anything you must size ahead works the same way. Watch a small community school deciding how many classrooms to build.

See the same discipline sizing classrooms

The old way is the trap: after one crowded year they panic and build three rooms, then the local population shifts and a room sits empty for a decade - money that could have paid for a librarian; or after a quiet year they do nothing, and two years on they're turning children away. They also assume each room holds 30 pupils (the brochure number) when the true effective number is 24. The discipline instead:

• Estimate need (range): from local birth rates and new housing - low 220, likely 240-250, high 280.
• Check true capacity: a room works at 24, not 30 - so 10 rooms truly hold 240.
• Size the gap with headroom: true 240 only just covers the low end of likely need, so they build one more room (11 x 24 = 264) - a deliberate choice of headroom, sized to avoid the mission-killing pain of turning a child away.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think about what you must size ahead of time - vehicles for deliveries, storage for a co-op, staff for a Saturday shift, the size of a home for a growing family. You've felt the swing between "not enough" and "too much". Stop fighting the last war:

  1. Estimate your real future need as a range. Don't guess one number - find the signals and describe the future as low, likely, high.
  2. Measure what you can truly deliver. Forget the label on the box; what does your asset, space or team actually produce in a real day?
  3. Size the gap on purpose, and revisit. Compare the need range to your true delivery, choose your headroom deliberately - weighing the human cost of turning people away against the waste of idle resources - and re-check as the future updates.

Always fighting the last war was never foolishness - reacting to fresh pain is the most human thing there is, and no one ever taught you to estimate the need or to distrust the brochure; the swing was a missing habit, not a failing in you. And this was never really about predicting the future perfectly - no one can. It's about making an honest, clear-eyed choice with two true numbers and a margin you set on purpose, so that the size you land on serves the people who depend on you - enough that you needn't turn them away, lean enough that nothing idle is stolen from what could help them elsewhere. Size for the people and the purse, deliberately, and revisit with open eyes: that is how you stop losing the war, and start quietly winning it.
