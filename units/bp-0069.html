Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Lwandile, the regional engineering manager for a large logistics company, responsible for the assets at three big distribution centres in three different cities.

This week you are at the inland centre. A critical conveyor has failed, stopping a whole picking line. The local team calls this a "Priority 1" event and fills out a form, F-11B. Last week at the coastal centre a similar failure on a dock leveller was called a "Code Red" and logged on a tablet with a completely different set of questions. The third site calls it "Asset Down" and writes it in a paper logbook. One team has a brilliant, low-cost fix for a common fault on the forklifts that saves them thousands of Rand a month - and the other two have never heard of it.

When head office asks you for a simple report on "urgent maintenance spend" across your region, you spend two days phoning each site, asking them to translate their local reports into a format you can use. You feel less like a manager and more like a translator. You are running three separate businesses, not one regional operation.

Every one of my sites runs assets a completely different way - I feel like a translator, not a manager; how do I make us one organisation that learns, without crushing what each site does well?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to get everyone in a room and write one perfect, detailed manual for how everything must be done - every form, every term, every procedure standardised. You spend months on it. But when you roll it out, the sites resent it: it feels like a rulebook from a head office that does not understand their reality. They nod in the meetings, then back at the warehouse quietly go back to the old ways that work for them, and the big perfect manual gathers dust. So you try the opposite - trust the sites completely: "they know their equipment best." But nothing changes. The islands stay islands; the brilliant forklift fix never reaches the others; you still cannot compare performance, move people easily, or get a clear picture of your region. The waste and frustration continue.

A rigid central system is ignored, but no system at all means no learning and no teamwork. So before you read on: what is the right balance between a shared system and local freedom?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you realise the goal is not total uniformity - it is connection. You need to standardise the BACKBONE, not the bones: a shared spine that holds the organisation together, not a rigid straitjacket that stops it moving. You agree the few critical things that MUST be the same, and leave freedom for everything else. Five moves:

  • See the cost of islands: the breakthrough begins by naming the real cost of every site doing it its own way: you do not have one organisation, you have a collection of islands. The price is heavy and mostly invisible - no shared learning (a brilliant fix at one site never reaches the others), no comparison (you cannot tell which site is doing well or badly), no portability (a person or a part cannot move between sites without re-learning everything), and no economy of scale (everyone reinvents the same wheel and buys the same things three different ways). Get every site to agree on THIS first - that isolation is making everyone's job harder and the organisation poorer - because the cure asks something of them, and they will only give it if they feel the cost themselves.
  • Standardise the backbone, not the bones: the cure is NOT to standardise everything - it is to agree the small set of things that MUST be common for the organisation to work as one, and standardise only those. Define the backbone together: one shared language (a single set of priority names, P1/P2/P3, that mean the same everywhere); the few key data fields any breakdown report must capture; a common process for the things that cross sites, like ordering critical spares. This is the WHAT and the WHY - the shared spine. Keep it deliberately small: a backbone of a few vital, genuinely-common things gets adopted; a 'backbone' that tries to standardise every bone becomes the rigid manual nobody follows. This is bp-0158's focus-on-the-vital-few applied to a management system.
  • Leave room for local fit: this is the move that makes the system survive contact with reality: having fixed the WHAT and WHY, hand the HOW to each site. One site can keep its paper logbook, another a spreadsheet, another a tablet app - as long as each captures the agreed key fields and speaks the agreed language. Local teams know their own context, their own kit and their own constraints, and a system that respects that is used willingly; a system that dictates every detail from head office is resented and quietly ignored, no matter how good it looks on paper. The goal is a shared spine, not a straitjacket - honour the local knowledge as the asset it is, and let it adapt the HOW so the backbone actually lives.
  • Make learning flow: once the backbone is shared, the organisation can finally learn from itself - which is the whole point. Because every site now records breakdowns in the same language, the low-cost forklift fix discovered at one site is logged on the shared system, seen by the others, tried, and saves them real money in the first month. A common spine turns one site's lesson into everyone's lesson: improvements travel, performance can be compared fairly, head office can see and help instead of being flown blind, and people can move between sites and already speak the language. The system stops being paperwork and becomes the thing that compounds every good idea across the whole organisation.
  • Start small and prove it: do not try to boil the ocean with a giant system dropped from head office - it will collapse under its own weight and its own resentment. Start with ONE shared process that everyone feels the pain of: critical breakdown reporting, say. Agree its small backbone, let each site fit the HOW, and show the win - the report that took two days now takes minutes, the fix that travelled and saved money. When people SEE one shared process make their lives easier, they become eager to apply the same thinking to the next one, and the backbone grows from real, proven wins rather than from a mandate. A small system that is used and trusted beats a complete one that is ignored.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know how to do this - think about a family that loves to cook, with members in different houses.

See the backbone in a shared recipe

At first everyone makes "Grandma's famous chicken soup" their own way - sometimes great, sometimes not, and at a cousin's house it tastes nothing like home. So the family agrees a backbone for the recipe:

• the WHAT is standardised: it must use chicken, carrots and celery; the name is always "Grandma's soup"; success is measured by how many people ask for a second bowl.
• the HOW stays local: one uses a pressure cooker to save time, one roasts the vegetables first for flavour, one adds a herb from their own garden.

Now the soup is consistently good everywhere but keeps its local character - and when one person discovers that roasting the vegetables makes a huge difference, that tip spreads easily, because everyone started from the same shared WHAT. A system that connects them, without forcing them all to be identical.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own organisation. Where do you have islands - different teams or sites doing the same basic work in completely different ways, with no way to learn from each other? You do not need a giant, top-down project. You can start small.

  1. Find one shared process - one thing everyone does, like reporting a safety near-miss or requesting a new tool.
  2. Name the backbone. Ask the teams: "what are the 3-5 things about this that absolutely must be the same for all of us to work together?" - the terminology, the priority levels, the key information recorded.
  3. Protect the local HOW. Explicitly give teams freedom to run the rest the way that works for them, as long as it supports the shared backbone.
  4. Share the wins. When one team's local HOW proves a brilliant improvement, use the shared system to make it visible to everyone.
R

Name your islands, the one shared process you would start with, and the 3-5 backbone things that must be common. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Define the backbone of one shared process across your sites together.)

This was never about taking control away from your skilled people on the ground - their local knowledge is exactly the asset worth protecting, and a backbone is what lets one person's good idea reach everyone instead of dying on its own island. You are not building a rulebook; you are building the shared spine that turns a scattered collection of sites into one organisation that learns from itself, and makes everyone's work clearer and lighter.
