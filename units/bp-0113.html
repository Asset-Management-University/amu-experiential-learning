Caught between breakdowns and wasted money

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and Tank You now runs a small fleet - a few bakkies, all of them out earning every day. And keeping them running is quietly driving you mad.

Last month you paid a big bill for a full service on the newest bakkie - belts, filters, parts swapped out "to be safe", most of them perfectly fine. Money well spent, you told yourself. Then on Tuesday, the OTHER bakkie's clutch died in the middle of a delivery run - no warning, a furious customer, Sipho scrambling to rescue the load. A part you had not thought about, gone, at the worst moment.

And it hits you that you keep losing the same two ways. When you DON'T service something, it ambushes you. When you DO, you often spend good money on parts that had plenty of life left. You are pouring money in at both ends - and STILL getting caught out. Every choice is a guess, and the guessing is bleeding you.

I'm ambushed when things break, and I waste money when I try to prevent it. How do I know WHAT to look after, and WHEN - instead of just guessing?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You have only ever had two settings: fix-it-when-it-breaks, or service-everything-just-in-case. Both feel responsible, and both keep failing you - because both are guessing. The newest bakkie and the oldest do not wear the same way; the wiper blades and the clutch do not deserve the same treatment. Treating them all alike is the mistake.

Before you read on, think: you have a fleet and a history of breakdowns sitting in your memory (and on a pile of invoices). How could that history tell you what to do - and would EVERY part get the same plan?

I keep guessing. Either I wait for things to break (and get ambushed), or I service everything just in case (and waste money on parts that were fine). Both are guessing. Maybe the answer is to stop guessing and start KNOWING.
If I wrote down every breakdown - what failed, when, what it cost - I'd start to see patterns. Some parts fail randomly and cheaply; some wear out on a clock; some warn me before they go. They can't all need the same treatment.
So I match each part to its own plan. But FIRST: is it dangerous or mission-stopping if it fails? If yes, I can never just let it break. Only after that safety question do I choose: let it run, service on a schedule, or watch for warning signs.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The way out is to stop guessing and let your own history tell you the truth - then give each part the plan it actually needs. This is exactly how the professionals run fleets, factories, even power stations:

  • Stop guessing - keep a failure log: write down what breaks, when, and what it costs. A cheap notebook is enough. The pattern you can't see in your head appears on the page.
  • FIRST GATE - safety and mission (never skip this): before anything else, ask: would this part failing put someone in DANGER, or stop the work entirely? If YES, you may NEVER let it run to failure - it must be Scheduled or Watched. (Cheap does not mean safe to skip - a brake part is cheap AND life-critical.)
  • RUN-TO-FAILURE - for the cheap and harmless: if a part is cheap, non-critical, and fails with no danger, the smartest, cheapest plan is to simply use it until it breaks, then replace it fast. Don't waste effort 'preventing' what is harmless.
  • SCHEDULED - for predictable wear: parts that wear out on a fairly predictable clock (brake pads, oil, tyres) - replace them on an interval, BEFORE they fail.
  • WATCH-THE-SIGNS - for the costly and critical: big, expensive, mission-critical parts that warn you before they go (a new noise, heat, vibration) - watch for the warning signs and act early, before the failure.

Read the order carefully, because it matters more than anything else here: the safety gate comes FIRST. Before you ever call something "cheap, let it run to failure", you ask whether its failure could hurt someone or stop the whole operation. Brake pads are not expensive - but you would never, ever run them to failure, because the cost of that failure is measured in lives, not rands. Cheapness earns a part nothing until it has passed the safety question.

After that gate, the logic is simple and freeing: spend your maintenance effort where it actually pays. Let the harmless cheap things break. Service the predictable things on time. Watch the costly, critical things for their warning signs. No more pouring money in at both ends.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad opens a cheap notebook, writes down a few months of breakdowns, and sorts the parts - safety question first, every time.

See Muhammad sort three parts - and the same logic in a power station

Wiper blade - Safety/mission-critical? In normal conditions, no - cheap, and easy to keep a spare. → RUN-TO-FAILURE. Keep a spare in the bakkie; swap it when it goes. (But if you drive through heavy rain or thick dust, where a blade suddenly becomes vision-critical, you might move it to SCHEDULED - criticality depends on YOUR conditions, and that judgement is yours to make.)

Brake pads - Safety-critical? YES - so run-to-failure is forbidden, no matter how cheap they are. They wear on a predictable clock. → SCHEDULED. Replace on distance, before they're gone.

Engine / clutch - Mission-critical and expensive? YES. And it warns you first - a new noise, a smell, rising heat. → WATCH-THE-SIGNS. Train the drivers to report the early signals; act before it strands them.

The same three choices, scaled up (for the engineers): a power station runs a cheap standby pump to failure (there's a backup); it changes filters on a schedule; and it puts sensors on the giant, critical compressor to watch its vibration and heat - acting on the warning signs long before it fails. A bakkie or a billion-rand plant: the logic is identical.

Notice the wiper and the clutch got opposite plans - and that is the whole point. Guessing treats everything the same. Knowing gives each part exactly the care it's worth, and not a rand more.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You stopped pouring money in at both ends the moment you stopped treating every part the same. A cheap notebook and one clear order of questions turned guessing into knowing. This works for anything you depend on and have to keep running:

  1. Keep a failure log. Write down what breaks, when, and what it cost. Let the pattern show itself instead of trusting your memory.
  2. Ask the safety question FIRST. For each important part: would its failure endanger someone or stop everything? If yes, it can never be left to just break - it must be scheduled or watched.
  3. Then match the plan to the part. Cheap and harmless → run it to failure. Predictable wear → service on a schedule. Costly, critical, and it warns you → watch for the signs and act early.
R

Start the log this week, and sort one machine's parts into the three plans - safety first. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

This is the real heart of looking after valuable things - the same skill that keeps a single bakkie or a nation's power running. The person who masters it stops being a victim of breakdowns and becomes the one who decides, on purpose, exactly where their care and money go.
