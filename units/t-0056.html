Most of our pipes were laid in one push decades ago - how do I forecast the huge renewal bill before it breaks over us, and put a trustworthy number in the 30-year budget?

T1: The Ache

It is half past four on a Thursday at the Sediba Water Works, the treatment plant and reservoir yard that feeds a small town in a dry inland region. You are the asset engineer. On the desk is a thick lever-arch file - the pipe and plant register, every buried main, every pump, every reservoir the town owns.

The treasurer, Mr Brandt, stopped by an hour ago. Calm, but tired. "Every year we patch what bursts and pay for it out of that year's budget," he said. "Last year it was R3 million in emergency repairs; the year before, R1 million. I cannot plan a town on surprises. I need a number I can put in the long-term budget - and I need to trust it." He tapped the file once and left.

You look at the register and feel a cold drop in your stomach. Most of the town's water mains went in during one big push - 1975 to 1982, when the new suburbs went up. That is about 60 kilometres of pipe, all roughly the same age, all expected to last about 70 years. They were laid together. They will wear out together. The tempting thing is to take last year's repair spend, add a little for inflation, and write that flat line as the budget for every year ahead. Quick. Fits on one line. But a flat line hides the truth: those 60 kilometres will not fail evenly, a little each year - they will come due in a cluster, decades from now, a single huge wave of renewal a flat budget will never see coming.

I need one trustworthy number for the long-term budget - I cannot plan a town on surprises. But most of our pipes were laid in one push decades ago, so how do I see the huge renewal bill coming before it breaks over us?

Before you read on, decide. About sixty kilometres of the town’s water mains went in during one push between 1975 and 1982. They are expected to last about seventy years. They were laid together. He wants one number he can trust for the long-term budget. Write down how you would produce it. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one hides

• “Take last year’s repair spend and add inflation.” Quick, fits on one line, and it is the flat line that hides everything. Sixty kilometres laid together will not fail evenly, a little each year — and a flat budget is precisely the instrument that cannot see a cluster coming.

• “Base it on the current failure rate — that’s real data.” It is real, and it is a measurement of pipe that is not yet old. Today’s rate is the calm before the cohort arrives, and extrapolating it forward projects the calm rather than the wave.

• “Replace a fixed number of kilometres a year and keep it steady.” A real policy, and the question is whether the fixed number is enough — which you cannot answer without knowing what is coming due and when. Steady is a comfort, not a forecast.

• “We can’t forecast decades out — too much uncertainty.” Honest-sounding and it hands the problem to somebody who will not be able to solve it either. The install dates and the expected lives are the two things you actually do know, and a committee told nothing will save nothing.

• “Deal with it when it happens — borrow then.” It is a decision, made silently, to leave a collapsed water system and a bill to whoever is sitting in this chair in twenty years. Every year it goes unsaid is a year of saving that cannot be recovered.

Hold on to the two facts already in front of you: they were laid together, and they will wear out together. Everything else is arithmetic.

You need a forecast that runs for decades and shows the wave before it breaks, so the committee can save for it in time. Not a guess, not last year's number repeated - a method you can defend, line by line. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

What you need is a long-range capital-renewal forecast: a year-by-year estimate, running for decades, of the money needed to replace assets as they reach the end of their life. When a big group of assets was built at the same time, that forecast shows a tall hump of spending far in the future. The hump has a name - the renewal bow-wave, like the wall of water a ship pushes ahead of itself. The whole point is to see the bow-wave early, while there is still time to save for it or flatten it. Watch it built for Sediba.

Step 1 - Build the age profile (where are the lumps?)

Sediba's water mains, grouped by install period: 60 km laid 1975-82 (avg 1979, asbestos-cement, R2.0m/km); 15 km in the 1990s (uPVC, R1.8m/km); 10 km in the 2010s (HDPE, R1.6m/km). Drawn as bars, one towers over the rest - 60 km around 1979. That single tall bar is the seed of the bow-wave; the other two are small and spread out.

Step 2 - Expected useful life, adjusted by condition data

Book life for asbestos-cement main is 70 years. But the 1979 mains burst 3 -> 5 -> 8 times a year (a clear rising trend) - a sign they are ageing slightly fast - so revise their life DOWN to 65 years. The 1990s uPVC (1 burst in 5 years) keeps its 60; the 2010s HDPE (0 bursts) keeps its 80.

Step 3 - Run the life-cycle model (place each cost in its due year)

1979 mains: 1979 + 65 = due 2044, 60 km x R2.0m = R120m. 1995 uPVC: 1995 + 60 = due 2055, 15 km x R1.8m = R27m. 2014 HDPE: due 2094 (outside the 30-year horizon). Three high-lift pumps (2005, 25-yr life): due 2030, R12m. Reservoir relining (2000, 40-yr): due 2040, R18m. Summed per year: 2030 R12m, 2040 R18m, 2044 R120m, 2055 R27m - a single spike of R120m in 2044, over four times the next-biggest year. That is the bow-wave a flat budget never sees.

Step 4 - Sensitivity check, then flatten the wave

Re-run the 1979 mains: life 60 -> due 2039; 65 -> 2044; 70 -> 2049 - the R120m wave lands in the 2039-2049 band, inside 20 years either way. Saving R120m over 18 years (2026-2044) is about R6.7m a year - far more plannable than a R120m shock. And flatten it: split the 60 km into three 20 km phases in 2040, 2044 and 2048 (R40m each, worst-bursting sections first) - one unaffordable spike becomes three fundable steps.

See what the forecast did. It replaced a comforting flat line with the truth: a R120 million wave landing around 2044, far larger than any other year, that "R3 million a year" would have sailed straight into. And it did not stop at the alarm - it turned the wave into a plan: save about R6.7 million a year from now, and phase the work into three fundable steps. This is stewardship across generations: a flat budget quietly leaves a collapsed water system for whoever comes next; an honest forecast lets this committee carry its own share of the load. Patching by surprise hides a wave. Forecasting it lets you turn a future crisis into a fundable climb.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the forecast by answering four plain questions, each with a tool.

  • 1. Build the age profile. Count the assets by install year (or age band). A tall bar - many assets of the same age - is the seed of a bow-wave.
  • 2. Set the useful life, adjusted by condition. Start from the book life, then revise it with real evidence (bursts, leaks, inspection scores). Rising failures = ageing faster = revise the life down.
  • 3. Run the life-cycle model. Renewal year ≈ install year + adjusted life; cost = quantity x replacement cost. Lay every cost on the timeline and sum what lands in each year.
  • 4. Sensitivity-check and flatten. Re-run with shorter and longer lives for the uncertainty band; then phase the renewals and save annually to tame the spike.

The chain is the whole idea: the age profile shows where the lumps are; useful life adjusted by condition says how long until they fall due; the life-cycle model turns that into money landing in each year; and the sensitivity check tells you how firm the wave is and how to tame it. This technique fits any organisation that owns long-lived assets installed in waves - water and sewer pipes, electricity networks, roads, bridges, buildings, large fleets - and it is built for the long horizon (decades, not next year). It is not for short-term operating budgets, for fast-changing assets like laptops or software (lives too short and uncertain for a 30-year line), or for deciding which single pipe to dig up on Monday - it forecasts the pattern of renewal, not the next individual repair.

Two disciplines keep the forecast honest. First, let the condition data move the life - a book life is only a starting guess; the bursts, leaks and inspection scores are the asset telling you how it is really ageing, so listen to them rather than trusting the textbook blindly. Second, a forecast is only worth the saving it triggers - the point is not a frightening chart but a funded plan: name the annual amount to set aside, name the phases, and put them in the budget. A bow-wave you can see and have saved for is no longer a crisis.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your renewal forecast honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A flat-line budget hides the wave. "Last year's spend plus inflation" feels safe and is the most dangerous number in the file - it is blind to a cluster of same-age assets falling due together. Forecast the pattern, do not repeat the past.

• Do not trust the book life blindly. A textbook life is a starting point, not a verdict. Move it with real condition evidence, or you will forecast the wrong year and save too little or too late.

• Never forecast on a single line. A 30-year guess pretended to be exact is a false comfort. Run it short and long, show the band, and plan for the early edge - not the convenient one.

• A spike is a planning failure, not a fact of nature. One unaffordable year almost always splits into several fundable ones. Phase the work (worst condition first), and the wave becomes a climb.

• A forecast you never save against is just a warning ignored. The output is not the chart - it is the line in next year's budget. If no money is set aside, the work was wasted.

You came in with a treasurer who wanted one honest number and a register that hid a time-bomb. You can give him the truth and a plan: sixty kilometres of pipe laid in one push around 1979, ageing slightly fast, so a R120 million renewal lands around 2044 - give or take five years - and a flat budget never sees it. Set aside about R6.7 million a year from now, phase the work into three R40 million steps, and every line traces back to the register, the burst records and the pipe lives. The bow-wave forecast turns one unaffordable R120 million crisis into a plan you can fund - see the wave early, save a little every year, and phase the work into a climb instead of a flood. That is how a town keeps its water on for the next generation, not just this one. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring your asset register and build the age profile together.)
