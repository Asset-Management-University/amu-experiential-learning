Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Idris. About a year ago your organisation upgraded its main computer server - the new one is fast and brilliant. The old one, Server-04, was unplugged from the network and left in the corner of the server room. You did what most people do: you stopped using it, moved on, and felt the job was done.

Then a letter arrives - the authorities, announcing a data-protection audit in two weeks - and suddenly the "dead" Server-04 is a very live problem. It's still on your records as an active machine. It's still powered on, quietly humming and drawing electricity. Its special software licence quietly auto-renewed three months ago, costing the mission thousands. And it still holds years of old, sensitive client information on its drives - which the auditors will want fully accounted for. The thing you thought you'd finished with was never actually finished at all; it has been silently costing money and growing a risk the whole time you weren't looking.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your mind races to make the problem vanish. Keep quiet and hope the auditors don't notice the old machine in the corner? Huge risk - it's on the very list they'll be checking. Act fast - wipe the drives and call a scrap dealer to haul it away tomorrow? Decisive, but dangerous: rushing could mean handling the client data improperly and breaking the law, or dumping the electronic waste in a way that harms the environment and brings a fine - and in the haste you throw away any value you could have recovered. Or just keep paying the bills, keep it humming, and call it a "cold backup" - now you're actively paying to keep a dead thing alive, and it'll still be sitting there next year, having cost even more.

Each path comes from the same root: seeing the ending as a problem to escape, not a job to be done. So before you read on: you stopped using it long ago, but its costs and duties didn't stop - what did you forget to actually do, and is this "asset" even an asset any more?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The moment you stop using something is not its end. The ending of a thing is a final stage of work - and when you skip it, its duties and costs don't stop with its usefulness; they keep running in the background, silently growing. Five moves:

  • See the trap - you never ended it, you just stopped using it: switching off or unplugging a thing is not 'finishing' it. The ending is a real job, with its own steps and a finish line - and you skipped that job. Stopping use felt like completion, but the undone work just went quiet, not away; that gap between 'stopped using' and 'actually ended' is where all the trouble grows.
  • A thing's duties outlive its usefulness: even when the server stopped being useful, its obligations carried on: it still had a duty to protect the data on it; its contracts and licences still auto-renewed; it still drew power and took space; and it still sat on your records as a live thing - now a false record. None of that stops just because you stopped looking - the dead thing keeps billing, keeps endangering, keeps lying on the books.
  • The hidden cost grows until something makes it visible: for a whole year the costs and risks grew silently - the R4,000 licence renewed, R3,600 of electricity burned, the data risk sat unmanaged (about R7,600 a year, quietly drained from the mission). Then an outside force - the audit letter - made the hidden problem suddenly visible and urgent. An accident, a surprise bill or an inspection does the same; and dealing with it in a rush always costs far more than a deliberate ending (which here would have been a one-off of perhaps R1,500).
  • Separate what it's still worth from what it still costs - honestly: look at the un-ended thing with clear eyes. On one side, its remaining VALUE: can it be sold, stripped for parts, repurposed? (the recovery ladder is your guide). On the other, its remaining OBLIGATION: the costs (licence, power, insurance, space), the duties (data protection, safety) and the hazards. Be honest - here the R7,600-a-year cost plus the data risk far outweigh a few old parts, so the thing you call an 'asset' is now costing more than it's worth: it's a liability.
  • Plan the ending as real work: treat the closure like a small project - an owner, a simple plan, a finish line. Goal: properly end it so all its costs and duties stop. Steps: get approval to dispose; have a certified expert securely wipe the data; formally cancel the licence so it never renews; arrange certified e-waste collection; recover any value; and update the records to show it's 'gone'. Done: when the records read 'gone' and you hold the data-destruction certificate - the drain on the mission is plugged.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about servers - any un-ended thing keeps billing you. Watch a family car.

See the same trap with a car you no longer need

You stopped using the old car six months ago when you bought a new one, but it's still parked in the drive. You think of it as an "asset" - something you'll "get around to selling".

But its obligations never stopped: you're still paying insurance on it every month, its licence is due, it's taking a parking space, and to sell it you'll need a service and a roadworthy test that cost money. Every month it sits there it loses value while quietly costing you cash.

So separate its value (what you could sell it for today) from its obligations (the ongoing insurance, licence, repairs, and the value it loses just sitting). Is it still worth what you imagine - or is it a hidden drain? Planning its ending - booking the service, listing it, cancelling the insurance once it's sold - is the real work that finally stops the costs.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look around your own world, at work or at home. What have you "finished with" but never truly ended - an old printer in the storeroom still on the insurance list? A subscription to a service you stopped using, still going out each month? A storage unit you haven't visited in ages, still billed each quarter? A closed building whose roof now leaks, a growing hazard and cost?

  1. See it for what it has become. An un-ended thing whose duties have outlived its usefulness - not finished, just quiet.
  2. Do the honest check. On paper, list what it still costs you (money, space, time, worry) and what duties or hazards it still carries - against what it is honestly still worth. Is it secretly costing more than it's worth?
  3. Plan its ending like a real job. Write the three or four steps to close it down properly - stop the costs, handle the data or hazard safely, recover the value, correct the record to "gone" - give the job an owner (it might be you) and a "done" line.

Treating an ending as a non-event was never laziness - endings are genuinely invisible work that almost everyone skips, and switching a thing off honestly feels like finishing with it. But a thing's duties are quieter and more patient than its usefulness; they wait, and they grow. So give your endings the same care you give your beginnings: end them deliberately, safely, recovering what value remains and correcting the record to the truth. Every silent drain you plug and every hidden hazard you remove is money, space, and safety handed back to the thing that actually matters - the people your work is here to serve.
