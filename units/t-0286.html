Step 1 of 5

The supplier says the new fleet is still teething and will settle, the contract says it must reach 600 hours between failures, and the headline number says it already has - so is it improving or getting worse?

T1: The Ache

Ramoshaba District Transport took delivery of twenty-two new buses eleven months ago. They carry about nine thousand commuters a day on six routes, and the depot has been in a bad mood for most of those eleven months.

You are the fleet engineer. There have been twelve breakdowns in service - the kind where a bus stops with people on it and another has to be found. Two of them stranded a full load on the R37 in the rain.

The supplier's account has been the same from the first month, delivered pleasantly and never in writing: they are still teething, they always are, they will settle. And it is a completely reasonable thing to say. New fleets do settle. Faults get found and fixed and the machines get better. You have seen it happen.

Today the letter arrives asking for final acceptance and release of the retention - R4.6 million. It quotes the one number in the contract: the fleet must demonstrate at least 600 hours between failures before acceptance. And it quotes the fleet's figure: nine thousand operating hours, twelve failures, 750 hours between failures. Above the requirement. Sign here.

The arithmetic is correct. You have checked it twice.

And you cannot shake the feeling in the depot, which is not evidence: it does not feel like a fleet that is settling. It feels like one that was getting better and has started getting worse. But "it feels like" is worth nothing against a signed contract and a correct calculation, and if you refuse to sign on a feeling you will be overruled by Friday - deservedly.

Their number is right and I still think this fleet is going backwards. How do I tell "settling in" from "getting worse" - out of the same log, in a way that survives a lawyer?

Before you read on, decide what you would do with that letter. Twelve breakdowns, 750 hours against a required 600, R4.6 million of retention, a supplier who has been patient and plausible, and a depot that does not believe it. Write down what you would actually do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five responses, and what each one can withstand

• "Sign it. The number meets the contract." Defensible, quick, and it is what the contract on its face requires. It also banks whatever is actually happening in that fleet, and if the depot's instinct is right the buses become the district's problem the moment the retention is released.

• "Refuse - we have had twelve breakdowns and two strandings." The honest reaction of somebody who has stood on the R37 in the rain, and it is a statement about the past. The supplier's answer is already prepared and it is a fair one: that is what teething looks like, and the contract measures hours between failures, not indignation.

• "Ask for another three months of data before deciding." Reasonable and it postpones a decision that the contract does not let you postpone indefinitely, while the retention accrues interest and the relationship sours. It also does not say what you would do differently with the extra data.

• "Compare the last six months with the first five." Now you are close - this is the right instinct, and with twelve failures split into two groups it will not survive scrutiny. Any competent challenger will ask how often a fleet that was perfectly steady would happen to produce those two halves, and nobody in the room will be able to answer.

• "Get a lawyer to look at the wording." Sometimes exactly right, and it argues about the sentence rather than the fleet. The wording is not the problem; the number satisfying it is.

• Look at what the headline figure actually is. Nine thousand hours divided by twelve failures is an average across the whole eleven months - it includes the bad early period on equal terms with last week. On a fleet that has been improving, that average understates where it is now. On a fleet that has been deteriorating, it flatters it. Either way it describes no particular moment, and the question on the table is not what the fleet averaged since handover. It is what the fleet is doing now, and where it is heading.

T2: The Tool in Action

List the failures by cumulative operating hours rather than by the gaps. Work out the headline average, and notice what it averages. Then fit the slope that says which way things are going - and use it to work out not what the fleet averaged, but what it has reached. Watch it run on the twenty-two buses.

Step 1 - List the failures by cumulative operating time, not by the gaps

Kagiso Ramoshaba pulls the fleet log and converts every breakdown to cumulative fleet operating hours since handover: 620, 1,340, 2,180, 3,050, 3,910, 4,700, 5,420, 6,180, 6,880, 7,550, 8,230, 8,880 hours, with the fleet now at 9,000 hours and twelve failures recorded. He looks at the gaps before he calculates anything: 620, 720, 840, 870, 860, 790, 720, 760, 700, 670, 680, 650. They widened for a while and then began to close. That is not what settling in looks like, and it is not something the total would ever have shown him.

Step 2 - Work out the headline figure everybody will quote, and see what it averages

Nine thousand hours divided by twelve failures is 750 hours between failures. The contract requires 600 before final acceptance and release of the retention. On that figure the fleet passes with room to spare, and the supplier's letter quotes exactly this number. Kagiso writes it on the board and then writes underneath it the thing nobody has said: this is the average of the whole eleven months, and the question on the table is not what the fleet averaged. It is what the fleet is now.

Step 3 - Fit the slope that says which way it is going

The fit gives a slope of 1.35. Below one would have meant the fleet was genuinely settling in; at one, steady. At 1.35 the failures are arriving faster as the hours accumulate - this fleet is getting worse, not better, and it has been doing so for months underneath a headline number that looks fine. Kagiso checks it against his own eye from Step 1: the gaps closing after an early improvement. The arithmetic and the eyeball agree, which is the only reason he trusts either.

Step 4 - Work out where it actually is now, and compare THAT with the requirement

The reliability the fleet has reached at 9,000 hours is 556 hours between failures. The requirement is 600. So the fleet does not meet the contract - it fails by 44 hours - and the headline 750 that would have passed it is an average that includes the better months at the beginning. Two numbers from one log, both correctly calculated, pointing opposite ways on a decision worth several million rand in retention. Kagiso puts both in the report with what each one means, because the difference between them is the finding.

Step 5 - Act on the direction, and be as fair to the other party as to your own

Kagiso does not recommend rejecting the fleet. He recommends withholding final acceptance and telling the supplier why, in the terms of the analysis: the trend is deteriorating, so there is a cause still in the fleet, and both parties need it found rather than signed over. And he writes the sentence that matters for the next fleet: had the slope come out at 0.7 with the same headline of 750, his recommendation would have been to accept early rather than hold the retention, because a supplier whose asset is genuinely improving should not be penalised by an average that lags behind their work. The method is not a weapon. It is the same question asked honestly in both directions.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the third and fourth are the pair that changes the answer.

  • 1. Cumulative operating time at each failure, not the gaps - and eyeball whether the gaps are widening or closing before calculating anything.
  • 2. Work out the headline average, and say out loud what it averages over.
  • 3. Fit the slope that gives the direction: below one improving, one steady, above one deteriorating.
  • 4. Calculate what the asset has reached, not what it averaged, and compare that with the requirement.
  • 5. Act on the direction, in both directions - a deteriorating trend means an unfound cause; an improving one means do not penalise the average's lag.

What you have done is a reliability growth analysis, and the model behind it is the Crow-AMSAA or power-law process (IEC 61164). The slope you fitted is beta, and it is the whole direction: below one the asset is growing more reliable, above one it is deteriorating. The average you started with is the cumulative MTBF - total time divided by number of failures - and the figure you finished with is the instantaneous or demonstrated MTBF, which is the reliability reached at the end of the period rather than averaged across it. That distinction is the entire unit: the two figures are both correct, they differ by however much the asset has changed, and the one that answers a question about next month is the instantaneous one. Quote the cumulative figure in a contract test and you are testing an average that includes the worst weeks of commissioning.

It fits anything new, repaired repeatedly, and expected to improve - a commissioned plant, a new fleet, a software release, a rebuilt machine, a new process. Two limits travel with it. It needs enough failures to fit a slope at all: with three or four, the slope is arithmetic rather than evidence, and saying so is part of using it properly. And it describes the asset's own history under its own conditions, so a change of duty, route or maintenance regime part-way through breaks the model rather than showing up in it - which means an unexplained slope is a reason to go and ask what changed, not a result.

Work out the headline average. Then look at the gaps and say, before any fitting, which way you think it is going - and check whether the arithmetic agrees with you.

Two disciplines decide whether this holds up in a room with money in it. First, form your view from the gaps before you fit anything, and say so if the fit disagrees with you. The eyeball and the arithmetic checking each other is what makes the finding trustworthy; when they disagree it is almost always because the cumulative times were entered as intervals, or because something changed mid-period that the model cannot see. A slope you cannot reconcile with the shape of your own data is a slope to investigate, not to report. Second, quote both figures every time, and say which question each answers. The temptation - on whichever side of the table you sit - is to quote the one that helps: the supplier reaches for the average, the client reaches for the instantaneous. Putting both in the report with a sentence on each removes the argument entirely, and it is the only version that survives somebody checking your work with the opposite interest.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the finding true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Intervals entered instead of cumulative times. The single most common error, and it produces a confident slope about nothing. The figures must be the running total of operating time since the asset entered service.

• The cumulative average quoted as the current state. It is an average over the whole period, including commissioning. On an improving asset it understates; on a deteriorating one it flatters. It answers a question about the past.

• Too few failures to fit anything. With three or four, the slope is arithmetic rather than evidence. Report the direction as an indication and say how many failures it rests on.

• Something changed mid-period. A new route, a new duty, a maintenance contract, a driver rotation. The model cannot see it and will express it as a trend. An unexplained slope is a question to go and ask, not a result to report.

• Improving from a bad start is still bad. A slope below one says the direction is right. It says nothing about whether the level reached is acceptable - which is what the instantaneous figure is for.

• Using it on a wear-out problem. This is a model of an asset being debugged, where faults are found and removed. An asset whose failures come from things wearing out is a different situation and this is the wrong instrument for it.

• Using it as a weapon. The method cuts both ways and must be allowed to: an improving trend is a reason to accept early and release money, exactly as a deteriorating one is a reason to withhold. Anybody who only ever reaches for it in one direction will be recognised, correctly, as arguing rather than analysing.

You had a letter with a correct number on it, a contract that the number satisfied, R4.6 million of retention, a supplier whose account was entirely plausible, and a depot's instinct that counts for nothing. You can put a single page back: the same twelve failures, the same nine thousand hours, the average of 750 that passes and the reliability actually reached of 556 that does not - and the slope of 1.35 that explains why two correct figures disagree. An average tells you what has happened. It cannot tell you which way you are going, and a fleet is accepted for what it will do next. Somewhere in those buses is a cause nobody has found yet, and the difference between signing and not signing is whether anybody goes looking for it. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
