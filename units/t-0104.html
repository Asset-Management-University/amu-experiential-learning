Step 1 of 5

Hold it, or risk it?

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. The main process pump shudders, then dies - the impeller has shattered. No disaster: you just fit the spare and you are running again in an hour. You head to the stores, find the shelf for that impeller... and it is empty.

You phone the maker. The quote comes back: twelve weeks to build and ship a new one.

Twelve weeks. For three months, Corathyl will limp along on reduced capacity, renting emergency pumps, paying overtime, explaining shortfalls to the municipality - a cost that climbs into the hundreds of thousands. All for the want of a single R40,000 part that could have been sitting on that shelf, ready. And yet, in the very same store room, you have shelves groaning with other spares - some worth a fortune - that have not been touched in a decade. Money frozen solid, right next to the gap that just crippled you.

I had a fortune in spares I never use, and not the one I desperately needed - which is now costing us hundreds of thousands while we wait. How do I decide, properly, which spares to actually keep on the shelf?

Before you read on, decide. The impeller costs R40,000. From the records it fails about once every four years. If it fails and the shelf is empty, a replacement takes twelve weeks, and three months of crippled output costs roughly R200,000. Holding a spare on the shelf ties up capital and space at around 20% of its value a year. Meanwhile the same store holds shelves of other spares, some worth a great deal, untouched in a decade. Your manager will want the reasoning. Write down whether you would hold one, and how you would justify it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the number that has nothing to do with it

• “R40,000 is a lot of money to leave sitting on a shelf. Don't hold it.” The instinctive answer, and it judges the decision by the item's price — which is the one number that tells you nothing about whether to hold it. A cheap part on a long lead time can stop you for a quarter; an expensive one available in an hour costs almost nothing to be without.

• “It only fails every four years. The odds are against needing it.” True, and half a thought. How often something is needed only matters alongside what happens when it is — and here that is three months of crippled output, which is not a risk you can shrug at because it is infrequent.

• “It's critical, so hold one. Obviously.” Probably the right answer, arrived at by a route that cannot be defended and cannot be applied to the next item. Say “critical, hold one” often enough and you produce the shelves of untouched spares already in that room.

• “Ask the supplier to guarantee faster delivery.” Genuinely the best move if it is real, because it attacks the thing that actually makes the absence expensive. It also needs to be verified rather than promised, and it does not answer today's question.

• “Clear out the dead stock and use the money for this.” Sensible housekeeping, and it is a separate decision. Those items are already bought; the money is gone whether they sit there or not.

• The reason the store has both too much and too little is that nothing here has ever been decided as a decision. And it can be, because two costs are directly comparable if you build them: what holding it costs for a year, and what the risk of not holding it costs for a year — the latter being how often you would need it multiplied by what being without it does to you. A spare is not a purchase; it is insurance, and insurance is judged against the exposure it covers rather than against its own price. Build both numbers here and the argument with your manager is over in one line — and, more usefully, the same two numbers can be built for every other item on those shelves.

There is a way to make that call as a clear, costed decision instead of a guess. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the one-page decision Onalenna built afterwards for that very impeller - turning "should we keep one on the shelf?" from a gut feeling into a number she could defend to any manager.

1. How often does it fail? (how likely we are to need it)

The impeller fails on average once every 4 years - so 0.25 failures a year. (How likely are we, in a given year, to actually need a spare?)

2. What does it cost to hold one for a year?

The impeller costs R40,000. Holding one on the shelf ties up capital and storage at about 20% a year = R8,000 a year.

3. What is the pain if we need one and the shelf is empty?

If it fails and the shelf is empty: a 12-week wait for a new one, and three months of crippled output - about R200,000, as a one-off. (Lead time is what makes a stockout hurt: a part you can get in an hour costs almost nothing to be without.)

4. The expected risk-cost per year (how often x how much it hurts)

Expected risk-cost a year = how often it fails x what a stockout costs = 0.25 x R200,000 = R50,000 a year.

5. The verdict (hold if holding < annual risk)

HOLD ONE SPARE. R8,000 a year to hold it is far cheaper than the R50,000 a year of risk you carry without it - a net benefit of about R42,000 a year. A costed decision, not a hunch.

Look at the two numbers that decide it: R8,000 a year to hold the spare, against R50,000 a year of risk carried by not holding it. Once you see them side by side, the choice is not a hunch - it is obvious. Hold the spare; it is the cheaper path by far. Now let us walk through how to build that decision for any part.

T3: The Blueprint

That tension - money frozen on a shelf on one side, the works crippled by a breakdown on the other - has a name, and a tool to resolve it: Reliability-Centred Spares (spares optimisation). The whole idea: holding a spare is not free, and not holding it is not free either - so you put both costs in the same currency, per year, and pick the cheaper.

Each box of the decision answers one plain question:

  • How often does it fail? The failure rate - how likely you are to need a spare in a year.
  • What does it cost to hold one for a year? The holding cost - the capital tied up in the part, plus storage (often a percentage of its price).
  • What is the pain if we need one and have none? The stockout cost - and this is driven above all by lead time: a part you can buy down the road in an hour costs almost nothing to be without; a part that takes twelve weeks to ship can cripple you.
  • What is the risk-cost per year? Multiply the first by the third: how often it fails, times what a stockout costs. That is what you quietly pay, on average, each year by going without.
  • The verdict. Compare the yearly holding cost with the yearly risk-cost. Hold the spare when holding it is cheaper than the risk you carry without it.

One vital exception keeps you honest: the insurance spare. For a part whose failure could be catastrophic - shut the whole plant, or risk lives - the stockout cost is so enormous that you hold the spare even if it almost never fails. There, the simple sum still points the same way; you just never let a low failure rate tempt you into going naked on something that could ruin you.

Does it scale? Onalenna deciding whether to stock a R40,000 impeller is the exact same logic an airline uses to decide whether to keep a R20-million spare engine waiting at a hub - both weigh the cost of holding against the crippling cost of a grounded asset. The numbers are worlds apart; the decision is identical.

T4: Your Turn

Now make a real spares call of your own. Pick one: a spare inner tube for your bike, a spare charger for your laptop, a backup power bank.

  1. How often do you need it? Roughly how many times a year does the thing fail (a puncture, a frayed cable)?
  2. What does it cost to hold one ready? Near enough, the price of the spare.
  3. What is the pain of being without it? What does it actually cost you - the missed lecture, the dead phone at the worst moment, and how long until you could get another (the lead time)?
  4. The yearly risk: how often you need it, times what going without costs.
  5. The verdict: is holding it cheaper than the risk? Buy it and keep it ready, or not?

T5: The Guardrails

The spares decision is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then sanity-check your call.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The squirrel. Holding everything "just in case", until the store room is a fortune in frozen capital that never moves. Every spare must earn its place on the shelf by this sum.

• The naked store. Holding almost nothing to look lean and cheap - then being crippled by a failure you could have seen coming. Lean is not the goal; the right cost is.

• Lead-time blindness. The single biggest driver, most often forgotten. A part you can fetch in an hour barely needs a shelf spare; a part that takes twelve weeks almost always does. Always ask: how long to get another?

• Treating an insurance spare like a consumable. Using the bare sum for a part whose failure could shut the plant or harm someone, and talking yourself out of it because "it hardly ever fails". For those, the cost of being without is near-bottomless - hold it.

• Set and forget. Failure rates, prices and lead times all change. A spares list never reviewed slowly drifts from right to wrong. Revisit the big calls as the data moves.

Onalenna did not lose three months and a fortune because she lacked spares - she had a store room full of them. She lost it because no one had ever weighed the cost of holding each one against the cost of being without it. Now you hold that small, sharp sum: how often you will need it, times what a gap costs, against what it costs to keep one ready. Do it for the parts that matter, and you will keep the right spare on the shelf - and free the money tied up in all the wrong ones.
