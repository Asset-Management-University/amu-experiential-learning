One honest sentence

T1: The Ache

You have something you are genuinely good at, and people encourage you - "you should sell these", "you could make a business of this". But the encouragement brings pressure, and the pressure brings confusion instead of clarity. Is this a hobby? A little side earner? A real business? You are not sure, so you cannot tell how seriously to take it, how much to invest, whether to feel guilty for not pushing harder.

And that confusion has a cost. You end up treating a joy like a job - loading it with targets and deadlines until the thing you loved feels like work - or you treat a would-be business like a hobby, drifting and half-committed while it quietly starves for want of real intent. Either way you get the worst of both. And when someone asks, plainly, "so what is your business?", the answer that comes out is vague - and it feels vague even to you.

People say I should turn what I am good at into a business - but I cannot even tell whether it is a hobby, a side hustle or a business, and I cannot say clearly what it would be. Where do I start?

Before you read on, decide. People keep telling you to turn it into a business — kindly, and with real confidence in you. Last year you took on so many orders that the thing you loved started to feel like a job, and you stopped enjoying it for months. The year before, somebody asked whether you could supply them regularly and you were vague, and never heard from them again. Last week at a braai someone asked plainly, “so what is your business?” — and what came out was woolly, and it felt woolly to you too. Write down where you would start — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what actually decides which thing you have

• “Register the business and make it official.” Puts a legal form around something you have not yet decided the nature of. Registration is a consequence of the decision rather than a substitute for it, and it can commit you to obligations you did not intend.

• “Take more orders and see where it goes.” Precisely what produced the year when you stopped enjoying it. Drifting into commitment is how a loved thing quietly becomes an obligation nobody chose.

• “Keep it as a hobby — I do not want the pressure.” Entirely legitimate, and worth saying out loud: a hobby is not a failed business. But if it is chosen it should be chosen, because drifting into it costs you the opportunity the same way drifting into the other cost you the pleasure.

• “Write a business plan.” A great deal of structure built on an unanswered question. Every section will ask what the thing is for, and you would be inventing answers to fill them in.

• “Ask the people telling me to do it what they think.” They are confident about your baking and know nothing about your intent — and intent is half of what settles this.

• The first thing to un-learn is that the activity tells you what you have. It does not. Baking, fixing, writing, growing — each can be a thing done for love, a thing that earns a little alongside something else, or a thing run to sustain itself. The difference is not in the doing. What decides it is your intent and the cash flow — what you actually want from it, and what money is genuinely moving. Those two answers together name it, and naming it honestly matters because each deserves different treatment: you do not demand a profit from something done for love, and you cannot run an enterprise on a pastime's footing. Notice that your two bad years were exactly these two errors, one in each direction. Then the second move: say it in one honest sentence — what it is, for whom, and what it does for them. That sentence is a test as much as a description. If you cannot say it plainly, you have not decided it yet — which is why it felt woolly to you at the braai, and why that feeling was accurate.

You start by naming it honestly - and then saying it in one sentence. Let us do both.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you find out what you are actually building - naming it honestly by intent and cash flow, deciding whether to convert it, and sharpening it into one honest sentence.

1. The three things it could be

The first thing to un-learn is that the activity tells you what you have. IT DOES NOT. Baking, fixing, writing, growing vegetables - each can be a HOBBY, a SIDE HUSTLE, or a BUSINESS, and the difference is not in the doing. A HOBBY is done for love; money is not the point, and it may even cost you money; there is NO intent to profit. A SIDE HUSTLE earns some money alongside a main income; the intent is extra cash, part-time, run informally, NOT YET as a full enterprise. A BUSINESS is run with the intent to make a consistent profit that can sustain it - designed around paying demand, customer expectations, and tracked revenue. Three genuinely different things, and naming which one you are holding is the whole first move - because the activity you are doing will not tell you, and everyone around you will assume they know.

2. What decides which: intent and cash flow

So the question is NEVER 'what is the activity?' but two others: WHAT IS YOUR INTENT? and WHAT IS THE CASH FLOW? Those two answers, together, tell you which of the three you are holding. And naming it honestly matters enormously, because EACH DESERVES TO BE TREATED DIFFERENTLY: you do not demand a profit from a hobby, and you cannot run a business on a hobby's footing. Get the name wrong and you MISTREAT THE THING - you either crush a joy under business pressure it was never meant to carry, or you starve a business by giving it only a hobby's commitment. The name is not paperwork; it is the decision that tells you HOW TO HOLD THE THING IN YOUR HANDS. Name it by intent and cash flow, and you know at once how seriously to take it, how much to invest, and whether the pressure you feel is one you should accept or let go of.

3. Whether - and when - to convert

If yours is a hobby and the world is nudging you to 'make it a business', treat that as a REAL DECISION with three honest conditions, not a foregone conclusion. Convert a hobby into a business only when: there is REAL PAYING DEMAND (strangers, not just kind friends, will pay); you ACTUALLY WANT THE INCOME and the enterprise, not just the compliment; and you are WILLING TO RUN IT AS A BUSINESS - to price for margin, meet deadlines, track the money - because that willingness changes your relationship to the thing forever. And here is what the encouragement never tells you: A HOBBY YOU LOVE IS A WHOLE AND WORTHY THING ON ITS OWN. IT DOES NOT NEED TO BECOME A BUSINESS TO BE VALUABLE. THE GOAL IS TO NAME WHAT YOU ARE BUILDING HONESTLY, SO YOU CAN TREAT IT CORRECTLY - NOT TO FORCE EVERYTHING ONTO A LADDER OF AMBITION. CHOOSING TO KEEP YOUR HOBBY A HOBBY IS A WISE AND LEGITIMATE CHOICE, NOT A FAILURE. Turning the thing you love into a thing you must profit from can quietly kill the love; sometimes the wisest founder keeps the joy and builds a business out of something else.

4. Say it in one honest sentence

Once it is a business, here is the test of whether you truly understand it, and it is simple and unforgiving: CAN YOU SAY IT IN ONE SENTENCE, in the form 'I HELP [SPECIFIC PERSON] ACHIEVE [SPECIFIC OUTCOME] BY [SPECIFIC METHOD]'? That sentence FORCES THE SPECIFICITY THAT VAGUENESS HIDES. 'I sell cakes' names an activity and tells you nothing. 'I help busy local parents give their child a memorable birthday by making custom celebration cakes delivered to their door' names a PERSON, an OUTCOME, and a METHOD - and suddenly the whole business is visible: who it is for, what it really delivers, and how. And understand what this sentence is FOR: THIS SENTENCE IS A TOOL FOR YOUR OWN CLARITY, NOT A SLOGAN FOR OTHERS. IT IS THE PROOF TO YOURSELF THAT YOU KNOW WHAT YOU ARE BUILDING. A PLAIN, HONEST SENTENCE THAT IS TRUE IS BETTER THAN A CLEVER, EMPTY ONE THAT IS NOT. Do not polish it for an audience; sharpen it until it is true.

5. Test the sentence - and read what it reveals

The sentence is NOT finished when it sounds nice; it is finished when it PASSES A TEST and TELLS YOU SOMETHING. Run it against the viability filters you use on any idea: is the DEMAND real, is the METHOD a genuine advantage, can you SUSTAIN it, does the MARGIN work? Rewrite it until it holds. Then read it as a DIAGNOSTIC, because each blank is a mirror. The [SPECIFIC PERSON] reveals whether you know your target or are quietly trying to serve everyone - and TRYING TO SERVE EVERYONE MEANS SERVING NO ONE. The [SPECIFIC OUTCOME] reveals whether you are selling what the customer actually wants or only what you happen to make. And the [SPECIFIC METHOD] reveals your DIFFERENTIATION - the thing you do that is genuinely yours and hard to copy. A vague sentence is not a wording problem; it is a THINKING problem, and it is showing you exactly where the thinking is not yet done. IF YOU CANNOT WRITE THE SENTENCE YET, IT DOES NOT MEAN YOU HAVE FAILED. IT MEANS THE THINKING IS NOT YET FINISHED. THIS EXERCISE IS THE TOOL TO HELP YOU FINISH IT.

Boxes 1 to 3 name the thing. Boxes 4 and 5 sharpen it until you can see it. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Does it scale? This is how you get clear on anything you are about to commit to - a project, a role, a cause, a change of direction. Naming a thing honestly and stating it sharply is not a preliminary you rush past to get to the real work; it very often is the real work, because you cannot build well what you have not yet clearly named.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. Someone is genuinely skilled at repairing bicycles - friends bring theirs round, and they fix them beautifully in the garage on weekends. People keep saying "you should do this for a living."

  1. Model the three forms. Show how bicycle repair could be a hobby, a side hustle, or a business for this person - using intent and cash flow, not the activity, to tell them apart.
  2. Choose and justify. Decide which of the three this person should choose, and justify it - and remember that keeping it a hobby is a completely valid answer if that is what fits their intent and life.
  3. Write the sentence. If they choose to build a business, write its one-sentence concept in the form "I help [specific person] achieve [specific outcome] by [specific method]."
  4. Read the sentence. Name what your sentence reveals about the target (the person) and the differentiation (the method).

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the three forms: as a hobby, they fix friends' bikes for the love of it and money never changes hands (or they are out of pocket); as a side hustle, neighbours start paying a bit and they earn on weekends alongside their job; as a business, they decide to make a sustaining living from it - pricing for margin, taking bookings, tracking the money. The mark of a real answer is that the activity is identical in all three and only the intent and cash flow change.

Second, the choice: any of the three can be right, and the justification must rest on this person's real intent, demand and willingness - a strong answer might convert to a business (real demand, wants the income, ready to run it) or might keep it a hobby ("the garage on Saturdays is how I unwind; charging for it would take the joy, and my income is fine") - and the second is not a lesser answer.

Third, the sentence: for the business version, something like "I help daily commuters keep riding safely by offering fast, honest, same-week bicycle repairs near where they work." Specific person (daily commuters), specific outcome (keep riding safely), specific method (fast, honest, local repairs) - not "I fix bikes."

Fourth, what it reveals: the person shows the target is commuters (not "everyone with a bike"), which shapes location, hours and pricing; and the method shows the differentiation is speed, honesty and convenience - which is a real, defensible advantage a learner can build on, unlike "I am good at fixing bikes", which everyone claims.

T5: The Guardrails

To build it well, you must first name it honestly - a business you cannot name in one honest sentence is a business you cannot yet sell, differentiate, or test.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Letting the activity decide. Baking is not a hobby, a hustle or a business by itself. Your intent and cash flow decide which - name those, not the activity.

• Treating a hobby like a business. Loading targets and deadlines onto a joy crushes it. If it is a hobby, let it be one, and do not demand a profit from it.

• Treating a business like a hobby. A venture you mean to live on needs real intent, pricing and tracking. Half-commitment starves it. Give a business a business's footing.

• Assuming every hobby must become a business. It need not. Keeping a hobby a hobby is a wise, legitimate choice - not a failure of ambition.

• Converting and killing the joy. Turning what you love into what you must profit from can dim the love. Convert with eyes open, and be willing to walk away from it.

• A vague concept sentence. "I sell things people like" tells you nothing. Force the specific person, outcome and method until the business is visible.

• Trying to serve everyone. A sentence with no specific person serves no one. Name the target narrowly; you can widen later.

• A clever empty sentence. The sentence is for your own clarity, not applause. A plain true sentence beats a slick empty one every time.
