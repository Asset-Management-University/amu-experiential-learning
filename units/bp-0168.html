Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Mahlatse, the Asset Manager at a ferro-alloys smelter and quarry on the edge of a small town. The plant melts ore into ferro-alloy in two electric furnaces and runs a fleet of haul trucks between the quarry and the furnace yard. From your office window you can see Furnace 2, a wall of grey steel, breathing heat into the afternoon; the air carries the dry, metallic smell of hot slag. On your desk sit two things, side by side.

The first is a printed email from Khulekani, the Finance Director. The subject line reads: "Lender ESG questionnaire - due 14 days." The bank refinancing the plant's R140 million loan has attached a 9-page form, and it wants hard numbers: tonnes of carbon dioxide released per tonne of alloy produced, for last year AND this year so they can see the trend; litres of water drawn from the river, and how much was recycled; lost-time injuries per million hours worked, with the cause of each one; and energy used per tonne, split between grid power and the plant's own diesel. The second is a letter, on heavy paper, from the provincial environmental regulator - a formal notice. Your annual licence to discharge water and run the furnaces is up for renewal, and this time the renewal requires an AUDITED environmental and social return: the same kinds of numbers, but signed off as accurate by an independent auditor. The deadline is stamped in bold: six weeks.

Khulekani puts his head round your door. He does not come in. "The bank says no acceptable ESG return, no refinance," he says. "And the regulator can suspend the discharge licence. If the licence goes, the furnaces go cold." He lets that sit. "You manage the assets. The numbers come off the assets. So - these are your numbers. I need them in fourteen days." Your stomach tightens, and you open the systems you actually have. The maintenance system logs work orders and spare parts; it does NOT record carbon, water or energy. The truck fleet has fuel cards, so you can find total diesel bought last year - about 480,000 litres - but nobody has ever split that between the haul trucks and the standby generators. The furnaces draw grid power and you have twelve municipal electricity bills in a folder, but they give a total in rand and kilowatt-hours for the whole plant, not per tonne, not by furnace. Water? There is one meter on the river abstraction pipe; someone reads it monthly, but you cannot find where the readings are kept. Injuries are in a hardback book in the safety officer's office, written by hand.

You do some quick sums. Last year you produced roughly 42,000 tonnes of alloy. You can get a rough carbon figure - take the electricity, apply a standard factor, add the diesel - but the furnace electrode paste also releases carbon when it burns, and you have no idea how much; your gut says the real number could be a fifth higher on the electrode side. And a quiet, tempting voice says: the bank just wants a form filled in. Take the diesel total, the electricity total, apply the standard factors you found online, divide by tonnes, send a clean set of numbers. It will look complete. Nobody is going to walk the plant and check. But you remember the word on the regulator's letter: AUDITED. An auditor WILL ask where each number came from. And if this year's quick guess is lower than next year's properly-measured number, your "improving trend" will reverse, and the bank will think the plant is getting dirtier when really you just started counting honestly.

The bank wants audited carbon, water and injury numbers in 14 days and the regulator wants them auditor-signed in six weeks - but my maintenance system logs work orders, not carbon; the diesel is on cards, the power on twelve bills, the water on one meter nobody logs, the injuries in a handwritten book. Where do I start?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The deadline is fourteen days and a clean guess would look complete. "Apply standard factors to the diesel and electricity totals, divide by tonnes, and send it." But the regulator's word is AUDITED, and an auditor asks where each number came from; a confident figure with no workings is exactly what they reject. "Tell the bank we cannot produce the numbers - the systems were never built for it." But the numbers DO come off the assets; most are already measured, just scattered on bills, cards, a meter and a book - 'cannot produce' is surrender, not truth. "Leave out the awkward electrode-paste carbon - it is small and nobody will notice." But if it turns out to matter, next year's honest number jumps and your improving trend reverses, which is worse than counting it now. So before you read on: name what this demand really IS (a form to fill, or a system to build?), and the one move you would make before sending a single number.

I am Mahlatse, the Asset Manager at a ferro-alloys smelter - two electric furnaces, a haul-truck fleet. Two things sit on my desk. A printed email from the Finance Director, Khulekani: 'Lender ESG questionnaire - due 14 days.' The bank refinancing our R140 million loan wants hard numbers - tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne of alloy for last year AND this year (for the trend), litres of water drawn and recycled, lost-time injuries per million hours with the cause of each, energy per tonne split between grid and diesel. And a formal letter from the provincial environmental regulator: our licence to discharge water and run the furnaces is up for renewal, and this time it needs an AUDITED environmental and social return - the same numbers, signed off by an independent auditor, in six weeks. Khulekani put his head round the door: 'No acceptable ESG return, no refinance. The regulator can suspend the discharge licence. If the licence goes, the furnaces go cold. You manage the assets - these are your numbers.' But my systems were built to run the work, not report on it: the maintenance system logs work orders and spares, not carbon; the trucks have fuel cards (about 480,000 litres of diesel last year) but nobody split trucks from generators; the furnaces draw grid power and I have twelve municipal bills, but only a whole-plant total in rand and kWh; water is one meter on the river pipe that someone reads monthly but I cannot find the log; injuries are in a hardback book in the safety officer's office. We made about 42,000 tonnes of alloy last year. I could send a quick guess - electricity plus diesel, standard factors off the internet - but the regulator's word is AUDITED. Where do I start?
The trap is thinking the job is FINDING four numbers by Friday. It is not. The job is building a way to PRODUCE the same numbers, the same way, every year, that someone outside can check. The bank's questionnaire and the regulator's audited return are not two homework sheets - they are the first two CUSTOMERS of a non-financial (ESG) data system the plant has never built: a documented way of turning what the assets actually do (burn fuel, draw power, abstract water, hurt or not hurt people) into numbers that carry an audit trail. So do not start filling in their form. Sort every required number into three honest piles - measured and recorded, measured but not recorded, not measured at all - and you will find most of the 'impossible' numbers are already being measured, just scattered and unstored (the electricity is on the bills, the diesel on the cards, the water on the meter, the injuries in the book); only a couple are genuinely not measured yet. Rescue what exists before you buy a single meter. Then beware the quick guess: the tempting voice says electricity plus diesel is basically the real carbon number and the furnace electrode paste is a small extra to wave away - but before you send it, run the cheapest probe that could prove you wrong, and if the awkward number turns out to matter, count it NOW, because a clean guess this year that jumps next year makes your improving trend reverse. The real question is not 'which four numbers do I send?' but 'how do I build figures an auditor can re-trace, graded honestly, that improve rather than embarrass me next year?'
Five moves turn a panic into a defensible first ESG return. (1) IT IS A MEASUREMENT SYSTEM YOU DO NOT HAVE, NOT A FORM TO FILL: the questionnaire and the audited return are the first two customers of a non-financial data system - build the first version of it, do not hunt four figures. (2) SORT EVERY NUMBER INTO THREE PILES (measured+recorded / measured-but-not-recorded / not-measured) and RESCUE WHAT EXISTS before buying a meter: electricity (bills), diesel (cards), water (the meter), injuries (the book) are all already measured, just scattered; only paste-carbon and recycled-water are genuinely not measured. (3) SET A SUCCESS LINE, THEN RUN THE CHEAPEST PROBE: the belief that electricity-plus-diesel is basically the real carbon number - success line set first ('safe to leave the paste out only if it is under 5%'), then a one-afternoon stores probe (about 900 tonnes of paste bought x a published carbon factor). THE SURPRISE: the paste adds about 2,400 tonnes of carbon dioxide - about 7%, breaking the line - so it is no longer next year's problem, you count it NOW to protect the trend. (4) BUILD EACH NUMBER WITH A STATED QUALITY, NOT FALSE PRECISION: grade every figure (good / partial / estimated / not yet measured), keep the workings (they ARE the audit trail), and report 'not yet measured' for recycled water rather than a false zero. (5) MATCH THE ASSURANCE TO EACH CUSTOMER: the bank (14 days) gets the figures plus a one-page data-quality note (a plant that knows where its numbers are weak is more fundable than false confidence); the regulator (audited, 6 weeks) gets the strong numbers audit-ready and the estimated ones with the method attached and a dated improvement plan - an auditor can sign off a transparent estimate, never a confident number with no workings. The total comes to about 34,700 tonnes, 0.83 per tonne of alloy - and the difference between a number an auditor rejects and one they sign is not its size, it is whether you can show where it came from.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is how you worked it through - including the part where reality refused to match your first guess. Five moves:

  • It is a measurement system you do not have, not a form to fill: the trap was thinking the job was FINDING numbers. It was not. The job was building a way to produce the same numbers, the same way, every year, that someone outside can check. The bank's questionnaire and the regulator's audited return are not two homework sheets - they are the first two CUSTOMERS of a reporting system the plant has never built. Once you saw it that way, the panic settled: you were not hunting for four figures by Friday, you were standing up the first version of a thing that has to keep running. What the plant needs is a basic non-financial data system - a deliberate, documented way of turning what the assets actually DO (burn fuel, draw power, abstract water, hurt or not hurt people) into environmental, social and governance numbers that carry an audit trail. ESG reporting is not a marketing exercise; it is asset data, measured at the source, recorded the same way each time, with the workings kept. (Demonstrating to stakeholders what a spend already DELIVERED is the kindred reporting problem of bp-0142; here the data does not exist yet, so the work is building the measurement system, not proving a return.)
  • Sort every number into three piles, and rescue what exists before buying a meter: instead of guessing, you took each figure the bank wanted and asked: measured and recorded? measured but not recorded? not measured at all? Grid electricity - twelve municipal bills, total only: MEASURED, POORLY RECORDED. Diesel - fuel-card statements, about 480,000 litres a year: MEASURED, NOT ALLOCATED (total known, split unknown). River water - one meter on the abstraction pipe: MEASURED, READINGS LOST (the meter works; nobody keeps the log). Electrode-paste carbon - nothing: NOT MEASURED AT ALL (needs a calculation method, not a meter). Injuries - a handwritten book: RECORDED ON PAPER (real, but not in a form you can total or audit). Recycled water - nothing: NOT MEASURED. The three piles told you exactly where to spend your fourteen days. You do NOT start by buying meters; you start by RESCUING the data that already exists - the bills, the cards, the river meter, the injury book - and being honest about the small number of things you genuinely cannot measure yet. Most of what the bank wanted was already being measured; it was just scattered, unsplit, and unstored - and naming which pile each number is in turns an impossible demand into a short, ordered list of jobs.
  • Set a success line, then run the cheapest probe that could break it: here was the belief you were quietly running on: the quick number - electricity plus diesel - is basically the real number, and the electrode paste is a small awkward extra you can wave away. You needed to know if that was true before you sent anything, because the whole 'improving trend' rested on it. So you set a success line IN ADVANCE, before pulling a single record: 'My quick number is safe to send only if the part I am leaving out - the paste carbon - is small enough to ignore, say under 5% of the total. If it is bigger, the quick number is a trap and I must count the paste now.' Then you ran the cheapest honest probe you could - no new meters, no consultant: you walked to the stores system and pulled one figure, how much electrode paste the plant bought last year (about 900 tonnes), and looked up the published carbon-content figure for that paste type. Two numbers, an afternoon. THE SURPRISE: the result broke your success line, hard. The paste added roughly 2,400 tonnes of carbon dioxide - not a rounding error but about 7% of the total, almost exactly the gut feeling you had feared. The quick number you had been ready to send was not a slightly-rough version of the truth - it was a TRAP that would have reversed your trend next year, making the plant look like it was getting dirtier when really you had just started counting honestly. So you stopped, recalculated the whole carbon figure with the paste included, and counted it NOW, this fortnight - the probe, not a hunch, told you it mattered.
  • Build each number with a stated quality, not false precision: for each figure, you wrote down not just the number but how you got it and how good it is - its data quality, the part an auditor lives or dies by. ELECTRICITY: the twelve bills summed to about 31 million kWh; you could not yet split it by furnace, so you said exactly that; carbon at the published national grid factor (about 1.0 kg of carbon dioxide per kWh) came to roughly 31,000 tonnes; quality GOOD (metered, billed, third-party records). DIESEL: 480,000 litres at the standard factor (about 2.7 kg per litre) is roughly 1,300 tonnes; you reported the total and flagged the truck/generator split as next year's work; quality GOOD TOTAL, WEAK BREAKDOWN. ELECTRODE PASTE: about 900 tonnes purchased times the published carbon-content figure, roughly 2,400 tonnes - the very gap your success line had told you to watch; quality ESTIMATED, said plainly. WATER: last year's readings found eventually in a supervisor's notebook, about 210 million litres; recycled water genuinely not measured, so reported as 'not yet measured' rather than zero, because zero is a lie and 'not measured' is the truth. INJURIES: counted with the safety officer, 4 lost-time injuries over about 1.9 million hours worked, a rate of about 2.1 per million hours, and you typed the book into a sheet so next year it totals itself. The total came to about 34,700 tonnes of carbon dioxide, or 0.83 tonnes per tonne of alloy - against a quick first guess of 32,300; the paste was the missing 2,400. And every number is reproducible by hand: 31 million kWh times 1.0 is 31,000 tonnes; 480,000 litres times 2.7 is about 1,300 tonnes; 900 tonnes of paste times the published factor is about 2,400 tonnes; the sum is about 34,700; divided by 42,000 tonnes of alloy is about 0.83. Nothing is a black box. The workings ARE the audit trail.
  • Match the assurance to each customer - give the auditor a trail, not a guess: the bank and the regulator wanted similar numbers but needed different ASSURANCE, and that decided what you sent each. To the BANK, in fourteen days, you sent the figures with a one-page data-quality note beside each: what is metered, what is estimated, what is not yet measured, and what you will fix by next year. A bank funding for years wants to see a plant that KNOWS where its numbers are weak - that is more fundable than false confidence, even though it takes nerve to send. To the REGULATOR, for the AUDITED return, you did NOT try to make every number audit-grade in six weeks. You made the strong ones audit-ready (electricity, diesel, water, injuries - all backed by bills, card statements, meter logs and a now-typed injury register) and you submitted the estimated ones (paste carbon, recycled water) with the calculation method attached and a dated improvement plan. An auditor can sign off an estimate when the method is transparent and consistent; an auditor cannot sign off a confident number with no workings. Same plant, same assets, running the whole time - and the difference between a number the auditor rejects and one they sign is not the size of the number, it is whether you can show where it came from. (Only learning you are failing when an outsider audits you is the related warning of bp-0150; here you turn the outsider's demand into the system that keeps you honest.)

See where it lands. Not a clean guess that detonates next year, but an honest, graded, traceable first return - the strong numbers audit-ready, the weak ones estimated with the method shown, and the awkward paste counted now. When someone with power demands numbers your operation was never built to report, do not fill in their form - build the first version of a small reporting system. List every number; sort each into measured-and-recorded, measured-but-not-recorded, or not-measured-at-all; rescue what already exists before buying a single meter; grade the quality of every figure in plain words and keep the workings, because the workings ARE the audit trail; match the assurance to each customer; and count the awkward number NOW so next year's honest measurement improves the trend instead of reversing it. The difference between a number an auditor rejects and one they sign is not its size - it is whether you can show where it came from. An honest estimate with its method shown is more defensible, and more fundable, than a confident number you cannot trace.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You have seen it worked once, in a smelter. Now take one step in a world that looks completely different - because the deep problem is the same, and your job is to spot it through the disguise.

See the software team asked to "just screenshot the dashboards"

You run a small software team, keeping a cloud service running for paying customers. A big new enterprise client will only sign if you hand over a set of operating numbers about your service: your uptime (the share of time the service was up), your average response time, the number of security incidents last year and how fast you closed each one, and who has access to customer data. Their procurement team wants it in ten days - and, here is the second customer, their security auditor will then independently verify whatever you send before the contract goes live. At first glance this feels easy. You are a software shop: everything is already measured - your monitoring dashboard shows uptime and response time in real time, your ticketing tool logs every incident, your cloud console lists every user. Nothing is in a handwritten book. So you think: I will just screenshot the dashboards and send them.

Sit with it before you do. What do you notice about the word "auditor" here? Look back at the smelter - there too, one customer wanted a form and the other wanted the numbers independently checked. It is the same two-customer shape. A screenshot of a live dashboard is fine for the procurement team. But an auditor cannot verify "99.9% uptime" from a screenshot - they need to know over what period, measured by what, and whether it can be reproduced from records six months from now. "Measured live" is not the same as "measured in a way someone outside can check later" - that is the audit trail again, in a different outfit. Now go down their list one number at a time, the way you went down the bank's. Most are in the smelter's middle pile - measured but not recorded usefully: uptime is DISPLAYED but never TOTALLED into a defensible yearly figure; incidents are LOGGED but never COUNTED and TIMED; access is VISIBLE in the console today but nobody kept a record of who had access last year. Live data on a screen is exactly like the river meter that nobody logs - the measurement happens, but it evaporates; it has to be collated and stored to count. And where is your electrode paste - the number you would quietly leave out? Almost certainly the security incidents, specifically the near-misses and the ones nobody formally logged: your tidy ticket list shows only what someone bothered to raise, the honest number may be higher, and "zero incidents" is the screenshot version of the smelter's clean carbon guess - it looks great this year and detonates next year when the auditor finds one you did not report. Just like the paste, you count it now, grade it estimated where the records are thin, and protect the trend. A different domain, a different scale, no handwritten books in sight, and yet the very same problem: "measured" is not the same as "collated, traceable, and checkable." Whenever a powerful customer demands numbers about how your operation behaves, sort them into the three piles, rescue what exists, find the awkward number and count it now, grade every figure honestly, and give the auditor an audit trail, not a screenshot. The disguise changes; the move does not.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The problem-type is far bigger than a smelter or carbon: someone with power over you - a funder, a regulator, a buyer, a head office - demands a set of numbers about how your operation behaves, and you discover your systems were built to run the work, not to REPORT on it, so the data either does not exist, exists but is scattered and unrecorded, or can only be guessed - yet the demand is real, dated, and tied to your survival. Find it where you are:

  1. Name the demand and its teeth. Who is asking you for numbers about your operation that you do not currently keep - a lender, a regulator, a big customer, a grant funder, a certification body? What happens if you cannot answer: lost finance, lost licence, lost contract, lost accreditation?
  2. Sort your required numbers into the three piles. For each, decide: measured and recorded, measured but not recorded, or not measured at all? Notice how many "impossible" numbers are really just scattered or unstored - already there, waiting to be rescued before you buy a single meter.
  3. Find your electrode paste. Where is the number you would quietly leave out because it is hard, embarrassing, or genuinely not measured? What is the danger of sending a clean number now that omits it, if next year you have to count it honestly?
  4. Grade the quality, match the assurance, do the smallest honest version. Beside one number you could produce today, write how you got it and how good it is (metered / estimated / not yet measured). Ask what each customer actually needs - "good enough to inform a decision" versus "good enough to be signed off". Then produce the strong numbers properly, estimate the weak ones with the method written down, name what you cannot yet measure, and date the fix.

You will know it worked when you can say whether the awkward number is small enough to omit or big enough that you must count it now. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
