The job grew the moment I opened it up - do I quietly do the bigger job, or stop and have the awkward conversation?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Omar, a builder, and you've given a customer a fixed price of R4,000 to fix a damp patch on their living-room wall: one day's work, one neat price, one finished wall - all agreed. You get going and knock off the old, damp plaster. The covers are off - and the problem is far bigger than anyone could see from outside: the wooden frame inside the wall is soft and rotten. Plastering over it would be pointless and wrong; the wall wouldn't be safe. To do it properly you must replace that wood - more materials, more work, more time.

The work is right there, open and ready. The customer's budget, and their week's plan, are not. You feel a strong pull to just be helpful, to be the expert who solves the problem - and at the same time the sick realisation that this extra work costs real money and time that were never in the deal.

The job just grew the moment I opened it - do I quietly get on with the bigger job to be helpful, or stop everything, break my momentum, and have an awkward conversation that might annoy them?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Three instincts rise, each feeling like a solution. The Hero - just absorb it: do the bigger job and sort it later, swallowing the cost or quietly adding it to the bill; it feels like great service and keeps things moving - but you're spending the customer's money and time without their consent, taking a decision that's theirs. The Jobsworth - stick to the letter: plaster straight over the rot, having "fixed the damp patch you were paid for"; technically correct, but you've knowingly left a hidden, serious problem and betrayed their trust. The Magician - hide it and cut corners: rush or use cheaper materials to squeeze the bigger job into R4,000; dishonest, poor, and it fails later.

Each path skips the one question that matters. So before you read on: who actually owns this new cost and time - and who therefore has the right to decide what happens next?

I agreed a fixed R4,000 to fix a damp patch. Absorbing it spends their money and time without consent; doing only the bare letter leaves them with a hidden problem; cutting corners betrays them. Each feels like service or self-protection - but who actually OWNS this new cost and time, and who should get to decide?
The moment the job grows past what you agreed, it's a change, not a continuation - and the decision belongs to the customer. Stop and make it visible (show them what you found and why it matters); cost it clearly as separate new price + new time; re-agree before you do it. The reframe: stopping to re-agree IS the service; silent absorption is the loss - even though it feels the other way round.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The moment the job grows beyond what you agreed, it's no longer the same job - it's a distinct change that must be made visible, costed, and agreed before you do the extra work. The cost of stopping for a conversation is small and happens now; the cost of not stopping is huge and arrives later - a blown budget, a broken schedule, broken trust. Five moves:

  • See it as a change, not a continuation: the second you saw that rotten wood, the original job plan ended - this is a new decision point, not 'more of the same'. Stop thinking 'I'll just carry on' and start thinking 'we need to agree on a change'. The growth carries real cost and time that belong to someone else's budget and plan, not yours to spend silently.
  • Stop and make it visible: pause the work. Take a clear photo of the problem, go to the customer, and show them plainly: 'this is the wood inside the wall - it's rotten, the real cause of the damp. If we just plaster over it, the problem comes back and the wall won't be strong.' They can't make a fair decision about something they can't see.
  • Cost the change clearly - new price, new time: give a specific number and a specific time for the EXTRA work, separately - don't mumble 'it might be a bit more'. 'Replacing the rotten frame properly is an extra R3,000 in materials and labour and an extra day and a half; the original job stays R4,000.' Clear and honest, so the choice is real.
  • Re-agree before you proceed: this is the crucial step: hand the decision back to its owner. Ask 'how would you like to proceed?' - they might say yes, no, or 'can it wait?'. Whatever they choose, get a clear agreement and record it: 'so we're agreed, the new total is R7,000, finished by Wednesday - I'll message to confirm.' The choice was always theirs to make.
  • The reframe - stopping IS the service: it feels like stopping is bad service and quietly getting on is good service - the truth is the exact reverse. Quietly doing the work steals the customer's choice and spends their money without permission; stopping to explain and ask for consent respects their money, their plan, and their right to decide - it proves you're trustworthy. (And changes are cheapest handled the moment you find them, dearest once buried.)

Watch how it plays out for Omar. He stops, photographs the rotten frame, and shows the customer: "this is the wood inside the wall - it's rotten, the real cause of your damp; plaster over it and it comes straight back." He costs the change clearly and separately: "replacing it properly is an extra R3,000 and an extra day and a half - the original job stays R4,000." Then he hands the decision back: "how would you like to proceed?" The customer chooses, eyes open, and they confirm it - new total R7,000, finished by Wednesday. It felt like stopping was bad service and quietly getting on was good service - but the truth is the exact reverse: quietly doing the work would have spent their money and stolen their choice; stopping to explain and ask is the highest service there is - it respects their money, their plan, and their right to decide, and it proves Omar can be trusted. Stopping to re-agree IS the service; silent absorption is the loss.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

The covers come off non-building jobs too - any agreed work that quietly grows. Watch a freelancer.

See the same move with a freelancer's "while you're at it..."

You're hired to write a 10-page report for a fixed fee - the job is agreed. Halfway through, the client emails: "while you're at it, could you also make a 20-slide presentation from it? And a one-page summary? And a few social-media posts?" Each request feels small, but together they're a whole new job.

• The trap: just do it all to be helpful, work through the weekend, and quietly grow resentful.
• The move: you pause and reply: "great ideas for getting more value from the report. The presentation, summary and posts are outside the 10-page report we agreed - I'd be glad to do them. I've scoped them: an extra R6,000, adding about three days to delivery. Shall I add that to the project?" You've seen the change, made it visible, costed it, and handed the decision back - served them with honesty, not resentment.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of a time an agreed task started to grow - a project at work, a favour for a friend, a job you were paid for:

  1. Name the original deal, and the moment it grew. What fixed cost, time or effort did you commit to - and at what point did "the covers come off" and the real job turn out bigger? Be honest about what changed.
  2. Name whose it is to decide. Whose budget, plan or trust was about to be spent without their consent? That person owns the decision - not you.
  3. Stop, make it visible, cost it, re-agree. Show them plainly what you found; put a clear, separate price and time on the extra; and get a fresh agreement before you do it.
R

Name the job that grew and how you'd re-agree it next time. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Practise the stop-and-show conversation together.)

The urge to just quietly do the extra work comes from a good place - a wish to be helpful and do a proper job. The discipline is to channel that helpfulness into honesty. Stopping to re-agree isn't a failure of service or a sign of being difficult; it's the mark of a true professional - it hands people back the choices that are rightfully theirs, and it builds a trust that outlasts any single job. The work will always be right there, open and ready. The other person's consent is the thing you must never quietly help yourself to.
