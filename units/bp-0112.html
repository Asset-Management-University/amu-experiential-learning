Every quote is a guess - and I always lose

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and Tank You has just won a real prize: the contract to supply and install gas systems - cylinders, regulators and piping to the stoves - across the new housing development. When they asked how long and how much, you did what you always do - you pictured it, felt it out, and said with confidence: "About two days, two of my guys." You quoted on that, and you won.

Then the job met the real world. The ground was harder than you pictured. A fitting was the wrong size and someone had to drive back for another. One site wasn't ready and your guys stood waiting. Two days became four; two guys became three. The job you were so proud to win quietly turned into a job that LOST money - you paid more to do it than they paid you for it.

And you know the other side of this trap too well. The last time you got burned like this, you over-corrected on the next quote - padded it thick to be safe - and lost the bid to a competitor who came in lower. So here is the cage you live in: quote too low and you bleed money; quote too high and you lose the work. Your guessing betrays you both ways.

Every quote I give is a guess, and I'm always wrong - I lose money when I'm low and lose the job when I'm high. How do I actually KNOW what a job will take?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Notice where your two-day number actually came from: your gut, your memory, and a quiet hope that it'd go smoothly. But your memory of past jobs is kinder than the jobs really were - it forgets the hard ground and the wrong fitting. Estimating from a rosy memory is why you're always short.

Before you read on: you don't have to guess the WHOLE job in one frightening lump. And you're not starting from nothing - you've done jobs like this before. So what could you do instead of guessing the total off the top of your head?

I guessed two days off the top of my head and it took four. The trouble is I'm estimating from hope and memory - and my memory of past jobs is far rosier than they really were.
A whole job is too big to judge in one go. But its pieces - site prep, the install, testing - aren't. And I actually have records of how long similar pieces took before, if I'd only use them instead of my gut.
So: break the job into pieces, price each from what it REALLY took last time, add an honest margin for surprises, and afterwards record what it actually took - so my next quote is truer still.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You replace one big hopeful guess with a small, grounded build-up. Four steps turn estimating from a gamble into a skill:

  • The trap: you can't win by guessing: guess LOW and the job you won loses money; guess HIGH (padding to feel safe) and you lose the bid to a rival. Guessing is guaranteed to be wrong - the problem is the method, not you.
  • Step 1 - break the job into pieces: a whole job is impossible to judge; its parts are not. Split it (e.g. site prep / the work itself / testing) and estimate the small, known pieces.
  • Step 2 - use what it REALLY took last time: estimate each piece from your RECORDS of similar past jobs, not your memory. Memory is an optimist - it always recalls things faster and cheaper than they truly were.
  • Step 3 - add an honest allowance: something unexpected always turns up. Add a fair margin for it - openly, on purpose. That's not dishonest padding; it's planning for reality.
  • Step 4 - record estimate vs actual, every time: afterwards, write down what it REALLY took against what you'd guessed. THIS is why you keep records - each job makes your next estimate truer.

Be clear about what this does and doesn't promise: it will not make you a fortune-teller. A wild gut-guess is often half-wrong; a grounded estimate is usually close - and gets closer every job as your records grow. The win isn't a perfect prophecy; it's moving from "always wrong, both ways" to "honestly close, and improving." As you learned before - these are estimates, not prophecies.

And notice the quiet gift hidden here: this is finally the answer to "why bother keeping all those records?" Your books and your job notes were never just history - they are the raw material for seeing the future a little more clearly each time.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So for the next install, Muhammad refuses to guess. He sits with his job notes and builds the number up.

See the gut-guess become a grounded estimate

The old gut-guess: "2 days." Priced for a tidy profit. (Reality, last time, was nearer 4 - which is exactly why that job lost money.)

The grounded build-up - broken into pieces, each from what it REALLY took:

• Site prep (mounting brackets, positioning the cylinders): last jobs ran ~1 full day, not the half-day he likes to imagine.
• The install itself (piping, regulators, fittings): ~1.5 days.
• Testing + fixing gas leaks: ~0.5 day.
• Subtotal: 3 days.

• Honest allowance for surprises (~17%): +0.5 day - a realistic ~3.5 days.

The result: he quotes the job at a fair price built on 3.5 days - high enough to actually profit, low enough to still win it against a padder who blindly doubled. It comes in close to budget. Then he writes down the ACTUAL (it took 3.5 days) next to his estimate - so the NEXT install he quotes will be truer still.

For the engineers: this is exactly how a real project or maintenance job is costed - break it into tasks, price each from historical actuals, add a stated contingency, then capture the real outcome to sharpen the next bid. Same method, bakkie to refinery.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The cage wasn't that you were bad at the work - it was that you were estimating the work by hope. Trade the hopeful guess for a grounded build-up, and you start winning fair-priced work that actually pays. For anything you have to estimate - a quote, a deadline, a budget, even time for a task:

  1. Break the job into smaller pieces. A whole job is hard to judge; its parts are not.
  2. Estimate each piece from recorded actuals - what it REALLY took last time, not your rosy memory.
  3. Add an honest, open allowance for surprises (say 15-20%) - planning for reality, never secret padding.
  4. Afterwards, record your estimate against the actual. That single habit makes every future estimate truer - it's why the records are worth keeping.
R

Take one thing you'll estimate soon, build it up from past actuals instead of guessing, and write down how it really turns out. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

Anyone can throw out a confident number. The professional - the one who wins good work and never bleeds on a job - quietly builds that number from reality, and lets every job teach the next. That is the difference between hoping and knowing.
