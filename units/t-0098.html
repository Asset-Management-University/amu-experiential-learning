Step 1 of 5

An honest map of how good you really are

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. By every measure you should feel good: you built the systems, you audited them, you passed. And yet your team is exhausted, and the feeling will not lift - that you are running flat out just to stay in the same place. A surprise pump failure here. A budget blown on unplanned work there. Always reacting.

Then, in a corridor, your manager asks the simplest question in the world: "We're clearly busy - but are we actually getting better? And if I gave you money to improve next year, where would you even put it?"

And you have nothing. Not because you don't care - because you have never honestly stepped back and measured how good Corathyl's asset management really is, area by area. You have been fixing whatever shouts loudest. You have compliance, but no compass. You are moving, but you have no map - so you cannot say where you are strong, where you are dangerously weak, or where one rand of effort would do the most good.

We are busy and we pass our audits - but I cannot honestly say how good we actually are, or where to aim our effort to improve. How do I turn "we work hard" into a real map of where we stand and where to go next?

Before you read on, decide. Corathyl passes its audits. Its maintenance is genuinely good — planned, scheduled, consistent, and the fitters are proud of it. Its asset data is poor and everybody knows it, though nobody has ever said so in a meeting. Risk gets looked at when something happens. Roles are broadly understood and written down nowhere. You have next year's improvement money to allocate, and the maintenance team has a well-argued proposal ready. Write down where you would put the money — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the best-argued proposal is the wrong one

• “Fund the maintenance proposal — it is the strongest case on the table.” It is the strongest case, and that is the problem. Maintenance is where the capable, articulate people are, so it produces the best proposals — and it is already doing what it needs to do. Money there buys polish on the one thing that is not broken.

• “Spread it evenly so every area improves a bit.” Fair-looking and it buys nothing anywhere: too little to lift the area that is genuinely weak, spread across areas that did not need it.

• “Fix whatever failed most recently.” That is what has been happening, described as a decision. It means your improvement programme is authored by chance.

• “Ask each area what it needs.” You will get honest answers, all of them larger than the budget, and they will be argued in proportion to each area's confidence rather than its need — which returns you to the first answer.

• “We pass the audits, so put it where it grows the business.” Passing an audit means meeting a floor. It says nothing about whether you are strong, and nothing at all about where you are weak.

• None of them can work, because none of them has the one thing needed to allocate anything: a picture of how capable each area actually is. And when that picture is drawn, two things about it are counter-intuitive. Each area is rated against how good it needs to be rather than against perfection — so an area already at the level its job requires is one where more effort is waste, however good the proposal. And the useful information is in the gaps rather than the scores: a high score tells you nothing to do. Drawn honestly here, the map would say something uncomfortable and specific — that the excellent, well-advocated area needs nothing, and the money belongs in the quiet one that everybody knows is poor and nobody has ever spoken for. The area with no advocate is exactly the area a map is needed to find.

There is a tool that draws exactly that map. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is Corathyl's asset management, measured honestly, on one page. Each area is rated for how capable it really is today, and how capable it actually needs to be. Read down it - and watch where the map tells you to aim.

1. Strategic Planning

How well our asset goals line up with what Corathyl is here to do. Current: 2, Developing (we have plans, not yet applied consistently). Target: 3, Competent. Gap: 1.

2. Asset Information

How accurate, complete and usable our asset data is. Current: 1, Aware (we know it is poor, no real plan yet). Target: 3. Gap: 2. >> PRIORITY: our biggest critical gap - bad data quietly undermines every other decision.

3. Maintenance Delivery

How we plan, schedule and carry out the work. Current: 3, Competent (systematic and consistent). Target: 3. Gap: 0. Already where it needs to be - do NOT pour next year's effort in here.

4. Risk Management

How we find and control our asset risks. Current: 2, Developing. Target: 3. Gap: 1.

5. Governance and Leadership

Look at the shape of it. Maintenance is already where it needs to be (a 3) - pouring more effort there would be polishing what is already done. But Asset Information is a 1 against a needed 3 - the biggest, most critical gap, quietly weakening every decision above it. The map just answered your manager's question for you: next year's money goes into asset information. That is the gift - not a score to brag about, but a direction. Now let us walk through how to draw it.

T3: The Blueprint

This is an Asset Management Maturity Assessment. It uses a recognised scale from the GFMAM / IAM (the global asset-management bodies) to rate how capable an organisation truly is, area by area. The whole idea: you cannot improve what you have never honestly measured.

The scale has six honest levels:

  • 0 - Innocent: we have not even recognised this is needed.
  • 1 - Aware: we know it matters, but have no real plan yet.
  • 2 - Developing: we have credible, resourced plans, and are working them.
  • 3 - Competent: we do it systematically and consistently. (This is the line that matches conformance to ISO 55001 - a solid, effective system.)
  • 4 - Optimising: we are actively tuning it to get more value.
  • 5 - Excellent: we are among the leading practitioners anywhere.

Now the method, each part as a question:

  • Where are we now? (Current) Rate each area honestly, against evidence, not hope. A "3" must be provable - systematic and consistent - not just a couple of good days.
  • Where do we need to be? (Target) Here is the move most people miss: you do not aim for 5 everywhere. Target is set by need and risk, not vanity. For a community works, "Competent" (3) is the right target for most areas - chasing "Excellent" on a low-risk area is just gold-plating, money burned. A higher target needs a real reason.
  • What is the gap, and which one first? (Priority) The gap is Target minus Current. Your priority is the biggest gap on the area that matters most - that is where effort pays. (At Corathyl: Asset Information, a 2-level gap on something everything else depends on.)

One area deserves a note: Governance and Leadership. A mature organisation has clear, separate lines of assurance - the three lines of defence: those who own and manage risk day-to-day (the operators), those who oversee and set the policy (the managers), and an independent audit that checks them both. At low maturity these blur into one person marking their own work; at high maturity they are distinct and effective.

Does it scale? Onalenna's one page rates Corathyl's handful of critical areas to aim a small team for two quarters. A national refinery runs the same assessment across all 39 asset-management subject areas, benchmarks itself against global peers, and uses the result to justify a five-year plan and secure hundreds of millions in capital. The same honest scale; vastly different stakes.

T4: Your Turn

Now you draw your own honest map. Pick a system you actually run - your study routine, a student society's finances, the stock-take at a part-time job, a team you lead. Choose three or four areas that matter for it (for example: Planning, Information/Records, Doing the Work, Managing Risk). For each, rate where you are now and where you honestly need to be, find the gap, and name one first action on your priority area.

Remember the honesty rule: a true "1" is worth far more than a flattering "3". A low score is not a mark of shame - it is simply your starting line, drawn clearly so you know which way to run.

T5: The Guardrails

A maturity map is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then strengthen any rating that needs it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Vanity rating. Scoring yourself higher than the evidence supports, to look good. It only fools you - and it aims your effort at the wrong place. An honest low score is the most useful number on the page.

• Targeting 5 everywhere. "Excellent" on a low-risk area is not excellence - it is gold-plating, money spent for no real gain. Set each target by need and risk. For most things, "Competent" is the right summit.

• Rating by feeling, not evidence. "It feels like a 3." Prove it: where is the systematic, consistent record? No evidence, no score above it.

• The map with no first step. A beautiful assessment that leads to no action is just decoration. The whole point is the priority gap and the one move you make on it.

• Mistaking busy for mature. Lots of activity can hide a low-maturity system - frantic firefighting is a sign of a "2", not a "4". Maturity is calm, consistent capability, not heroics.

Onalenna walked into that corridor with no answer. She walked out, a day later, with a one-page map that told her - and her manager - exactly where Corathyl was strong, where it was dangerously thin, and precisely where the next rand of effort would do the most good. That is what the tool gives you: not a grade to fear, but a direction to trust. Measure honestly, aim at the gap that matters, and you will never again confuse being busy with getting better.
