Step 1 of 5

The old bakkie needs an R18,000 repair - again - and a shiny new one is R280,000. How do I tell which is the cheaper FUTURE, not just the cheaper day, once I count every cost over its whole life?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning at the Tank You depot. You are standing next to the old delivery bakkie - the one the business has run for years - with the bonnet up. Tholo, the mechanic from down the road, wipes his hands on a rag and looks at you the way people look when the news is going to cost you money. "It's the gearbox this time," he says. "I can fix it. About R18,000, and I'll need the bakkie for four days." The smell of old engine oil and hot metal comes off the block, and you can hear the morning's first orders pinging on the phone in your pocket - gas someone is waiting for, that this bakkie was meant to deliver today.

In your hand is the repair book. It is not the first big bill: last year it cost R22,000 in repairs, the year before R15,000. The fuel is getting worse too - it now drinks about R4,200 a month in diesel. The bakkie would sell for maybe R60,000 today, in this condition.

Across the yard, your phone has a dealer's website open. A newer, more fuel-efficient bakkie is R280,000 - it would barely sip diesel, maybe R2,800 a month. One voice says: just buy the new one, be done with the breakdowns. Another says: R280,000? Are you mad? Pay Tholo the R18,000 and move on. Both feel right; both could be wrong. You are not comparing R18,000 against R280,000 - that is one bad day against one big day. You are really comparing two different futures: years of an old bakkie that keeps surprising you, against years of a new one you are still paying off.

I am not choosing between an R18,000 bill and an R280,000 price - I am choosing between two futures. How do I put both on the same table, in the same money, and see which actually costs less over its whole life?

Before you read on, decide. Repair the old bakkie for R18,000 and run it five more years: diesel about R4,200 a month, repairs averaging R20,000 a year and worsening, licence and insurance about R12,000 a year, worth maybe R10,000 at the end. Or replace it: R280,000, less R60,000 for the old one today; diesel about R2,800 a month, repairs about R5,000 a year, licence and insurance about R16,000, worth about R120,000 in five years. Money costs about 10%. Write down which future costs less, and how you would show it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two biggest numbers most of them leave out

• “Repair it. R18,000 against R280,000 is not close.” That sets one payment beside another payment and calls the result a decision. It is the sentence that keeps failing vehicles on the road for years.

• “Replace it — the new one drinks less and breaks less.” Both true, and it is a list of advantages rather than a comparison. Advantages cannot tell you whether they are worth R280,000.

• “Add up five years of each and see.” The right shape, and it treats a rand spent in year five as though it were a rand spent today — which is the whole reason two futures can look equal and not be.

• “Compare what each costs a month.” Useful and incomplete, because it quietly omits the two largest single numbers on the page: what you pay on day one, and what the thing is worth when you let it go.

• “Keep repairing until it dies, then decide.” A decision made by default. The repair bill is already worsening, so postponing the choice does not preserve it — it makes it more expensive.

• The move is to stop comparing payments and compare futures: the same window for both, every cost each one causes across it, including the ones that never arrive as an invoice — the fuel difference month after month, and the resale, which is money coming back rather than a cost. Bring every future rand into today’s money so the two are the same kind of number, and only then add them up. And when you have an answer, push on it: a comparison like this usually turns on one or two assumptions — how fast the old bakkie’s repairs are climbing, what diesel does — and finding which one it hangs on tells you exactly where the real argument is.

You need a reliable way to put both futures on the same table, in the same money, so you can see which one actually costs you less over its whole life - not just today. You need a method, not a gut feel. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is whole-life costing (WLC, also called total cost of ownership) fed into a net-present-value comparison. Count every cost an asset causes from the day you get it to the day you let it go - not just the sticker price - then shrink each future cost to today's money (discounting) and add them up, so two very different options can be compared fairly. Watch it run on the bakkie decision, the way Ibraaheem and Muhammad would at the depot table.

Step 1 - Name the options, set the window and the rate

Option A: repair and keep the old bakkie (pay R18,000 now, run it five more years). Option B: replace it (buy the newer bakkie for R280,000 now, sell the old one today for R60,000). The fair window is 5 years - about how much longer the old bakkie could realistically last - and the discount rate is 10%. The choice is not R18,000 versus R280,000; it is two five-year futures.

Step 2 - List every whole-life cost (not just the sticker)

Option A (repair and keep): R18,000 now; diesel R4,200/month (R50,400/year); future repairs about R20,000/year (it has averaged R15k to R22k and worsens); licence and insurance about R12,000/year; resale about R10,000 at year 5. Option B (replace): R280,000 now, minus R60,000 from selling the old one; diesel R2,800/month (R33,600/year); repairs about R5,000/year; licence and insurance about R16,000/year; resale about R120,000 at year 5.

Step 3 - Lay out the yearly cash flows

Option A: year 0 = R18,000 (the repair); years 1 to 5 = R50,400 + R20,000 + R12,000 = R82,400 each, and year 5 also carries the -R10,000 sale, so R72,400. Option B: year 0 = R280,000 - R60,000 = R220,000; years 1 to 5 = R33,600 + R5,000 + R16,000 = R54,600 each, and year 5 also carries the -R120,000 sale, so -R65,400 - a net inflow that year.

Step 4 - Discount to present value, and sum to NPV

At 10%, the factors are 1.000, 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683, 0.621. Discounting each year and summing: Option A's NPV is R324,085; Option B's is R352,415. So repairing is cheaper by about R28,000 in today's money. The loud 'R280,000 versus R18,000' made replacing feel insane - yet once fuel and repairs are counted over five years, the gap is only R28,000, not R262,000. The quiet costs nearly closed it.

Step 5 - Sensitivity check (how fragile is the answer?)

Two guesses do the heavy work. If the old bakkie's repairs run R35,000 a year instead of R20,000, that adds about R56,900 in today's money, pushing Option A to about R381,000 - now MORE than B: the decision flips. A diesel jump widening the gap by R1,000 a month flips it too. So the honest answer is 'repair - but only just, and only if the old bakkie behaves': repair this time, but if repairs in the next 12 months top about R30,000, replace without hesitation.

See what the method did. The loud numbers - R280,000 against R18,000 - screamed that replacing was insane; the quiet costs of fuel and repairs, counted over five years and brought into today's money, nearly closed the gap to R28,000. And then the sensitivity check did the most honest thing of all: it showed the answer is a knife-edge that flips if the old bakkie's repairs climb a little. This is the mission in it: a small business's money is hard-won and scarce, and the two ways to waste it are equal and opposite - the shock of a big sticker price that scares you out of a sound buy, and the false economy of a cheap repair that bleeds you for years. Whole-life costing defends against both by making every cost, loud and quiet, stand in the same daylight. And the sensitivity check keeps you humble: a tidy number built on guesses is an illusion, but knowing how fragile that number is - and exactly what to watch - is the real wisdom. Money is the measure of the right long-term choice here, not the master; the aim is a clear-eyed decision a family can trust.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the appraisal in five moves.

  • 1. Name the options, set the window and the rate. Two options; the years both must cover; the discount rate.
  • 2. List every whole-life cost. Capital, fuel, repairs, insurance, licence - and resale as a minus.
  • 3. Lay out the yearly cash flows. Each cost in the year it happens; money-in is negative.
  • 4. Discount to present value, and sum to NPV. Shrink each year to today's money; add up; the lower total wins.
  • 5. Sensitivity check. Stress the big guesses; see whether the answer flips.

The ideas underneath. Whole-life costing (WLC / total cost of ownership) means adding up every cost an asset causes across its life, because the sticker price is the loudest number but the quiet running costs are where the real money usually lives. Money in the future is worth less than money today (you would rather have R1,000 now than in three years), so to compare fairly you discount: a cost of R100 one year away at 10% is worth R100 / 1.10 = R90.91 today, and a cost N years away is divided by (1 + rate) multiplied by itself N times. The discount rate is your yearly cost of waiting - near what borrowing costs, often 8 to 12% for a small business. A future amount shrunk to today is its present value; the sum of all an option's present values is its net present value (NPV), and the lower NPV is the cheaper future - comparing options this way is investment appraisal. This fits choices that run over several years, where the options have very different cost shapes (cheap-to-buy-but-costly-to-run versus the reverse) and the money matters; it is not worth the maths for a small, short-lived, one-off buy, or when you genuinely cannot estimate the future costs (get better numbers first - a tidy calculation on wild guesses just hides the guessing).

Two disciplines keep the appraisal honest. First, chase the quiet costs harder than the loud one - the sticker price is easy and already shouting; it is the fuel, the next repair, the insurance, year after year, that decide the answer, and a whole-life cost that skips them is just a sticker price wearing a suit. Second, never trust a single NPV without stressing it - your numbers are guesses about the future, so change the one or two that matter most and watch what happens; if a small, plausible change flips the decision, the honest output is not "do B" but "it is a knife-edge - do B for now, and watch this number", which is far more useful than false certainty.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your whole-life costing true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The sticker price is the loudest, not the largest. A big purchase price or a sharp repair bill grabs the eye; the fuel and repairs over years usually decide the money. Count the quiet costs.

• Future money is worth less - so discount it. A rand in year five hurts less than a rand today. Bring every future cost into today's money before you compare, or you are adding apples to oranges.

• Resale is a real, negative cost. What you get back at the end - a trade-in, a sale - is money in. Put it in the right year, as a minus, or you will overstate the cost of the option that holds its value.

• Compare the same years. Both options must cover the same window, or the longer one looks falsely dearer. Same window, same rate, same money - then the totals mean something.

• A number you have not stressed is a guess in disguise. Change the big assumptions; if the answer flips, say so. The fragile truth beats the confident illusion every time.

You came to the depot with a gearbox bill, a tempting new bakkie, and two voices that both felt right. You can leave with one clear table: both futures, in today's money, showing repair wins by about R28,000 - and the honest knife-edge, that the win holds only if the old bakkie's repairs stay below about R30,000 next year. The goal is to choose the cheaper future, not the cheaper day - and to know exactly how fragile that answer is. That is how a small business keeps its hard-won money working for it over years: not by fearing the big price or trusting the cheap fix, but by putting every cost in the same daylight and watching the few that could change the answer.
