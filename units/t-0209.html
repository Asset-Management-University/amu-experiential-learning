A date is not a plan

T1: The Ache

It is six o'clock in the morning. The crew is on site, boots on, ready for the pump-three overhaul that has been on the schedule for a week. And nothing happens.

The seal drawn from the store is the wrong size. No isolation permit was raised, so the pump cannot legally be opened. The special puller needed to withdraw the rotating element is out on another job across the plant. And the work order, when someone finally reads it properly, says four words: "overhaul pump 3."

You are the planner, and you did put this job on the schedule. You gave it a date, a crew, a slot. You believed that was planning it. But a date told the crew when to arrive; it did not make the job ready for them when they got there - and now eight skilled people stand in the yard, on the clock, waiting for a plan that was never actually made.

I scheduled the job, so why is the crew standing idle? What is the difference between a job that has a date and a job that is actually ready to be done - and whose job is it to make it ready?

Before you read on, decide. Six o'clock. Eight fitters in the yard, on the clock, for a pump overhaul you scheduled — a date, a named crew, a slot. The work order says four words: “overhaul pump 3.” The seal kit was never drawn from stores. There is no permit. The line has not been isolated. Nobody has the drawings and the access platform is still on the other job. You are the planner and you put this job on the schedule, and you believed that was planning it. Write down what would have made this job actually ready — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the three acts hiding in one word

• “Send someone for the parts now and start late.” Today's rescue, and it means eight people paid to wait while one drives to stores. It also guarantees the same morning next month, because nothing about how the job was prepared has changed.

• “The supervisor should have checked the night before.” Pushes readiness onto somebody with no time and no authority to draw parts or raise permits, and it will work occasionally, which is worse — it hides the gap without closing it.

• “Give the crew more notice.” More warning of an unready job. They would arrive earlier at a site with no permit and no isolation.

• “Write longer work orders.” Closer, and a fuller description of the work still does not draw the parts, raise the permit or book the platform. The document is a symptom; the missing thing is the preparation it should describe.

• “I did plan it — it was on the schedule with a crew.” This is the belief that put them in the yard, and it is worth stating plainly: a date is the scheduler's product. It tells the crew when to arrive and does nothing to make the job ready for them.

• Three different acts hide inside the one word. Planning answers what needs to be done and how — tasks, parts, tools, skills, permits, isolation, drawings, access. It is technical thinking and it looks at one job in depth. Scheduling answers when it happens and by whom — fitting ready jobs into time and people. It is logistical thinking and it looks across many jobs in time. Executing is doing it. These are genuinely different kinds of work with competing horizons, which is why in a larger team they become different people. And the part most often misread is what the planner does during execution: not picking up a spanner, but having made this job ready beforehand and now working ahead on the jobs that follow — because a planner pulled into today's execution stops producing tomorrow's ready work, and the yard fills again next week. Your deliverable was never the date. It was a job that can be started the moment the crew arrives.

Here is what planning really is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is what a maintenance planner actually produces - separating planning from scheduling, making the job ready, setting the two scopes, working the process, and sequencing the activity list.

1. Plan, schedule, execute - three different acts

START BY SEPARATING THREE THINGS THAT HIDE INSIDE THE ONE WORD 'PLANNING', BECAUSE BLURRING THEM IS EXACTLY WHAT PUT THE CREW IN THE YARD AT SIX O'CLOCK. PLAN WORK ANSWERS WHAT NEEDS TO BE DONE AND HOW - the technical content of the job: the tasks, the parts, the tools, the skills, the permits, the isolation, the drawings, the access. It is technical thinking, and it looks at one job in DEPTH. SCHEDULE WORK ANSWERS WHEN IT HAPPENS AND BY WHOM - the logistics: fitting ready jobs into time and people. It is logistical thinking, and it looks ACROSS many jobs in time. EXECUTE WORK is doing it. These are genuinely different kinds of work, which is why, as a team grows, the planner and the scheduler become different people - the skills and the time horizons compete when combined. THE MISTAKE THAT CAUSED THE DISASTER WAS TREATING 'IT IS ON THE SCHEDULE' AS 'IT IS PLANNED'. A date is the scheduler's product. It tells the crew when to arrive; it does nothing to make the job ready for them when they get there. AND THE PLANNER'S ROLE DURING EXECUTION IS THE ONE PEOPLE MOST MISREAD: IT IS NOT TO PICK UP A SPANNER. It is to have made the job ready beforehand, and then, while the crew works, to keep it ready (clear a blocker, confirm a part) and to CAPTURE WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED - the real hours, the surprise found under the cover, the extra part nobody expected - so the NEXT plan is better. A planner pulled onto the tools is a planner not planning the next job. Planning is the opposite of the reactive scramble: it looks forward and prepares, so the work can be calm.

2. The planner produces a READY JOB, not a date

IF A SCHEDULER PRODUCES A DATE AND A TECHNICIAN PRODUCES A REPAIR, ASK THE SHARP QUESTION: WHAT DOES THE PLANNER PRODUCE? THE ANSWER IS A READY JOB. Concretely, it is a WORK PACKAGE, and it has a definite content: the breakdown of the tasks and how long each should take; the procedure for how to do each task correctly; the safety and risk requirements; ALL the parts, equipment, services and materials; and the technical information - the drawings, the specifications, the acceptance limits. THE TEST OF A READY JOB IS BRUTAL AND SIMPLE: WHEN THE CREW ARRIVES, IS EVERYTHING THERE TO DO THE JOB RIGHT THE FIRST TIME - the correct parts in hand, the right tools available, the permit already raised, the line ready to isolate, and clear steps to follow? If yes, it was planned. If the crew has to stop and hunt for a seal, wait for a permit, or walk to another job to borrow a puller, it was not planned - it was merely named and dated. THIS IS WHY A JOB IS NOT 'PLANNED' THE MOMENT IT HAS A DATE; IT IS PLANNED ONLY WHEN IT IS READY TO BE SCHEDULED - complete, with nothing missing that would stop the crew starting. Mark it ready-to-schedule only when it truly is, because a job marked ready that is not ready is a promise the plant cannot keep. AND HERE IS THE DIGNITY OF IT: THE SIX-O'CLOCK FAILURE WAS NOT THE CREW BEING CARELESS OR THE PLANNER BEING LAZY. IT WAS A JOB GIVEN A DATE BEFORE IT WAS MADE READY - A SYSTEM CONDITION, NOT A PERSONAL FAILING - and the fix is a better planning practice, not a harder-working person.

3. Scope of work vs job scoping - two different magnitudes

THIS IS THE DISTINCTION THAT QUIETLY DECIDES WHETHER A PLAN HOLDS, AND MOST PLANNERS NEVER NAME IT - SO NAME IT. SCOPE OF WORK IS THE BOUNDARY OF THE WHOLE BODY OF WORK - everything a shutdown or project does and does not include. IT IS WIDE: these forty jobs are in, those are out; the pump and its motor are in, the downstream pipework is a separate job. It answers 'what are we doing overall, and what is explicitly NOT ours?' JOB SCOPING IS THE DEPTH OF ONE JOB - exactly what a single task needs. IT IS NARROW: this pump, these six steps in order, this seal kit, this torque figure, this permit, ninety minutes. It answers 'how, precisely, is this ONE thing done?' THEY ARE DIFFERENT MAGNITUDES OF THE SAME DISCIPLINE - ONE IS THE CAMPAIGN, THE OTHER IS THE TASK - and the mistake is to do one and think you have done the other. YOU NEED BOTH, AND EACH FAILS IN ITS OWN WAY WHEN SKIPPED. Skip the SCOPE OF WORK and the job grows without limit, because nobody drew the boundary, so once the covers come off the work swallows the budget and the schedule - that is scope creep, and it is a scope-of-work failure. Skip the JOB SCOPING and the crew arrives to missing parts, because nobody detailed the work, so the day is lost to the store and the tool crib - that is the six-o'clock disaster, and it is a job-scoping failure. A useful picture: the scope of work is the guest list and menu for the whole feast - who is coming, what dishes are on and off; the job scope is the recipe for a single dish - the exact ingredients, steps and timing. You cannot cook the feast with only a guest list, and you cannot cater it with only one recipe. NAME WHICH MAGNITUDE YOU ARE WORKING AT, AND DO BOTH.

4. Work the planning process in order

PLANNING IS NOT ONE ACT BUT A SHORT SEQUENCE, AND DOING IT IN ORDER IS WHAT ACTUALLY MAKES THE JOB READY. IDENTIFY the work - the request, the inspection finding, the routine that has come due. SCOPE it - and here you WALK THE JOB DOWN, you physically go and look, because the drawing does not show the seized valve, the access that is not there, or the scaffold that must go up before anyone can start; the best estimate comes from someone who has stood in front of the actual asset. DETAIL the tasks, the parts, the tools, the skills, the permits and the access. ESTIMATE the durations and resources honestly, built from the steps rather than guessed. PACKAGE it into a complete work order and MARK IT READY TO SCHEDULE - but only when it truly is, with nothing missing. Then HAND IT TO SCHEDULING for a date and a crew (that is their act, not yours). SUPPORT the execution without doing it. And CLOSE OUT AND FEED BACK - update the asset's history and record what was really found, so the next plan starts from the truth rather than from the same hopeful assumptions. THAT LAST STEP IS WHERE PLANNING COMPOUNDS OVER TIME, and it carries a rule that keeps it humane and useful: CAPTURE WHAT WAS FOUND, NOT WHO WAS AT FAULT. The scored shaft discovered under the cover is data to improve the next plan - a fact to anticipate next time - never a reason to blame the person who found it or the person who missed it. A planning process that hunts for fault stops getting honest feedback, and a planning process with no honest feedback stops improving.

5. Compile the activity list in execution sequence

THE THING THAT TURNS A SCOPED JOB INTO AN ACTUAL PLAN IS THE ACTIVITY DESCRIPTION LIST - AND THE ONE RULE THAT MAKES IT WORK IS THIS: COMPILE IT IN THE SEQUENCE THE WORK PHYSICALLY HAPPENS, NOT IN ANY OTHER ORDER. Walk the job in execution order: raise the permit and isolate, then lock out and prove dead, then drain, then dismantle, then inspect and measure, then repair or replace, then reassemble, then align, then test, then return to service and sign off. WHY THE ORDER MATTERS SO MUCH: THE SEQUENCE IS WHAT REVEALS WHAT YOU NEED AND WHEN. You cannot know which parts to order until you have walked to the 'inspect and measure' step and seen what wears; you cannot know the permit is needed until you have put 'isolate' first; you cannot estimate the time until the steps are laid out in order. A LIST COMPILED OUT OF EXECUTION ORDER - grouped by trade, or by whatever came to mind first - HIDES THE VERY DEPENDENCIES THAT MAKE THE JOB PLANNABLE, and you end up ordering the wrong parts, missing a permit, or discovering at the machine that step four had to come before step two. The sequenced activity list is the engine of readiness: from it fall the parts, the tools, the permits, the skills and the honest time estimate, in the order the crew will actually need them. Get the sequence right and the work package almost writes itself; get it wrong and you have planned a different job from the one that will actually be done. LIST THE WORK IN THE ORDER IT HAPPENS, AND LET THE SEQUENCE TELL YOU WHAT THE JOB NEEDS.

T3: The Blueprint

The planner's job is not to put a date on the work; it is to make the work ready for a date. A scheduled job is not a ready job. Five questions:

  • Plan, schedule, or execute? Three different acts. Plan = what and how. Schedule = when and who. Execute = do it. Putting a job on the schedule is not planning it.
  • Is the job ready? The planner produces a ready job - a work package so complete the crew arrives to every part, tool, permit and step already there. Ready-to-schedule only when complete.
  • Scope of work, or job scope? Two magnitudes. Scope of work is the boundary of the whole body of work; job scoping is the depth of one task. You need both.
  • What is the process? Identify, scope, detail, estimate, package, then hand to scheduling, support, and feed back. Planning looks forward and prepares.
  • In what order do I list the activities? The sequence the work actually happens. Isolate before dismantle, inspect before you order, test before you hand back - the sequence reveals what you need.

Planning is not scheduling, and neither is execution. Three different things hide inside the one word "planning," and blurring them is what put the crew in the yard. Schedule work answers when it happens and by whom - the logistics of fitting ready jobs into time and people. Execute work is doing it. These are genuinely different kinds of thinking: planning is technical and looks at one job in depth; scheduling is logistical and looks across many jobs in time. And the planner's role during execution is the one people most misread - it is not to pick up a spanner. It is to have made the job ready beforehand, and then to capture what actually happened - the real hours, the surprise found under the cover, the extra part nobody expected - so the next plan is better. Planning is the opposite of the reactive scramble: it looks forward and prepares, so the work can be calm.

The planner's product is a ready job. If a scheduler produces a date and a technician produces a repair, what does the planner produce? A ready job. Concretely, a work package: the breakdown of tasks and how long each should take, the procedure for how to do each one, the safety and risk requirements, all the parts and equipment and materials, and the technical information - drawings, specifications, acceptance limits. The test of it is brutal and simple: when the crew arrives, is everything there to do the job right the first time - the correct parts, the right tools, the permit already raised, the line ready to isolate, and clear steps to follow? A job is not "planned" the moment it has a date; it is planned only when it is ready to be scheduled - complete, with nothing missing that would stop the crew. The six-o'clock failure was not a scheduling mistake and it was not the crew being careless. It was a job that was given a date before it was made ready - a system condition, not a personal failing, and the fix is a better planning practice, not a harder-working person.

Scope of work and job scoping - two different magnitudes. This is the distinction that quietly decides whether a plan holds, and most people never name it. Scope of work is the boundary of the whole body of work - everything a shutdown or project does and does not include. It is wide: these forty jobs are in, those are out; the pump and its motor are in, the downstream pipework is a separate job. It answers "what are we doing overall, and what is explicitly not ours?" The six-o'clock disaster was a job-scoping failure; the shutdown that doubles in size is a scope-of-work failure. Name which magnitude you are working at, and do both.

The planning process, in order. Planning is not one act but a short sequence, and doing it in order is what makes the job ready. Identify the work (the request, the inspection finding, the routine that has come due). Scope it - and here you walk the job down, go and look, because the drawing does not show the seized valve, the access that is not there, or the scaffold that must go up first. Detail the tasks, parts, tools, skills, permits and access. Estimate the durations and resources honestly. Package it into a complete work order and mark it ready to schedule only when it truly is. One honest boundary. Readiness is a product, and a product takes time to make. A planner cannot turn a one-line request into a ready job with no hours in the day to plan it - if the system does not give the planner time to plan, it will not get ready jobs, and that is a resourcing decision, not the planner's failing. The role only pays back - and it pays back several times over - when it is given the time to do the work of readiness before the crew arrives, not squeezed in between emergencies.

Does it scale? A student who writes "revise for exam" on Monday has scheduled nothing and planned nothing. Planning it is scoping the work (which topics are in, which are already known and out), scoping each session (this chapter, these three problem sets, ninety minutes), and sequencing it (the foundational topic before the one built on it). A date in a diary is not a plan; a ready, scoped, sequenced set of sessions is - and the exam and the pump overhaul succeed on exactly the same distinction.

T4: Your Turn

Here is a real one. A work order lands on your desk. In full, it reads: "Overhaul gearbox on Conveyor 04." That is the whole plan. It already has a date - Thursday - and a crew. Make it ready.

  1. Name the three acts. For this job, say in one line each what plan work, schedule work and execute work mean - and which one has actually been done so far.
  2. Draw the boundary (scope of work). State what is in this job and name at least two things that are explicitly out (and are therefore separate jobs, not free additions once the covers are off).
  3. Scope the job (job scoping). Compile the activity description list for the gearbox overhaul in the correct execution sequence - the order the work physically happens.
  4. List what falls out. From that sequenced list, name the parts, the tools, the permit, and the time estimate the job needs. (Notice you could not know these until you had the sequence.)
  5. Close the loop. Describe the planner's role while the crew is executing on Thursday, and name one thing they must capture to make the next gearbox plan better.

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the three acts. Plan work = decide what the gearbox overhaul needs and how it is done (tasks, parts, tools, permit, steps). Schedule work = decide when (Thursday) and who (the crew). Execute work = the crew doing it on the day. Only the scheduling has been done - the job has a date and a crew but no plan, which is the whole trap: a scheduled job masquerading as a ready one.

Second, the boundary. In: the gearbox on Conveyor 04 - isolate, remove, strip, inspect, replace worn internals, refit, test. Out (separate jobs, not to be swept in when the covers come off): the conveyor belt itself, the drive motor's alignment and internals, the structural rollers, the electrical supply beyond isolation. Naming these out is what stops the Thursday job quietly turning into a three-day rebuild - the scope-of-work boundary is the defence against scope creep.

Third, the sequence - listed in the order the work truly happens, for example: raise permit and isolate (electrical and mechanical) -> lock out and prove dead -> drain the oil -> disconnect couplings and guards -> remove the gearbox to the workshop (or strip in place) -> open and inspect gears and bearings -> measure and record wear -> replace worn gears, bearings and seals -> reassemble to spec -> refill with the correct oil -> refit and re-couple -> align -> test run and check for noise, heat and leaks -> return to service and sign off. A list in any other order plans the job wrong.

Fourth, what falls out of that sequence: the parts (gear set, bearing kit, seals, the correct grade and quantity of oil, gaskets), the tools (lifting gear for the gearbox, pullers, alignment equipment, torque wrench), the permit (electrical and mechanical isolation, lock-out), and an honest time estimate built from the steps, not guessed. The point to see: you could not list the parts and tools until you had walked the sequence - the sequenced activity list is the engine that produces the readiness.

Fifth, the loop. On Thursday the planner does not pick up a spanner - they made the job ready, and now they keep it ready (clear a blocker, confirm the part) and then capture what was actually found: the real hours the job took, and the surprise under the cover - say the input shaft was scored, not just the bearings. That goes into the asset history and the next plan, recorded as what was found, not who was at fault, so next time the scored shaft is anticipated, not rediscovered at six in the morning.

T5: The Guardrails

A job on the schedule is not a job that is ready. The date tells the crew when to come; only the plan lets them start.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Believing a scheduled job is a planned job. A date is the scheduler's product; a ready job is the planner's. Putting work on the calendar without making it ready is the six-o'clock disaster in the making.

• The one-line work order. "Overhaul pump 3" is a wish, not a plan. If the crew cannot start from what is written, it was never planned - only named.

• Confusing scope of work with job scoping. One is the boundary of the whole body of work; the other is the depth of a single task. Skip the first and the job grows without limit; skip the second and the crew arrives to missing parts.

• Compiling the activity list out of order. The list must follow the sequence the work actually happens - isolate before dismantle, inspect before you order. Any other order plans the job wrong and hides what you need.

• The planner doing the work. The planner's job during execution is to have made it ready and to capture what happened - not to pick up a spanner. A planner pulled onto the tools is a planner not planning the next job.

• Skipping the walkdown. The drawing does not show the seized valve or the missing access. Scope the job by going and looking, or you will scope it wrong from a desk.

• Capturing no feedback. If nobody records what was really found, the next plan repeats today's miss. Capture what was found, not who was at fault - it is data for the next plan, not blame.

• Blaming the crew for a readiness failure. An idle crew at six is a process that failed to ready the job - a system condition, not a personal failing. Fix the practice, not the person.

The crew stood idle at six o'clock because the job had a date but was never made ready, and the planner had mistaken scheduling for planning. Plan is not schedule and neither is execute - the planner's product is a ready job, a work package so complete the crew arrives to every part, tool, permit and step already there; set the two scopes, the wide boundary of the whole body of work and the deep detail of the single task, and do both; work the process in order - identify, scope by walking it down, detail, estimate, package to ready, hand off, support, and feed back what was found; and compile the activity list in the sequence the work actually happens, because that sequence is what reveals the parts, the tools and the time. The planner's job is not to put a date on the work; it is to make the work ready for a date - and where the plan honestly needs more preparation time, that is the plan doing its job, not the planner failing at theirs. Next time a one-line work order lands on your desk, do not schedule it - ready it: scope it, sequence it, and make it so complete that the crew who arrives has nothing to do but the work.
