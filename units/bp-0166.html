Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sanele, the new finance director for the municipality. The report on your desk is for the old water-treatment plant - a concrete giant that has served the town for forty-five years. It is reliable, but it is nearing the end of its design life. You flip to the appendix: "End-of-Life Cost Estimate".

The number makes you sit up straight: R150 million. That is the estimated cost to safely decommission the plant, remove the structures, and remediate the site to modern standards. The plant has another fifteen years of service, at most.

You pull up the asset register. The plant's current value on the books is only R20 million. You start searching the accounts, digging back through years of financial statements, looking for the "Decommissioning Fund". There is not one. For forty-five years the town has enjoyed the benefit of the plant, but nobody ever set aside a single cent for the clean-up. The bill for the ending is coming. It is huge, it is certain, and the treasury is empty.

The cost of shutting this down safely is coming - it is huge, it is certain, and we never saved a cent for it; how did we let this liability creep up on us, and what do we do now?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your mind races through the easy options, the ways to make this someone else's problem. "Ignore it - it is fifteen years away." Let the next finance director, the next council, handle it. But this only makes it worse: the R150 million liability exists NOW, whether it is on the books or not, and kicking the can just shortens the time you have to prepare - wait ten years and you must find R30 million a year for the last five, an impossible sum. Deferring does not make the bill smaller; it makes the eventual panic bigger. "Just abandon it when it stops working." Why not lock the gates and walk away? Because the engineers' report is clear: that would be illegal and dangerous - residual chemicals and unsafe structures mean the regulator comes with huge fines, the clean-up order still stands, and the obligation and its cost follow you, landing on the taxpayer anyway, but as an emergency, not a plan. "We will find the money when the time comes." Take out a huge loan in fifteen years? But you would be borrowing a fortune for an activity that produces zero income - the plant will be dead. It is the worst possible time to ask a bank for money; it is like trying to get a giant mortgage for a funeral.

None of the easy answers work - you cannot ignore it, dodge it, or magic the money up later. So before you read on: how do you face a giant, certain, future cost NOW?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop seeing the plant's life in two acts (build and operate) and start seeing it in three. An asset's life has three acts: birth, work, and death - and only fools budget for two, because the bill for the third act, the ending, always arrives. The fix is not to find the money in a panic at the end; it is to make the asset pay for its own ending, a little at a time, while it works. Five moves:

  • Name the liability - dying costs money: the first move is to drag the cost out of the shadows and name it as what it is: a real, certain, and often enormous future LIABILITY. Every asset will one day die - be decommissioned, removed, its site remediated, its materials safely disposed of - and that ending has a price tag, here R150 million, that does not become real only when the day arrives; it is real now. The reason it creeps up on people is precisely that it is large and far off, so it gets filed under 'future, not my problem' and never spoken of - and a cost nobody will name is a cost nobody will fund. So put the number on the table and on the agenda: 'the safe ending of this plant will cost about R150 million, it is coming, and right now we have nothing set aside.' Naming it honestly is uncomfortable, and it is the only thing that makes everything that follows possible; you cannot provision for a liability you will not admit you have.
  • Think whole-life - cradle to grave, not cradle to gate: the deeper error is measuring an asset's cost from cradle to GATE (what it costs to buy and run) instead of cradle to GRAVE (what it costs across its entire life, including its ending). An asset's true cost has three acts - birth, work and death - and judging it on only the first two flatters cheap-to-buy, expensive-to-retire assets and punishes the honest. A plant, a landfill, a mine, a fuel-storage site that was 'cheap' to build but ruinous to clean up was never cheap at all; the saving was an illusion borrowed from the future. So whenever you choose or compare assets - and certainly when you choose the NEXT plant to replace this one - put the whole-life cost, end-of-life included, into the decision from day one. The cheapest asset to buy is often the most expensive to own, and the bill comes due exactly when you can least afford it.
  • Admit the liability exists from day one: this is the truth the empty Decommissioning Fund was built on denying: the obligation to safely end the plant began the very moment it was built, and it has been sitting on the books - acknowledged or not - growing with every year of use, for forty-five years. Pretending it was not there did not make it smaller or further away; it simply converted a manageable, fund-able liability into a looming shock. An honest set of accounts shows what you OWE as well as what you own, and a decommissioning obligation is a debt as real as any loan - one you owe to the future. So put it on the balance sheet, in the open, today: not to frighten anyone, but because a liability you can see is one you can plan for, and a liability you hide is one that ambushes whoever is unlucky enough to be in the chair when it finally lands. Honesty about what you owe is the foundation of facing it.
  • Provision steadily - make the asset fund its own ending: here is the cure, and it is gentle if you start in time: instead of scrambling for a catastrophic lump at the end, set aside a small, regular sum over the asset's remaining working life, funded by the value the asset earns while it still works - so the plant pays for its own funeral, a little at a time. The arithmetic is simple and kind: R150 million needed in fifteen years is R10 million a year, set aside now from the revenue the plant helps generate - a real cost, but a survivable one, and nothing like the impossible R30-million-a-year scramble of someone who waited until five years were left. This is a sinking fund, or a decommissioning provision, and its logic is the same whether the asset is a municipal plant or a household roof: the person who got the benefit of the asset is the person who pays, fairly, for its safe conclusion - not some unlucky successor. Start small, start now, and let time and the asset's own earnings do the work.
  • Get a real estimate and revisit it: a provision is only as honest as the number under it, so build it on a proper, professional estimate of the end-of-life cost - not a hopeful guess that quietly leaves you under-funded - and then keep that number alive. Costs do not stand still: clean-up standards tighten, disposal and remediation get more expensive, technology and regulations change, and the estimate that looked right ten years ago is usually too low today. So revisit it every few years, have it re-estimated, and adjust the annual provision up (or occasionally down) so it stays on track to meet the real bill when it comes. This keeps the plan from drifting into a comfortable fiction - a fund that feels responsible but is quietly falling short. The discipline is simple: a true estimate, a matching provision, and an honest review, repeated - so that when the ending finally arrives, the money is genuinely there, and the promise you made to the future is one you actually kept.

See Sanele face it honestly. She takes the plan to the council with a hard, clear message: "This is not a new cost. It is an old cost we have been ignoring. Do we set aside R10 million a year now, or face a R150 million bankruptcy in fifteen years?" It means R10 million less for other things each year, and it is not a popular message - but framed as the truth it is, the council agrees. The Decommissioning Provision is created, funded each year from the revenue the plant helps generate, and for the first time the asset starts paying for its own funeral. The liability that was creeping up in the dark is now a known number with a plan against it. Only fools budget for two of an asset's three acts. The bill for the ending always arrives - so the wise make the asset pay for its own funeral, a little at a time, while it still earns. Name the liability honestly, judge assets cradle-to-grave not cradle-to-gate, admit the obligation exists from day one, provision steadily against a real estimate, and revisit it - or be ambushed by an unfunded liability that can cost more than the asset was ever worth.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - it is not just for giant plants. Think about the roof on a house.

See the asset that funds its own new roof

You know that in about twenty years the roof will need a complete replacement, and it will cost a fortune - say R120,000. You could ignore it for nineteen years and then face a sudden, stressful R120,000 bill the day it starts leaking, scrambling for the money or going into debt at the worst moment.

Or you could be wise and create a "roof fund". Divide the cost by the time you have: R120,000 over twenty years is R6,000 a year, which is R500 a month. You put R500 into a separate savings account every month, and when the roofer hands you the bill in twenty years, you simply pay it - no panic, no debt. The house quietly "paid" for its own new roof over its life. A municipal plant, a delivery fleet, a factory machine: every one of them is that roof, only with more zeros. The ending always comes; the only question is whether you saved for it while the asset earned.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This principle of provisioning is about facing the true, whole-life cost of things - it is financial honesty. Where in your world is there a huge, certain, future cost you are not saving for? A business asset reaching its end, a vehicle that will one day need replacing or costly disposal, the roof on your house, a major piece of equipment:

  1. Name the cost. Find one big future liability and, being honest, write down what it will cost to deal with when the time comes - even a rough guess.
  2. Admit it is a liability now. That future cost is a debt you already owe - to your future self or your organisation. Acknowledge it instead of hiding it.
  3. Start a provision. Work out a small, regular amount to set aside now (the cost divided by the years you have). Open a separate account, or just track it on a spreadsheet, and start making the regular "payments".
  4. Revisit the estimate. Once a year, look at your number, ask if it is still realistic, and adjust your provision up or down as needed.

Name the big future cost you have not been saving for, the rough size of it, and the small regular sum you will start setting aside now. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Size one real end-of-life liability and design its provision together.)

This is not just good accounting - it is a way to make life easier for the people who come after you: your colleagues, your community, your family, or even just your future self. You are choosing not to leave them a financial mess to clean up, and to face the true cost of things from beginning to end. Every asset has three acts; budget for all three, and the ending becomes a plan instead of an ambush.
