T1: The Ache

You are standing on the wet, gritty floor of a raw-water pump station, a thick maintenance file open across the railing in front of you. Through the open door you can hear the deep hum of the duty pump pulling river water up to the treatment works, and the air smells of damp concrete and chlorine. This station feeds clean water to 80,000 people. If both pumps stop, the town's reservoirs run dry in about 14 hours.

The file is the current maintenance plan, and every line was copied from the pump supplier's manual years ago: grease every month, overhaul every 12 months, replace bearings every 2 years. No one has ever asked why. The station foreman, Zwelibanzi, stands beside you with his arms folded, tired of it. "We strip that pump down every year whether it needs it or not," he says. "Half the time the parts we throw out are fine. But last winter the one part that did fail - the seal - was nowhere on the schedule. We were doing plenty of work. None of it stopped the breakdown that mattered."

You look at the numbers and your stomach tightens. Last year the station spent R310,000 on planned maintenance and still had two unplanned shutdowns - one of which nearly emptied the reservoir before the standby pump came online. Some tasks on this list are pure waste. Some real failures have no task at all. And one of them, on a hot December day, would leave the town with no water. You cannot keep guessing.

Before you read on, decide. The pump’s job is to deliver at least 200 l/s at 45 m head to a town of 80,000. There is a duty pump and a standby. Four things can go wrong: a bearing seizes and the pump stops dead; a seal fails, sprays, floods the bearing housing and causes a second failure; the impeller wears and output falls slowly; and the standby’s start relay fails — which nobody would notice, because nothing happens until the day it is needed. If the duty stops and the standby does not start, the town is dry in 14 hours. The current plan came off the manual years ago. Write down how you would decide what to actually do — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the failure that is different from the others

• “Follow the manual — the makers know the machine.” They know the machine; they do not know this river, this grit, this duty, or that there is a standby behind it. The manual is written for every installation and therefore for none.

• “Do everything more often. Be safe.” Spends more on the tasks that are already not preventing the failures, and opens healthy machines more often — which causes its own failures.

• “Look at what has failed before and maintain those things.” Sensible and backward-looking, and it will never catch the relay, because the relay has never visibly failed. It is failing silently right now for all anybody knows.

• “Rank the four failures by cost and start at the top.” Nearly right, and it treats consequence as one thing. A bearing seizure is expensive and obvious; the relay costs nothing on its own and is catastrophic in combination.

• “Put condition monitoring on the pump.” A good answer to some of these failure modes and no answer at all to a relay that either works or does not when asked.

• The move is to stop treating the manual as the input and treat the plan as the output — worked out one failure mode at a time, from what this pump must do here. Start from the job, not the machine; name each way the job can stop; then ask what actually happens when it does, because that is what decides whether any task is worth its money. And one of these four is a different species: the standby relay fails invisibly. Nothing announces it, nothing suffers from it alone, and its only consequence appears in combination with another failure — which is precisely why no history and no inspection round will ever surface it, and why it needs a task whose whole purpose is to find it rather than to prevent it. That is the failure that was on no list, and it is the one that nearly cut the town’s water.

The answer is to stop treating the manual as the input and start treating the maintenance plan as the output - of asking, for this pump in this river, what can fail, what it would cost, and which task (if any) is worth doing. Here is that tool, worked on this very pump.

T2: The Tool in Action

The plan in your hands has the classic disease: maintenance built on guesswork - tasks that exist because a manual said so, never tested against what actually fails. The cure is Reliability-Centred Maintenance (RCM): work one failure mode at a time through four questions, and let the answer fall out. Watch the same four boxes turn four different failures on this pump into four different, correct tasks.

Box 1 - Function and functional failure (start from what it must DO)

Function: pump at least 200 l/s of raw water at 45 m head. Functional failures: pumps less than 200 l/s (partial loss); pumps nothing (total loss); and for the standby, fails to start within 30 seconds on demand - and note, nobody sees that last one until the day it is needed.

Box 2 - Failure mode and effect (the specific cause, and what actually happens)

FM3, bearing seizes: grit-worn bearings lock the shaft, the pump stops SUDDENLY; the standby should start - if it does the town is fine, if not, 14 hours to dry taps. (The seal-failure mode is sneakier: it sprays water, floods the bearing housing, and a small failure causes a bigger one - that was last winter.)

Box 3 - Consequence - ASK IN ORDER

The standby relay failing is HIDDEN - nothing visible happens, and on its own it harms nothing; but combined with the duty bearing seizing it means no water to 80,000 people (a multiple failure - the nightmare). The bearing seizing is OPERATIONAL-high (total loss of the duty pump, the standby the only net). The impeller wear is OPERATIONAL (gradual, measurable).

Box 4 - The task decision (applicable AND effective - or an honest default)

Each consequence earns a DIFFERENT answer. Hidden relay -> FAILURE-FINDING: test-start the standby weekly (you cannot prevent it and cannot let it run to failure, so you check whether it has already failed - this closes the exact gap that nearly emptied the reservoir). Bearing -> ON-CONDITION: monthly vibration check (it warns for weeks), replacing the wasteful 'replace every 2 years'. Impeller -> ON-CONDITION: monitor flow weekly, act below 210 l/s. Seal -> SCHEDULED restoration at 18 months + a daily operator leak check.

Look at what the logic did. The same four questions gave four completely different answers - a vibration check, a flow check, a scheduled seal change, and a weekly standby test - because each failure has a different consequence, and the consequence decides the task. The most important move is Box 3's order: asking "is it hidden?" first caught the standby relay - the silent failure that, paired with a duty-pump seizure, was the exact nightmare that nearly emptied the reservoir. No habit-built plan would ever have found it, because nothing about it is visible until the day it matters. Maintenance stopped being a list copied from a manual and became the output of asking what can fail and what it would cost.

T3: The Blueprint

You build an RCM analysis with a small team who know the kit - the operator, the maintainer, the engineer - and real failure history, not one person alone at a desk. First write down the operating context (the same pump in a different world gets a different plan: here, silty water, a standby, 14 hours of reservoir). Then walk each failure mode through the four boxes.

  • 1. Function and functional failure. State what it must do as a number ("pump at least 200 l/s"), then the specific ways it fails to meet that. A function with no number cannot fail in a way you can measure.
  • 2. Failure mode and effect. The specific cause (bearing seizes, seal leaks, impeller worn by grit), then what actually happens, step by step - drawn from history and the people who run it, not the manual.
  • 3. Consequence - asked in order. Is it hidden? then safety/environment? then operational? then just repair cost? The order is the engine of the whole method - a hidden failure is dangerous precisely because it waits for a second failure to turn it into a disaster.
  • 4. The task decision. Pick the task that is both applicable (it physically works for this mode) and effective (worth its cost given the consequence) - or an honest default.

RCM has the full discipline behind it - the seven questions of the standard SAE JA1011 (functions, functional failures, failure modes, effects, consequences, tasks, default actions), with the guide JA1012 - but the four boxes above are its working heart. Two of its answers surprise people, and both are honest. Doing nothing can be right: run-to-failure means deciding, on purpose, to let something cheap and harmless fail and fix it then. And for a hidden failure you cannot use any on-condition task - you would not know it had failed - so you use a failure-finding task: periodically test whether it has already failed, often enough that the chance of it and the thing it backs up both being failed at once is acceptably small. RCM fits equipment whose failure has real safety, environmental or large production consequences; it is overkill for a light bulb, it is time-consuming, and it is only as good as its inputs - guess the failure modes and you get a confident, wrong plan.

The test of a good RCM line is honest: each box must change what you do. If Box 3 says "hidden", Box 4 must be a failure-finding test, not a calendar overhaul. If Box 3 says "cheap and harmless", the right answer may be to stop a task you are paying for and let it run to failure. Do a few of the failures that frighten you most, with the people who run the kit in the room, and you will end up doing less work that does not matter and more of the work that prevents the failure that would actually hurt someone.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your RCM honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Ask "is it hidden?" first, always. The consequence order is not decoration - it is the engine. A hidden failure (a standby that will not start, an alarm that will not sound) harms nothing on its own, which is exactly why it is missed; its danger is the second failure that finds it. Catch it first or you have not done RCM.

• A function needs a number. "Pump well" cannot fail in a way you can measure. "Pump at least 200 l/s at 45 m head" can. Without a performance standard, every later step is guesswork wearing a worksheet.

• A task must be applicable AND effective. Applicable means it physically works for this mode (a calendar overhaul does nothing for a random electrical failure); effective means it is worth its cost given the consequence. Many habitual tasks pass neither - and "we have always done it" is not a reason.

• Doing less can be the right answer. Run-to-failure is a legitimate, deliberate choice for cheap, harmless failures - not laziness. Spending money to prevent a failure that costs almost nothing is its own kind of waste.

• It is only as good as its inputs. RCM needs real failure history and the people who run the kit in the room. Guess the failure modes or the consequences and you get a confident, wrong plan - and it describes the equipment as it is now: change the duty or the design, and parts of the analysis must be redone.

The old plan was not lazy - it was just unexamined, every line inherited from a manual written by someone who never saw this river, this grit, this duty. What RCM gives you is the discipline to ask why of every task, and to let the consequence - not the calendar - choose the answer. A world-class maintenance strategy is not a list of tasks copied from a manual; it is the output of a rigorous, evidence-based analysis that links every action to a specific failure and its consequence. Get that right and you stop stripping good pumps while the failure that matters waits, unwatched, for a hot December. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one real asset and its failure history, and work three failure modes through the boxes together.)
