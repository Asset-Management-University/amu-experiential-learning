Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Ayesha, and you run a small community clinic you're proud of. You did the responsible thing: for a year you collected every patient feedback slip from the suggestion box - a thick folder of them. Now you have a small budget to make one big improvement, so you open the folder and do the sensible thing: you count. Of 200 slips, 90 - almost half - mention the "long wait time". It's by far the biggest, loudest complaint, so the decision seems obvious: you spend the whole budget on a new check-in system to speed things up.

Three months later, your patient numbers are still dropping. Confused, you go back to the folder - but this time you don't just count, you read. Tucked inside are twelve slips that say something different: "the receptionist was rude and dismissed my pain"; "I couldn't get my mother's wheelchair through the door"; a few about a doctor too rushed to listen. And it hits you: the people who complained about waiting were annoyed, but they came back. The people who felt dismissed, or couldn't physically get in - they never returned, and they told everyone why. You had the data all along.

I collected all this information and used the obvious number - so why did it lead me to fix the loud problem and not the one quietly breaking trust, and how do I get the data to tell me what actually matters?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

When the decision hits a dead end, the first instinct is to collect even more data - but you don't have too little information; you asked it the wrong question, and a thousand more "long wait" slips would only shout louder and drown the quiet, important truths. So you think about giving up on the records and trusting your gut - but your gut has its own blind spots (it didn't know about the wheelchair or the new receptionist), and binning the slips throws away your patients' real experiences. The commonest move is to keep doing the same thing - grab the biggest number, the highest reading, the average. But that's the trap: the data didn't lie - those 90 "wait" complaints were real - they just answered the question "what is the most common complaint?", when your decision needed the answer to "what problem is costing us our patients' trust?"

So before you read on: the data was true and you used the obvious number, yet it fixed the wrong thing - what question did your decision actually need, that the easy number never answered?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Raw data - a pile of records, a list of numbers - is not a decision. Turning one into the other is a deliberate skill: stop reaching for the easy, obvious number, and instead figure out the exact question your decision needs, then build that answer from your data. Five moves:

  • See the trap - the easy number answers the wrong question: the first, biggest, or average number is tempting because it feels simple and definite - but it quietly answers ITS OWN question ('what's most frequent?', 'what's the middle value?'), which is rarely the question your real-world decision actually needs. Reaching for it feels like analysis; it's really just grabbing what's easy to see.
  • Frequency is not importance: the most COMMON thing is not always the most IMPORTANT thing. At the clinic 'long waits' happened most often (high frequency), but 'feeling dismissed' mattered more (high importance) because it's what drove people away for good. Count is not weight - you must learn to weigh your records by what they mean for the decision, not just tally how many there are.
  • A high reading is not a change: a number that has always been big is just background; a number that has suddenly appeared or started to climb is a signal. If 'long waits' is always around 90, that's a steady state - but if 'rude receptionist' slips only started three months ago, that CHANGE is the real story. Your decision often needs 'what's new / what's getting worse?', not 'what's biggest?'.
  • Name your question, then ask the data - and read the words: before you touch the records, say the decision out loud ('choose one thing to fix to stop losing patients'), then turn it into a sharp question for the data - not 'what's the biggest complaint?' but 'which complaints are shared by the patients who didn't return?' or 'what's new lately?' - and sort/compare to answer THAT. And don't ignore the WORDS: 'rude' and 'couldn't get the wheelchair in' aren't numbers; the richest answer - the why - hides in what people took the time to write.
  • Stay honest - the answer is a claim, not the truth: the answer you build is a powerful CLAIM, not the absolute truth: it's only as good as your records, it narrows where you look but never replaces the looking, and the final 'what matters most' may still need YOU to weigh a value the numbers can't see. Treat it as a light that shows where to look next - it informs your judgement, it doesn't replace it.

See how it would have saved Ayesha's budget. Her real decision was "fix one thing to stop losing patients", so the question was never "what's the most common complaint?" but "which complaints do the patients who left share?" Ask the data that - and read the words - and the twelve slips about dignity and access leap out as the true story, where the 90 about waiting were a steady background annoyance people forgave. She'd have spent the budget on a ramp, a wider door, and kindness at the desk - and kept the families she was there to serve. Raw data is not a decision: the easy number answers the wrong question, frequency is not importance, and a high reading is not a change - so name the question your decision needs, ask the data THAT (and read the words), and treat the answer as a claim that informs your judgement, never replaces it.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about feedback slips - the same trap hides in any pile of records. Watch a teacher.

See the same move with a class's test results

A teacher has 30 pupils' test results and must decide what to re-teach.

• The easy number: she works out the class average - 65%. "Not bad," she thinks, and plans to move on. The average answered "how did the class do overall?"
• The question her decision needed: "what topic must I re-teach to help the pupils who are struggling?"
• Ask the data that: she ignores the overall average and looks question by question - and finds 10 pupils all scored zero on the four questions about fractions. A third of her class is completely lost on a key topic, a fact the comfortable 65% average completely hid.

Same skill: the average wasn't wrong, it just answered the wrong question - asking the right one found the ten pupils about to be left behind.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You have a body of records - feedback forms, a breakdown log, a year of sales notes, test marks, a staff survey - and a decision is coming. Before you count anything, stop, and resist grabbing the easy number (the biggest count, the highest reading, the average):

  1. Name the decision, then the exact question. Say out loud what you must decide, then turn it into the sharp question your data must answer - not "what's biggest?" but the one your decision truly needs.
  2. Check what kind of question it is. Do you need what's most common (frequency) or what matters most (importance)? The current level, or a change? And what's in the words people wrote, not just the counts?
  3. Ask the data that - then weigh it. Build the comparison or sort the records to answer that question. The answer is a powerful claim, not the absolute truth - a light that shows where to look next, informing your judgement, never replacing it.

Collecting all that data was the responsible, caring thing to do, and reaching for the biggest number is the most natural move in the world - almost no one is ever taught to do otherwise. But a number can only answer the question it was built to answer, and your decision deserves its own. So before the count, find the question; weigh what matters, not just what is loud; read the words people trusted you with; and let the answer guide your judgement rather than stand in for it. That is how a folder of slips becomes a clinic that keeps its families - how information, asked the right question, becomes a decision that truly serves.
