Step 1 of 5

The inspector has stopped my boiler over a corroded patch and the code only tells me how to build a new one - how do I work out whether this used, thinned vessel is still safe to run, by Monday?

T1: The Ache

Ngobese Laundry washes and presses linen for three hospitals and two clinics. Fourteen people work there. Everything in the building runs on steam from one horizontal receiver in the boiler house, bought used in 2009.

The insurance inspector came on Thursday. He found a corroded patch on the lower shell, measured it, and left a written prohibition: the vessel may not be operated until a competent person demonstrates it is fit for service. He was doing his job properly and he was right to do it.

You are that competent person, and it is Friday afternoon.

On the bench is the original manufacturer's data: design pressure 10 bar, shell 8.0 mm when new. The design code is beside it, and here is what stops you - the code tells you how to build a new vessel. It has a great deal to say about the material, the welds and the thickness a fresh shell must have. It has nothing at all to say about a sixteen-year-old shell with 5.1 mm left in one patch.

Mrs Mtolo, who owns the laundry, is standing in the doorway not saying anything, which is worse than if she were. Three hospitals have no clean linen from Monday. Fourteen people are paid weekly. A replacement receiver is about R400,000, which the business does not have, and the quotation is eleven weeks anyway.

And you can feel both wrong answers pulling at you. You could condemn it - safe for you, and it closes the laundry. You could sign it off because it has run for sixteen years and looks alright - and it is a pressure vessel a few metres from where people work.

The code tells me what a new vessel must be, and this one is used and thinned. How do I work out whether what is left is enough for what it actually has to do?

Before you read on, decide what you would do by Monday. A prohibition notice, 5.1 mm in the worst patch, 8.0 mm when new, fourteen jobs, three hospitals, no money and eleven weeks' lead time. Write down what you would do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one is really resting on

• "Condemn it. It has lost more than a third of its wall." Defensible, protects you completely, and rests on a comparison with what it was built as. Hold on to the number in that sentence, because a third sounds fatal and the built thickness is not the safety limit - and if that is wrong, this answer closes a business and empties three hospital linen stores for no reason.

• "Sign it. Sixteen years and it has never leaked." Reaches for history, and history is genuinely evidence about the past. It is not a statement about the wall that is there now, and it is the answer that gets people hurt - a vessel gives no warning that it is on its last millimetre.

• "Get a specialist assessment done." Correct if this turns out to be difficult, and it takes weeks and money the laundry does not have. It also assumes the question is hard before finding out whether it is - and a great many of these turn out to be answerable in an afternoon.

• "Weld a patch over it." The instinct of a practical person, and it is a repair to a pressure part which needs its own procedure, qualification and inspection - and cannot be designed until somebody has established what thickness is actually required.

• "Average the readings across the patch - 6.4 mm sounds much better." Nobody says this out loud and the pull is real, because it is a friendlier number that makes the problem disappear. It would be a measurement of a vessel that does not exist. Walls fail where they are thinnest and nowhere else.

• What every one of these is missing is a single number nobody in that boiler house has: the minimum thickness this shell actually needs for the pressure it actually carries. Until that exists, "5.1 mm" is a fact with nothing to compare it against - and every answer above is a judgement about a margin whose size is unknown.

T2: The Tool in Action

Measure what is left at the worst point. Work out the minimum needed for the duty it is actually doing. Compare them, and say plainly whether it passes today. Then use two measurements in time to say how long that stays true - and remember you can ease the duty instead of replacing the thing. Watch it run in the boiler house.

Step 1 - Measure what is actually left, at the worst point, not the average

The shell was 8.0 mm when new. Nomvula Ngobese takes ultrasonic readings on a grid across the corroded patch on the lower shell and records the worst of them: 5.1 mm, not the 6.4 mm average across the patch. She writes down the instrument, the calibration block, the date and her own name. The temptation to use the average is real - it is a friendlier number and it would make the whole problem go away - and it would be a measurement of a vessel that does not exist.

Step 2 - Work out the minimum this thing actually needs for the duty it is doing

For this shell, at 10 bar, in this material and diameter, the minimum required is 4.6 mm. The 8.0 mm it was built with included a deliberate 3.4 mm allowance for exactly the corrosion that has now happened - it was designed to be eaten. Nomvula puts the two numbers side by side on one line, and the room changes: what is left is 5.1 mm and what is needed is 4.6 mm. Nobody in that laundry had known there was any margin at all.

Step 3 - Compare them, and say plainly whether it passes today

5.1 mm against 4.6 mm required: 0.5 mm of margin remaining. So the vessel is fit for service at 10 bar today, and Nomvula can write that down and sign it. She also writes what the screen is not: a simple comparison at the worst point, deliberately conservative, which is why passing it is meaningful and why failing it is not the end of the story.

Step 4 - Work out how long that stays true, and set a date

The 2021 inspection recorded 6.3 mm at the same patch; today it is 5.1 mm. That is 1.2 mm in five years, about 0.24 mm a year. From 5.1 mm down to the required 4.6 mm is 0.5 mm, so roughly two years. Nomvula's verdict is therefore not 'it is fine' but: fit for service at 10 bar, re-measure the same grid in twelve months, and have a replacement vessel budgeted and ordered inside two years. Two years is a plan. 'It is fine' is not.

Step 5 - Remember you can change the duty instead of the item

Nomvula asks what pressure the laundry actually needs, and it turns out the presses run happily at 8 bar - 10 bar was simply what was set. At 8 bar the minimum required drops to about 3.7 mm, so from 5.1 mm there is 1.4 mm to lose rather than 0.5, and at 0.24 mm a year that is nearly six years instead of two. Turning down a pressure-control setting costs nothing and buys four years to save for the replacement. The vessel did not change. What was asked of it did.

See what one comparison did. The shell was built at 8.0 mm and needs 4.6 mm - so the designer deliberately put 3.4 mm there to be eaten, and 2.9 mm of it has gone. What is left is 5.1 mm against 4.6 mm required: fit for service today, and nobody in that laundry had known there was any margin at all. Then the two findings that turn a verdict into a plan: about two years before it reaches the minimum, so a re-measure at twelve months and a replacement budgeted inside two - and the presses only need 8 bar, which lowers the bar the damaged wall has to clear and buys nearly six years instead of two, for the price of turning down a setting. Fourteen jobs and three hospitals' linen were resting on a number that took an afternoon to produce. It was not a favour to anybody. It was the honest answer, and it happened to be the kind one.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the second is the one that changes everything.

  • 1. Measure at the worst point, never the average, and record who measured it and how.
  • 2. Work out the minimum required for the actual duty - not the thickness it was built with.
  • 3. Compare, and say plainly whether it passes today. If it does not, no further arithmetic changes that.
  • 4. Get a rate of loss from two measurements in time, and set the date the margin runs out.
  • 5. Ask what easing the duty would buy before condemning the item.

What you have done is a Level 1 fitness-for-service screening - the first and cheapest tier of a formal framework for judging damaged equipment. Its central distinction is the one the room did not have: the as-built thickness is what was fitted, and the minimum required thickness is what the duty demands, and the gap between them is the corrosion allowance, which exists to be consumed. The measured loss over time gives a corrosion rate, and the years until the remaining material reaches the minimum is the remaining life - which is the actual output, because a pass with no date attached is not a decision. Lowering the duty to lower the minimum required is derating, and it is the move most often forgotten because attention is on the item rather than on what is being asked of it.

It fits any damaged thing whose duty you can quantify - vessels, pipes, structures, ropes, floors, tanks. Two limits matter. It is deliberately conservative, which is what makes it trustworthy: passing it means something, and failing it does not mean the item is unsafe - it means this cheap screen cannot clear it, and a deeper assessment may still do so honestly. Where the damage is a crack rather than general thinning, this screen is the wrong instrument altogether and the assessment in t-0063 is where that goes. And it says nothing about why the damage is happening: a vessel found fit for two more years while whatever is corroding it continues unexamined has been assessed, not looked after.

Two disciplines keep the assessment honest, and the first will be the hardest thing you do all week. Use the worst point, and refuse the average even when the average would solve everybody's problem. There will be a moment in that boiler house, with a business owner in the doorway and fourteen jobs in the room, when 6.4 mm is available and true and would let you sign - and it describes a vessel that does not exist, because walls fail at their thinnest point and nowhere else. Second, never issue a pass without a date and a re-measurement. A fitness assessment is a statement about a wall on the day it was measured, and it quietly becomes a permanent clearance the moment it is filed without an expiry. The date is the part that protects people; the verdict is only the part that lets them work today.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the verdict true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• As-built is not the safety limit. New items carry deliberate allowance on top of the minimum. Judging remaining material against what it was built with condemns sound equipment - and it is the single most common error here.

• The worst point, always. Averaging across damage describes something that does not exist. It is a friendlier number and it is not a measurement.

• A pass without a date is not a pass. Two measurements give a rate, a rate gives a date, and the date is the output. File a verdict without one and it becomes a permanent clearance nobody revisits.

• Failing this screen does not condemn the item. It is deliberately conservative. Failing means the cheap question cannot clear it, and a deeper assessment might - which is a different piece of work, not a formality.

• A crack is not thinning. This screen handles loss of material. Cracks behave differently and are assessed differently; using this method on one gives a confident wrong answer.

• Ease the duty before condemning the item. The minimum required follows the load, so a lower pressure, lighter load or cooler temperature lowers the bar. It is frequently free and frequently forgotten.

• Being fit is not being cared for. Nothing in this assessment asks why the damage is happening. A vessel cleared for two years while the cause continues has been measured, not looked after.

You went into that boiler house on a Friday afternoon with a prohibition notice, a code that only describes new vessels, fourteen people's wages and three hospitals' linen, and both easy answers pulling at you. You can come out with one signed page: 5.1 mm at the worst point against 4.6 mm required, fit for service at 10 bar today, about two years of margin at the measured rate of loss, re-measure the same grid in twelve months - and a note that running at 8 bar, which is all the presses need, turns two years into nearly six. This is how a used and damaged thing gets judged on what it actually has to do, rather than condemned for no longer being new or passed for having survived. The honest answer and the kind one are not always the same. It is worth knowing how to find out which you are holding. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
