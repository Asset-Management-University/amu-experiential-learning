Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sibonelo, the depot manager for a regional railway. It is a wet Tuesday, and you are walking through the "boneyard" at the far end of the yard - the place where old equipment goes to die. The stores controller has just called with bad news: the last spare traction motor for a mainline locomotive is faulty, a new one is R180,000 with a four-month lead time, and that locomotive is now officially out of service, crippling your ability to move freight.

As the rain runs down your neck, your torch beam lands on a familiar shape half-sunk in the mud: a traction motor, identical to the one you need, covered in rust and bird droppings. It was pulled from a locomotive six months ago. You know, with a sickening certainty, that it was probably repairable - for a fraction of the cost of a new one it could have been sitting on the shelf, ready. Instead it is scrap. Your R180,000 problem is rusting in a puddle while your critical locomotive stands idle.

You look around at the acres of silent, rusting metal.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is anger: "have a big clear-out" - get the scrap merchants in and clear this mess. The yard looks tidy for a week, but you have thrown the baby out with the bathwater: that repairable traction motor is now melted down, its value gone forever, and you still have to buy the new one. Your second instinct is the opposite, fear: "keep everything - nothing leaves the depot without my signature". Soon every corner of the depot overflows with removed components. Nothing is logged, nothing can be found; a working pump might be in one of a hundred unlabelled crates, but you will never find it when you need it - the value is just as lost as if you had scrapped it. Your third attempt is a memo: "all staff must store removed components correctly." It changes nothing - a fitter who has just finished a twelve-hour shift in the rain will not carefully label a two-ton gearbox; he puts it where it is easiest, which is out of the way.

The problem is not the people; it is the lack of a system. So before you read on: the question is not "how do I get people to be tidier?" - it is "how do we make it easy to do the right thing with a part the moment it comes out?"

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop seeing a removed part as rubbish and see it for what it is: value sitting at a fork in the road. The moment a component comes out of service is not a disposal; it is a decision. You build a simple system around that decision. Five moves:

  • See it - value sitting at a fork, not waste: the breakthrough is to stop seeing a removed component as rubbish and see it for what it is: value sitting at a fork in the road. The moment a part comes out of service is not a disposal - it is a decision, and the lazy default of 'chuck it in the yard' silently makes the worst choice every time, throwing away a R180,000 traction motor that a fraction-of-the-cost repair would have returned to the shelf. The component left service; its value did not have to. Everything else follows from this one shift: if a removed part is a decision waiting to be made, then your job is not to tidy a scrapyard but to build the small system that forces a deliberate choice at the fork - because the scrap heap should be a choice, never a default.
  • Capture it the instant it is removed: value leaks fastest in the gap between 'removed' and 'decided' - the part dumped at the back of the yard is lost, rusted or forgotten before anyone ever judges it. So capture it the moment it comes off the machine: a simple, tough tag with the serial number and the fault, and it goes not to the boneyard but to a single, designated Triage Bay near the workshop. This is the same insight - a thing not recorded and put somewhere known is a thing lost - and it answers the real question (not 'be tidier' but 'make it easy to do the right thing'): give the tired fitter one obvious place to put the gearbox, and the value survives long enough for a decision.
  • Triage each item deliberately: in the bay, every item gets a deliberate decision rather than a default: can we repair and reuse it? strip it for good components? return it to the supplier under warranty? redeploy it elsewhere? or is it truly, finally, scrap? Scrap is the LAST option, chosen on the evidence (this motor has failed three times - the history informs the call), never the lazy first one. Put a clock on it so the bay does not become a new boneyard: nothing sits more than about 48 hours before its fate is decided and it moves on. The discipline is simply that a human makes a conscious choice for each item - that single habit is what turns a yard of rusting value into recovered, usable assets.
  • Concentrate on the high-value rotables: do not try to recover everything - fussing over nuts and bolts will drown the system and recover little. Concentrate the triage effort on your ROTABLES: the few dozen types of expensive, repairable items that carry the great majority of the recoverable value - the traction motors, brake units, bogie frames and power converters. A handful of item types typically hold most of the money sitting in the yard, so a small, focused effort on them returns far more than spreading thin across everything. This is the same focusing discipline: aim your scarce attention where the value is concentrated, and let the cheap consumables look after themselves.
  • Close the loop back to the shelf: recovery only counts when the recovered item is actually available next time - otherwise you have merely moved the value from the yard to the workshop floor. So close the loop: when a traction motor comes back from repair it is not left leaning against a wall, it is booked back into the main stores, given a verified 'serviceable' status, and put on the shelf, so the system shows it as available (this is what feeds the spares, and it relies on the records to prove what was done to it). The boneyard shrinks to a small managed scrap pile; the stores fill with reliable repaired parts at a fraction of new; the budget stretches and the locomotives spend more time earning their keep. Recovery that never reaches the shelf is just tidier waste.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know how to do this - think about clearing out a cluttered garage or attic.

See the same moves at home

The wrong way is to either throw everything in a skip (scrapping value) or just shuffle the boxes around (hoarding). The right way treats each item as if it is at a fork. You pick up one thing - an old bicycle - capture it, and then triage:

• Repair and reuse: does it just need new tyres to be usable again?
• Redeploy: is it fine, but the kids have outgrown it? Give it to a neighbour.
• Recover value: is it a sought-after model? Sell it.
• Scrap: is the frame broken and rusted through? Now, and only now, is it scrap.

By making a conscious decision for each item - especially the few high-value ones (the good power tools, the solid furniture) - you recover the value and clear the space, instead of just creating tidier waste.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look around your own place of work. Where is your boneyard - the corner where things get dumped "for now"? That is where your value is leaking away. You do not need a big budget or new software; you can start tomorrow.

  1. Go to your boneyard and find one single, obviously high-value item left to die - a pump, a motor, a gearbox.
  2. Ask two questions: what would a new one cost, and what would it likely cost to repair this one? The difference is the value you are letting rust.
  3. Clear a single pallet space or small bay near the workshop and put up a sign: "TRIAGE BAY - for items awaiting a decision." All major removed components go there first, tagged with serial and fault, for no more than about 48 hours.
  4. List your Top 5 most expensive, most critical repairable parts - and focus all your energy on recovering just those five.
R

Name your boneyard, the one high-value item you would rescue first, and your Top 5 rotables to focus on. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Set up a simple triage bay and a close-the-loop route back to stores together.)

This is not just about being tidy or saving a bit of money - it is professional stewardship: recovering the value that fuels your mission and makes everyone's life easier, the reliable affordable part there when a colleague needs it. When something leaves service, its value has not - so give it a deliberate decision at the fork.
