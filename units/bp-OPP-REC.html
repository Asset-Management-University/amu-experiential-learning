The problem everybody has stopped seeing

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad. You have just worked out something you cannot unknow: every rand you live on comes from Uncle Yusuf's shop, and the shop is having its worst year. You are not panicking. You have decided, quite calmly, that you need a second way to earn - and you are going to find one.

That was three weeks ago.

Since then you have thought about it constantly, and you have nothing. Everyone tells you the same thing: find a gap in the market. So you look. And what you see is a township full of businesses that already exist, run by people with more money than you, more experience than you, and a van. Every idea you have, somebody is already doing. Every idea nobody is doing, you can see why.

And a thought has started arriving at night, and it is the honest one, and it is the reason this hurts: maybe opportunities are simply for other people. People who are cleverer, or luckier, or who were shown something you were not.

I have looked and looked and there is nothing there. Where do people actually find opportunities - and why can everyone else see them except me?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your instinct is to search harder. Look wider, think longer, find something new - something nobody has thought of. And when that fails, to go and ask people: what business should I start?

Both fail, and not because you are doing them badly.

Hunting for a new idea puts you in direct competition with everyone who has money and experience - and almost no first business in the world actually starts that way. It also happens entirely inside your head, where there is no evidence, only opinion. And asking people what business you should start asks them to imagine, which human beings are unreliable at. Ask the same person what irritated them yesterday, and they will tell you the truth immediately.

So before you read on, sit with that difference. You have been looking for something that is not there yet. What if the thing you are looking for is already there, in front of you, every day - and the only reason you cannot see it is that you have got used to it?

I keep trying to think up an idea nobody has had. But every business I admire solved something ordinary. Maybe I am looking in the wrong direction - inside my head, instead of out at what people actually do.
What if an opportunity is not a clever idea at all, but an irritation people have simply got used to? Something they work around every single time, and have stopped even mentioning - because it is just how things are.
Stop thinking and start watching. Look for the workaround, the apology, and the thing that is normal and shouldn't be. Then ask one question of each: what are these people already paying for it - in time, in money, or in dignity? Count it. A count is evidence; an idea is a feeling.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You stop looking for an idea, and start looking for a friction - a difficulty people have already accepted, and are already paying for:

  • The trap - looking for an idea: hunting for something NEW puts you in competition with everyone who has money and experience, and it happens inside your head where there is no evidence. Almost no first business starts with a new idea. It starts with an old irritation.
  • The reframe - you are looking for a friction, not an idea: an opportunity is a difficulty people have already accepted and are already paying for. Acceptance is what makes it invisible - and invisible is exactly why it is still there for you to find.
  • Signal 1 - the workaround: what do people do the long way round, every time, because there is no better way? Watch what they DO, not what they say.
  • Signal 2 - the apology: what do people apologise for, explain, or make excuses about? 'Sorry, I'll have to come back with the car.' An apology marks the exact spot where something does not work.
  • Signal 3 - the thing that is normal and shouldn't be: the strongest signal and the hardest to see, because everybody has agreed not to notice it. Including, usually, you.
  • The test - what are they already paying?: apply this to every signal: what does this friction cost them in time, in money, or in dignity? If nobody is paying anything, there is no proven need. If they are already paying, the need is real and you do not have to invent it.
  • The discipline - write down what you SAW: who, doing what, how often, and what it cost them. Not what you think, not what you assume. Count it over a week. A count is evidence you can hand to a doubting brother; an idea is a feeling you cannot.

Here is why this changes everything, and it is not motivational - it is structural. A gap in the market is invisible because nobody has thought of it. A friction is invisible for the opposite reason: everybody has stopped noticing it. Acceptance is the camouflage. And acceptance is exactly why it is still sitting there, unsolved, for somebody who is willing to look.

You have not been failing to see. You have been looking in the wrong direction - inward, for an idea - when the whole answer is outward, in front of you, being carried past you every day.

And now the part that matters more than the method. You are about to start watching people struggle, and looking for money in it. There is a version of this that turns your neighbours into a resource - and if this unit taught you that, it would have done you harm.

So hold this, and hold it above everything else you have just read:

The opportunity is not in their struggle. It is in the service that ends it.

You are not counting other people's difficulty in order to profit from it. You are counting it because you cannot end something you have not troubled to measure. The woman dragging a cylinder to the taxi is not your market. She is the person whose problem a service could solve - and the count is how you find out if a service could be sustained by solving it for enough people.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad stops thinking, and starts standing behind the till with his eyes open.

See what was in front of him the whole time

The moment. A woman buys a gas cylinder. She half-carries, half-drags it to the door and out to the taxi rank. The taxi driver looks at it and shakes his head - it will not fit, not with passengers. She stands on the pavement with it. Uncle Yusuf sees Muhammad watching and shrugs. "It happens."

The realisation. Muhammad has watched that, or done it himself, perhaps two hundred times. He has never once seen it as a problem - because everybody accepts it. It happens. Two words that had made an entire difficulty invisible for years.

What he does next is the discipline. He does not design a service. He does not tell anyone his plan - he does not have one. He takes a page from the order book and, for one week, he counts.

• Total customers buying gas that week: 100.

• Of those, carrying it away with no car: 70.

• Of those 70, visibly struggling with the weight or the shape: 40.

• Complaining the cylinder was under-filled or the seal was already broken: 5.

• Refused by a taxi because of the cylinder: 2.

The count that matters. Add the three kinds of visible failure: 40 struggles + 5 complaints + 2 refusals = 47.

In one week, at one shop, buying gas produced 47 separate visible moments of struggle, distrust or outright failure. Not 47 people - some of those 47 moments happened to the same person twice. Forty-seven moments. That precision matters, and it is the difference between a number you can defend and a number that sounds good.

What he has, and what he does not have. He has a quantified problem. He does not have a business, a price, a plan, or a single person who has said they would pay him. That comes next, and it will be harder. But he is no longer a young man with a feeling. He is a young man with a page.

And what the 47 is for. It is not a revenue forecast - nobody has bought anything. It is the size of a difficulty, measured, at one till, in one week. That is all it claims, and claiming exactly that much is what makes it worth something.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Muhammad did not become cleverer that week. He changed what he was pointing at. The gas problem had been walking past him for years, and it took him seven days of counting to see something that had always been there.

Now do the same, in your own street, your own school, your own taxi rank, your own home. For one week:

  1. Watch, do not think. Carry something to write on. You are collecting what you SAW - who, doing what, how often - not what you assume.
  2. Look for the three signals. The workaround people do every time. The apology or excuse. And hardest of all, the thing that is normal and shouldn't be.
  3. Apply the one test. What are these people already paying for this - in time, in money, or in dignity? If nobody pays anything, let it go. If they are already paying, the need is real and you did not have to invent it.
  4. Count it. Not once - across the week. Then write the one sentence: "I saw [how many] people at [where] paying the price of [what], [how often]."

And keep it to that. Do not design a solution yet. Do not work out what you would charge. Do not go and ask anybody whether they would buy - that is real work and it comes next, and doing it now, on a hunch, is how people lose money they could not afford to lose. This week you are only finding out what is true.

You are not short of opportunities. You are surrounded by them, and every one of them is disguised as something normal. The skill is not cleverness - it is being willing to look at something everybody has agreed not to see, and to write down what is actually happening.

And when you find it, remember what you are looking at. The opportunity is not in their struggle. It is in the service that ends it.
