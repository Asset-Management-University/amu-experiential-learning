Proof your system actually works

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. You have built good things this year: a clear Policy, a real Plan, a strategy for trustworthy information, a Management-of-Change process so no change bites you again. You believe they are being followed. The team nods. Things feel under control.

But a quiet question will not leave you alone: how do I actually know?

You believe the change process is used every time. You believe the information is kept current. But belief is not the same as knowing. You have confidence - and no evidence. And confidence without evidence, for a thing that keeps a town's water safe, is just hope wearing a hard hat.

So one afternoon you decide to look. You pull the last few changes from the register and check each one for its Management-of-Change record. Four are there, signed, clean. The fifth - a recent "small software tweak" to a secondary transfer pump - has no record at all. It bypassed the entire process you built. And you only found it by chance, because you happened to look today. The hole could have been there for months.

I built these systems and I trust they are working - but I have no real way to know, and I only catch the holes by luck. How do I check, honestly and systematically, that we actually do what we say we do?

Before you read on, decide. You built the change process here and you believe in it. Today, looking for something else, you pulled the last five changes: four have complete signed records. The fifth — a small software tweak to a secondary transfer pump — has no record at all. It went through nobody. You found it entirely by chance, because you happened to look today. Nothing has gone wrong as a result. Write down what you would do — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the sentence that should worry you most

• “Find out who did it and remind them of the process.” Reasonable, and it treats the one instance you happened to see. It also teaches everybody that being found out is what happens when a gap surfaces, which makes the next gap quieter.

• “Re-issue the procedure and re-train everybody.” The procedure exists and four out of five followed it, so it is largely working. Re-training people who are already complying does not tell you anything about the ones who are not.

• “Nothing went wrong — note it and move on.” The most defensible-sounding answer, and it turns on a fact you have no evidence for: nothing has gone wrong that you know about, and knowing about things is precisely what is in question.

• “Ask the team whether they are following it.” Willing people will sincerely tell you their intentions. They will not tell you about the tweak they did not think counted as a change, because they did not think it counted.

• “Tighten the process so this cannot happen.” The process was fine. It was bypassed, and you do not yet know how often.

• The sentence to sit with is I found it by chance. If luck is your detection method, then the number of gaps you have found tells you about your luck rather than about your systems, and you cannot say how long any of them has been open. So replace the belief with a check — and the check earns its authority from what it refuses to do. It starts from a rule you actually agreed, not from your view of good practice, or it becomes an argument. It looks at evidence — records, artefacts, things that exist — rather than asking people whether they comply. It is done by somebody independent of the work, because nobody can check their own compliance. It grades what it finds, because an isolated lapse and a systemic failure need different answers and treating them alike either panics the place or lets a real hole through. And it asks what the system needs rather than who was at fault — because the moment it becomes a search for blame, the evidence it runs on stops being offered.

There is a disciplined tool for exactly this - a way to replace hope with proof. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is what "checking honestly" actually looks like on one page - Onalenna's own check of whether her change process is really being followed. Notice it has no opinions in it. Only the rule, the proof, and the verdict.

1. What We're Checking - and Who Checks (independence)

Audit area: is our Management of Change process actually being followed on the critical pumps? Auditor: Onalenna - who did NOT make these changes herself. (An auditor must be independent of the work being checked.)

2. The Requirement (the rule we agreed to follow)

The rule we agreed: every change goes through a Management of Change record, signed off by the lead engineer BEFORE the work starts (our own MOC procedure). A requirement can be one of our own promises, or a clause of ISO 55001.

3. The Evidence (the objective proof I examined)

What I examined: I pulled the last five changes from the register and checked each for a completed, signed MOC record. Four had one. One - a recent software tweak on a secondary transfer pump - had no MOC at all.

4. The Finding, and Its Grade

Finding: Minor Nonconformity. The MOC process exists and is mostly followed. This one bypassed change - a tweak to a secondary, non-critical pump - is an isolated lapse, not a critical, immediate risk. (It would be a MAJOR NC if this had hit a primary safety system like the chlorine dosing, or if the sign-off step was ignored across the board.)

5. What the System Needs (not who to blame)

What the system needs: the change register and the work permit are not linked, so a job can start with no MOC. The fix is in the system - no permit issued without an MOC number. An audit is a health check for the system, not a hunt for someone to punish.

See the spine of it: a rule we agreed, the evidence actually examined, an honest verdict, and a fix aimed at the system - never at a person. That one bypassed change is no longer a lucky catch; it is a recorded finding with a fix that stops it recurring. That is the difference between hoping and knowing. Now let us walk through it.

T3: The Blueprint

This is an Internal Audit record - the tool defined in ISO 55001, clause 9.2, carried out following the principles of ISO 19011 (the guide to auditing any management system). It is how an organisation checks itself: does our system actually conform to our own promises and to the standard, and is it really working? It turns hope into knowledge.

Each part, as the question it answers:

  • 1. What We're Checking, and Who Checks - What area, and is the auditor independent? You audit a slice at a time. And you cannot audit your own work - of course it looks fine to you. Independence is what makes the verdict trustworthy.
  • 2. The Requirement - What was the rule we agreed to follow? It can be a promise from your own Policy or Plan, or a clause of ISO 55001. No rule, nothing to audit against.
  • 3. The Evidence - What objective proof did I actually examine? A signed record, a log, a meter reading, the physical state of a thing. Not a feeling - a fact you can point to.
  • 4. The Finding and Its Grade - Does the evidence match the rule? Conforms (it matches); a Minor Nonconformity (an isolated lapse where the system is generally effective); a Major Nonconformity (a key part of the system is missing or ignored, OR a single lapse that creates a serious, direct risk); or an Opportunity for Improvement (it matches, but could be better).
  • 5. What the System Needs - What would stop this recurring? The finding points to a fix in the process, which is the whole purpose: not to catch people, but to close the gap.

Four principles hold the whole thing up:

• Evidence, not opinion. The auditor's golden rule: no evidence, no finding. If you cannot point to a record or an observation, it is just your opinion - and opinion has no place in an audit.
• The system, not the person. An audit is a health check for your system, not a performance review of your people. A good doctor does not blame you for a high reading; they find it, name it, and treat the cause. We do the same for our system - with dignity, never blame.
• Independence. The auditor must be independent of the work being checked, or it is not an audit - it is marking your own homework.
• Planned, not panicked. Onalenna's chance discovery is the ache; the answer is not a one-off panic check but a planned, repeating cycle of audits (an audit programme) that watches the system's health on purpose, at set intervals.

Does it scale? At Corathyl, Onalenna audits three change records against her own procedure. At an airline, an independent team audits three years of landing-gear maintenance data against aviation law and ISO 55001 - the very evidence it must show to keep its certification and its right to fly. The principle is identical: evidence against criteria.

T4: Your Turn

Now you be the auditor. Pick a simple system you know with written-down rules: a sports club's safety routine, a society's money process, a shared house's chores, your own weekly backup or study plan.

  1. Name three requirements. Three specific promises the system makes ("the treasurer keeps a receipt for every payment").
  2. Hunt the evidence. Go and find the objective proof - the receipts, the minutes, the calendar, the saved files. Be a detective, not a judge.
  3. Record honest findings. For each, does the evidence match the rule? Grade it. Remember: a nonconformity is not a failure - it is a discovery that lets you improve the system before it fails for real.

Start with just one requirement, all the way across. The habit is the prize: rule, evidence, verdict, fix - no opinion, no blame.

T5: The Guardrails

An audit is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then strengthen any box that needs it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The opinion. "I feel the process is a bit weak." Useless. Tie every finding to evidence: "Minor NC - three of the last five forms were unsigned, against our own procedure." No evidence, no finding.

• The blame game. An audit that ends with someone being disciplined has failed. The error is a symptom; fix the system that let it happen, not the person who got caught in it.

• Minor mistaken for major (and back). A minor nonconformity is an isolated lapse in a system that basically works. A major is when a whole part of the system is missing or ignored, OR when a single lapse creates a serious risk. For example, one missed fire-extinguisher check is minor - but if that was the only extinguisher protecting a fuel store, the high risk makes it major. Grading by gut inflates panic or hides danger - grade by what the evidence shows about the system and the risk.

• Marking your own homework. Auditing your own area. You will not see your own blind spots. Get someone independent of the work.

• The evidence-free finding. A verdict with no note of what you looked at. Your record must let the next person follow the exact trail to the same conclusion.

That bypassed change Onalenna found by luck is now a finding with a fix - and a hundred others like it will be caught on purpose, not by chance, because she has a way to look. That is the whole gift of the audit: it replaces hope with proof, and it does it without blame, because its quarrel is with the system, never the person. Master it, and you will always be able to answer the hardest, most honest question in engineering - "can you prove it?" - with a quiet "yes, here it is".
