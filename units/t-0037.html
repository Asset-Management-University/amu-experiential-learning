Step 1 of 5

The same bearing has seized four times at almost the same age - is that bad luck or a pattern, and what does the pattern tell me to change?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a Tuesday at Khanya Bottling, a fruit-juice cannery on the edge of Springs, east of Johannesburg. You are the reliability engineer. You walk onto the Line 2 floor and the smell of warm syrup and machine oil hangs in the air. The line is stopped. Again.

In your hand is a printout from the maintenance system. The top line reads: Filler Bearing FB-204 - seized - 03:15. This is the fourth seizure on that same bearing position since January. It is now June.

Standing next to the stopped filler is Pinky Mahlangu, the night shift's senior fitter, still in her overalls past the end of her shift. She is not angry. She is tired, and she says the thing that stings the most: "We keep swapping this bearing. Every time, it lasts a bit, then it goes. Are we just unlucky, or is something wrong?"

Each Line 2 stop costs the plant about R18 000 an hour in lost production, and the line was down 3 hours last night - R54 000 gone before sunrise. Your plant manager has asked you, by Friday, for a recommendation: do we keep replacing this bearing on failure, or change how we maintain it - and she wants the reason, not a gut feel.

The file has been marked up already, so you have clean numbers to work with. Three bearings failed, at 400, 440 and 480 running hours. Two more are still running, at 1 900 hours each. One job card was unreadable and is excluded.

You look at those three failure times for a long while. 400, 440, 480. They are almost the same number. Whatever else is going on, these bearings are not dying at random moments - they are dying at an age, and the age keeps repeating. You can feel that in the figures, and Pinky can feel it on the floor, and neither of you can say it in a way that would survive a Friday meeting or tell you what to actually do differently on Monday.

The three failures cluster at almost the same age. Is that bad luck or a pattern - and if it is a pattern, what does it tell me to change?

Before you read on, decide. Failures at 400, 440 and 480 hours, two survivors past 1,900, a R54,000 night and a Friday deadline. Write down what you would recommend and what those three numbers tell you - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does with 400, 440, 480

• "Fit a better bearing - buy the premium brand." Reasonable and it may even help, and it answers a question nobody has asked yet. Until you know whether these are dying of age or of installation, a better part may simply be a more expensive part failing at 470 hours.

• "Four failures in six months - increase the inspection frequency." Sensible-sounding and it is the answer for a completely different pattern. Inspection catches things that fail unpredictably; it is an expensive way to watch something that dies on a schedule.

• "Take the average, 440 hours, and replace on that." The closest of the five, and it happens to land near the right action for the wrong reason - it uses the three failures and cannot use the two survivors, and it has no way of telling you whether replacing on a schedule is the right kind of answer at all.

• "Chase the root cause - it must be lubrication or over-tension." Almost certainly worth doing and it is a guess about which cause until something narrows it. Notice that the shape of the failure history is itself evidence about that: a part dying at a repeatable age is telling you something different from one dying at random ages.

• "Run to failure - it is only a bearing." It is only a bearing and it is R54,000 a night, four times a year, and a fitter working past the end of her shift.

• What the first five share is that they jump to an action without reading what the data most clearly shows: how the chance of failing changes as the part gets older. Failures that get less likely with age, failures that are equally likely at any age, and failures that get more likely with age are three different diseases, they have three different cures, and the cure for one is a waste of money on the others. That is a number you can compute from these five rows, and it decides which of the five answers above is right.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the method run start to finish on FB-204's marked-up file: three failures at 400, 440 and 480 hours, two survivors at 1 900 hours.

Step 1 - Look at the shape of the failure times before computing anything. 400, 440, 480. They sit within 80 hours of each other, and none of them is anywhere near the survivors at 1 900. Two things are worth saying out loud at this point, because the fit will confirm both and you should have seen them first. The failures cluster, which is what an age-related death looks like - random failures scatter across the whole range, with as many at 60 hours as at 600. And the survivors sit far beyond the cluster, which says the bearings are capable of much longer lives than the failed ones achieved.

Step 2 - Fit the life distribution, keeping the survivors in. You feed all five rows into a Weibull fit - the three failures as failures, the two survivors as censored rows, which the method takes directly. Software does the arithmetic; what it is doing is finding the curve that best explains both "three died at about 440" and "two are still alive at 1 900". The survivors matter: without them the fit has no evidence that a long life is possible at all, and comes out unfairly pessimistic.

Step 3 - Read the first number, and let it name the disease. The fit returns a shape of 4.7. That single figure is the answer to Pinky's question, and it is read like this:

ShapeWhat is happeningWhat it means on the floor
less than 1failures get less likely as the part agesdying young - fitting, handling, wrong part, contamination
about 1failures are equally likely at any agerandom - age tells you nothing; replacing on a schedule buys nothing
above 1failures get more likely as the part ageswearing out - the part tires at a predictable age

4.7 is far above 1, so these bearings are wearing out, hard and predictably. That is Pinky's answer: not unlucky - something is wearing them out on a schedule. And notice what it rules out. It is not the dying-young pattern, so a fitting fault is not the first suspect. It is not random, so more inspection is not the fix. Two of the five answers on the previous page are now off the table on evidence rather than opinion.

Step 4 - Read the second number, and let it set the timing. The fit also returns a characteristic life of about 470 hours - the age by which roughly 63 out of 100 of these bearings will have failed. Now the pattern has a clock on it: about 470 running hours, which on a line running 20 hours a day is roughly 23 days.

Step 5 - Turn the pattern into the policy, because each pattern has its own. This is the step that pays for the analysis. Wearing out at a predictable age is the one case where replacing before it fails genuinely buys something - so you plan the change at about 400 hours, comfortably inside the 470, and a three-hour breakdown at 03:15 becomes a thirty-minute planned swap on a day shift. Had the shape come back near 1, the same recommendation would have been waste: you cannot schedule your way out of failures that ignore age, and the money would have gone to detection instead. Had it come back below 1, scheduled replacement would have been worse than waste - you would be pulling good bearings out and reintroducing whatever fitting fault was killing the new ones.

Step 6 - Say what the fit cannot tell you, and hand that on. The number says these bearings wear out at about 470 hours. It does not say why they wear out that fast, and a bearing in this duty should not. Three failures is also a thin fit - enough to act on with the survivors supporting it, not enough to quote to a decimal. So Friday's recommendation has two halves and stating both is what gets it believed: change the policy now, from run-to-failure to a planned swap at 400 hours; and open an investigation into why 470 hours is the life at all, starting with lubrication and shaft tension. Otherwise you will have made the failures punctual instead of making them rare - which is worth doing, and is not the same thing.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the fourth is where most people stop too early.

  • 1. Look at the failure times first - clustered, or scattered across the range?
  • 2. Fit the distribution with the survivors kept in as censored rows.
  • 3. Read the shape: dying young, random, or wearing out.
  • 4. Read the characteristic life for the timing.
  • 5. Choose the policy the pattern calls for, and say what the fit cannot tell you.

The method is Weibull analysis (say it "WHY-bull", after the engineer Waloddi Weibull), and it fits a curve to times-to-failure. Its two outputs are the two you used. The shape, written beta, says how the chance of failing changes with age - below 1 falling, about 1 flat, above 1 rising - and the characteristic life, written eta, is the age by which about 63 in 100 have failed. They are not two findings but two readings of one curve, and neither is any use without the other: a shape with no clock cannot be scheduled, and a clock with no shape does not tell you whether scheduling is the right idea in the first place.

Which is the point of the whole unit, and the reason it beats an average. The pattern chooses the policy. Wearing out means failure is predictable, so planned replacement before the characteristic life converts breakdowns into scheduled work. Random means age carries no information, so replacing early buys nothing at all and the money belongs in detection - watching for the failure rather than anticipating it. Dying young means the new ones are the fragile ones, so scheduled replacement actively makes things worse and the answer is to fix the installation, the handling or the part. An average life of 440 hours is compatible with all three, which is exactly why it cannot direct a decision.

When it fits: you have times-to-failure for one failure mode, in running time, and you want to know the pattern and the timing. Three limits. Two or three failures give a weak fit - treat it as a direction with survivors supporting it, not a life quoted to a decimal, and say which you have. Several failure modes fitted as one pile produce a curve that describes none of them, and the tell is a fitted shape near 1 when the floor plainly sees wear-out. And the fit is silent on cause: it says that the part wears out at an age, never why, and stopping at the number leaves you with punctual failures rather than rare ones.

Four neighbouring questions have their own units, and this method needs the first of them before it can run at all. Turning a maintenance file into rows you can fit - one failure mode each, in running time, with the items that have not failed kept as evidence rather than dropped - is t-0293. Whether one part's failure stops the whole line or something else carries on, which decides how much a failure is worth preventing, is t-0012. Ranking every way an asset can fail, worst first on one agreed ruler, is t-0017. And tracing every path to one named catastrophe you have decided you will not have is t-0273.

T4: Your Turn

Try each task before opening its reveal. Carry the numbers yourself.

Task 1 (warm-up) - Read a shape. A pump impeller on Line 1 gives a shape of 0.6 and a characteristic life of 800 h. Your colleague says "it's wearing out, let's start replacing it on a schedule." In two sentences, say whether you agree and what a shape of 0.6 actually means.

What good looks like

You disagree. A shape below 1 means failures get less likely as the part ages - the new ones are the fragile ones - so a fixed replacement schedule is exactly the wrong response: you would keep pulling out impellers that had survived their risky period and putting in fresh ones at their most vulnerable. The right move is to find what is killing them young: installation method, handling damage, contamination, or the part itself.

Task 2 - The same clock, a different disease. Two assets both come back with a characteristic life of 500 hours. Asset A has a shape of 3.8; asset B has a shape of 1.0. Your budget allows one intervention on each. What do you do for each, and why are they not the same?

What good looks like

Same clock, opposite policies. A (shape 3.8) is wearing out: failure is age-related and therefore predictable, so plan a replacement comfortably before 500 h and convert breakdowns into scheduled work. B (shape 1.0) is random: age carries no information, so replacing it at 400 h buys nothing - the fresh one is exactly as likely to fail tomorrow as the old one was. B's money goes to detection (a condition check, an alarm, a spare held ready) or to reducing the consequence of a failure you cannot anticipate. Good answers say plainly that the characteristic life alone could not have told you this.

Task 3 - The pile that hides two diseases. A gearbox has eleven failures over two years. Six are "seal leak" at 900, 1,100, 950, 1,000, 1,050 and 980 h; five are "bearing collapse" at 200, 4,000, 700, 3,100 and 1,400 h. Fitted as one pile, the shape comes back at 1.2. What has gone wrong, and what do you do?

What good looks like

Two different failure modes have been fitted as one, so the curve describes neither and the 1.2 is an artefact of mixing them - it would have you conclude "roughly random, do not schedule", which is wrong for at least one of the two. Split them and fit each on its own: the seal leaks cluster tightly around 1,000 h and will come back with a high shape, wearing out, so they can be scheduled; the bearing collapses scatter from 200 to 4,000 h and will come back near 1, random, so they need detection instead. One asset can be carrying two diseases at once and need both answers. Good answers notice the clustering difference in the raw numbers before trusting any fitted figure.

Now take your own problem asset - the one that keeps stopping - and run the five moves on it. Work from a file that has already been marked up one row, one failure mode, in running time, with the survivors kept.

Move 1. Write your failure times in ascending order. Are they clustered within a narrow band, or spread across the whole range? Write your expectation down before you fit anything - it is the check on the fit, and it is worth being wrong out loud.

Move 2. Fit, with every censored row included. Failures used: ______. Censored rows kept: ______.

Move 3. Shape = ______ (circle: below 1 dying young / about 1 random / above 1 wearing out). Does it match what you wrote in Move 1? If not, suspect two failure modes in one pile before you suspect your instinct.

Move 4. Characteristic life = ______ hours. In days at your running hours: ______.

Move 5. The policy the pattern calls for: wearing out → planned replacement at about ______ hours; random → detection or consequence-reduction, name it: ______; dying young → the installation or supply cause to fix: ______. Then the sentence that keeps you honest: this fit rests on ______ failures, and it says nothing about why - the investigation I am opening alongside it is ______.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the reading honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The shape read backwards. The costliest mistake here: scheduled replacement recommended for a part dying young, or run-to-failure accepted for a clear wear-out. Below 1, the new ones are the fragile ones - fix the cause, never time-replace. Above 1, the old ones are - replace before the characteristic life.

• Two failure modes fitted as one pile. The tell is a shape near 1 while the floor plainly sees wear-out, or a plot with a kink in it. Separate the modes and fit each alone; one asset can carry two diseases and need both answers.

• A curve trusted from one or two failures. Few points give a confident-looking pair of numbers and a weak fit. Treat it as a direction, say how many failures it rests on, and let the survivors do their work.

• The characteristic life quoted as "the life". It is the age by which about 63 in 100 have failed, which means a good few fail well before it. Plan replacement inside it, not at it.

• The clock read without the shape. A characteristic life of 500 hours means opposite things at a shape of 3.8 and a shape of 1.0. The two numbers are one reading; never carry one into a meeting without the other.

• Survivors left out of the fit. The items that have not failed are evidence that longer lives are possible, and dropping them biases the answer pessimistic every time - the direction never averages out.

• The number mistaken for the cause. A fitted shape says that a part wears out at an age, never why. Stop at the number and you will have made the failures punctual rather than rare, which is worth doing and is not the same thing.

You came onto the Line 2 floor with a fourth seizure, a tired fitter's question, and a Friday deadline. You can leave with an answer to the question she actually asked: not unlucky - a shape of 4.7 says something is wearing these bearings out at a repeatable age, about 470 running hours. Because the pattern is age-related, the policy that fits it is a planned swap at 400 hours, turning a R54,000 night into a thirty-minute day-shift job - and because the fit says nothing about cause, the investigation into why 470 hours opens alongside it, not instead of it. The pattern chooses the policy; an average life would have been compatible with all three patterns and could not have chosen anything. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one asset's failure times and read their shape together.)
