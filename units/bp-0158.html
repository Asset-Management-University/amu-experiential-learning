Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Thabang, and you look after a small group of buildings for a community trust - homes for the elderly, a few family blocks. You're good at your job, but one problem keeps you awake. The buildings are all ageing, and every year the list of things that need replacing - roofs, wiring, boilers, pipes - is longer than the money you're given. Worse, the gap is growing: what you didn't fix last year is a year older and likelier to fail, and new things keep joining the list. You feel like you're bailing out a boat filling faster than you can throw the water out.

You do your best - you fix whatever broke most recently, or whatever someone complained about loudest. Then a boiler fails in mid-winter in the elderly block; people are cold and at risk. Your boss asks, "why didn't you replace this one? We knew it was old." And you have no good answer - you can't really say, "because I was fixing a different urgent thing, and I had no clear rule for which mattered more."

I can never fund everything and the gap keeps growing - so how do I choose what the limited money fixes first, in a way I can stand behind when something I skipped fails?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your instinct is to find a simple, fair rule. You try fixing the oldest things first - tidy, but an old pipe in a storeroom might last five more years while a younger, hard-worked boiler whose failure leaves twenty people cold gets skipped; age isn't danger. You try the biggest or most visible - repainting the main entrance gets you noticed, but it spends the money that could have replaced three failing geysers, any one of which could burst and flood a home; you fixed an inconvenience and left a catastrophe waiting. You try whatever was complained about loudest - so the squeaky gate gets greased while the silent corrosion in the main pipe goes unnoticed, until it bursts. And just demanding more money falls flat - you can't justify more until you can show what the current money buys and what risk you carry without it.

These rules feel fair, but they all miss the one thing that matters. So before you read on: age, size and loudness all pick the wrong things - what single thing should you rank by instead, and what must you do with what falls below the line?

You can't create the money, so the win isn't 'fund everything' - it's a defensible RULE for what the money fixes first, plus an honest, costed record of what you're choosing to leave. The gap grows whether you fund it or not, so name it: state the HONEST long-term need, not the wished-for budget. Rank every item by HARM-AVOIDED PER RAND (how much harm each rand of spending prevents) and fund from the top - a R50k roof-fix that stops a R500k flood beats a cosmetic upgrade, whatever their age or size. Then draw the line where the money runs out and WRITE DOWN what's below it and what it'll cost if it fails. That gives you a rule you can say out loud and a visible account of the risk you're carrying - which is the honest argument for more funding.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You cannot create money out of thin air - so the win isn't to magically fund everything. It's a defensible rule for what your limited money fixes first, and an honest, visible record of the risk you're choosing to carry. Five moves:

  • See the trap - the gap grows whether you fund it or not: the whole collection needs more than you'll ever be given, and it decays every period regardless of what you fix - so the shortfall isn't a one-off, it's a widening wave: what you skip this year is older and likelier to fail next year. And the first quiet lie - pretending the budget IS the need - makes every choice downstream impossible to defend. Name the real gap before anything else.
  • The comfortable rules fund the wrong things: sorting by AGE, by SIZE/visibility, or by WHO-COMPLAINED-LOUDEST all feel fair, but none tracks HARM: age isn't danger (an old pipe in a storeroom may be fine); a visible cosmetic upgrade can swallow the money that would have stopped a flood; the squeaky gate gets greased while the silent corroding pipe bursts. When the skipped thing fails, 'I fixed the oldest first' is no defence at all.
  • State the honest long-term need, not the wished-for budget: stop starting from the small budget; start from the truth - what it would ACTUALLY cost to keep everything sound over the long term. Say the need is R10 million a year and the budget is R6 million: the gap is R4 million - name it. You cannot allocate money honestly, or manage a problem, that you're pretending is smaller than it is.
  • Rank by harm-avoided per rand: put every competing item on one common measure: how much harm does each rand of spending here prevent (how bad if it fails, and how likely, against what it costs to fix)? A R50,000 roof repair that stops a R500,000 flood and keeps three families housed goes to the top; a R200,000 cosmetic lobby upgrade that prevents only 'looking dated' goes to the bottom. This one question cuts through age, size and noise, and puts the money where it protects people most - and it's a rule you can say out loud.
  • Draw the line and record what's below it: fund from the top of the ranked list until the money runs out, then draw a hard line - and write down what sits below it as an honest risk list: for each, the cost to fix and the consequence of not (e.g. 'Boiler, Block C - R120,000 - high chance of winter failure leaving 20 elderly residents cold'). This makes no new money, but it gives you two things you never had: a defensible answer when something below the line fails ('it was on our risk list; with R6m we had to prevent greater harm first'), and the honest, costed account of carried risk that is your single strongest argument for more funding.

See it change Thabang's nights. He names the real gap (a R10m need against a R6m budget), ranks every job by how much harm each rand prevents, funds from the top, draws the line, and writes the honest risk list of what's below it - the Block C boiler among them, with its cost and its winter consequence. When that boiler does fail, the question "why didn't you fix it?" finally has an answer: "it was on our risk list; with R6m we had to prevent greater harm first - here's the rule, and here's exactly what it would have cost." The same shortfall, but no longer a source of shame or surprise - a managed, honest account. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only for buildings - any collection wearing out at once works the same way. Watch a household.

See the same rule around a family's big-ticket items

The car is old, the roof needs attention, the geyser is making strange noises, and the fridge is on its last legs - and there isn't money for all four. So run the rule:

• Honest need: replacing all four would be about R120,000. The budget - what's actually saved - is R30,000. Name the gap.
• Rank by harm-avoided per rand: the car (a motorway failure is a safety risk and you can't get to work) and the geyser (a burst floods the house) prevent the most harm; the roof is medium; the fridge is low harm, high annoyance.
• Draw the line: the R30,000 goes to the car first; the fridge waits.
• Record the risk, out loud: "we're fixing the car first; we're accepting the risk the geyser or roof might fail this year, and we'll watch them closely." Anxiety becomes a clear, shared, defensible family decision.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at the collection of things you're responsible for that are wearing out - a home's big-ticket items, a small fleet, a community's shared assets - where the list of needs grows faster than the money. Stop letting anxiety, age, size or the loudest voice decide for you:

  1. State the honest need. Write down what it would really cost to keep everything sound, and name the gap - don't flinch from it.
  2. Rank by harm-avoided per rand, and fund from the top. For each item ask "how much harm does this spending stop?" - put the most harm-prevented-per-rand first, and fund down until your real budget runs out.
  3. Draw the line and write your risk list. Name what you're not funding and what each could cost if it fails - the honest account of the risk you're carrying.

None of this is your failing - you were handed a structural impossibility (more decay than money, every year) and no honest rule to ration it; bailing that boat at all is quiet heroism, not carelessness. What was missing was never your effort - it was a way to choose you could defend, and the courage to write down the truth of what you can't yet reach. And that record is not cold accounting: it spends scarce money where it shields people from the most harm, it refuses to hide the risk they're carrying until something breaks and hurts someone, and it becomes the honest, costed case for the funding they actually need. A defensible decision isn't what you chose to fund - it's the rule you used, and whether you can say, out loud and without shame, what you chose to go without, and why.
