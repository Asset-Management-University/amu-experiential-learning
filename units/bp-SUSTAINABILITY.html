Step 1 of 5

It feels greedy to charge people who have so little - but giving my work away is killing the very service they need. What do I do?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sinegugu, and your phone-help service is, by every measure that matters, working. The elders love you. You have narrowed to what you do safely and beautifully, and you are quietly building the harder skills. Word has spread. There is just one problem, and it is the one that is going to end it.

You have been doing it for free. Or near enough - a few rand here, nothing there, whatever feels right, which is usually nothing, because how do you charge a grandmother on a pension to help her reach her grandchildren with a video call? It felt wrong. It felt unkind. So you gave it away.

And now your own airtime is gone by the middle of the month. The transport to reach people comes out of money you do not have. You have taken on cleaning jobs just to eat - which means less and less time for the very elders you started this for. You are exhausted, you are broke, and the beautiful thing you built is dying in your hands. Not for lack of love. Not for lack of skill. Not for lack of need. It is dying because it cannot sustain the one person holding it up: you.

It feels so wrong to charge people who have so little. But if I do not, this service that I love, that they truly need, is going to die - and then I help no one. Must a good heart always end in an empty pocket?

You are caught in a trap that catches almost every good-hearted person who ever tried to help. And there is a way out of it - but you have to see the trap clearly first.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You try to find a way to keep going without charging. Maybe work faster, help more people in less time. Maybe cut your own costs to the bone. Maybe just push through, be tougher, need less. You try them all, and the maths never changes: every hour you give away still costs you airtime, transport, and a piece of your own life - and there is a hard floor below which you simply cannot go, because you also have to eat. "Be more generous" is not a plan. It is the trap tightening.

Before you read on, look honestly at what your "free" service actually costs - and who is paying it right now.

Are 'free' services really free? Right now, who is paying the cost - in transport, data, time and pure exhaustion? It is me. And I am running out - which means soon there will be no service at all.
Think of a strong tree. Does it give ALL its energy away, or does it first make enough sugar to keep its own trunk and roots alive - SO THAT it can bear fruit, season after season? Self-care is not the opposite of service; it is what makes service last.
What if charging a fair price is not about TAKING from people - but about making sure I can keep GIVING to them, for years instead of months? The question is not 'how do I get the most?' It is 'what does this work need to survive and keep serving?'

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is the truth the trap hides from you, drawn from the oldest teacher there is:

  • The Peach Tree Principle: A peach tree does something that looks selfish: before it grows a single peach, it spends the spring making sugar for ITSELF - feeding its own roots and trunk. Only a tree that has fed itself has the strength to bear fruit for everyone else. Self-sustenance is not the enemy of service; it is its PRECONDITION. If you cannot take care of yourself, you cannot take care of others. Charging fairly is not greed - it is making the sugar that lets you keep giving fruit, for years.
  • The caution: Kwame's gardens (you have met him before): Remember Kwame, from the four corners - his community gardens were loved, skilled, and truly needed. But he and his friends paid for everything out of their own pockets, and when the pockets ran empty there was no money for tools, soil or transport. They took other jobs to survive, and the beautiful gardens went to weed. A noble effort that could not feed itself now feeds no one. 'Free' is not the same as 'good' - if free means it dies.
  • The path that lasted: Lwanda's Wheels: Lwanda started a bicycle delivery service for the spaza shops and households on his street - and at first did it for almost nothing, until he was broke and could not even fix his bike. Instead of quitting, he worked out his true cost and a fair return and set a small, honest fee per delivery (about R15). The shops gladly paid - he saved them time - and for those who could not, one delivery in ten was free, carried by the rest. Three years on, 'Lwanda's Wheels' still runs: a second, stronger bike, a friend trained to help, and FAR more people served than a heroic free month ever could have. Fair pricing did not betray his mission - it is the only reason his mission still exists.

So the question was never "how do I make money from people who have little?" The real question is: what does this work need so that it can keep serving - for years, not months? You answer it the same way the tree does - by making sure the work feeds the one who does it. Here is that worked out for your phone-help service, in four honest steps:

1. THE TRUE COST OF SERVING - what it really takes (your time is not free)

Airtime, data and transport to reach people: about R800 a month. And my own time - roughly 40 hours - which is NOT free; I have to eat and pay rent. Counting a basic R2,200 for my time, the true monthly cost is R800 + R2,200 = R3,000. 'Free' was never free - I was just paying all of it myself, until I ran out.

2. A FAIR RETURN - the sugar the tree keeps for its own roots

A little above bare survival, so this can last and grow: a buffer for the month a phone breaks, a bit saved toward a better one, room to not burn out. Say R600 a month. This is the sugar the tree keeps for its own roots - the strength to keep bearing fruit, year after year.

3. A SUSTAINABLE PRICE - the honest number that lets the work endure

Add them: R3,000 true cost + R600 fair return = R3,600 a month to truly sustain the service. If I help about 30 elders a month, that is R3,600 / 30 = R120 each - an honest, fair price for an hour of patient, trusted help. Not greed; the number that lets the work survive.

4. HOW WE SERVE EVERYONE - the price is what LETS you keep the door open (the 'so that')

Some cannot pay R120 - and they are exactly who I started this for. So the fair price is what LETS me reach them: families working in the city gladly sponsor their parents; those who can pay a little more, do; and one session in six is free for real hardship, carried by the rest. A sustainable service can choose to be generous. A dying one cannot help anyone at all.

Look at what that did. It turned "charging feels greedy" into something completely different: a sustainable price is the thing that lets you keep the door open to the poorest. The R120 is not extraction. It is the sugar that keeps the tree alive - so that the one-in-six free sessions, the sponsored grandmothers, the years of service still to come, are all possible. A free service that dies helps no one next month. A sustainable one helps everyone, for a very long time.

The move: do not give your work away until it kills the work. Cost it honestly (your time included), add a fair return so it can last, set a sustainable price - and use that very price to build in how you reach those who cannot pay. Sustain the work, so the work can serve.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Trace the bridge in Sinegugu's own canvas, because it is the whole point of this unit.

How does charging MORE honestly let her give MORE freely?

Follow the logic across her four boxes. Because she finally counted the true cost (box 1) and allowed herself a fair return (box 2), she could set a sustainable price (box 3) - and only then, standing on solid ground instead of sinking, could she design box 4: the family sponsorships, the one-in-six free sessions, the sliding scale. A drowning person cannot pull anyone else from the water. Sinegugu had to get her own head above the surface - not out of selfishness, but so that she had the strength to reach back for the others. That is the bridge this whole unit stands on: sustain yourself SO THAT you can serve. The "so that" is everything. Self-sustenance that does not flow back into service is just selfishness; service that does not sustain itself is just a slow collapse. Bind the two together, and you have something that can actually last.

So Sinegugu sets her fair price, with her gentle accessibility plan beside it. She braces for anger and loses almost no one - the families are relieved to finally be able to support their parents properly, and the elders, far from feeling exploited, are glad the help they depend on is not going to vanish. For the first time, she is not exhausted. And because she is not exhausted, she can serve more people, more warmly, for years to come.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Whatever good thing you want to build, it will face this same test: it can only serve for as long as it can sustain the person serving. So build the sustaining in from the start. Take your own idea and work the four honest steps.
