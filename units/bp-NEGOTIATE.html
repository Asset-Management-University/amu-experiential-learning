She paid less - just by asking

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad. Every week you buy your gas from the same supplier, at whatever price he quotes you. You pay it without a word. Asking for a better price? The thought makes you wince - it feels cheeky, ungrateful, like he'd be insulted and might just stop selling to you. So you take the first number. You always have - the first price, the first wage you were ever offered, the first terms anyone put in front of you.

Then, over tea, another reseller mentions what she pays for the very same gas, the very same amount - and it's noticeably less than you. Same supplier-type, same volume. You ask how. She shrugs: "I asked him for a better price for a regular customer. He said yes." That's it. That's the whole secret. She just... asked.

The realisation lands like a cold stone. For months - years, really - you've been paying more than you needed to, not because you couldn't afford to ask, but because you were afraid to. And it isn't just the gas. It's every first-offer you ever accepted in your life, each one a small fortune quietly handed away, all because asking felt rude.

I always take the first offer because asking feels rude or risky - and it's quietly cost me a fortune. How do I ask for a fair deal without it being a fight, or feeling like I'm begging?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You can feel two wrong paths pulling at you. The first is to stay as you are - take the number, keep the peace, don't make it awkward. (That's the path that just cost you all that money.) The second, in a flash of frustration, is to go in hard - demand a lower price, treat it like a battle to win. But that would sour a supplier you depend on, and you sense it would backfire. Neither feels right - because both miss what a negotiation actually is.

Before you read on: the other reseller didn't fight and didn't beg - she just asked, and got a yes. What do you think she understood about that conversation that you didn't? And what might she have thought about before she opened her mouth?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The reseller's secret wasn't nerve or cleverness. It was that she understood what a fair negotiation really is - and she prepared. Here's the whole of it:

  • Taking the first offer is a choice - and it's costing you: every wage, price, or term you accept without asking quietly hands money and power to the other side. Over a lifetime it adds up to a fortune. The barrier isn't ability; it's the fear that asking is rude or risky.
  • A fair negotiation is a conversation, not a fight or a beg: it's a normal, expected, respectful search for a deal that works for BOTH sides - the other person assumes you'll ask. Asking isn't greedy; silence is just you agreeing to whatever suits them.
  • Prepare three things BEFORE you open your mouth: (1) your WALK-AWAY - what you'd do if there's no deal, so you bargain from calm not desperation; (2) your VALUE + reasons - why a fairer number is fair, so you ask on grounds, not nerve; (3) THEIR needs - what they want, so you can find the overlap.
  • Then ask calmly, trade, and be willing to walk: name a fair number (or ask for theirs), stay friendly, and TRADE - give on what's cheap to you for what's dear to you, so you both win. And mean your walk-away. The riskiest move of all is to say nothing.

The keystone is the first preparation - your walk-away - so let it land. When you have somewhere else to go if there's no deal, you stop being a desperate person hoping for a favour and become a calm person exploring an option. That calm is the difference between asking and begging. It isn't a threat you wave in their face; it's a quiet anchor inside you that keeps your voice steady. Without it, you're at their mercy. With it, you're an equal.

And carry the spirit of it for life: this is not about becoming hard, or a shark, or someone who squeezes people. A good negotiator asks for a fair deal and looks for the trade where everyone wins - that's how relationships survive the deal. You don't have to change who you are to stop being taken advantage of. You only have to break the silence.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So the next time the gas order is due, Muhammad doesn't just pay the quote. He prepares his three things, then has a calm conversation.

See the three things prepared - and the calm ask that works

1. His walk-away: he's quietly found another supplier who'd sell to him a little cheaper. He doesn't WANT to switch - but knowing he could means he can talk without fear. (The anchor.)

2. His value + reasons: he buys reliably every single week, always pays on time, and his orders are growing as Tank You grows. He's a customer worth keeping - and now he can say why.

3. The supplier's needs: the supplier wants steady, dependable customers who pay and keep coming back - exactly what Muhammad is. There's an overlap to find, not just a price to fight over.

The calm ask: "I've really valued buying from you. I'm ordering more now as we grow, and honestly I've had a lower offer elsewhere - but I'd rather stay with you. Could you do a better price for a loyal customer who's buying more?"

The trade (both win): the supplier doesn't just drop the price for nothing - he offers a discount IF Muhammad commits to a bigger standing weekly order. Muhammad gives him what's cheap to give (guaranteed regular volume) for what's dear to get (a lower unit price). The supplier gains a locked-in growing customer; Muhammad gains real savings every week. Nobody lost; both won - and the relationship is stronger, not strained.

The contrast: all those months of silently paying the first number, Muhammad assumed asking would cost him the supplier. It would have done the opposite. The fear was the only thing that was ever really costing him.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You were never bad at deals - you were just silent in them, and silence always sides with the other person. Break it, calmly and prepared, and you claim your fair share for the rest of your life. The next time anyone offers you a wage, a price, a rent, or terms:

  1. Don't take the first offer on the spot. A fair ask is normal and expected - the other side isn't offended; they're waiting for it. Silence is the only real mistake.
  2. Prepare your three things first. Your walk-away (what you'll do if there's no deal - so you stay calm); your value and reasons (why a fairer number is fair); and their needs (so you can find a trade that helps you both).
  3. Ask calmly, and trade to a both-win. Stay warm, name a fair number, and offer something cheap-to-you for something dear-to-you. You're not fighting them; you're solving it together. And mean your walk-away.

One thing to know, so you reach for the right tool: this is the skill for getting a better deal when you are already at the table - the price, the wage, the terms. Getting a bigger party to want to deal with you in the first place, when you have no leverage at all, is a different skill - there you win by creating something good for THEM, so they invite you in. One tool gets you to the table; this one wins you a fair share once you are there.

A lifetime of taking the first offer is a lifetime of being quietly decided for. You don't need to be loud, or hard, or anyone other than yourself - you only need to prepare a little and break the silence. That single, calm, respectful "could you do better?" - asked again and again across your life - is worth more than almost any raise you'll ever be handed.
