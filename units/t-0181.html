Step 1 of 5

The business case promised what the data cannot say

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and the municipality has put you on the asset committee. In front of you is a business case for a structural health monitoring system on the Stellaford River crossing - 1,100 metres, 14 spans, 42 years old, 18,000 vehicles a day.

It is a competent document. The system costs R6.8 million to install and R900,000 a year to run. It supports deferring a R95 million deck strengthening by six years. Present value of the benefit R27.89 million, present value of the cost R12.84 million, net R15.05 million, benefit-cost ratio 2.17. There is a sensor schedule any engineer would sign.

You check the arithmetic twice. It holds.

And then you notice what the whole R15.05 million rests on. Deferring a strengthening is a judgement about how bad the deterioration is - and this bridge has never once given anybody a recording of what "bad" looks like on it.

The numbers are right and I still cannot sign this. How do I evaluate what a monitoring system is actually worth, and how do I tell the difference between a case that is optimistic and one that is promising something the data cannot say?

Before you read on, decide. The case is in front of you and it is a good one — the kind a competent engineer would write. Monitoring costs R6.8m capital plus R900,000 a year; the benefit is deferring a R95m deck strengthening by six years; the net comes out at R15.05m positive. You check the arithmetic twice and it holds. Then you notice what the whole thing rests on: deferring that strengthening is a judgement about how bad the deterioration is — and this bridge has never once given anybody a recording of what 'bad' looks like on it. Write down how you would evaluate what this system is actually worth — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the test the arithmetic cannot perform

• “The arithmetic holds — approve it.” The arithmetic is not where the fault is. A case can be internally perfect and rest on information that will not exist, and no amount of recalculation reveals that.

• “Apply a discount to the benefit to be prudent.” Treats the problem as optimism, when it is a different kind of error entirely. Haircutting a promise the data cannot keep leaves a smaller promise the data still cannot keep.

• “Ask for a bigger safety margin in the numbers.” Same move, differently dressed. You would be negotiating the size of a benefit whose existence is the actual question.

• “Benchmark it against other monitoring installations.” There is no accepted way to demonstrate a return on these systems, so there is no industry comparable to hide behind — the case stands or falls on this decision and this structure.

• “Reject it — we cannot verify the benefit.” May be where you land, and stated now it is an instinct rather than a finding, and it will lose to a spreadsheet in the meeting.

• Start where the case should have started: name the decision the system would change. A system justified by what it measures is justified by nothing — and note that the benefit is not the R95m but the difference between spending it then and spending it later, which the case has already done correctly and which is the honest, much smaller number. Then apply the test the arithmetic cannot. Monitoring capabilities form a hierarchy of increasing ambition — noticing something has changed, saying where, saying how severe, saying how long remains — and each level demands strictly more than the last, in ways data alone often cannot supply. So ask: which level does this promised decision require, and can this system reach it? Deferring a major intervention is a judgement about how severe, which sits high in that hierarchy — and a system that can detect change on a structure that has never recorded what 'bad' looks like cannot support it, however good its sensors. That is the distinction you must be able to make: this case is not optimistic, it is promising information that will not exist.

Here is the business case rebuilt the way the framework demands.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the case built on operational evaluation - the decision, the damage definition, the axiom test, the operating reality, and an honest account of what may be claimed.

1. Name the decision the system changes - and what changing it is worth

START WITH THE DECISION, NOT THE SENSORS, because a system justified by what it measures is justified by nothing. THE DECISION HERE: an age-based policy schedules a R95 MILLION deck strengthening on the crossing in THREE YEARS. The engineers believe the deck has more life, and monitoring is proposed to support DEFERRING that spend by SIX YEARS. THE VALUE OF THE DEFERRAL, at 8 per cent: the spend already sits in year 3, so its present value is R95m / 1.08^3 = R75.41m; moved to year 9 it is R95m / 1.08^9 = R47.52m. THE BENEFIT IS THE DIFFERENCE - R27.89 MILLION, not R95 million and not R35 million. Against it, the system costs R6.8m capital plus R900,000 a year for ten years, which at a 10-year annuity factor of 6.7101 is R6.04m - a PRESENT VALUE COST OF R12.84 MILLION. NET R15.05 MILLION, BENEFIT-COST RATIO 2.17. And note what you may NOT do: THERE IS NO WIDELY ACCEPTED PROCEDURE TO DEMONSTRATE RATE OF RETURN ON INVESTMENT IN AN SHM SYSTEM, so there is no industry benchmark to borrow and no comparable to hide behind. The case stands or falls on this decision, on this structure.

2. Define damage for THIS structure - and say which level of identification the decision needs

OPERATIONAL EVALUATION - the first stage of the Farrar and Worden statistical pattern recognition paradigm - SETS THE BOUNDARIES OF THE DAMAGE IDENTIFICATION PROBLEM BEFORE ANY ENGINEERING IS SPECIFIED, and its second question is HOW IS DAMAGE DEFINED FOR THIS SYSTEM. Not 'deterioration'. On a 42-year-old post-tensioned concrete deck: SECTION LOSS FROM CHLORIDE-INDUCED CORROSION OF TENDONS, AND BEARING SEIZURE ALTERING THE SUPPORT CONDITION - each showing as a significant change in stiffness and therefore in the modal properties. Loss of effective prestress is also a critical mechanism, though detecting it directly through modal changes before cracking occurs is a known challenge for the method. A definition this specific is what makes a result falsifiable; 'we will monitor the health of the bridge' cannot be wrong, which is why it is worthless. THEN NAME THE LEVEL THE DECISION NEEDS. The identification hierarchy runs: EXISTENCE (is there damage), LOCATION (where), TYPE AND SEVERITY (what kind, how bad), and PROGNOSIS (how much life remains). DEFERRING A R95 MILLION STRENGTHENING IS A SEVERITY JUDGEMENT - LEVEL 3, edging into level 4. Write that down, because box 3 is about to test whether you can get there.

3. Test the promise against the axioms - what level is actually REACHABLE?

THIS IS THE BOX THAT KILLS BAD BUSINESS CASES, AND IT COSTS NOTHING TO RUN. AXIOM III: unsupervised learning on monitoring data can identify THE EXISTENCE AND LOCATION of damage, but using that data to classify THE TYPE AND SEVERITY OF DAMAGE requires SUPERVISED LEARNING - which means training data recorded from the structure IN ITS DAMAGED STATES. NOW ASK WHAT DATA YOU WILL HAVE. The crossing has never been damaged in a way anyone instrumented. There is no damaged-state data, there is no plan to acquire any, and nobody is going to damage a live bridge to get it. SO THE SYSTEM AS PROPOSED CAN REACH LEVEL 2 AND NO FURTHER - it can tell you something has changed and roughly where. THIS SYSTEM CANNOT TELL YOU HOW BAD. Be exact about what that does and does not mean: severity is entirely knowable on a concrete deck BY OTHER MEANS - coring, half-cell potential survey, load testing, physics-based assessment - and engineers establish it that way every week. What Axiom III rules out is a CLASSIFIER LEARNING SEVERITY FROM MONITORING DATA ALONE with no damaged-state examples to learn from. The limit is on the method, not on the profession. AND THE ENTIRE R15.05 MILLION IN BOX 1 RESTED ON A SEVERITY JUDGEMENT. The business case is promising what the data cannot say. This is not a reason to abandon the system - it is the reason to write an HONEST case instead of a flattering one, and box 5 does that. Note also AXIOM IVa: SENSORS CANNOT MEASURE DAMAGE - feature extraction and statistical classification are necessary to convert sensor data into damage information. No instrument on the market outputs 'damage'. Everything you get is inference from a change in response, which is why the next box matters as much as this one.

4. State the operational and environmental conditions, the data limitations - and what they do to the budget

OPERATIONAL EVALUATION'S THIRD AND FOURTH QUESTIONS: what are the OPERATIONAL AND ENVIRONMENTAL CONDITIONS, and what are the LIMITATIONS ON ACQUIRING DATA IN THE OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT. THE ONE THAT SINKS PROJECTS IS TEMPERATURE. In a full-year monitoring study of a concrete highway bridge, a swing from about MINUS 14 TO PLUS 23 DEGREES C MOVED THE FIRST FOUR MODAL FREQUENCIES BY 5 TO 12 PER CENT - LARGER THAN THE CHANGE REAL DAMAGE WOULD PRODUCE. An uncorrected system therefore reports winter as damage and hides damage in summer. Add traffic mass loading, which changes the response hour by hour, and access limits: no possession of the deck without traffic accommodation, and no under-bridge work in flood season. NOW LOOK AT WHAT HONEST HANDLING COSTS. The R6.8m capital: 24 accelerometers at R48,000 = R1,152,000; 36 temperature sensors at R9,500 = R342,000; 8 strain gauges at R31,000 = R248,000; data acquisition, cabling, power and enclosures R1,900,000; baseline modal survey and structural model calibration R1,450,000; data platform and normalisation model development R1,320,000; installation access and traffic accommodation R388,000. SENSOR HARDWARE IS R1,742,000 - 25.6 PER CENT OF THE CAPITAL. THE OTHER 74.4 PER CENT BUYS THE THING THAT TURNS DATA INTO DAMAGE INFORMATION. That split is Axiom IVa written in Rand, and a business case that budgets for sensors has budgeted for a quarter of the system.

5. Present the justifications separately - life-safety first, economic re-scoped, and the route to more costed

THE FRAMEWORK ASKS FOR THE LIFE-SAFETY AND/OR ECONOMIC JUSTIFICATION, AND THE ORDER IS NOT DECORATIVE. (1) THE LIFE-SAFETY CASE COMES FIRST AND IS NOT MONETISED. This crossing carries 18,000 VEHICLES A DAY and is inspected every five years; between inspections nobody is watching. A LEVEL 1 AND 2 SYSTEM - existence and location - gives continuous early warning that something has changed, which is exactly the function the reachable level DOES support. Argue it rigorously on consequence of failure and warning time. DO NOT PUT A RAND FIGURE ON IT: monetising it invites someone to trade it against the R15m, and a number you invented would lose that trade. UNMONETISED IS NOT UNIMPORTANT - IT IS PREREQUISITE, and if the life-safety case does not stand, the economic case is irrelevant. (2) THE ECONOMIC CASE, RE-SCOPED TO WHAT IS REACHABLE. Strike the R15.05m deferral claim, because box 3 showed it cannot be supported. Claim instead what level 2 genuinely buys: inspection targeted by evidence rather than by calendar, and detection of a developing defect between five-yearly cycles. Compute that separately and honestly, and it will be a smaller number. A SMALLER TRUE NUMBER SURVIVES SCRUTINY; A LARGER FALSE ONE DESTROYS THE PROPOSER'S CREDIBILITY THE FIRST TIME IT IS TESTED. (3) COST THE ROUTE TO LEVEL 3 RATHER THAN ASSUMING IT. Severity assessment is reachable - through a validated physics-based structural model whose simulated damage states supply the supervised training data, or through data from a POPULATION of similar structures where some ARE damaged. Both are real programmes with real costs. PUT THEM IN AS A PRICED OPTION, NOT AS AN ASSUMPTION, and the committee can decide whether to buy the higher level with its eyes open.

Box 3 is the one that costs nothing to run and changes everything, and box 5 is what a professional does with its answer. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A monitoring system is justified not by what it measures but by which decision it changes - and the Farrar and Worden framework is what stops you promising a decision the data cannot support. Five questions:

  • What decision does this change, and what is that worth? Operational evaluation comes before engineering. Name the decision, compute its value on this asset, and remember there is no widely accepted procedure to demonstrate rate of return on an SHM system - so there is no benchmark to borrow.
  • What counts as damage here? The damage definition. Chloride-induced section loss in tendons, a seized bearing changing the support condition, and loss of effective prestress - the last a critical mechanism that a modal system struggles to see directly before cracking, which is itself worth stating in the case. Specific enough that a result could be wrong - because "monitor the health of the bridge" cannot be, which is why it is worthless.
  • Which level does the decision need? The identification hierarchy: existence, location, type and severity, prognosis. Deferring R95 million is a severity judgement. Level 3.
  • Which level can you actually reach? Axiom III. Unsupervised learning on monitoring data gives existence and location; classifying type and severity from that data requires supervised learning, meaning training data from the structure in its damaged states. There is none, and nobody will damage a live bridge to make some. So the system reaches level 2, and the R15.05 million was never available. Note the boundary carefully: severity is knowable on a concrete deck by coring, half-cell survey, load testing and physics-based assessment - the limit is on what a classifier can learn from monitoring data, not on what engineers can determine.
  • What else moves that is not damage, and what does handling it cost? Environmental normalisation and Axiom IVa. Temperature alone moved the first four modal frequencies of a monitored concrete highway bridge by 5 to 12 per cent across a 37 degree swing - more than damage would. And sensors cannot measure damage, so most of the budget buys the conversion, not the instruments.

The arithmetic that makes the point, and the trap inside it. The R95 million is already three years away, so its present value is R95m / 1.083 = R75.41m; pushed to year nine it is R47.52m. The benefit is the difference, R27.89 million. Discount it as though the spend were happening today and you get R35.13 million instead - R7.24 million of benefit that does not exist, produced by nothing more than forgetting where the baseline sat. Against a present-value cost of R12.84 million that is the difference between a ratio of 2.74 and a ratio of 2.17. Both look respectable. Only one is true.

And the split that names where the money really goes. Of the R6.8 million capital, sensor hardware is R1,742,000 - 25.6 per cent. The other 74.4 per cent is data acquisition, the baseline survey, the calibrated structural model, the normalisation development and the access to install it. That is Axiom IVa written in Rand: sensors cannot measure damage, so three quarters of the money buys the thing that turns a change in response into a statement about a structure. A case that budgets for sensors has budgeted for a quarter of the system, and it will run out of money at exactly the point where the data starts arriving.

The part that is not a calculation. This crossing carries 18,000 vehicles a day and is inspected once every five years. The life-safety justification is made first, on its own terms, and it is not monetised - because the moment you attach an invented Rand figure to it, somebody may trade it against the economic case, and your invented figure will lose. Unmonetised is not unimportant. It is prerequisite. If the life-safety case does not stand, the economic one is irrelevant; and a level 2 system - continuous warning that something has changed between five-yearly inspections - is precisely the function the reachable level does support.

One honest boundary. You are producing and evaluating the business case: the decision, the damage definition, the level test, the operating conditions, the budget and the honest justification. You are not designing the sensor array, not writing the classification algorithms, and not approving the spend. Your document is what the committee decides on.

Does it scale? A hospital will not fund a scanner because it produces images. It funds it because of the decisions the images change - who is operated on, who is sent home - and if a scanner cannot distinguish severity, no one pretends it can, however good the pictures are. Your monitoring system is that scanner, and box 3 is the question nobody enjoys asking.

T4: Your Turn

The port authority at Meeubaai brings a business case to the same committee. The asset is the 340 m approach trestle to the bulk berth, a piled reinforced-concrete structure, 38 years old, in a marine splash zone.

ItemFigure
Proposed monitoring systemR4.2 million capital, R560,000 a year for eight years
Decision it claims to changeDefer a R48 million deck rehabilitation, currently scheduled in 4 years, by 5 years
Discount rate9 per cent
Stated benefit in the case"R48 million deferred - a saving of R48 million"
Damage definition in the case"Monitor structural integrity and deterioration"
Available dataSix years of visual inspection reports; no instrumentation ever fitted; two similar trestles at other berths, both undamaged
EnvironmentTidal range 1.8 m; splash-zone chloride exposure; berthing impacts logged but not instrumented
  1. The decision and its value. Redo the benefit properly and say what is wrong with the figure in the case.
  2. Damage definition. Rewrite it so a result could be falsified, and name the level the deferral decision requires.
  3. The axiom test. Given the available data, state the level actually reachable and what that does to the claimed value.
  4. Conditions and limits. Name what varies here that is not damage, and say what the case has probably under-budgeted.
  5. The honest case. Write the three justifications separately - life-safety, re-scoped economics, and a priced route to the level you cannot currently reach.

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, your step 1 should give a benefit of R11.90 million, not R48 million - R48m / 1.094 = R34.00m against R48m / 1.099 = R22.10m. Against a present-value cost of R7.30 million that is a net of R4.60 million and a ratio of 1.63. The case overstated the benefit fourfold by treating a deferral as a saving, and a benefit of R48 million on a system costing R7.3 million should have looked too good to anyone reading it.

Second, step 3 is the point of the exercise and it has a wrinkle the bridge did not have. There is no damaged-state data here either - but there are two similar trestles at other berths. That is the beginning of a population, and a population is one of the two real routes to severity assessment. It is not a route today, because both are undamaged. Say so precisely rather than either dismissing it or claiming it.

Third, in step 4, notice that berthing impacts are logged but not instrumented. A trestle's response changes when a vessel comes alongside, and a system that cannot tell a berthing from an anomaly will produce alarms every time a ship arrives. That is an operational condition, it is knowable, and correcting for it is not in the R4.2 million.

T5: The Guardrails

Monitoring business cases fail in ways that make them read better and promise more.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Justifying the system by what it measures. Sensor counts, sampling rates and channel lists are not a value proposition. Name the decision that changes, or there is nothing to fund.

• Promising severity or remaining life with no damaged-state data. Axiom III is not a technicality, it is the boundary of the claim. Existence and location are reachable unsupervised; how bad it is, is not.

• Treating a deferral as a saving. R48 million deferred is not R48 million saved. The benefit is the difference between two present values, and getting this wrong overstated one case fourfold.

• Discounting from today when the spend was already in the future. On the crossing that manufactured R7.24 million of benefit out of nothing - the difference between a ratio of 2.74 and the true 2.17.

• Blurring life-safety and economic justification into one number. Neither can then be examined. Make the safety case first, on its own terms, and leave it unmonetised.

• Budgeting for sensors. They were 25.6 per cent of the capital here. The rest buys the conversion of data into damage information, which is where the project actually lives.

• Ignoring environmental variation. Temperature moved modal frequencies more than damage would. An uncorrected system reports the seasons and calls them findings.

• A damage definition that cannot be wrong. "Monitor structural integrity" survives every result, which means it tests nothing. Name the mechanisms.

• Borrowing a return-on-investment figure from another industry. There is no widely accepted procedure for demonstrating SHM return on investment, so a quoted benchmark is somebody else's asset and somebody else's decision.

• Treating the baseline survey as optional. It is the reference every later measurement is compared against. Cut it and the system has nothing to detect change from.

• Assuming the route to a higher level rather than pricing it. A validated physics-based model or a population of similar structures can get you to severity. Both are programmes with costs. Put them in as an option the committee can buy.

Onalenna could not fault the arithmetic and could not sign the case. Name the decision the system changes and value it properly, remembering there is no accepted return-on-investment benchmark to lean on; define damage specifically enough that a result could be falsified, and name the level of identification the decision requires; test that level against the data you will actually have, because existence and location come unsupervised while severity does not; state the operational and environmental conditions and let them size the budget honestly, since sensors cannot measure damage and were only a quarter of the capital here; and present life-safety and economic justifications separately, claiming only what the reachable level supports and pricing the route to anything more. A business case that promises what the data cannot say does not fail at the committee. It fails three years later, in front of the people who believed it. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take a monitoring proposal from your own organisation and find the decision it claims to change.
