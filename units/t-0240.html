The wrapper and the contents

T1: The Ache

You have money to invest, and you are ready. Then you hit the wall of acronyms. RA. TFSA. ETF. Unit trust. Discretionary. Everyone you ask pushes a different one as the answer, and none of them explains how the pieces fit. Worst of all, you cannot even tell which of these are alternatives to each other and which are meant to go together - is an ETF instead of a TFSA, or inside it? You cannot tell the container from the food.

So the confusion does what confusion always does with money: it freezes you. You either do nothing and leave the money drifting in a bank account, or you pick one blindly because someone confident told you to. Either way, a decision that should be simple has become a fog.

I have money to invest but I am drowning in vehicles - RA, TFSA, ETF, unit trust - and I cannot even tell which are alternatives and which combine. How do I actually use them?

Before you read on, decide. You have money to put away and a list of names that all arrived in the same conversation, as though they were alternatives to one another. Somebody at work said confidently that you should just do the one he does. You have been trying to work out whether one of them goes instead of another or inside it, and you cannot tell — the way they are talked about gives you no clue, and the more you read the less certain you are. So the money has sat where it is for four months while you try to be sure. You are not slow; the terms genuinely are presented as if you must pick one. Write down how you would sort this out — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question that cannot be answered

• “Do what my colleague does.” His circumstances, timescale and obligations are not yours, and you would be adopting an answer without knowing the question it was answering. It also leaves you no better able to judge the next decision.

• “Pick whichever sounds safest.” Chooses on the feel of a word. Several of these names describe things that are not even the same kind of thing, so “safest” is being applied across a category boundary you cannot yet see.

• “Wait until I understand it all properly.” Four months have gone already. The understanding you are waiting for is not going to arrive from more of the same reading, because the reading is what is confusing you.

• “Compare them side by side in a table.” Sensible, and it will produce a table with rows that do not correspond — because, as the next paragraph shows, some of these things are not alternatives at all, and a table implies they are.

• “Leave it in the bank — at least it is not going anywhere.” The default the confusion is pushing you toward, and it is a decision with its own cost, already being paid for four months.

• The fog clears the moment you see that these things sit on two different layers. Asking which of two to choose is often a question that cannot be answered, because it compares different kinds of thing — like asking whether to buy a lunchbox or a sandwich. Some of them are containers: they carry a rule about how the money inside is treated and how easily you can reach it. Others are contents: the actual holdings that sit inside a container and do the growing. You never choose between the layers — you make two choices and put one inside the other, and the impossible question simply dissolves. Two things follow. They are chosen against different questions: one is about treatment and access, the other about what the money is actually invested in. And each container carries a real trade-off — an advantage paid for with a restriction, usually about when or how you can get at the money — so they are matched to purpose rather than ranked, since the right container for money needed soon is not the one for money needed decades away. One thing more, and it is not a footnote: the specific rules and limits attached to any container are set externally and change, so check them against the current official source yourself, at the time you act — not from memory, and not from anybody's confident summary, including a colleague's.

The fog clears the moment you see that a vehicle has two layers. Let us learn them.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you use investment vehicles well - seeing the two layers, knowing the main wrappers and contents, matching each to its job, and verifying the numbers that change.

1. Two layers: the wrapper and the contents

Here is the idea that dissolves the whole fog. When people ask 'should I get a tax-free savings account or an ETF?', they are asking a question THAT CANNOT BE ANSWERED, because it compares two different KINDS of thing - LIKE ASKING 'SHOULD I BUY A LUNCHBOX OR A SANDWICH?'. A tax-free savings account is a WRAPPER: a container with a tax rule around it. An ETF is a CONTENT: an actual investment you put INSIDE a wrapper. So you never choose between them; YOU MAKE TWO CHOICES - which wrapper, and which contents - and then you put the contents inside the wrapper. THE WRAPPER DECIDES THE TAX; THE CONTENTS DECIDE THE GROWTH. Once you see the two layers, the ETF-versus-TFSA question simply disappears: YOU CAN HOLD AN ETF INSIDE A TFSA. This is education, not personal financial advice - always verify current rules and limits with SARS before you act.

2. The wrappers - and the honest trade-off in each

The wrapper decides how your money is TAXED and how easily you can REACH it. Three main ones, each with a real cost, presented straight so you choose with clear eyes. RETIREMENT ANNUITY (RA): your contributions are TAX-DEDUCTIBLE now (up to a legislated share of income) and the growth inside is tax-free - powerful, and worth more the higher your tax rate; THE TRADE-OFF is the money is LOCKED AWAY FOR RETIREMENT (a portion reachable under the two-pot arrangement) and its investments are constrained by a risk-limiting regulation. TAX-FREE SAVINGS ACCOUNT (TFSA): no deduction going in, but ALL growth and ALL withdrawals are tax-free and you can access it any time; THE TRADE-OFF is an annual limit and a LIFETIME CONTRIBUTION CAP that generally does not come back once used - so it is precious, best spent on long-term growth. DISCRETIONARY (ORDINARY) INVESTMENT: no tax break (growth is taxed), but no limits and full flexibility; THE TRADE-OFF is you give up the shelter for unrestricted access.

3. The contents - what actually grows

Inside the wrapper you hold the actual investments, and the two most common for an ordinary investor are: an EXCHANGE-TRADED FUND (ETF) - a fund that simply TRACKS AN INDEX (a whole market at once), trades on the exchange, and is typically LOW-COST AND PASSIVE; and a UNIT TRUST - a fund priced once a day, often ACTIVELY MANAGED by someone trying to beat the market (usually at a higher fee). The crucial point: THESE ARE CONTENTS, and THE SAME KIND OF CONTENT CAN SIT INSIDE ANY WRAPPER - an ETF in a TFSA, an ETF in an RA, a unit trust in a discretionary account. The low-cost diversified default is usually an ETF; you would choose a unit trust when you specifically want active management and accept its cost. The contents decide the GROWTH; the wrapper decides the TAX - two separate layers, two separate choices.

4. Choose the wrapper for the goal, the contents for the plan

Now the two choices become simple, because each answers a DIFFERENT question. WRAPPER - WHAT IS THE MONEY FOR? Money you truly will not touch until retirement, where the up-front deduction helps most, goes into an RA; money you want to grow tax-free but keep reachable goes into a TFSA; money beyond those goes into a DISCRETIONARY account. CONTENTS - WHAT DOES MY PLAN NEED? Usually a low-cost, diversified holding, chosen the same way you would build any long-term investment plan. So a young saver putting away R1,000 a month for a distant goal they might need to reach in an emergency chooses the TFSA wrapper (tax-free and accessible) and a low-cost diversified ETF as the contents - AND PUTS THE ETF INSIDE THE TFSA. An older, higher-earning person with a secure income leans on an RA for the deduction and locked growth, tops up a TFSA, and holds low-cost funds in both. SAME BUILDING BLOCKS; DIFFERENT COMBINATION FOR A DIFFERENT LIFE.

5. The numbers change - and a vehicle is not the plan

Two honesties hold the whole thing together. FIRST, THE NUMBERS CHANGE: the contribution limits, the deduction cap, the tax-free retirement lump sum, the regulation percentages are set by law and adjusted regularly, so you learn what each vehicle IS (durable) but you VERIFY THE CURRENT FIGURES AT THE SOURCE - the official revenue service, SARS - before you act, never trusting a number you read once on a blog. Our R1,000-a-month saver, before committing, checks the SARS website to confirm the current annual TFSA limit and sees their R12,000 a year sits well within it. SECOND, A VEHICLE IS A TOOL FOR A PLAN, NOT THE PLAN ITSELF: the wrapper and the contents serve your goal, your contribution and your allocation - the plan comes first, the vehicle carries it. And plainly: THESE ARE NOT TOOLS FOR THE WEALTHY; THEY ARE TOOLS FOR ANYONE BUILDING WEALTH, STARTING WITH WHAT YOU HAVE - you can open a TFSA or buy an ETF with a modest monthly amount, with no lump sum or big salary needed. This is education, not personal advice; for a large or complex decision a qualified independent adviser is worth it.

Before the boxes, one honesty to hold throughout. This technique teaches you what these tools are and how to think about them. It is education, not personal financial advice. Your situation is unique. For large or complex decisions, you may choose to consult a qualified, independent financial adviser. Always verify current tax rules and contribution limits with the official revenue service (SARS) before you act. Now, the two layers.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. You have R500 a month to invest for a goal that is ten or more years away - but which you might need to reach in an emergency before then.

  1. Spot the error. A friend tells you to "choose between a global ETF and a TFSA." In one sentence, explain the error in their question.
  2. Choose the wrapper. Based on your goal, which wrapper would you choose - RA, TFSA, or discretionary? In one sentence, say why.
  3. Choose the contents. What kind of contents would you look for to put inside your chosen wrapper? In one sentence, say why.
  4. Verify. Before you invest, name one specific number you would confirm on the official SARS website.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the error: an ETF and a TFSA are not alternatives - a TFSA is a wrapper and an ETF is a content, so you do not choose between them; you can hold the ETF inside the TFSA. (The lunchbox-and-sandwich mistake.)

Second, the wrapper: a TFSA fits best - the goal is long-term (so tax-free growth is valuable) but you might need emergency access (which the TFSA allows, unlike the retirement-locked RA), and R500 a month sits comfortably within the limits. A discretionary account would work for access but throws away the tax shelter.

Third, the contents: a low-cost, diversified ETF - broad market exposure, low fees that do not eat the long-term growth, exactly the kind of holding a long-term plan calls for. (A unit trust only if you specifically wanted active management and accepted the higher cost.)

Fourth, verify: the current annual (and lifetime) TFSA contribution limit on the SARS site - to confirm your R6,000 a year (R500 x 12) is within the cap and to know how much room remains - rather than trusting a figure from a blog that may be out of date.

T5: The Guardrails

Do not choose between a TFSA and an ETF. Choose your wrapper for the tax and the goal, and your contents for the growth, and put the one inside the other.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Comparing a wrapper to a content. "ETF or TFSA?" is a lunchbox-or-sandwich question. Separate the two layers and choose each on its own merits.

• Chasing the vehicle instead of the plan. The vehicle carries the plan; it is not the plan. Set your goal, contribution and allocation first, then pick the vehicle to hold them.

• Trusting a limit you read once. Contribution caps, deduction limits and tax rules change by law. Verify the current figures at the source (SARS) before you act.

• Spending the TFSA lifetime cap on short-term money. That contribution room is precious and does not come back. Reserve it for long-term, tax-free growth.

• Locking money you will need in an RA. The RA deduction is valuable, but the money is tied to retirement. Only put money there you truly will not need before then.

• Defaulting to a high-cost actively-managed fund. Fees compound against you over decades. A low-cost diversified fund is the sensible default unless you have a clear reason otherwise.

• Thinking you need a lump sum. These vehicles work at modest monthly amounts. Start with what you have.

• Taking this as personal advice. This is education. For a large or complex decision, a qualified independent adviser is worth it - and verify the rules for your own situation.
