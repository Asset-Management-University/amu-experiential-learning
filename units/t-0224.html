Twice the cost

T1: The Ache

Your team runs pump station A. The new report is on the board, and the headline is brutal: station A costs R200,000 a year to maintain, and station B costs R100,000. Twice as much. The meeting is tomorrow, and everyone in the room will read that one line as a verdict on your team.

And you know, in your bones, that it is not the whole truth. Your station runs older pumps, on a harsher, higher-solids duty, at several times the throughput of station B. Your people work harder, with worse kit, on a tougher job. But none of that is on the page - just the raw number, sitting there like a judgement waiting to fall.

The number is not false. It is just not a fair comparison - because it sets two stations doing completely different jobs side by side and pretends they are the same.

A raw number says my team is twice as expensive - but we do a far harder job. How do I turn a misleading comparison into a fair one, and find where anyone is actually underperforming?

Before you read on, decide. The slide says station A costs twice what station B costs. It is arithmetically correct. Not on the slide: station A runs plant nearly twenty years older, on a higher-solids duty, at several times the throughput. Your people work harder, with worse kit, on a tougher job — and none of that is on the page. Just the raw number, sitting there like a judgement waiting to fall, in a room where you cannot object without sounding defensive. Write down how you would turn this into a fair comparison, and find where anybody is genuinely underperforming — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what a bare number can mean

• “Explain the differences in the meeting.” Necessary and, delivered as a response to a number already on the wall, it lands as excuses. The figure is quantified and your explanation is not, so the figure wins.

• “Challenge the number — it is misleading.” It is not wrong, which is the difficulty: attack the arithmetic and you will lose, because the arithmetic is sound. What is unsound is the comparison.

• “Ask for the comparison to be withdrawn.” Removes the only reference anybody has and leaves you back with a bare figure that means nothing — including any evidence that you are doing well.

• “Cut costs at station A until the numbers match.” Chases a comparison rather than a purpose, and the fastest way to match B's cost on A's duty is to stop doing things that A genuinely needs.

• “Compare against ourselves over time instead.” Safer and self-limiting: it tells you whether you are consistent, not whether you are any good, and if you have always been mediocre it will reassure you every year.

• Start with what the report forgot: a number in isolation means nothing at all. Is a pump that fails eight times a year good or bad? You genuinely cannot say — a raw figure carries no meaning until it is set against something, and putting one on a wall as though it speaks for itself produces either complacency or alarm, both unjustified. Comparing is the disciplined way of supplying that something, and watch what it does: eight failures a year, against a sector norm of three and our own best identical unit at two is no longer a number but a diagnosis with a gap and a proven target. The figure was data; the reference made it information. But the comparison must be like for like, adjusted for what genuinely differs — scale, duty, age, conditions — because the whole value depends on the reference being relevant, and yours is not. And then the discipline that decides whether any of this helps: a gap is a question, not a verdict. It says look here, not somebody is failing — and a gap read as a judgement makes people defend their numbers instead of investigating them, which destroys the only thing the exercise was for.

Here is how you benchmark honestly.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you compare fairly and find the real gap - seeing that good is a comparison, choosing the right reference, normalising to compare like with like, reading the gap as a pointer, and acting on it while keeping it fair.

1. Good is a comparison

START WITH THE THING THE REPORT ON THE BOARD FORGOT: A NUMBER IN ISOLATION MEANS NOTHING. Is a pump that fails eight times a year good or bad? Is R200,000 a year a lot to spend on a station? You genuinely CANNOT SAY - a raw figure carries no meaning at all until you set it against something. This is not a minor point; it is the whole foundation of the unit, and it is the mistake the board report made by putting a number on the wall as if the number spoke for itself. BENCHMARKING IS THE DISCIPLINED WAY OF SUPPLYING THAT SOMETHING: measuring your asset performance against a RELEVANT REFERENCE to find out where you stand and how big the gap is. Watch the transformation it performs. WITHOUT a benchmark you have 'our pumps fail eight times a year' - a bare number that provokes either complacency or panic, both unjustified. WITH a benchmark you have 'our pumps fail eight times a year, against an industry norm of three and our own best identical unit at two, so there is a gap of six and a proven target of two' - which is a DIAGNOSIS WITH A GAP AND A TARGET, something you can actually act on. The number was DATA; the benchmark made it INFORMATION, by giving it a reference against which it finally means something. So the first move, always, when someone hands you a performance number and asks if it is good, is to refuse the question as posed and ask the real one: good COMPARED TO WHAT?

2. The four references - each a different question

'A RELEVANT REFERENCE' IS NOT ONE THING - THERE ARE FOUR FAMILIES OF BENCHMARK, AND CHOOSING THE RIGHT ONE DEPENDS ENTIRELY ON WHAT YOU WANT TO LEARN. INTERNAL BENCHMARKING compares against your OWN - your best identical pump against your worst, this station against that one, this year against last - and it answers 'ARE WE IMPROVING, AND WHY IS OUR BEST UNIT BETTER THAN OUR WORST?'. It is the EASIEST, FAIREST AND MOST UNDER-USED, because the things being compared are already alike and you already own all the data - and it is where you should nearly always start. COMPETITIVE OR INDUSTRY BENCHMARKING compares against a PEER OR AN INDUSTRY NORM and answers 'ARE WE BEHIND THE FIELD?' - useful for knowing whether a whole operation is competitive, though the data is harder to get and harder to make like-for-like. FUNCTIONAL BENCHMARKING compares against a SIMILAR-BUT-NOT-IDENTICAL operation, often a leader in a compatible area, and answers 'WHAT COULD WE BORROW FROM HOW THEY DO IT?' - the source of fresh ideas from outside your own patch. And BEST-IN-CLASS BENCHMARKING compares against the GENUINELY BEST performer of a process, wherever they are and whatever their industry, and answers 'WHAT DOES WORLD-CLASS ACTUALLY LOOK LIKE?' - the most ambitious, aimed at a step change rather than a nudge. THE SKILL IS MATCHING THE REFERENCE TO THE QUESTION: do not reach for a hard-to-get industry number when the question 'why is our best pump better than our worst?' can be answered internally, today, with data you already hold. Start internal, because it is the fairest and the readiest; reach outward when the question genuinely needs it.

3. Compare like with like - normalise, or you have nothing

HERE IS THE ERROR THAT MADE THE BOARD REPORT A LIE, AND IT IS THE COMMONEST AND MOST DAMAGING MISTAKE IN ALL OF BENCHMARKING: COMPARING UNLIKE THINGS. Station A against station B LOOKED like a comparison, but they are not alike - A runs older pumps, on a harsher high-solids duty, at several times the throughput - so A's 'worse' number is not worse PERFORMANCE, it is a DIFFERENT JOB, and acting on it means chasing a gap that is not real. BEFORE ANY COMPARISON MEANS ANYTHING, YOU MUST NORMALISE FOR THE DIFFERENCES THAT MATTER - size, duty, age, operating context, load - so that you are comparing performance and not circumstance. Watch what normalising does to the board report, and let it shock you. Station A spends R200,000 but produces FOUR MILLION units of throughput a year, so its maintenance cost is R200,000 / 4,000,000 = R0.05 PER UNIT. Station B spends R100,000 and produces ONE MILLION units, so its cost is R100,000 / 1,000,000 = R0.10 PER UNIT. On a like-for-like basis, STATION A IS ACTUALLY HALF THE COST OF STATION B - the raw ratio did not merely narrow, IT REVERSED, and the team about to be blamed is in fact the stronger performer. THIS IS WHY AN UN-NORMALISED BENCHMARK IS WORSE THAN NO BENCHMARK: no benchmark leaves you uncertain, but an un-normalised one gives you a CONFIDENT, PRECISE, WRONG CONCLUSION that would have punished the better team and rewarded the worse. Normalisation is not a technicality you can skip when time is short - it is the entire difference between a fair comparison and an unjust one, and the first question to ask of any benchmark someone hands you is: were these things actually alike, and if not, what has been adjusted for the difference?

4. The gap is a pointer, not a score

ONCE YOU HAVE A FAIR, LIKE-WITH-LIKE COMPARISON, A REAL GAP CAN FINALLY APPEAR - AND THE CRUCIAL DISCIPLINE IS WHAT YOU DO WITH IT. HOLD THIS TIGHT: A BENCHMARK TELLS YOU THAT A GAP EXISTS; IT NEVER TELLS YOU WHY. So a benchmark is the START OF AN INVESTIGATION, NOT A VERDICT, and its whole value is the QUESTION IT FORCES you to ask. See it in the example. Onalenna benchmarks each station's IDENTICAL-MODEL pumps INTERNALLY - a genuine like-with-like comparison, same model, same station, same duty - and finds a real gap: three of station B's pumps fail about once a year, but the fourth fails THREE TIMES a year. Because they are identical and identically used, that gap is genuine and it POINTS somewhere: 'why does this one pump fail three times as often as its twins?' She investigates and finds it is running on a different, over-stretched maintenance interval - a fixable, proven improvement she would NEVER have gone looking for without the benchmark forcing the question. THE VALUE WAS NOT THE NUMBER; IT WAS THE QUESTION THE NUMBER FORCED - and the answer was an improvement that was available all along, invisible until the benchmark pointed at it. NOW THE TRAP, WHICH DESTROYS ALL OF THIS: TREATING THE BENCHMARK AS A SCORE. A score is something you DEFEND (explain away your bad number), GAME (pick a flattering reference), or use to SHAME (rank the league table and blame the bottom). Every one of those throws away the only thing a benchmark was ever good for - the honest gap that shows you a better way is possible. A gap you cannot yet explain is the MOST VALUABLE finding you can get, because it is improvement you did not know was available; a score is a dead end. Read the gap as a pointer to a question, follow the question to a cause, and the cause to a fix - that is benchmarking doing its actual work.

5. Act on the gap - and keep it fair

TWO TRUTHS CARRY THIS WHOLE UNIT, AND BOTH ARE ABOUT PEOPLE AS MUCH AS NUMBERS - THEY ARE THE NON-NEGOTIABLE HEART OF IT. FIRST, ON FAIRNESS: A FAIR COMPARISON RESPECTS THE PEOPLE DOING THE WORK. WE NORMALISE A BENCHMARK NOT JUST FOR TECHNICAL ACCURACY, BUT FOR HUMAN FAIRNESS. IT ENSURES NO PERSON OR TEAM IS UNFAIRLY JUDGED BY A NUMBER THAT DOES NOT REFLECT THE TRUE DIFFICULTY OF THEIR WORK. AN UN-NORMALISED COMPARISON IS NOT JUST WRONG; IT IS UNJUST. The station A team was one meeting away from being blamed for doing a harder job WELL, condemned by a comparison that was never fair - and normalising was the act that protected them, which is why it is a duty and not a nicety. SECOND, ON RECEIVING A GAP: A BENCHMARK THAT SHOWS A GAP IS NOT A JUDGEMENT. IT IS A GIFT. IT IS A MAP TO AN IMPROVEMENT YOU DID NOT KNOW WAS AVAILABLE, DISCOVERED BY SOMEONE ELSE, THAT YOU CAN NOW LEARN FROM FOR FREE. THE CORRECT RESPONSE TO DISCOVERING YOU ARE BEHIND IS NOT SHAME OR DEFENSIVENESS. IT IS CURIOSITY. The pump failing three times a year was not an accusation against its maintainers; it was a free discovery of a better maintenance interval, waiting to be found and then rolled out to protect the rest. So HERE IS HOW TO ACT ON A GAP: once it is fair and real, go and find WHAT THE BETTER PERFORMER DOES DIFFERENTLY, and adopt it - that is the entire payoff, the improvement someone has already proven possible, now yours. And hold the discipline that keeps it human: never wield a benchmark as a stick before it is a fair, normalised comparison; never game it to look good; and meet every gap with 'what do they do differently?' rather than 'how do we defend our number?'. A benchmark used this way makes a whole organisation better and no one smaller; used as a league table, it makes everyone defensive and no one better.

T3: The Blueprint

The value of a benchmark is not the number it gives you, but the question it forces you to ask. Five questions:

  • Is this number good or bad? You cannot say in isolation - good is a comparison. A cost or a failure rate means nothing until you compare it against a relevant reference.
  • Compare against what? Four references - internal (your own best or your past), competitive/industry, functional (a similar operation), best-in-class - each answering a different question.
  • Is the comparison fair? Compare like with like - normalise. Adjust for size, duty, age and context first, or the comparison is a confident, wrong (and unjust) conclusion.
  • What does the gap tell me? That a gap exists, never why. A benchmark is a pointer, not a score - the start of an investigation, not a verdict.
  • What do I do with it? Find what the better performer does, and adopt it - a benchmark is a free map, not a stick. Keep it fair so no one is unjustly judged.

Good is a comparison. Start with the thing the report on the board forgot: a number in isolation means nothing. Is a pump that fails eight times a year good or bad? Is R200,000 a year a lot to spend? You genuinely cannot say - a raw figure carries no meaning until you set it against something. Benchmarking is the disciplined way of supplying that something: measuring your asset's performance against a relevant reference to find out where you stand and how big the gap is. It is the move from a bare number - "our pumps fail eight times a year" - to a diagnosis with a gap and a target: "our pumps fail eight times a year, against an industry norm of three and our own best identical unit at two - so there is a gap of six, and a proven target of two." The number was data; the benchmark made it information.

The four references - each a different question. "A relevant reference" is not one thing; there are four families, and choosing the right one depends on what you want to learn. Internal benchmarking compares against your own - your best identical pump against your worst, or this year against last - and it answers "are we improving, and why is our best unit better than our worst?" (it is the easiest, fairest and most under-used, because the comparison is already like-with-like). Competitive or industry benchmarking compares against a peer or an industry norm and answers "are we behind the field?" Functional benchmarking compares against a similar-but-not-identical operation, often a leader in a compatible area, and answers "what could we borrow from how they do it?" And best-in-class benchmarking compares against the genuinely best performer of a process, wherever they are, and answers "what does world-class actually look like?" Pick the reference that fits your question - and start internal, because it is the fairest and you already have the data.

Treat the benchmark as a score - to defend, to game, or to shame the bottom of a league table - and you throw away the only thing it was ever good for.

A benchmark is a gift, and fairness is not optional. Two truths carry this whole unit, and both are about people as much as numbers. First, on fairness: a fair comparison respects the people doing the work. We normalise a benchmark not just for technical accuracy, but for human fairness. It ensures no person or team is unfairly judged by a number that does not reflect the true difficulty of their work. An un-normalised comparison is not just wrong; it is unjust. The station A team was about to be blamed for doing a harder job well. Second, on receiving a gap: a benchmark that shows a gap is not a judgement. It is a gift. It is a map to an improvement you did not know was available, discovered by someone else, that you can now learn from for free. The correct response to discovering you are behind is not shame or defensiveness. It is curiosity. The pump failing three times a year was not an accusation against its maintainers - it was a free discovery of a better interval, waiting to be found. So never wield a benchmark as a stick before it is a fair comparison, never game it to look good, and meet a gap with "what do they do differently?" not "how do we defend our number?"

Does it scale? A student comparing their exam mark to a friend's learns nothing until they compare like with like - the friend who scored higher had studied the subject for two years longer, or sat an easier paper - and the useful benchmark is not "am I worse?" but "what does the person who does this well actually do?" Comparison without normalisation is how people wrongly conclude they are failing; comparison as a curious search for a better way is how they actually improve. A plant and a life both learn that the gap is a map, not a verdict.

T4: Your Turn

You are Onalenna. You have finished analysing the two pump stations, and tomorrow's meeting will discuss the raw report. Write the one-page memo for your Director. Here are your figures.

Station AR200,000/yr maintenance; 4,000,000 units throughput/yr; older pumps, harsh high-solids duty
Station BR100,000/yr maintenance; 1,000,000 units throughput/yr; newer pumps, easy duty
Inside station BFour identical pumps: three fail ~1x/year each; the fourth fails ~3x/year

The memo must do three things:

  1. Correct the record. Show the raw cost comparison and explain, with numbers, why it is misleading - then present the normalised, like-for-like comparison.
  2. Identify the real opportunity. State the genuine performance gap you found through the internal benchmark at station B, and quantify it.
  3. Recommend the next step. State the question the real gap forces, and the specific, low-cost investigation you will run to answer it.

The test of a real memo: three things.

First, correcting the record: the raw report shows station A at twice station B's cost (R200,000 vs R100,000), but this compares two stations doing very different jobs and is not a like-for-like comparison. Normalised to cost per unit of throughput: station A is R200,000 / 4,000,000 = R0.05 per unit; station B is R100,000 / 1,000,000 = R0.10 per unit. On a fair, like-for-like basis station A is half the cost of station B - the raw comparison reversed once the difference in duty and throughput was accounted for, and station A is, if anything, the stronger performer.

Second, the real opportunity: the genuine, like-with-like gap is inside station B - four identical pumps on the same duty, three failing about once a year and the fourth failing three times a year. Because they are identical and identically used, that is a true benchmark and a real gap: the fourth pump is failing three times as often as its twins for a reason that is not the pump.

Third, the next step: the gap is a pointer, so the question is "why does that one pump fail three times as often as its identical twins?" - and the low-cost investigation is to compare its maintenance interval, operating settings and history against the other three (a desk exercise, no spend), which will very likely reveal a fixable difference - a stretched interval, a wrong setting - that can be corrected and, if it is the cause, rolled out to protect the rest. (A strong memo also names the dignity point: normalising protected the station A team from an unjust blame, and the station B gap is an improvement to chase with curiosity, not a fault to punish.)

T5: The Guardrails

A raw comparison of two different jobs is not a benchmark - it is a rumour with a ratio. The value of a benchmark is the question it forces, never the score it delivers.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Judging a number in isolation. A cost or a failure rate alone means nothing. It becomes information only when compared against a relevant reference.

• Comparing unlike things. Two assets on different sizes, duties or ages are not comparable as they stand. The worse raw number may be the harder job, not worse performance.

• Skipping normalisation. An un-normalised benchmark is a confident, wrong conclusion - and an unjust one. Adjust for size, duty, age and context before you compare.

• Treating the benchmark as a score. The number says a gap exists, not why. It is the start of an investigation, not a verdict to be defended or celebrated.

• Gaming the comparison. Choosing a flattering reference or a convenient normalisation to look good throws away the only value the benchmark had - the honest gap.

• Using it to shame the bottom. A gap is a free map to improvement, not a stick. Meet "we are behind" with curiosity - what do they do differently? - not blame.

• Benchmarking and never acting. A gap you find and ignore is worse than one you never measured. The point is to adopt what the better performer proved possible.

• Wrong reference for the question. Internal for "why is our best better than our worst?"; industry for "are we behind?"; best-in-class for "what is world-class?". Match the reference to what you want to learn.

The board said station A cost twice as much and the team was about to be blamed, but the number compared two stations doing entirely different jobs and, normalised to cost per unit, station A was actually the cheaper of the two. So benchmark honestly: a number in isolation is meaningless, so compare against a relevant reference - internal against your own, industry against a peer, best-in-class against the finest - choosing the one that fits your question; compare like with like by normalising for size, duty, age and context, because an un-normalised comparison is not just wrong but unjust; read a fair gap as a pointer and not a score, letting it start an investigation into why the better performer is better; and act on what you find, adopting the proven improvement. The value of a benchmark is the question it forces, not the score it delivers - so normalise for fairness before you judge anyone, and meet a gap with curiosity, because it is a free map to a better way, not a verdict on the people. The next time a raw number ranks one team below another, do not reach for blame - ask first whether it was ever a fair comparison, and then ask what the better result has to teach.
