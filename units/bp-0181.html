The business runs on me running myself into the ground and I'm breaking before it does

Movement 1 - The Simulation

Before you begin - a note of care. This unit is about what happens when a person carries a whole business on their own body, and starts to break under it. If that is close to where you are right now - exhausted, frightened, not sleeping, feeling like you cannot stop - please read gently, and at your own pace. You do not have to do this alone, and you do not have to wait until you collapse to ask for help. If things feel heavy, you can talk to someone today: SADAG's free 24-hour crisis helpline is 0800 567 567, or you can SMS 31393 and a counsellor will call you back. There is more at the foot of this page. You matter more than the business does.

It is just after eight on a Tuesday night, and you are finally home. You are Muhammad, and you run Tank You, the gas-cylinder delivery business, with your brother Ibraaheem. You have just parked the bakkie after a fifteen-hour day that started at five this morning. The ache in your lower back is a familiar friend by now. You swing your legs out of the cab and the smell of your own sweat mixes with the faint, sweet smell of LPG that never quite leaves the bakkie, or your clothes.

In your hand is your phone, and the screen will not stop. Seven unread messages from customers, one of them asking for a delivery tonight, now. A missed call from a regular you forgot to phone back. A message from your auntie in the family group asking if you are coming for lunch on Sunday, and underneath it your mother has replied: "he is always too busy for us now." You have not answered.

You do the sum without wanting to. You and Ibraaheem are sleeping maybe five hours a night. You work from five in the morning until eight at night, six days a week, sometimes seven. The business is growing - it clears about R30,000 a month for the two families, good money, real money you are proud of. But there is exactly one person who takes the orders, answers the phone, and plans the route. That person is you. You both load the cylinders at the depot, drive the route, swap the tanks at the door, and collect the money. If you stop for a day, the day's work simply does not happen. If you stop for a week, you are not sure the business survives it.

Lately you have been making small mistakes. You delivered to the wrong address last week and had to drive back across town. You snapped at a good customer on the phone who was only asking a question. Your wife stood at the kitchen door last night, not shouting, which was worse. She said, quietly, "I feel like I am raising our children by myself." You did not see your youngest awake this morning. You might not see her awake tomorrow morning either.

You sit in the dark cab a moment longer with the phone glowing in your hand, and somewhere underneath the tiredness is a small, cold fear you have not let yourself look at: what happens to all of this - the family, the business, me - if the person it all runs on simply gives out?

Where do you start? What is the real problem here, and what would you need to look at before you could fix it?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Before you read how Muhammad thinks it through, stop and make your own best attempt. Write a few lines. Don't aim for perfect - aim for honest. Use these to focus your thinking:

  1. Make a list of everything that only happens because you do it - taking the orders, planning the route, answering the phone, chasing the payments, keeping the book. How many of those could carry on tomorrow if you were not there?
  2. "Push through, just work harder, rest after this month" has been the plan since the business started. Look at the small mistakes, the snapping, the ache in your back. What is that plan quietly doing to the person the business depends on?
  3. The business is making good money - about R30,000 clear a month. So why does it still feel like it is failing? What is being spent to earn that money that does not show up on any invoice?
  4. Look back at your answers to 1-3 - they keep circling the same thing. Say it in one plain sentence: what is the single thing this whole business is running on, and what is happening to it? Once it has a name, the move to make is easier to see.
Go back to your own list - the orders, the route, the phone, the chasing, the book. Do not count how many items are on it. Ask a harder question of each one: if you were not there tomorrow, would that thing happen at all, or would it simply wait for you? Now look at how many are in the second group. That number is not a workload. It is a description of something.
It is tempting to say the answer is time, or money, or the size of the business - so test those. More hours are not available; you are already using them. More money is not the problem; the business clears about R30,000 a month. And a bigger business would need MORE of the thing that is already running out. So it is not any of the three. Look instead at what every item on your list has in common, and at the ache in your back and the snapping, which are on no invoice.
Everything on the list runs through one thing, and that thing is not a system, a process or a resource the business owns. It is a person - and it is being spent. Write your sentence about THAT: name what the business runs on, and say plainly what is happening to it. Do not soften the second half; the plan of pushing through has been running since the business started, and the sentence is only useful if it says what that plan is costing.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Uncle Yusuf, who runs the corner shop across the road from the gas depot, saw Muhammad one Friday morning, took one look at him, and called him over. "Come," he said. "Leave the bakkie. Have a cooldrink with an old man." Muhammad had stood behind that counter himself, not so long ago. Here is how the two of them worked it through.

First, they drew the picture honestly. Uncle Yusuf took a flattened cardboard box and the pen from his shirt pocket. "Tell me everything you and Ibraaheem do in one day," he said. Muhammad listed it all - up at half past four, drive to the depot, buy the stock, load the cylinders, drive the route, swap the tanks, collect the cash, chase the ones who did not pay, answer the phone all day, plan tomorrow's route at night, keep the book. Next to most lines Yusuf wrote two names: Muhammad, Ibraaheem. But next to "take the orders", "plan the route" and "answer the phone", he wrote only one: Muhammad. Putting it on paper made the problem legible. The business did not have a sales problem. It had two people doing everything, and nothing in place for the day one of them cannot.

Then they named what it was costing - not in rands, in the man. Uncle Yusuf asked the hard questions. "How many hours of sleep?" Five. "When did you last take a full day off - no phone, no bakkie?" Muhammad could not remember. "When did you last sit through supper with your family without that phone buzzing on the table?" He went quiet. The small mistakes, the snapping at a good customer, the permanent ache in his back - Yusuf pointed at them one by one. "Those are not separate problems, my boy," he said. "Those are the warning lights on the dashboard. That is the engine telling you it is running too hot." Muhammad had been treating his own exhaustion as a personal failing - I just need to be stronger - when it was a signal that the way the business ran was unsurvivable for a human being.

Then they looked honestly at the plan that was not a plan. "Work harder, rest after this month" had been the strategy for a year. They tested it the way you test anything: does it work? It did not. The harder Muhammad worked, the more mistakes he made, the more his back hurt, the shorter his temper became. Working harder was not protecting the business - it was slowly damaging the one part of the business that could not be replaced by Monday: him. And it had no end. There would always be another cold front, another rush of orders. "After this month" was not rest. It was a way of never resting while feeling like you meant to.

Then they sorted the real fixes into three honest buckets.

  1. Boundaries - deciding, in advance, what the business is not allowed to take. A hard stop at seven. One full day a week where the bakkie stays parked and the phone is for family only. Boundaries feel like they cost the business orders. What they actually buy is a person still standing in a year's time.
  2. Delegation - getting work off the two pairs of hands. Not all of it at once, but the parts someone else can do. The first step was smaller than hiring anyone: sharing the phone, sharing the planning, getting the tasks that only Muhammad did off Muhammad. Every task shared is a task that no longer stops dead when one brother is sick, or simply asleep. (Writing the route down on one page came out of this conversation, and it is what later made it possible to bring in Sipho as a driver - but that is a different lesson, and it came after this one.)
  3. Recovery - building rest into how the business runs, rather than bolting it on "after this month". Sleep treated as part of the work, not stolen from it. A real day off each week. And the one Uncle Yusuf pressed hardest: telling his wife, and his brother, how tired he actually was. Saying out loud that it was too much.

None of this made Muhammad a worse businessman. It made him a business that could survive its founder being human. A business that requires one person never to rest is not a strong business - it is a business with one part in it that is already wearing out, and that part is a person.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own life, try one in-between case - a little harder than Muhammad's, because here the danger is wearing a disguise.

Mmapula runs a busy creche, and she has staff. Six women work for her, looking after forty children. On paper she is not Muhammad at all - she is the boss, she has a team, she does not lift gas cylinders at five in the morning. And yet she has not had a full weekend in a year, she takes every difficult parent call herself, she does the wages by hand every Friday night, she is the only one who can calm the children when something goes wrong, and last week she cried in her car in the car park for no reason she could name. But I have staff, she keeps telling herself. I have already delegated. This cannot be that.

Work it with me. Answer each hint before you open it.

Hint 1 - what exactly have her six staff taken off her?

They mind the children. Now list what they have not taken: the wages, the parent complaints, the crises, the decisions, the emotional load of being the one everyone turns to. How many of those could carry on for a week without Mmapula? (Answer before reading the next hint.)

Hint 2 - apply the single-point test from the worked solution

Having staff is not the same as having shared the load. Mmapula delegated the visible work - minding children - and kept every piece of the invisible work: money, conflict, emotion, judgement. The business looks like it rests on six people. The part that would stop dead if she fell over rests on one. Her concentration is almost as dangerous as Muhammad's - it is just better hidden, because the staff make it look solved.

Hint 3 - now use the same three buckets

Which invisible task could she actually hand over - a bookkeeper for the wages, a senior staff member trained to take the routine parent calls - and what single boundary (one protected weekend, one phone-off evening) would let her recover, before the crying in the car park becomes something worse?

The lesson of this step: having staff is not the test. Having genuinely shared the load that would otherwise stop without you - that is the test. A founder can look surrounded by help and still be the single point of human effort the whole thing depends on. Watch the invisible load especially - money, decisions, emotion - because that is the part that hides. Hold that lens as you turn to your own world now.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Muhammad's problem was not "a demanding business". It was a business that ran on one person's relentless effort, with no boundaries, no shared load and no recovery - until that person began to break before the business did. That same shape shows up far beyond a delivery bakkie. Find it where you are - and be gentle with yourself while you do.

  1. List every task your work or business needs done in a week, and write next to each one who could do it if you were not there. If your name is on nearly every line - the visible work and the invisible work - you have this problem.
  2. Look honestly at your own warning lights, the way Uncle Yusuf made Muhammad look at his: sleep, rest, your temper, your body, the people at home telling you something. Which ones have you been calling "just being busy"? How long have they been going on?
  3. "I'll rest after this month, this order, this season" - how many times have you said that now? Be honest about whether the "after" has ever actually arrived.
  4. Pick the one boundary within your reach this week - a real day off, the phone face-down at supper, a hard evening stop - and decide it in advance, before a busy week talks you out of it.
  5. Pick the one task you could hand to someone else - even something small, even if it costs a little money - and name who you would ask, and when. The invisible work counts most: the books, the decisions, the calls you take because "only I can".
  6. Notice who you will tell. Is there someone at home, a friend like Uncle Yusuf, a doctor or a counsellor you have been meaning to talk to about how you actually feel? Recovery is not a reward for finishing. It is part of how the work gets to continue at all.

If the warning lights are loud - the chest, the dread, the crying you cannot explain - please reach for proper help this week, not after this month.

You are allowed to build a business that does not require you to break. In fact, that is the only kind worth building.
