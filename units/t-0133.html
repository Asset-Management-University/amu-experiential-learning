Step 1 of 5

There was no villain

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. The board is about to approve the Stellaford upgrade programme, and before it does, somebody has asked a fair question: how did the last big one go wrong? So you are handed the file on the Eastern Pipeline Upgrade - approved at R 50m over 12 months, delivered at R 115m over 28 - and asked to write the lessons-learned note.

You read the whole file. Every minute of it. And you cannot find the mistake.

There is no villain. There is no incompetence, no missing calculation, no ignored warning. Every single decision in that file was defensible at the time it was taken, by capable people, with the information that was actually in front of them. You check it twice, because you do not believe it either.

Yet the outcome crippled the capital programme for four years. And your note keeps coming out as one of two useless things. Either we must be more careful next time - which everybody nods at, and which changes nothing, and which you would be ashamed to sign. Or a list of names, which would be unjust, and which you also know to be simply untrue.

Something wrecked that project. It is in the file. You can feel it moving behind the decisions like a ghost in the machine, and you have no vocabulary for it at all.

Every decision was reasonable and the outcome was a disaster. If nobody was careless and nobody was dishonest, then what exactly do I write down - and what do I ask the board to change?

Before you read on, decide. Approved at R50m over 12 months. Delivered at R115m over 28 months. Nobody was hurt, nothing failed in service, the pipeline works — and the capital programme behind it did not recover for four years. Go through the file and you find nothing to condemn. The original estimate was built properly from this project's own drawings. When the survey found rock over two kilometres in month five, the estimate was raised by R4m and the date left alone. In month fourteen, with R62m spent against about forty per cent complete, a further R30m was approved and the work went on. Every one of those was defensible on the day. Write down what you would actually put in the note to the board — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the thing moving behind the decisions

• “Name the people who approved it.” Unjust, and — the part that should stop you — simply untrue. Each approval was reasonable on what was known that day, so a list of names is a false account, and it teaches everyone that the lesson is to be elsewhere when the hard decisions arrive.

• “Write it up neutrally: the project was more complex than anticipated.” True, unfalsifiable and useless. You would be signing something that changes nothing, and you know it.

• “Recommend better estimating and tighter controls.” The recommendation that always gets written and never bites, because it asks the same people to be more careful about the same task in the same conditions.

• “It was bad luck — the rock was genuinely unforeseeable.” The rock, perhaps. But look at what happened after the rock was found: a material discovery produced an eight per cent revision and no change to the date at all. That part was not luck.

• “Recommend a contingency percentage on future projects.” Sensible and insufficient, because a contingency set by the same reasoning that set the estimate inherits the same optimism, and gets spent early for the same reasons.

• What you are missing is a vocabulary. The thing that wrecked this is real, is in the file, and is not a person: it is a set of predictable patterns in how judgement behaves under particular conditions. An estimate built entirely from this project's own particulars and never tested against what comparable projects actually cost. A first number that then holds everything in place, so a serious discovery yields a token revision. Evidence read for the reasons the plan can still stand. And further in, the pull of what has already been spent. Name those at each decision point and an inexplicable outcome becomes a mechanism — something that can be argued about and designed against. Two disciplines make it usable: separate a bias from an incentive, since one is how minds work under uncertainty and the other is what somebody was rationally rewarded for, and they need different remedies; and because no mechanism is fixed by asking people to try harder, answer each one with a guardrail that would have changed the decision whoever was making it. There is no column for a name.

There is a form of post-mortem for precisely this, and it has no column for blame. Here is a finished one.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the note she finally wrote. One page, five boxes, and not one person named in it.

1. The outcome - facts only, no adjectives

CORATHYL EASTERN PIPELINE UPGRADE. Approved: R 50.0m over 12 months. Delivered: R 115.0m over 28 months. That is a 130% cost overrun and a 133% schedule overrun. Nobody was injured. Nothing failed in service. The pipeline works. The capital programme behind it did not recover for four years, because the money came from somewhere.

2. The decision points - where the outcome was actually determined

A - Month 0, budget approval: R 50.0m, estimated bottom-up from this pipeline's own drawings and quantities. B - Month 5, the geotechnical survey finds unexpected rock over 2 km; the estimate is raised by R 4.0m and the completion date is not changed at all. C - Month 14, R 62.0m spent against about 40% completion; a further R 30.0m is approved and work continues. D - Month minus 6, before approval: the demand forecast in the business case was produced by the same team that needed the project approved.

3. The mechanism at each point - name it, and separate bias from incentive

A - PLANNING FALLACY and OPTIMISM BIAS: an inside-view estimate, built entirely from this project's own specifics and never tested against what comparable projects actually cost. B - ANCHORING on the original R 50.0m, so a material finding produced an 8% revision; with CONFIRMATION BIAS, the survey read for reasons the plan could still hold. C - ESCALATION OF COMMITMENT and the SUNK COST FALLACY: at R 62.0m for 40% complete the forecast at completion was already 62.0 / 0.4 = R 155.0m, so R 30.0m was never going to be enough, and everyone in that room could have done that division. D - MECHANISM: Strategic Misrepresentation (incentive-driven). DIAGNOSIS: The business case was developed by the same team that would benefit from its approval. This structure creates a systemic incentive to produce an optimistic forecast. Note the wording: it describes a STRUCTURE, not a person, and it is the only form in which this finding may be recorded.

4. The causal chain - how the mechanism actually produced the number

The low anchor at A set a 12-month programme. That programme left no float for a full geotechnical investigation, so the site work was curtailed to fit. The rock over 2 km was therefore discovered during construction rather than before it, when it cost roughly six times more to deal with. The 8% revision at B then preserved the anchor rather than correcting it, so by C the project was already past the point where any single approval could recover it. Not one of those steps required anybody to be careless.

5. The process guardrails - impersonal, checkable, and honest about their limits

1. REFERENCE CLASS FORECASTING with a mandatory uplift before approval: the 24 comparable bulk pipeline projects in the province had a median overrun of 60%, which would have taken the R 50.0m estimate to R 80.0m at the gate. 2. A mandatory PREMORTEM above a stated value threshold. 3. Independent estimate review by someone with no stake in the approval. 4. Defined RE-BASELINE TRIGGERS, so that continuing past a variance threshold is an explicit recorded decision instead of a drift. 5. SEPARATION OF THE FORECASTER FROM THE BENEFICIARY - the only structural answer to D. AND THE HONEST NOTE: R 80.0m would still have understated the R 115.0m outcome. These tools narrow the error. They do not produce the right number, and anyone who tells you otherwise has just demonstrated the bias this whole document is about.

Look at what the shape of it does. There is a column for mechanism and a column for process guardrail. There is nowhere to put a name, and that is not squeamishness - it is precision. A name explains nothing and predicts nothing; the next project will have different people in it and will fail the same way. And notice box 3, decision C: at R 62m for 40% complete, the finish was already forecast at R 155m. Anybody in that room could have done that division. Nobody did, and the unit is about to tell you why that is completely normal.

T3: The Blueprint

Before the names, the feelings - because you have had every one of these, on something much smaller than a pipeline.

You have felt the pull to keep pouring money into something failing because of how much you have already put in. That is escalation of commitment, driven by the sunk cost fallacy. You have looked at worrying new information and found yourself reading it for the parts that let the plan survive. That is confirmation bias. And you have been given a number at the start - a budget, a date, a quoted price - and afterwards every adjustment felt like a deviation from the real number rather than a correction of a wrong one. That is anchoring. None of those made you careless. They made you human, and they are documented, predictable and measurable.

So the post-mortem asks five questions:

  • What actually happened? Facts only, no adjectives. R 50m to R 115m; 12 months to 28. The moment a judgement enters box 1, everyone starts arguing about the judgement instead of the project.
  • Where was the outcome really decided? Not everywhere - in three to five moments, usually early, usually in a meeting nobody remembers as important. Finding those decision points is most of the work.
  • What mechanism was operating? Here is the central distinction of the whole unit, and it is the one people get wrong. Some underestimation is unintentional: the planning fallacy, our documented tendency to underestimate cost, time and risk while overestimating benefit - and it persists even when we know that comparable projects overran. That is optimism bias, and nobody chose it. But some underestimation is intentional: strategic misrepresentation, where a forecast is optimistic because an optimistic forecast is what gets projects approved and funded. These two look identical in a spreadsheet and they need completely different fixes. Training, checklists and awareness do nothing whatsoever to an incentive.
  • How did the mechanism produce the number? A label is not a finding. "Anchoring" explains nothing on its own; the causal chain does - low anchor, no float, curtailed site investigation, rock found during construction at six times the cost. Write one sentence per link so that anyone who disagrees can point at the exact link they doubt.
  • What would have caught it? Only process answers count. The strongest is the outside view: instead of estimating from this project's own specifics, go and look at what actually happened to comparable projects. Operationally that is reference class forecasting - identify a class of past similar projects (twenty to thirty is the usual minimum), establish the distribution of overruns, then uplift your estimate accordingly. Alongside it, the premortem: before approving, the team assumes the project has already failed and works backwards to explain why. It uses hindsight against the planning fallacy, and it has been found to improve people's ability to identify reasons for future failure by around thirty per cent.

Now the hardest professional judgement in this unit, and the reason it is bounded so tightly. Decision D is not a bias. It is an incentive: the business case was written by the team that needed it approved. You must be able to name that, because a post-mortem that finds only cognitive bias leaves the incentive structure completely intact and quietly guarantees the next failure. But you record it in exactly one form, and never any other:

MECHANISM: Strategic misrepresentation (incentive-driven).
DIAGNOSIS: The business case was developed by the same team that would benefit from its approval. This structure creates a systemic incentive to produce an optimistic forecast.

Read what that does. It describes a structure. It makes no claim about anybody's intent - which you could not establish anyway, and which is not your business. And it points straight at a fix a board can actually implement: separate the forecaster from the beneficiary. That is the whole discipline in three lines. Diagnose a process, recommend a process, and never diagnose a person. You are not a psychologist and this is not an investigation into anyone's character.

Does it scale? A root cause analysis of a failed bearing does not trace the fault to the fitter; it traces it to a lubrication schedule or a missing check in the procedure. A behavioural post-mortem traces a budget overrun not to the planner but to an estimating process that had no independent review in it.

T4: Your Turn

The Stellaford pump station refurbishment, from the scheme's own records. Approved at R 8.0m over 6 months; delivered at R 17.6m over 15 months. From the file:

  • The estimate was built from the last refurbishment done at Corathyl - a newer station - without checking what comparable refurbishments elsewhere had actually cost.
  • At month 3 the switchgear was found to be non-compliant. The estimate was raised by R 0.6m and the completion date was left unchanged.
  • At month 9, R 11.0m had been spent against roughly 50% completion. A further R 4.0m was approved and work continued.
  • The benefit case - a projected reduction in pumping energy cost - was prepared by the depot that had been asking for this refurbishment for three years.
  1. Box 1. State the outcome in facts only. Compute the cost and schedule overruns.
  2. Box 2 and 3. Name the mechanism at each of the four decision points. At least one must be a bias and at least one must be an incentive - and you must be able to say which is which and why it matters.
  3. Do the division nobody did. At month 9, what was the forecast at completion? Was R 4.0m ever going to be enough?
  4. Box 4. Write ONE causal chain, one sentence per link, from a mechanism to the money.
  5. Box 5. Propose two process guardrails. Then state honestly what they would not have caught.

The test of a real answer: read your finished note. Your diagnosis of an incentive must describe a structural arrangement of roles (e.g. "the forecaster and beneficiary were the same team"). But if any part of your note reads as a criticism of the people in those roles, rewrite it. The test is this: could you show your note to the people involved, and have them agree that you have described the system they were working in, without feeling personally accused? If not, it is not finished. And if every mechanism you named was a bias, look again: you may have written the comfortable version.

T5: The Guardrails

This tool is unusually easy to misuse, because it hands you sophisticated language for things people already want to say about each other. Guard it carefully.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Diagnosing a person instead of a process. The moment a name or an identifiable role appears in your mechanism column, the document stops being a post-mortem and becomes a complaint. It will also be resisted, buried, and correctly ignored.

• Bias language as sophisticated blame. "He was anchored" and "that team is prone to optimism bias" are blame wearing a laboratory coat. The vocabulary exists to describe mechanisms that operate on everybody, including you, including whoever is reading your report.

• Calling an incentive a bias. The comfortable error, and the expensive one. If the structure rewarded an optimistic number, no amount of training, awareness or care will fix it - only changing who produces the forecast will. Diagnose it as bias and the incentive survives your entire report untouched.

• Calling a bias an incentive. The opposite error, and it is unjust. If the estimate was simply built inside-view by people doing their honest best, saying the numbers were shaped deliberately is an accusation you cannot support.

• Hindsight bias in you, the analyst. You know how it ended; they did not. Judge every decision only on the information available at the time it was made. If your finding requires the decision-maker to have known the future, it is not a finding.

• Recommending things that are not processes. "Be more careful", "improve communication", "strengthen governance". Nobody can implement these, nobody can audit them, and everybody will agree to them - which is exactly why they are so popular in lessons-learned notes.

• A reference class of three. Two or three remembered projects is not the outside view; it is the inside view with anecdotes. If you cannot assemble a proper class, say so plainly rather than dressing up a small sample as data.

• Believing the tools will save you. Reference class forecasting would have moved that estimate from R 50m to R 80m, and the project still cost R 115m. A premortem finds perhaps a third more of the threats. These tools narrow the error; they do not deliver the right number. Any claim otherwise is the very overconfidence the whole method exists to detect.

Onalenna read a file full of reasonable decisions that added up to a disaster, and had no words for it. Now you do. State the outcome without adjectives, find the few moments where it was really decided, name the mechanism at each and be honest about whether it was a bias or an incentive, write the chain from mechanism to money so it can be argued with, and recommend only changes a stranger could audit. Then say what your recommendations will not catch. Do that and you have done something rarer than a good project: you have made the next one less likely to fail, without needing anybody to have been the villain.
