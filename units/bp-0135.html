Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Olwethu, the maintenance manager at a large quarry. On Monday morning the weekly work plan is on your office whiteboard, and it is a thing of beauty - you and your planner spent all of last Thursday getting it right. It has proactive work: new bearings for the conveyor that feeds the primary crusher, a full service for the de-watering pumps, an alignment check on the secondary crusher's motor. Your best team is assigned. Everyone has agreed.

By Tuesday afternoon the plan is already bleeding. A screen deck cracks and aggregate spills everywhere, so you pull the team off the conveyor job to weld it up. By Wednesday lunchtime it is a bloodbath: the secondary crusher has jammed solid, and the crew who were meant to be servicing the pumps are clearing a tonne of rock from a machine that should be running - and a stopped crusher costs the quarry about R8,000 an hour in lost aggregate. The proactive work, the work that stops these failures from happening, is pushed to "next week" again.

You stare at the whiteboard, at the neat jobs that now look like a fantasy. Your phone rings - the production manager, asking why the plan is in pieces - and that familiar, sinking feeling of failure settles in.

What is the point of making a plan if reality always destroys it - by Wednesday my whole maintenance schedule is fiction?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

At first you think it is a planning problem, so you try to fix the plan. Your first instinct is to plan in more detail - hour-by-hour schedules, step-by-step instructions. But a perfect twenty-page plan to replace bearings is useless when the team holding it is sent to un-jam a crusher; the plan was never the problem. Your next instinct is to just work harder - Saturday overtime to catch up the planned jobs you missed. It works for a while, then the team tires, burnout climbs, people make more mistakes under pressure, the overtime eats your budget, and because everyone is exhausted more things break the following week. You are only spinning the loop faster. Your last, desperate thought is to stop planning altogether and just react to whatever breaks. That is a disaster: with no plan there is no proactive work at all, the plant's health gets worse and worse, and the breakdowns multiply.

You realise you have been asking the wrong question. It is not 'plan harder' or 'plan less'. So before you read on: how do you stop the fires from STARTING, when all your time is spent putting them out?

A plan destroyed by emergencies is not a planning problem - it is a RELIABILITY problem wearing a planning mask. You are in a doom loop: too much reactive breakdown work steals the time needed for proactive work, so more breaks down, so there is even less time - firefighting forever. You cannot escape it by running faster; you must break it by force. Five moves: (1) SEE THE LOOP (reactive crowds out proactive which creates more reactive). (2) MEASURE IT - for every hour worked, planned or unplanned? (the first week is often a shocking 75% unplanned); you cannot manage what you do not measure. (3) RING-FENCE a small, non-negotiable slice of capacity for proactive work that emergent jobs may NOT raid (one team, 7-10am, protected unless it is a true safety crisis or a whole-plant stoppage) - hold the line. (4) TRIAGE emergencies honestly - safety risk or whole-plant stop jumps the queue; much 'urgent' work can wait for the next planned slot; stop letting urgency masquerade as importance. (5) ATTACK THE ROOT - feed every breakdown into root-cause work and capture the history so the fires stop starting. Stop firefighting long enough to fireproof.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you see the problem clearly: this is not a planning problem, it is a RELIABILITY problem wearing a planning mask. You are trapped in a doom loop - too much reactive breakdown work steals the time needed for proactive work, so more things break, which creates even more reactive work; firefighting forever. The only way to win is to break the loop by force. Five moves:

  • See the loop - a reliability problem wearing a planning mask: the breakthrough is to stop seeing this as a planning failure and see the doom loop for what it is. Too much reactive, breakdown work steals the time you would need for the proactive work that PREVENTS breakdowns - so more things break, which creates even more reactive work, which leaves even less time to prevent the next failure. Firefighting forever. The crucial consequence: you cannot escape this loop by running faster or planning harder - a better plan and a more tired team just spin it quicker. You have to stop and change the rules of the game, because the broken schedule is only the symptom; the disease is that the fires keep starting and nobody has time to stop them.
  • Measure it - the reactive ratio: you cannot manage what you do not measure, and you cannot argue for change with a feeling. So start a dead-simple log: for every hour your team works, mark it Planned (from the weekly schedule) or Unplanned (a breakdown). Do not judge - just count. The first week the number is usually shocking - perhaps three of every four hours are unplanned - and that single figure, the reactive ratio, is your baseline and your argument. It turns 'the plan keeps breaking' from a vague complaint into a hard number you can watch fall as the loop loosens, and it tells everyone, honestly, how deep the firefighting really runs.
  • Ring-fence a slice of proactive time: this is the big, brave move that actually breaks the loop: declare a small, non-negotiable slice of capacity that emergent work is NOT allowed to raid. Even one team, from 7am to 10am each day, ring-fenced as proactive-only - not pulled off for ANY breakdown unless it is an immediate safety crisis or the whole plant is down. It feels reckless when fires are burning, but it is the only way out: that protected time is what you use to do the preventive work that stops tomorrow's fires. Start small if you must - even a little protected proactive time, fiercely defended, begins to bend the loop the other way. Hold the line; the pressure to surrender it is exactly the loop fighting back.
  • Triage emergencies honestly: the loop feeds on false urgency - treating every breakdown as a top priority that justifies smashing the plan. So triage honestly at the moment the call comes: is this a SAFETY risk, or has it stopped the WHOLE operation? Then it genuinely jumps the queue. But a great deal of 'emergent' work is not an emergency at all - it is planned work that was never planned, or an inconvenient fault that could wait safely until the next planned slot. Much 'do it now' can become 'do it Thursday, properly'. Stop letting urgency masquerade as importance, and a surprising share of the emergencies that were shredding your week simply move back into the plan.
  • Attack the root - fireproof, don't just firefight: ring-fencing buys you time; this is what you spend it on. Treat every breakdown that does get through as a valuable lesson rather than just a fire to put out: the cracked screen deck investigated (root-cause discipline) reveals a vibration that had been worsening for months - fix that and the deck stops cracking. Capture each failure in the work history so the patterns become visible. Every root cause you eliminate is a fire that never starts again, which returns time to the proactive slice, which lets you eliminate the next one. That is the doom loop finally running in reverse - the only durable way out of firefighting is to fireproof.

See it transform Olwethu's week. She stops trying to out-run the loop and starts a simple log: in the first week, three of every four hours are unplanned - a shocking 75%. She makes the brave move and ring-fences one team, 7am to 10am every day, as proactive-only, not pulled off for anything short of a safety crisis or a whole-plant stop, and she holds the line when the loop fights back. She triages honestly, and many 'urgent' jobs turn out to be able to wait for a proper planned slot. And every breakdown that does get through becomes a lesson: the cracked screen deck is investigated, a months-old worsening vibration found and fixed, so it stops cracking. Slowly the 75% unplanned falls to 60%, then 40%; the plan holds to Thursday, then Friday; the team is less stressed and proud of a more reliable quarry; the production manager stops phoning to complain and starts phoning to say thank you. A plan destroyed by emergencies is not a planning problem - it is a reliability problem wearing a planning mask, and you cannot escape the doom loop by running faster. Break it by force: measure your reactive ratio, ring-fence a small non-negotiable slice of proactive time that emergencies may not raid, triage false urgency honestly, and turn every failure into a root cause you eliminate - so the fires stop starting. Stop firefighting long enough to fireproof: protect a little proactive time today and there is far less fire tomorrow.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This is not only about quarries - your own working day runs the same loop. You plan to do two important things, a key report and a presentation (your proactive work), and the day is hijacked by 'breakdowns': a flood of emails, colleagues with "a quick question", urgent calls. By evening you are busy and stressed but the report is untouched. That is the doom loop, in miniature.

See the same moves on a hijacked day

• Measure: how much of today went to your plan versus reacting to others?
• Ring-fence: block 90 minutes tomorrow morning as protected "focus time" - email off, phone on silent.
• Triage: when a colleague comes by, ask "is this urgent-urgent, or can we talk at 11am?" - most things can wait.
• Attack the root: if the same person interrupts every day, have a gentle chat about a better way to connect that respects the focus time.

By protecting one small part of the day you guarantee the important work gets done - and you break the cycle of constant reaction, one protected hour at a time.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

If your schedule is the victim of constant emergencies, you are living in a firefighting doom loop right now. It is time to stop blaming the plan and start fireproofing. Three actions you can take this week:

  1. Start measuring. One simple sheet: for every job your team completes, a mark in 'Planned' or 'Unplanned'. Do not judge the numbers - just see them. That is your starting point.
  2. Propose a small experiment. Ask your manager and team: "can we protect just one person, for just two hours, on just one day next week, to do one important proactive job without interruption?" Make it a safe, small test.
  3. Pick one recurring failure - the one that makes everyone groan because it happens so often. Next time it breaks, gather the team for fifteen minutes and ask "why did this really happen?" - one level deeper than you normally would.

Name your doom loop, your reactive ratio (even a guess), and the small slice of proactive time you would ring-fence first. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Measure one week's planned-vs-unplanned and design the first protected slot together.)

This was never about firefighters who fight too much - your team are the heroes holding a broken system together, and praising them for fighting fires while never giving them time to prevent fires is the real failure. Your job is not to build a perfect plan for an imperfect world; it is to be the leader who calmly builds a small wall against the chaos - the protected time, safety and space your team needs to make the future more reliable, one fireproofed hour at a time.
