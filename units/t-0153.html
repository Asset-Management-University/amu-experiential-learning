A colour where I needed a number

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have to decide whether to invest a large sum in a second, independent safeguard on the chlorine store. It is a serious decision - chlorine is dangerous, the money is real, and the board wants a recommendation you can defend.

So you do what you always do with a risk question: you fill in a risk matrix. Likelihood against consequence, and out comes a colour: high.

And you sit there, because "high" does not answer the question. It does not tell you whether the safeguards already in place reduce the risk enough. It does not tell you by how much a second safeguard would reduce it further. It does not tell you whether the spend is justified or wasted. It is a colour, and the decision needs a number. You can feel that the tool is wrong for the question - but the risk matrix is the only technique you ever reach for, and you have a vague sense there is a whole standard full of other techniques, and no idea how to choose from it.

My go-to risk matrix gave me a colour, and this decision needs a number - so the technique was wrong for the question. There are dozens of risk techniques in the standard; how do I choose the right one for a given decision, and justify it?

Before you read on, decide. The question is whether a second, independent safeguard on the chlorine store is justified. You did what you always do: scored likelihood against consequence, and the cell came out amber. The finance director then asked how much risk the second safeguard actually removes, and what that reduction is worth against its cost — and you cannot answer, because you cannot subtract one colour from another. Nothing you did was wrong. You have a vague sense there is a whole standard full of other techniques and no idea how to choose from it. Write down how you would pick a method for a given decision — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two questions that select the tool

• “Use a finer scale — more levels in the matrix.” Gives you more colours. A finer ordering is still an ordering, and the director asked for a quantity that can be set against a cost.

• “Assign numbers to the bands and multiply them.” The dangerous answer, because it produces something that looks like a quantity. Ranks are labels, not measurements — multiplying band three by band four yields a number with no units and no meaning, which will nonetheless be quoted in a board paper.

• “Get a specialist to do a proper quantitative study.” Sometimes right, and it hands over the judgement you are missing rather than acquiring it. You would still not know what to ask them for, which is how expensive studies answer the wrong question.

• “Amber means act — recommend the safeguard.” Treats the tool's output as a decision rule. It also cannot survive the follow-up question, which is not whether to act but whether this action is worth this money.

• “The matrix is the standard approach — it should be good enough.” It is a good instrument being asked a question it was never built to answer, and it will always produce an output. That is exactly why the mismatch does not announce itself.

• Select the technique from what the decision needs, on two axes. First, which part of the work you are actually doing: finding out what could go wrong, examining one specific scenario in depth, or comparing and prioritising things already understood. Those are different activities served by different methods, and reaching for a comparison tool when the task is analysis is the commonest error there is — and it is yours. Second, whether the decision needs a description or a quantity, which is settled by what the answer must support: ranking a portfolio needs an ordering, justifying a large spend needs something that can be weighed against a cost. And here is what most people never discover — the discipline's own standards are catalogues of dozens of techniques, organised precisely to support this choice. The sense that there is one universal tool is an artefact of how you were trained, not of the field. Then, as always, record the choice and argue it.

There is a catalogue for exactly this, and choosing from it well fits on one page. Here it is, for the chlorine decision.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the technique justification - the right method chosen for the decision, and argued.

1. What is the decision, and which risk step does it need? (identify / analyse / evaluate)

The decision: is a second, independent safeguard on the chlorine store justified? That is not a 'what could go wrong' question (identification is already done) - it is ANALYSIS of a specific scenario: how much does the existing protection reduce the risk, and how much would a second layer add? And it needs a QUANTITATIVE-ish answer, because the decision is to spend or not spend a large sum - you cannot justify that with a colour. Naming the step and the output the decision needs is what points you at the right technique.

2. What does the catalogue offer for that step? (IEC 31010 as a menu)

IEC 31010:2019 is a CATALOGUE of about forty risk techniques, not a single method - and its whole purpose is to help you choose. It organises techniques by the risk step they serve: to IDENTIFY (brainstorming, checklists, structured what-if, HAZOP); to ANALYSE (fault tree analysis, event tree analysis, LOPA, bow-tie, Bayesian networks); to EVALUATE and compare (a risk matrix, cost-benefit/ALARP, decision analysis). Cutting across all of these is the qualitative-versus-quantitative axis: some give a description or a ranking, some give numbers. It is a menu matched to context, not a ranking of 'best' techniques.

3. Which technique fits the step and the output needed? (select by fit)

Match to this decision. It needs ANALYSIS with a semi-quantitative output, so a qualitative RISK MATRIX cannot answer it - it would give 'high' again, a colour, when the decision needs to know by how much each safeguard reduces the risk. A BRAINSTORM only identifies; it cannot analyse. LOPA - Layer of Protection Analysis - is the fit: it is a semi-quantitative technique built precisely to test whether the independent protection layers reduce a specific scenario's risk to a tolerable level, which is exactly the question. The technique follows the step and the output, not the habit.

4. Can I justify it in one page? (the justification)

The one-page justification: THE DECISION - is a second chlorine safeguard justified? THE STEP AND OUTPUT - analysis of a specific scenario, needing a semi-quantitative measure of risk reduction. TECHNIQUE CHOSEN - LOPA. WHY NOT THE MATRIX - it gives a qualitative colour, not the layer-by-layer risk reduction the spend decision requires. WHY NOT A FULL QUANTITATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT - LOPA gives sufficient confidence for this decision at a fraction of the cost and data; a full QRA is not warranted. That argument, on one page, is the deliverable - a defensible technique choice, not a reflex.

5. Is it the lightest technique that gives the confidence needed? (proportionate)

The governing principle: the LIGHTEST technique that gives the confidence the decision actually needs. Too light - the risk matrix here - gives false confidence: a reassuring or alarming colour that cannot support the spend either way. Too heavy - a full quantitative risk assessment for a single safeguard question - burns weeks and demands data you may not have, to answer something LOPA settles. LOPA sits proportionately between them: more than a matrix, less than a QRA, and exactly matched to 'is one more layer justified?'. Choosing the technique that fits, not the one you know or the fanciest one, is the whole skill.

Notice the technique is chosen last, after the decision has been sized on two questions: which risk step does it need, and does it need a qualitative or a quantitative answer? Those two decide the choice - and they immediately rule out the matrix Onalenna reached for by habit. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

IEC 31010 is a catalogue of about forty risk techniques, and the skill is choosing the right one - not reaching for the one you know. Five questions:

  • What is the decision, and which risk step does it need? Risk assessment (per ISO 31000) has three steps: identify (what can happen), analyse (understand a particular risk - its causes, likelihood, consequences), and evaluate (compare against criteria, compare options, decide on treatment). A technique good for one step is not the one for another. The chlorine decision is analysis of a specific scenario, and it needs a quantitative answer.
  • What does the catalogue offer for that step? Treat IEC 31010 as a menu. To identify: brainstorming, checklists, HAZOP. To analyse: fault tree analysis, event tree analysis, LOPA, bow-tie, Bayesian networks. To evaluate: a risk matrix, cost-benefit and ALARP, decision analysis. And cutting across all of them, the qualitative-versus-quantitative axis: does the decision need a description or ranking, or actual numbers?
  • Which technique fits the step and the output needed? Match on both. The chlorine decision needs analysis with a semi-quantitative output, so a qualitative risk matrix cannot answer it - it gives a colour, not the risk reduction. A brainstorm only identifies. LOPA - Layer of Protection Analysis - is the fit: a semi-quantitative technique built to test whether the independent protection layers reduce a specific scenario's risk to a tolerable level, which is exactly the question. The technique follows the step and the output, not the habit.
  • Can I justify it in one page? Write the argument: the decision, the step and output it needs, the technique chosen, and why the familiar tool cannot answer it while a heavier one is not warranted. A technique choice you can defend on one page is a professional decision; a reflex reach for the matrix is not.
  • Is it the lightest technique that gives the confidence needed? Proportionate rigour, again. Too light - the matrix - gives false confidence. Too heavy - a full quantitative risk assessment for one safeguard question - burns weeks and demands data you may not have. LOPA sits between them, exactly matched to the decision.

One honest boundary. You select and justify the technique from the catalogue. You are not re-learning how to run each one - facilitating a HAZOP or a LOPA, drawing a bow-tie, are separate skills with their own units. You are matching the technique to the decision, on two axes: which risk step, and qualitative or quantitative. (This is the same discipline as choosing the right root-cause method for a failure - proportionate method selection - pointed at risk decisions rather than investigations.)

Does it scale? It is choosing the right measuring instrument - a ruler, a set of calipers, or a laser scanner - by the precision the job actually needs, not by the fanciest tool you own or the first one your hand falls on in the drawer.

T4: Your Turn

Two different risk decisions arrive:

  • A. A new dosing skid is being installed and nobody has yet worked out what could go wrong with it - the team needs a structured way to surface the hazards.
  • B. Three different maintenance-interval options for a critical asset need to be compared, so the one with the best balance of risk and cost can be chosen.
  1. The step. For each, name which risk-assessment step it needs - identify, analyse, or evaluate.
  2. The output. For each, say whether the output should be qualitative or quantitative.
  3. The technique. Name a suitable IEC 31010 technique for each.
  4. Justify one. Write one line saying why your technique fits its decision.

The test of a real answer: your two techniques should be different, because A is an identification problem and B is an evaluation/comparison problem. If you chose the same technique - or a risk matrix - for both, look again at the step each decision actually needs; the step, not habit, picks the tool.

T5: The Guardrails

Technique selection goes wrong in a few consistent ways, most of them a version of "the tool I know".

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The risk matrix for everything. The most common reflex, and the one that started the ache. A matrix evaluates and ranks; it cannot analyse how much a safeguard reduces a risk. Reaching for it regardless of the decision is habit, not judgement.

• A colour where a number was needed. Using a qualitative technique when the decision needs a quantitative answer gives false confidence - a reassuring or alarming colour that cannot support a spend either way.

• A number where a colour would do. Reaching for a heavyweight quantitative model when the decision only needed hazards identified burns effort and demands data you may not have. Rigour beyond the decision's need is just cost.

• The wrong step. Asking an identification tool to do detailed analysis, or an evaluation tool to find hazards, is choosing for the wrong step. Name the step first, then the technique.

• Ignoring what the technique demands. Some techniques need data, expertise and time you do not have. A technique you cannot properly resource is the wrong choice, however well it fits in theory.

• Confusing selecting with running. The deliverable here is the justified choice, not the completed study. Choose well and the study has a chance; choose badly and no amount of effort in the wrong technique will save the decision.

• Reading the catalogue as a ranking. IEC 31010 is not a list of best-to-worst techniques; it is a menu matched to context. The "best" technique is the one that fits this decision's step, output and resources.

Onalenna reached for the one risk technique she knew and got a colour where the decision needed a number. Now she can open the catalogue on purpose: name the decision and the risk step it needs, decide whether the output must be qualitative or quantitative, choose the technique that fits, justify it on a page, and check it is the lightest one that gives the confidence required. IEC 31010 is a menu, not a default - and matching the technique to the decision is the difference between a defensible recommendation and a reassuring colour.
