My single-physics model said the bearing was fine at 71 degrees; it hit 94 and we tripped the pump at peak, leaving two towns short of water. How do I build a model I can bet a town's water on - one that tells me what it knows and what it does not?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on the pump-house floor at Kgwadi Water Utility, a small Highveld town, and the morning lift has started. In front of you is High-Lift Pump 3 - a 2.4 MW vertical pump pushing 1,100 litres a second up 180 metres to the reservoir that feeds three towns. On a steel bench is a laptop running a single-physics model a contractor built two years ago: it predicts bearing temperature from flow and speed alone.

The plant manager, Realeboga, stands next to you, arms folded. "Last Tuesday," she says, "your model told me the bearing was fine at 71 degrees. The real bearing hit 94. We tripped the pump at peak demand. Two towns went short on water for five hours." You can smell the warm oil off the gland housing, and under your hand the casing hums at a pitch you have learned to distrust.

You check the numbers. The old model used one physics: heat from friction. But the real bearing temperature depends on flow, on the cooling-water loop, on the vibration that climbs as the impeller wears, and on the metal expanding as it heats - things that push and pull on each other, which a single-physics model can never see. And worse: even when it is close, it gives a single number, 71, with no honest band around it. So you cannot tell Realeboga whether the truth is "71, give or take 2" or "71, give or take 25".

She has a R3.1 million budget line for an asset-decision system, and she will sign it to you or to an outside vendor. "I need a model I can bet a town's water on. One that tells me what it knows, and what it does not know. Can you build me that, or not?"

I need to build a twin that joins several physics together, corrects itself against the real pump's data, and reports its own uncertainty - so that when it gives a number, I know exactly how much to trust it.

Before you read on, decide. The existing model says Pump 3’s bearing will reach 71 °C. Nobody can tell Realeboga whether that means 71 give or take 2, or 71 give or take 25 — and the decision resting on it is whether to shed load half an hour early or trip at peak. The pump has worn: vibration is 4.1 mm/s against 2.6 when new. Every 10 °C of rise closes the shaft clearance by about 12 µm, which raises friction, which raises heat. There is R3.1 million on the table. Write down what you would build, and what would make its number worth betting a town’s water on — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and which part of “71” she is actually buying

• “Build a more detailed physics model.” Detail and trust are different things. A very precise model that never meets the real pump can be confidently wrong, and however fine it gets it still will not tell anybody how much to rely on it.

• “Train a model on the pump’s own data and let it learn.” It will learn what this pump has already done, and it will be just as confident about conditions it has never seen — which is precisely where the load-shedding decision lives.

• “Ask for a wider margin on the temperature limit and stop worrying.” This buries the question. The margin is paid for in production every single day, to cover an uncertainty nobody has measured — you are buying insurance against a risk of unknown size.

• “Model the whole pump properly — a complete digital copy of it.” The answer that sounds most like a twin. It produces something too slow to answer inside the thirty minutes the decision needs, and scoped to no decision in particular, which means it is never quite right for any of them.

• “Validate it once against the commissioning data and trust it.” Necessary — and one match is a statement about the past. The impeller has worn since; the pump the model was checked against no longer exists.

• What makes a number bettable is not detail. It is three things done together. Scope it to one decision, with the accuracy and the lead time written down (thirty minutes ahead, within ±3 °C) — a model that answers everything answers nothing well enough to sign. Join the physics that actually couple, including the loop the old single-physics model could never see: heat closes the clearance, the tighter clearance raises friction, the friction makes more heat. And keep correcting it against the real pump, so it tracks the machine as it wears rather than the machine as it was commissioned. Do that and the model stops saying 71 and starts saying 71, give or take 2. The ±2 is the thing Realeboga is buying. The 71 was never the hard part.

That is a high-fidelity hybrid digital twin. Here is how you build it.

T2: The Tool in Action

A digital twin is a living computer model of one specific real asset - not any pump, this pump - kept in step with the real thing by a constant data stream. High-fidelity means accurate enough to bet a decision on; hybrid means it fuses two powers: the laws of physics, and learning from real data. The point of the whole thing is a number you can trust, with an honest statement of how much. Watch it built for Pump 3.

Step 1 - Scope to the decision, list the coupled physics

The decision: 'predict bearing temperature 30 minutes ahead, with an honest uncertainty band, so we can shed load early instead of tripping at peak' - required accuracy +/- 3 C, lead time 30 minutes (the ISO 23247 idea of a twin scoped to a use case, not a general copy of the pump). The coupled physics: fluid (flow 1,100 L/s at 1,490 rpm, head 180 m); thermal (bearing friction makes heat, the cooling loop removes it); vibration (impeller wear, now 4.1 mm/s up from 2.6 when new); thermal expansion (every 10 C rise closes the shaft clearance by about 12 micrometres, raising friction, raising heat - a feedback loop the old single-physics model never saw).

Step 2 - Build the physics model, then make it fast (ROM)

The full multi-physics model (in Modelica) joins fluid, thermal and structural sub-models by the coupling equations. Validate it: a new bearing at 1,100 L/s predicts 68 C, and the commissioning logs show 67 C - within 1 C, so the coupled physics is sound. But the full model takes 8 seconds to simulate one minute of pump behaviour, far too slow for a sensor stream arriving every 2 seconds. So you build a ROM - run the full model across the operating envelope, fit a fast surrogate - and it runs the same minute in 40 milliseconds (200 times faster), matching the full model within 0.4 C.

Step 3 - Wire the live two-way link (ISO 23247)

Physical to digital: stream six live tags - flow, speed, bearing temperature, cooling inlet temperature, vibration, motor current - into the twin every 2 seconds. Digital to physical: the twin's 30-minute-ahead prediction and its uncertainty band flow to the control-room dashboard, and later to an automatic early-load-shed signal. The asset, the data and the twin are registered under the ISO 23247 entities, so every link is explicit and auditable.

Step 4 - Correct the drift with data (the PINN)

Compared to the real bearing over 90 days, the ROM reads low by a growing amount - 1 C in month one, 4 C by month three - because the worn impeller and a fouled cooling loop are not in the physics. Train a PINN on the residual (real minus ROM), constrained to obey the heat-balance equation. After training, the hybrid (ROM + PINN) tracks the real bearing within 1.2 C across the 90 days - down from a 4 C physics-only error.

Step 5 - Quantify the trust, then hand the decision to the human

Combine three errors in quadrature: sensor noise (+/- 0.5 C), ROM simplification (+/- 0.4 C), PINN scatter (+/- 1.0 C): sqrt(0.5^2 + 0.4^2 + 1.0^2) = sqrt(1.41) = about 1.2 C (1-sigma), so a 90% band is about +/- 2.0 C. Now the twin reports not '94' but '94 C, 90% likely between 92 and 96'. Tuesday's repeat: at 06:10 the twin predicts 93 C in 30 minutes, 90% band 91-95; the trip limit is 90 C, and the LOWER edge of the band (91) is already above it - so the dashboard flags 'shed 8% of load now'. You shed early at 06:12; the bearing peaks at 88 C; the pump runs through peak demand; no town goes short. You show Realeboga the band, not just the number - and she signs the budget.

See what the twin did. The old model gave a bare "71" and cost two towns five hours of water; the new one gives "93, ninety per cent sure it is between 91 and 95" - and that band is what let a person act in time. And see the mission in it, because the heart of this technique is a model honest about its own ignorance. The single-physics model was not just wrong, it was wrong with confidence - it handed over one clean number and hid how little it knew, and a manager bet a town's water on that false certainty and lost. The hybrid twin refuses to do that. It does the harder, humbler thing: it joins the tangled physics it can model, it corrects itself against the real pump for the drift it cannot, and then - crucially - it says out loud what it still does not know, as a band around every answer. That honesty is not a weakness in the tool; it is the whole point of it, the same integrity as an engineer who quotes an optimistic ceiling rather than a comforting promise. And notice who decides: the twin does not trip the pump. It shows Realeboga the number and the band, and she sheds the load - the machine carries the relentless computation it can do without rest, and the human keeps the judgement, now with honest information instead of false comfort. The R3.1 million is the measure; the aim is three towns' water kept flowing, a number worth trusting, and a person empowered to act before the bearing ever reaches the limit.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the twin in five steps.

  • 1. Scope to the decision, list the coupled physics. The decision sizes everything; the physics map the problem.
  • 2. Build the physics model, then make it fast. The multi-physics model, validated; then a real-time ROM.
  • 3. Wire the live two-way link. Bidirectional data flow - sensors in, prediction and band out.
  • 4. Correct the drift with data. A physics-constrained neural network on the residual.
  • 5. Quantify the trust, hand the decision to the human. The honest band, and a person to act on it.

The ideas underneath. A multi-physics model writes down the laws for several physical effects - fluid, heat, vibration - and how they couple, so the solver can capture the feedback a single-physics model misses. Because the full model is too slow to run live, you build a reduced-order model (ROM): a fast, accurate surrogate fitted to it, so the twin keeps up with the sensor stream in real time. The bidirectional data flow is what makes it a twin and not just a model: live data in, prediction and uncertainty out. No physics is perfect, so the real asset slowly drifts from the model as it wears and fouls; a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) - an AI constrained to obey the physical laws, so it cannot learn nonsense - is trained on the residual (real minus model) to close that gap. And uncertainty quantification (UQ) combines every error source into one honest band, turning a bare number into "94 C, ninety per cent likely between 92 and 96". It fits a high-value, critical asset with tangled physics, real sensor data, and a real decision riding on it; it is over-engineering for a cheap simple part, and impossible without sensors - match the complexity of the tool to the value of the problem.

Two disciplines keep the twin trustworthy. First, report the band, never the bare number - a single figure hides how much it should be trusted, and a confident wrong number is more dangerous than an honest uncertain one; the whole value of the twin is that it states what it does not know, so a person can weigh it. (When the whole band crosses the limit, as 91-95 does against a 90 trip, the decision is robust; when only part of it does, that doubt is itself the information.) Second, keep the human in the loop and match the tool to the value - the twin advises, it does not rule, because judgement under real stakes belongs to a person; and a nine-week hybrid twin earns its cost only on a critical asset, never on a part a simple model would serve.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your twin true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Report the band, not the bare number. A confident single figure that hides its own error is what cost two towns their water. The honest band is the whole point.

• Scope the twin to a decision. Not a general copy of the asset - a model built to answer one question well. The decision sizes the accuracy, the lead time, the physics you bother to include.

• Constrain the learning to physics. A neural network on the residual must obey the physical laws, so it corrects real drift without inventing impossible behaviour. Free-running AI on a safety asset is a trap.

• Keep the human in the loop. The twin advises with its uncertainty shown; the person decides. Judgement under real stakes belongs to a human, now better informed.

• Match the tool to the value. A nine-week hybrid twin is for a critical asset where a real decision rides on it. On a cheap part, a basic model is the honest choice.

You came to the pump floor with a model that gave a clean number and hid its own ignorance, and a manager who had been burned by it. You can leave with a twin that joins the physics, corrects itself against the real pump, and says exactly how sure it is - so that when it predicts 93 with the whole band above the limit, a person sheds load at 06:12 and three towns keep their water. A model that reports not just a number, but an honest measure of its own certainty - so a human can act in time, on what the model knows, and what it knows it does not. That is what earns the right to be bet on: not a confident guess, but a true number wearing its uncertainty in the open, handed to a person free to act. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
