My job offer said R12,000 a month - I planned my whole life around it - so why did only R9,800 land in my account?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tumiso, and you got the job. Your first proper, grown-up job - and the offer letter said it plainly: R12,000 a month. That night you could barely sleep for the planning. R12,000. You could finally move into your own small place, send R1,000 home every month to help your family the way they always helped you, and still keep a little for yourself. For the first time, the future felt mapped out and bright. You signed the lease. You told your mother the good news, and what you would send.

Then the first payday comes. Your phone pings with the deposit notification and your heart lifts - this is the moment. You open the banking app, and there it is: R9,800.

Not R12,000. R9,800. You stare at it, and the warmth drains out of you. Two thousand, two hundred rand - gone - before you touched a single cent of it. That is the rent money. That is part of what you promised your mother. Your mind races: is it a mistake? Did the company cheat you? Did they lie in the offer letter? And underneath the confusion is a hot, quiet shame - that you planned your whole new life around a number that was never really yours.

The offer said R12,000, and I built my whole life around it - the lease, the money I promised my family. But only R9,800 landed in my account. Where did the other R2,200 of MY money go - did they make a mistake, or did they lie to me?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

In the panic, three reactions tempt you - and each one misreads the payslip. "They cheated me - I'll march in and demand my full R12,000." But almost always there is no cheat: the R12,000 was the figure BEFORE the standard, legal deductions every salary has, and demanding 'all of it' only shows you do not yet understand how pay works. "Fine - so all these deductions are just my money being stolen." But that is not true either: some of it does go to the state, but a big part of it - your pension, your medical aid - is still YOURS, working for your future and your health. "This is too confusing - I'll just ignore the payslip and make whatever lands work." But not understanding your take-home is exactly how you overcommit, come up short every single month, and never spot a real error or a cent you have overpaid. The truth is gentler and far more useful than any of these.

So before you read on: the R12,000 was the figure BEFORE things came off; the R9,800 is what was left AFTER. What kinds of things get taken off a salary - and is all of it really 'gone'?

The R12,000 on your offer letter is the GROSS - the full figure before anything is taken off. The R9,800 that landed is the NET, your take-home. In between sits a stack of DEDUCTIONS, pulled off the top before you ever see the money. So the first question is not 'who stole my R2,200?' but 'what are the things that get taken off a salary, and where does each one actually go?'
Look at what makes up that R2,200. Some of it genuinely leaves your world - tax (PAYE) that runs the country, and UIF (a small insurance that pays YOU if you ever lose your job). But a big part of it does NOT leave at all - your pension contribution is you paying your own future self, and your medical aid is buying your own health cover. So how much of the R2,200 is really 'gone', and how much is just you, quietly looking after you?
Your GROSS is the headline; your NET (take-home) is your real money - always plan your life around the net, never the offer figure. Read your payslip line by line and know each deduction: PAYE (tax) and UIF leave your world (about R845 here); but your pension (about R900) and medical aid (about R455) - more than half of the deductions - are still YOURS, working for future-you. Nothing was stolen. Understanding your payslip is the first real act of taking charge of your money - and it sets up the next question: am I paying only the tax I must? (That's where claiming your rebates comes in.)

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Nobody cheated you. The payslip was never a robbery - it was a map you had simply never been taught to read. Five moves:

  • Gross is the headline - NET is your real money: The figure on your offer letter (R12,000) is your GROSS salary - the full amount before anything comes off. The money that actually lands in your account (R9,800) is your NET, or take-home pay. They are almost never the same, and no one warns you. The first act of taking control is simple: always find your NET, and treat that - not the gross headline - as your real money.
  • Read your payslip line by line: Between gross and net sits a list of DEDUCTIONS, and your payslip is the map showing exactly where each rand went. Do not look away from it - learn to read the main lines: PAYE (income tax), UIF (unemployment insurance), your pension or provident fund, and your medical aid. A person who reads their payslip is never mystified by their own money again.
  • Two kinds of deduction: money that leaves, and money that just MOVES: Here is the reframe that changes everything. Some deductions genuinely LEAVE your world: PAYE (tax, which runs the country - schools, clinics, roads) and UIF (a small insurance that pays YOU if you ever lose your job). But others do NOT leave at all - they are still YOURS, just moved: your pension contribution is you paying your own future self, and your medical aid is you buying your own health protection. A big part of what looked 'taken' is actually you, taking care of you.
  • Plan your whole life around your NET: This is the practical heart of it: your NET is the only number you should ever build your life on - your rent, your budget, what you can promise to family, what you save. Tumiso's pain came from planning around R12,000 when only R9,800 was ever going to land. Sign the lease, make the promises, set the budget against your take-home, and the shock never happens to you.
  • Name it - and ask the next smart question: This is gross-versus-net, and the shock you felt was normal - no one is taught this, it is not printed on the offer letter, and learning it is the first real act of owning your money. And once you can read your payslip and see exactly what tax you pay, a powerful next question opens up: am I paying only the tax I legally have to - or am I leaving money on the table I could claim back? (That is the very next thing worth learning.)

The whole thing lives in one line: the salary you are offered (gross) is not the money that lands (net) - and once you read your payslip, you see that much of the gap is not money lost at all, but you quietly paying your own future self. The shock you felt was not foolishness; it was simply that no one ever sat you down and showed you a payslip. Now you have seen one. You know to plan your life around your take-home, never the headline. You know that tax and UIF leave your world for real reasons, and that your pension and medical aid - more than half of what came off - are still yours, building your future and guarding your health. Nothing was stolen. And the person who understands their own payslip is the person who can never again be mystified, or quietly short-changed, by their own money.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own life, read Tumiso's payslip the way you would read a map - line by line.

See exactly where the R2,200 went

Start at the top, with the headline figure, and work down:

Gross salary: R12,000. Now the deductions come off:
- PAYE (income tax): - R725
- UIF (unemployment insurance): - R120
- Pension fund contribution: - R900
- Medical aid contribution: - R455

Add the deductions up: R725 + R120 + R900 + R455 = R2,200. And R12,000 - R2,200 = R9,800 - your net, take-home pay. So the R9,800 was exactly right all along.

Now the reframe that changes how it feels. Split those R2,200 into two groups:

Money that leaves your world: PAYE R725 (tax, which runs the country) + UIF R120 (insurance that pays YOU if you lose your job) = R845.
Money that is still YOURS, just moved: Pension R900 (you paying your future self) + Medical aid R455 (your own health protection) = R1,355.

Look at that: of the R2,200 that gave you such a fright, more than half - R1,355 - never left you at all. It is you, taking care of future-you. Only R845 truly went out into the world. The payslip was not a theft; it was mostly you, quietly looking after yourself. (Figures are illustrative - everyone's tax and deductions differ, and some jobs have no pension or medical - but the shape is the same for all.)

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You may not have a payslip yet - but the day you do, this is how you stay in charge of it:

  1. Always find your NET first. When an offer or a payslip lands, do not look at the big headline figure - look for the take-home, the money that actually reaches your account. That is your real number. Build everything - rent, budget, what you promise your family - on the net, never the gross.
  2. Read every line, and know what it is. Treat your payslip like a map, not a mystery. Find PAYE, UIF, pension, medical aid - and know, for each, whether it leaves your world (tax, UIF) or stays yours and works for you (pension, medical). A payslip you understand can never short-change you in the dark.
  3. See the deductions clearly - then ask the next question. Notice how much of your 'lost' money is really future-you being cared for. And once you can see exactly what tax you pay, hold this thought for what comes next: am I paying only the tax I legally must - or could I claim some of it back?

You stop being surprised by your own pay, you plan your life on what truly lands, and you see the quiet truth that a good part of what 'comes off' is you, looking after the older you.
