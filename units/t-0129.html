The question I could not answer

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and someone asks you a perfectly fair question. The works wants to run the heated process tank on a harder duty this winter. Will it hold temperature through a cold night, or will the supply fall over at four in the morning?

You go to the twin. You are good with the twin now - you keep it honest, you know exactly how far to trust it, you can tell anyone when it was last checked against the real plant. And it cannot answer this, because it was built for the configuration it was sold for, and this duty is not in it.

So you sit with the question, and you realise something uncomfortable about yourself. You have been a careful custodian of models other people built. You have never once built the smallest one yourself. And that means every question that falls outside somebody else's original scope is a question you simply cannot answer.

Which leaves two options, and you dislike both. Guess - on a town's water supply, in winter. Or run the real plant on the new duty and find out what happens, which is the same thing as guessing except that it is more expensive and the consequences are real.

I can maintain a model and judge how far to trust it, but I cannot MAKE one - so any question outside its original scope is one I have to guess at. How do I build a model of my own that is simple enough to defend and good enough to believe?

Before you read on, decide. You know how to use models. You maintain one, you keep it honest, you can say how far to trust it. Now you are asked what happens if the tank runs at the new duty — and no model you have covers that question. Which leaves two options and you dislike both: guess, on a town's water supply, in winter; or run the real plant on the new duty and find out, which is guessing except more expensive and with real consequences. There are instruments on that tank and years of readings. Write down how you would answer the question — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and how small a defensible model actually is

• “Get a specialist or a consultant in.” Often right, and notice what it costs beyond money: weeks, and a permanent arrangement in which every question outside an existing model is somebody else's to answer. It also does not help you judge the answer when it arrives.

• “Buy proper simulation software.” A tool is not a model. Software will happily let you build something elaborate and wrong, and the thing you are missing is not machinery — as the next paragraph shows, it is four moves.

• “Use experience — I know this plant.” Genuinely valuable inside the range you have seen, and this duty is outside it. Experience extrapolated is a guess wearing a uniform.

• “Try it at low load first and scale up.” Sensible risk management, and it is still the real plant and still the town's water. It also tells you about low load, and the question was about the new duty.

• “Find a similar plant that has done it.” Worth an afternoon, and you will spend it establishing whether “similar” is similar in the ways that matter, which requires roughly the understanding you would need to model it.

• Here is the thing nobody tells you: a model of one specific question is far smaller than you expect. Draw a boundary around the tank and write what crosses it. Then apply the one law that never fails — what is stored changes at the rate things enter minus the rate they leave — written for the quantity you care about. That single line is the model; everything after it is honesty about it. Then the decision that keeps it small: may you treat the thing as uniform, one value throughout rather than varying through space? That is defensible for a stirred tank and plainly wrong for a long pipe, and stating which is part of the model, not an admission. Connect components by the rule that holds in every domain — what is shared is equal, what flows sums to zero — so heat, fluid and electrical all assemble the same way. And when one thing in it is genuinely unknown, one good measurement pins it. Then use a second, different measurement to test it — because a model tuned on a measurement and checked against that same measurement has proved nothing at all.

It is far less mysterious than it looks. Let us show you a finished one - a whole model, on one page - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is a complete, defensible model of that tank - derived, built, calibrated and proved. It is smaller than you expect.

1. The boundary and the balance - draw the box, write what crosses it

Draw the box around the heated process tank. Then write conservation of energy: what is stored changes at the rate energy enters minus the rate it leaves. m x c x dT/dt = Q_in - UA x (T - T_ambient). That single line is the model - everything after it is honesty about the line.

2. The lump - may I treat this as uniform? (state it)

Assume ONE temperature for the whole tank. That is the lumped-parameter decision: properties vary in time only, not through space - which is what turns partial differential equations into a single ordinary one. Reasonable here because the tank is stirred and well mixed. It would NOT be reasonable for a long pipe with a real temperature gradient along it, and saying so is part of the model.

3. The connections - efforts equal, flows sum to zero

Join heater, tank and ambient at ports. At any connection the rule is the same in every domain: the EFFORT variables are equal and the FLOW variables sum to zero. Voltage and current, pressure and mass flow, temperature and heat rate. Write the equations and let the tool work out the solution order - that is acausal, equation-based modelling.

4. Calibrate - pin the one thing you do not know

Everything is known except the heat-loss coefficient UA. Dataset 1: heater 8,000 W, ambient 20 C, measured steady state 60 C. At steady state Q_in = UA x (T_ss - T_amb), so UA = 8000 / (60 - 20) = 200 W/K. One unknown, pinned by one measurement - not four unknowns tuned until the curve looks nice.

5. Validate - predict something it has never seen, then state the envelope

Dataset 2, never used in calibration: heater 6,000 W, ambient 15 C. The model PREDICTS 15 + 6000/200 = 45 C. Measured: 46 C - an error of 1 C, about 2%. THAT is the model earning belief. And the honest envelope: validated between roughly 6 and 8 kW with ambient 15 to 20 C. Outside that range it is an extrapolation, not a prediction.

Notice what is actually in there. One conservation law. One stated assumption. One rule for joining things together. One unknown, pinned by one measurement. And then the part that turns a drawing into evidence: a prediction of a case the model had never seen, checked against reality, with the error written down and the range of conditions it may honestly be used within. That last box is what separates a model from an opinion. Now let us walk the logic.

T3: The Blueprint

Building a physical model is not magic and it is not a specialist art. It is four ordinary moves, each answering a question:

  • Where is my boundary, and what crosses it? Draw the box. Then write conservation: the rate of change of what is stored inside equals what comes in minus what goes out. For the tank that is energy, and it gives you m x c x dT/dt = Q_in - UA x (T - T_ambient) in a single line. Almost every model you will build in your career starts exactly here - a boundary and a balance.
  • May I treat this as uniform? If you say the whole tank is at one temperature, you have made the lumped-parameter decision: properties vary in time but not through space. That is precisely what reduces a partial differential equation to an ordinary one you can actually solve. It is a decision, not a fact - honest for a well-mixed tank, dishonest for a long pipe with a real gradient down its length - and it must be written down either way.
  • How do the pieces join? Here is the idea worth carrying out of this unit. Look at three junctions side by side. At an electrical node the voltages are equal and the currents sum to zero. At a pipe junction the pressures are equal and the mass flows sum to zero. At a thermal interface the temperatures are equal and the heat flows sum to zero. That is not three rules that happen to rhyme - it is one rule. Physical variables come in pairs: an effort (voltage, pressure, temperature, force) and a flow (current, mass flow, heat rate, velocity). At any connection, efforts are equal and flows sum to zero. That single sentence is the engine of multi-domain - "multi-physics" - modelling: not many theories, one pattern. It is also why you can write the equations and the connections and let the software work out the solution order, instead of writing a solver yourself. That approach is called acausal or equation-based modelling, and it is what languages like Modelica exist to do.
  • What do I not know, and what would pin it down? Usually one or two parameters are genuinely unknown - here, the heat-loss coefficient. Calibration uses a dataset to fix them. Pin one unknown with one measurement; if you tune four unknowns at once you can make almost anything fit almost anything, which feels like success and proves nothing.

Then the move that makes it engineering rather than curve-drawing: validation. Take a case the model has never seen - different heater power, different ambient - predict it, and compare. Here the model predicted 45 degrees and the plant did 46: an error of about 2%. Say it plainly - never validate on the data you calibrated with. A model tuned to match history and then praised for matching history has told you nothing at all. And record the validated envelope: this model earned belief between about 6 and 8 kW with ambient 15 to 20 degrees. Beyond that it is extrapolating, and it should say so.

One honest boundary on this unit, so you know exactly what you now hold. You are learning to build and defend a small lumped-parameter model from conservation laws. You are not being made into a simulation specialist - finite element analysis and computational fluid dynamics are different disciplines with their own training. What you gain is the ability to turn a physical system into a defensible mathematical claim, and to prove that claim has predictive value. That is enough to answer most of the questions a working asset manager is actually asked, and enough to know when you need someone else.

Does it scale? A weather forecast is trusted because it has been tested against days it never saw when it was built; your model earns belief the same way and only that way - on plant data it did not see during calibration.

T4: Your Turn

Now write one. Choose something ordinary you can observe: a kettle cooling on a counter, a room warming through the morning, a phone battery discharging, a car engine warming up.

  1. Draw the boundary and write the balance. What is stored, what comes in, what goes out? One line.
  2. State the lump assumption - and say honestly where it would break down. ("One temperature for the whole kettle" is fine; "one temperature for the whole house" is probably not.)
  3. Name the unknown. Which single parameter do you not know? (It is very often a loss coefficient.)
  4. Design the calibration. What one measurement would pin it down?
  5. Design the validation. What different measurement, under conditions you did not calibrate on, would test whether it predicts? And what would you accept as a good enough error?

The test of a real answer: steps 4 and 5 must be different measurements. If your validation is the same experiment you calibrated on, you have not validated anything - you have simply admired your own arithmetic.

T5: The Guardrails

Model building goes wrong in a small number of very predictable ways. Glance through these before you trust a number your own model produced.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Validating on the calibration data. The most common error and the most flattering, because the fit is always excellent. It proves only that your arithmetic works. Hold back data the model never sees, always.

• Lumping something that is not uniform. A long pipe, a thick wall, a large unstirred tank - each has a real internal gradient, and pretending otherwise buries an error you will never find later. If you must lump it, say so and say what it costs you.

• Adding detail instead of adding evidence. A more elaborate model is not a more trustworthy one. Complexity feels like rigour and is not; only validation against unseen data earns trust.

• Tuning several unknowns at once. With enough free parameters you can fit anything, including nonsense. Pin one unknown with one measurement, and be suspicious of any model with more knobs than data.

• Forgetting the validated envelope. A model checked between 6 and 8 kW says nothing certain about 20 kW. Using it far outside the conditions ever tested is not modelling; it is hoping in equations.

• Believing a history match is a prediction. Matching what already happened is the easy half. The model only becomes useful at the moment it correctly tells you something you did not already know.

Onalenna could look after a model beautifully and still could not answer a straight question about her own plant, because she had never built one. Now you can: draw the boundary, write the balance, state honestly what you have lumped, join the pieces with the one rule that holds in every domain, pin your unknown with data, and then prove the thing predicts something it has never seen - and say over what range it may be believed. It is a small model. It is also a defensible one, and a small defensible model beats an elaborate unproven one every single time.
