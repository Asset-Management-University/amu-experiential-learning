I have eleven competing digital projects, a fixed R6m budget, and a CEO who wants them to add up to something - not eleven half-built things. How do I sequence them so each step makes the next possible, and draw it on one page I can defend?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning in the control room at Stellaford Water, the regional utility that pipes drinking water to nine towns and about 240,000 people. You are the asset manager. In your hand is a single sheet: a list of eleven digital projects, each pushed by a different manager, each marked "urgent".

The operations head wants sensors on every pump. The IT head wants to replace the old maintenance software. A young engineer has built a clever tool that predicts pipe bursts and wants money to grow it. A consultant has sent a glossy quote for an "AI water-loss platform" - R4.2 million over three years. Finance has given you one number for all of it this year: R6 million. Across the desk, the Chief Executive taps the sheet. "Pick what we do first," she says. "But I do not want eleven half-built things in two years. I want them to add up to something."

The easy move is loud in your head: fund the three cheapest and the burst tool because the engineer is keen and it sounds exciting. Quick wins; everyone goes home happy this week. But you have seen what happens next. Last time the utility bought clever tools with no order, and three now sit unused because the data they needed was never cleaned, and nobody can say what any of it was for. "Eleven half-built things" is exactly the answer you are afraid of giving in two years.

I have eleven competing digital projects, a fixed budget, and a CEO who wants them to add up to something. How do I put them in the right order - tied to real asset value, each step making the next possible - and draw it on one page I can defend?

Before you read on, decide. Eleven competing digital projects, R6 million for all of it, and a Chief Executive who has said the quiet part plainly: not eleven half-built things in two years — they must add up to something. Last time the utility bought clever tools with no order, and three sit unused because the data they needed was never cleaned. Write down how you would order them. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and where each one leaves you in two years

• “Fund the three cheapest and the exciting one.” Loud in your head, and everybody goes home happy this week. It is also precisely what produced the three tools sitting unused, bought because somebody was keen rather than because anything was ready for them.

• “Rank them by return on investment.” Disciplined, and it treats eleven projects as though each could stand alone. Some of these cannot be done at all until another is finished — and a project whose return is real but unreachable this year will score well and starve.

• “Start with the biggest platform — everything else plugs into it.” The most seductive answer in the room, and the one your own history has already refuted: the grandest tool starves fastest, because on day one it needs data nobody has cleaned.

• “Ask each sponsor to make their case and pick the strongest.” You will get eleven good cases, all urgent, all sincere. Advocacy measures how well somebody argues, not whether the utility can yet do the thing they are asking for.

• “Do them all a bit — give everyone something.” The fairest-feeling answer and it is her nightmare, written out: eleven half-built things in two years, and nobody able to say what any of it was for.

Her instruction is a test with two parts, and most orderings pass only one: pick what we do first, and make them add up to something. An order in which each step makes the next possible is the only kind that does both.

You do not need a guess. You need a reliable, repeatable way to put these initiatives in the right order - one that ties every project to real asset value and to the things the utility must be able to do first. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

You are building an enterprise digital asset-management roadmap - a staged plan that puts your data, digital and AI projects in an order that builds real value for your physical assets, one step making the next possible. The trick is that each project must earn its place by two tests, not one: its value (how much it cuts risk or cost for the assets that matter), and its capability (whether you can actually do it yet, or it depends on something you have not built). Watch all six moves run on Stellaford Water.

Step 1 - Anchor to asset value (a number, not an adjective)

Stellaford Water's groups: 480 km of bulk pipelines (bursts cost about R180,000 each, and about 30 a year is R5.4m/yr - the biggest money loss); 22 pump stations (an unplanned failure costs about R250,000 and risks a town running dry - the biggest service risk); 14 reservoirs; 3 treatment works. Value lives in the pipes and the pumps - hold that, because every project will have to point back to it.

Step 2 - List the initiatives, and score maturity honestly (1-5)

Eleven projects; the main six: A clean and merge the asset register (R0.6m), B sensors on the 22 pumps (R1.4m), C a new maintenance system / CMMS (R1.1m), D a burst-prediction AI (R0.5m), E an AI water-loss platform (R4.2m), F a control-room dashboard (R0.4m). Maturity today: a trustworthy asset register = 2 (three spreadsheets disagree); live pump data = 1 (manual, weekly); maintenance history = 2; data skills for AI = 2. Every advanced idea needs capabilities sitting at 1 or 2 - the warning.

Step 3 - Map the dependencies (data before AI)

A (clean register) depends on nothing - it is the floor. B mildly needs A (to know what to put sensors on); C needs A (to load into it); D needs A (clean records it can trust); F needs B (live data to show); E needs A AND B AND a year of collected data, so it sits at the very top of the ladder. A feeds almost everything; E needs the most below it.

Step 4 - Sequence into horizons (Now / Next / Later)

Within the R6m year-one budget: NOW (R2.4m) - A clean register (R0.6m, unlocks everything), B pump sensors (R1.4m, protects the high-risk pumps and starts collecting E's data), F dashboard (R0.4m, a cheap fast early win). NEXT (R1.6m) - C the new CMMS (R1.1m) and D burst prediction (R0.5m, now targeting the R5.4m/yr loss). LATER (R4.2m) - E, only now that the sensors have a year of data and the register is solid. You did NOT fund E first, though it shouted loudest; it would have failed on day one for lack of data.

Step 5 - Draw it on one page, name the value at each horizon

Three columns - NOW, NEXT, LATER - each with its spend, its projects, and one plain value line. NOW: 'we can SEE and TRUST our assets'. NEXT: 'we can PLAN and PREVENT failures'. LATER: 'we can PREDICT water loss at scale'. Every project sits after the thing it needs, and every box is defensible - so the Chief Executive gets her answer: the pieces add up.

See what the roadmap did. It took eleven loud, competing "urgent" projects and turned them into one ordered page where each step makes the next possible - and it refused to fund the biggest, shiniest AI platform first, because it would have starved on day one for lack of data. This is the mission under the strategy: scarce public money, raised from the people who drink this water, must build something that actually serves them - not a vendor's sales target, not a board's appetite for the word "AI", not the keenest engineer's excitement. The roadmap forces three honesties: it ties every project to a real number on a real asset; it admits, through the maturity score, how weak the starting line truly is; and it puts the unglamorous floor - clean data, one trustworthy register - before the glamorous roof. Foundations before flash; data before AI; the floor before the roof. Money is the measure of the value each step delivers; the budget is the constraint, not the goal. And the one-page picture is what lets you defend the order to anyone, and keeps the plan from scattering back into eleven half-built things.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the roadmap in five moves (folding the classic six steps - the worked example shows each).

  • 1. Anchor to asset value. The asset groups that matter and the value or risk each carries - a number, not an adjective.
  • 2. List the initiatives, and score maturity. Every candidate project, plus an honest 1-to-5 maturity of the capabilities they need.
  • 3. Map the dependencies. For each, what must exist first? Draw the arrows; data before AI.
  • 4. Sequence into horizons. Now / Next / Later, so nothing sits before what it depends on.
  • 5. Draw it on one page. A picture a non-engineer can read, with the value named at each horizon.

The ideas underneath. Keep three cousins separate on purpose: data (the records and readings you hold), digital tools (the software and sensors that use it), and AI (tools that learn patterns to predict or decide) - people mash them together, and keeping them apart is half the skill. A capability is a thing the organisation is able to do ("we can trust our pipe records"); projects are how you build each one. Maturity rates how good you are today, 1 (just starting) to 5 (excellent), so you are honest about the starting line. Foundational work (a clean asset register) is the floor; advanced work (AI prediction) is the upper floor - you cannot build the roof before the walls. A horizon is a named time-band (Now 0-12 months, Next 12-24, Later 24-36) - bands, not exact dates, because plans drift. And the asset register - the master list of what you own, where, and its condition - is the one foundation almost everything reads from. This fits when you have several competing digital ideas, limited budget and people, and assets whose value and risk differ; it is overkill for a single clearly-justified project, and the wrong tool when the real problem is a basic failure (no register, no budget) - fix the floor first, you cannot sequence on sand.

Two disciplines keep the roadmap honest. First, score your maturity before you believe any AI promise - the most expensive mistake in this whole field is buying an advanced tool while the capability it needs sits at 1 or 2; if your data maturity is low, the AI does not work, full stop, and the honest roadmap builds the data first however unglamorous that feels. Second, make every project point back to a number on an asset - "it's innovative", "everyone's doing AI", "the engineer is keen" are not reasons; the reason is "it cuts the R5.4m/yr burst loss" or "it protects the pump whose failure runs a town dry", and a project that cannot name its asset-value number does not belong on the page yet.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your roadmap true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Data before AI. The floor before the roof. An AI that predicts failure needs a clean record of past failures. Build the foundational data and register first, or the clever tools starve.

• Value AND capability - two tests, not one. A high-value project you cannot yet deliver is not ready. Ask both "how much is it worth?" and "can we actually do it?".

• Score maturity honestly. Three spreadsheets that disagree is a maturity of 2, not 4. The roadmap is only as good as the honesty of its starting line.

• Do not fund the loudest first. The most expensive, most exciting tool usually sits highest on the ladder and needs the most below it. Sequence by dependency, not by volume.

• One page, or it scatters. If a non-engineer cannot read the order and the value at each horizon, you cannot defend it - and it drifts back into eleven half-built things.

You came into the control room with eleven urgent projects, a fixed budget, and a CEO who wanted them to add up. You can leave with one page: the floor built first (a clean register, sensors, a dashboard), then the tools that stand on it (a new maintenance system, burst prediction), then the advanced layer last (the AI platform, bought only when it can actually work) - each box tied to a real asset value, each sitting after the thing it needs. The pieces add up: data before AI, the floor before the roof - one page you can defend. That is how scarce public money builds something real for the 240,000 people downstream: not eleven shiny fragments, but one staircase, climbed in order. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
