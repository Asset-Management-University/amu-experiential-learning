The cheapest bakkie could be the most expensive

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, 21. Tank You is registered, the orders are steady - and you cannot keep borrowing your parents' bakkie forever. You need your own. So you go looking, and it comes down to two.

One is a cheap bakkie a man down the road is selling - high mileage, a bit rough, history unknown, but only R25,000. The other is your parents' own bakkie - you know every scratch on it, it has been looked after for years - and they will sell it to you fairly at R40,000.

The arithmetic screams at you. R25,000! That saves us fifteen thousand rand - money we do not have to spare. One part of your mind has already decided: take the cheap one, keep the buffer. It is the responsible-sounding choice.

But there is a nag you cannot silence. This bakkie is not just transport - it IS the business. Every delivery rides on it. And a cold thought keeps surfacing: if that cheap one breaks down, I do not have a broken bakkie. I have no business that day. And the day after. The buffer you saved could vanish in one tow and one big repair.

So you are stuck between two kinds of sense - the cash in your hand, and a warning in your gut:

The cheap one saves R15,000 today - so why does taking it feel like a mistake? What am I not seeing?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You lay it out for Ibraaheem. "Twenty-five versus forty. The cheap one wins, surely?" He does not answer fast. He just says, "That's what it costs to BUY. What does it cost to OWN?"

The question stops you. You have only been comparing one number - the price on the windscreen. But you will pay for this bakkie long after the day you buy it.

Before you read on, try to see what the price tag hides. If you wanted to compare these two HONESTLY - not on what you pay once, but on what each truly costs you - what would you have to add up for each one?

I keep comparing the two on the one number on the windscreen: the price. R25,000 versus R40,000. But that nag won't leave - the price is what I pay ONCE. What about everything I'll pay after?
If the cheap one breaks down a lot, every breakday is a day with no deliveries - no income, angry customers. The repairs cost money AND the downtime costs money. None of that is on the windscreen.
So the real question isn't 'what does it cost to BUY?' It's 'what will it cost to OWN, over its whole life - price, fuel, repairs, and the days it leaves me stranded?' Compare THAT, and the cheap one might be the dear one.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Ibraaheem's question was the whole lesson: what does it cost to OWN? The price is just the first, loudest number. The real cost of anything is paid across its whole life - and once you count that, the cheapest to buy is very often the most expensive to own. Here is how you compare honestly:

  • The price tag is not the cost: the day-one price is just the first, most visible number. The true cost of a thing is paid over its WHOLE life.
  • Count the whole life - on one list: for each option: price + fuel/running + repairs + what it costs you when it is DOWN + what is left when you sell it. Like compared with like.
  • The heaviest cost is being DOWN: for anything that earns for you, a day it cannot work is a day you do not earn. This 'Cost of Unreliability' usually dwarfs the repair bill itself.
  • And ask: what if it fails BADLY?: a breakdown costs a day; a disaster - stranded mid-route with a full load - can cost the whole business. Weigh the consequence, not just the chance.
  • Buy RIGHT, not cheapest: the winner is the lowest WHOLE-LIFE cost - written down, so the choice is one you can defend. Very often the dearer thing is far cheaper to own.

The piece almost everyone misses is the heaviest one: the cost of being down. Your bakkie's whole job is to earn. A day it cannot drive is a day Tank You earns nothing - lost deliveries, customers who go elsewhere. For a thing that earns its keep, unreliability is not an inconvenience; it is the biggest cost of all. (This is the same sum a factory does for a R2-million pump or a city for a power station - only the size of the number changes. You are learning to think like an asset manager.)

And note what the calculation is really for: not to predict the future to the rand - these are honest estimates, not prophecies - but to see the true SHAPE of the choice, and to leave you with a decision written down that you can defend. Your gut already suspected the cheap one was a trap. The sum is how you prove it.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So you and Ibraaheem stop arguing about the price and add up the OWNING - two years of it, on the back of a page.

See the real cost of each bakkie, over two years

(Fuel and insurance are about the same for both, so leave them out - they do not change the DIFFERENCE between the two. Rough, honest estimates - not prophecies.)

The cheap bakkie - R25,000
Price: R25,000; Repairs over 2 years (rough, high-mileage, unknown history): ~R28,000; Lost income from breakdown days: ~R18,000; True 2-year cost: about R71,000.

The parents' bakkie - R40,000
Price: R40,000; Repairs over 2 years (known, well-maintained): ~R6,000; Lost income from breakdown days: ~R2,000; True 2-year cost: about R48,000.

The inversion: the bakkie that was R15,000 CHEAPER to buy is about R23,000 more expensive to OWN. The "bargain" was the trap your gut warned you about.

And the consequence: a breakdown costs a delivery day. But the cheap one, stranded on the highway with a full load of cylinders, is a different order of disaster - a risk to the cargo, the safety, and the name of Tank You itself. The dearer, known bakkie is not a luxury. It is the lifeline that keeps the whole business standing.

That is buying RIGHT: not the lowest price, but the lowest whole-life cost - and the choice you can defend, to your brother and to yourself.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You almost lost R23,000 to a number on a windscreen. The move you found - count the whole life, not the sticker - is a foundational move in asset management, and it is yours now, for every big thing you will ever buy.

For your own next big purchase - a phone, a fridge for a stall, a tool, a vehicle:

  1. Look past the price. Write the sticker price - then keep writing: running costs, likely repairs, and what it costs you if it fails when you need it.
  2. Weigh the cost of it being down. If the thing earns for you or you depend on it, a day without it has a real price. Count it - it is usually the biggest number of all.
  3. Choose the lowest WHOLE-LIFE cost - and write down why. Often that is not the cheapest one. A decision you can defend beats a bargain you will regret.
R

Do the whole-life sum on your next real purchase, and say your reasoning out loud to someone you trust. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

This one habit - asking "what will it cost to OWN?" before "what does it cost to BUY?" - is the difference between a business that quietly bleeds and one built to last. It is true for a township bakkie, and it is just as true for a mine's machine or a nation's power station. You are not just buying smarter. You are learning to manage assets.
