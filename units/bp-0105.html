Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tiisetso, the asset engineer for the bulk-water utility. It is a cold Tuesday morning and you are standing on a 140-metre concrete road bridge that also carries the main water pipeline into the town. Below you, 18 metres down, the dry riverbed waits for the rains that come maybe twice a year - and when they come, they come hard and fast. In your hand is the inspection report from your contractor, Mduduzi. It is two pages. He visited yesterday, the way he does once every two years - that is what the budget pays for. He looked at what he could see from the deck and from a cherry-picker that can reach the two end spans. His note is short: "Visible surfaces sound. Some staining under span 3. No access to mid-span soffit or the internal pier - recommend close inspection when funds allow."

You run your thumb over that last line. When funds allow. A full close-up inspection, with rope-access teams and a scaffold tower in the riverbed, would cost about R420,000 and take three weeks. You have R0 allocated for it this year. Here is what gnaws at you. The part of the bridge you most worry about - the mid-span underside and the inside of the central pier, where the water pipeline is strapped - is exactly the part nobody has looked at properly in six years. It is shaded, it stays damp, salts from years ago are still in the concrete. You have read enough reports to know that this is precisely where steel reinforcement quietly rusts, swells, and cracks the concrete from the inside. By the time a crack shows on a surface you CAN see, the damage underneath has often been growing for years.

You think of a footbridge that came down a few years ago, with no real warning the day before. You think of the school bus that crosses this bridge twice a day. You smell the faint sourness of damp concrete and old rust as the wind comes up off the riverbed. The cruel part is the timing. A structure like this does not fail politely on inspection day. It deteriorates CONTINUOUSLY - every wet night, every heavy truck - but you only ever LOOK at it once every two years, and even then only where you can reach. So damage advances in the dark, in the places you cannot get to, and the first real sign might be the failure itself.

Mduduzi's report is asking you to sign it off and file it. Your manager wants the form signed today so the bridge stays "inspected" on the asset register. The easy move - the tempting one - is to sign, file it, and trust that two-yearly looks have always been good enough before, so they will be good enough again. But you keep staring at that pier you cannot see.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is how you and Mduduzi actually resolved it. Five moves:

  • Name the real problem - the inaccessible gap between inspections: you started by naming the real problem correctly. It was never 'the inspection was too cheap.' The problem was that your knowledge of the bridge was SNAPSHOTS - taken rarely, only where a person could stand. And it was not just a gap in TIME (two years between visits) but a gap in SPACE: the parts you most feared - the mid-span underside and the inside of the central pier - are physically unreachable by routine methods, so degradation hides there, advancing in the dark every wet night and under every heavy truck, while the first real sign might be the failure itself. Two photographs, six years apart, of the OUTSIDE of a building tell you almost nothing about a fire smouldering inside it between them. Even a fresh R420,000 inspection tomorrow would still be one look, on one day, only where a person could stand - blind again the very next morning. More of the same kind of looking can never close a gap that is both rare and unreachable. That is the thing to fix: not the price of the snapshot, but the reliance on snapshots at all.
  • Separate 'going' from 'knowing' for an inaccessible asset: so you separated two things that had always been tangled together: GOING to a place, and GETTING INFORMATION from a place. A person has to go. Information does not. You can leave something behind that watches the spot for you and reports back - every hour, every day - even on the days no one is there, and even in the places a person cannot physically reach. That reframing is the whole answer. This is condition monitoring through PERMANENTLY-INSTALLED sensors - what the industry calls structural health monitoring. Instead of periodic, access-limited inspection, you fit instruments that stay on the structure and measure its behaviour continuously, especially at the spots you cannot otherwise reach; the structure tells you how it is doing, instead of you guessing between rare visits. (Listening for the leading signal of an accessible but surprise-prone rotating machine - a bearing that is a black box you CAN reach - is the sibling discipline of bp-0096; here the barrier is not surprise but physical access, and the cure is a permanent watch on a place a person simply cannot go.)
  • Instrument the blind spots - deploy a targeted sensor suite: working with Mduduzi, you chose what to 'listen' for at the three blind spots - the mid-span soffit, the inner central pier, and the pipeline straps - matching each sensor to a specific, slow failure mode of a concrete structure rather than one generic health metric. CRACK and STRAIN gauges on the mid-span underside and the pier, to catch a crack widening - a few hundredths of a millimetre of movement the eye would never see on a two-yearly visit. TILT and DISPLACEMENT sensors on the pier, so any slow lean or settlement shows up as a trend on a graph, not as a surprise. An embedded MOISTURE sensor and a simple CORROSION sensor near the reinforcement, because rusting steel changes the electrical behaviour of the concrete around it long before it cracks the surface. And a small VIBRATION sensor to log how the bridge flexes under each heavy truck - as damage grows, a structure's 'ring' changes, getting slightly softer, the way a cracked bell sounds different. All of it feeds a cheap solar-powered logger that sends a daily reading to your phone over the cell network and raises an alarm the moment any value crosses a threshold you set with Mduduzi. A suite, because a single sensor in the wrong spot tells you nothing about the failure mode it cannot feel.
  • Make the business case - a continuous watch versus one snapshot: then you did the honest sum, because more certainty always costs something. One deep inspection is R420,000 - and it buys you ONE look on ONE day, blind again the very next morning. The continuous watch is about R180,000 up front: as a share of that single deep look, 180,000 divided by 420,000 is 0.43, or 43%. Add the running cost of about R15,000 a year, and over five years that is 5 times 15,000, or R75,000, so the five-year total is 180,000 plus 75,000 equals R255,000 - still less than one single R420,000 snapshot. So for 43% of the cost of a single deep look up front, you bought a continuous watch on exactly the three places that frightened you, and even after five years of running costs you had spent R165,000 LESS than one R420,000 inspection, while being watched every day instead of once every two years. The watch does not replace the deep look forever; it tells you WHEN one is worth its R420,000 - and where to point it - so the rare expensive visit lands where it matters instead of being a routine snapshot of the parts you could already see.
  • Set a success line - let the structure speak, not your fear: the day the logger went live you wrote down, in advance, what would tell you the watch was working: 'If our reasoning is right, the first real warning will come from the central pier CORROSION sensor - the spot we fear most - climbing slowly, weeks or months ahead of any visible crack.' And what would make you doubt it: 'If a problem grows for a year somewhere the sensors stay flat, the watch is in the wrong places and we have bought false comfort.' Then you simply read the daily graphs the logger was already sending - a few minutes each morning, costing nothing more. And reality refused to follow your script. For the first two months the pier corrosion sensor - the one you were sure would speak first - barely moved. But after the first hard rains it was the TILT sensor on the pier that began to creep: a slow, steady lean, fractions of a degree, climbing more after each storm. The corrosion you had braced for was not the early signal at all; water collecting at the pier base and softening the founding was - SETTLEMENT, not corrosion. Worse, the vibration sensor showed the bridge's 'ring' changing most under the end span Mduduzi had called 'sound' - the one part a person COULD see, the one you had nearly left uninstrumented. The danger was not where your fear had pointed, and only the continuous data could have told you. So you changed course but kept the method: you re-pointed the watch (a drainage check and a second tilt sensor at the pier base, a spare gauge onto the 'sound' span) and scheduled a single targeted rope-access look - a fraction of the full R420,000 - at the pier base the tilt trend had flagged, because the bridge itself had told you where to spend. A real warning had reached you years before a collapse, on a structure no one had properly seen in six years. What changed was not WHETHER to watch but WHAT and WHERE - and you only learned that by letting the readings, not your hunch, be the judge. (You still need Mduduzi's eyes and judgement; the watch tells you where and when to send him.)

See where it lands. You did not sign the report and file it; you changed how the bridge talks to you - a daily watch on the three places you feared, for 43% of one deep look, that caught a real warning years before any collapse and told you where to spend. When something valuable degrades continuously in a place you can only reach now and then, the answer is almost never "inspect more often" or "buy a better one" - those are just more snapshots. Separate GOING from KNOWING: a person must go, but information need not, so leave a cheap, permanent watch behind that reports every day, even where no one can stand, and the structure warns you itself in the gap where the harm used to hide. Then let the readings, not your fear, tell you where the danger actually lives - because the signal you brace for is often not the one that speaks first.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Let us take one more rep together before you walk on your own - the same deep problem, but wearing very different clothes.

See the bakery cold room that fails overnight

Nomvelo runs a small bakery. Her cold room holds about R30,000 of stock - dough, cream fillings, frozen pies. Twice now in the past year the compressor has quietly failed overnight, and she only found out at 5 a.m. when she opened up to warm air and a fridge full of spoiled stock. Her fix so far has been to pay her refrigeration person to service the unit every six months at R1,200 a visit, and she is saving up to replace the whole compressor with a newer model. It is tempting to say: service it more often, or just buy the new compressor. Hold on a moment.

First, what do you notice about WHEN Nomvelo finds out there is a problem? She finds out at 5 a.m., standing in front of warm air - AFTER the harm is already done. Just like the bridge, her knowledge arrives as a snapshot: the six-monthly service is one look on one day, and the compressor can fail any of the 180 nights in between. More frequent services and a shinier compressor are both still the same KIND of looking - a person, going there, occasionally. Neither one watches the cold room on the night it actually fails. Now, where have we seen this exact shape before - something degrading continuously, checked only now and then, failing in the gap? It is the bridge again. The surface looks completely different - a R30,000 fridge instead of a 140-metre bridge, a baker instead of an asset engineer - but the deep structure is identical: a hidden, between-checks gap, and harm arriving before the next visit. So apply the same move. Separate going from knowing. Nomvelo does not need to GO to the cold room every night - she needs the cold room to TELL her every night. And if it could send her one signal before the stock spoiled, what would it be, and what would it cost? The earliest, simplest warning is temperature. A cheap Wi-Fi temperature sensor - well under R1,000 - sits in the cold room and pings her phone the moment the temperature climbs past, say, 5 degrees Celsius. That is the threshold; that is the alarm. Now a 2 a.m. failure wakes her at 2 a.m., not at 5 a.m. when it is too late - and a single saved load of stock (R30,000) dwarfs the sensor's price many times over. The six-monthly service still happens - the sensor does not replace her refrigeration person any more than it replaced Mduduzi - but now it tells her WHEN to call him, before the loss, not after. When something valuable degrades continuously in a place you only check now and then, the answer is not "check more often" or "buy a better one" - it is to separate going from knowing, and leave a cheap continuous signal behind so the thing warns you itself, in the gap where the harm used to hide. A bridge and a bakery fridge are the same problem - and so, very likely, is yours.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The bridge is just one shape of this problem. The real pattern is: something valuable deteriorates continuously, in places you cannot easily reach, but you only check it occasionally - so failure can arrive between checks, unseen. Find that pattern where you are:

  1. Name your "blind, between-visits" asset. What is the thing that quietly degrades where you cannot see it, that you only check now and then? A roof cavity, an underground pipe, a machine's inner bearing, a relationship you only "inspect" at a yearly review, your own health between two-yearly check-ups? Name it concretely - where it is, what it is, who depends on it.
  2. Separate "going" from "knowing". What would it cost to physically inspect the hidden part each time - and is there a way to get a CONTINUOUS signal from it instead, without going there each time? What could you leave behind to "watch" it for you?
  3. Decide what you'd listen for. If your hidden thing could send you one daily message before it failed, what would you most want it to tell you - a temperature, a vibration, a number creeping up, a moisture reading? Pick the one or two signals that would warn you earliest, and the threshold that says "go look now".
  4. Do the honest sum. Roughly, what does a continuous watch cost versus the occasional deep check - and what are you really buying with it? Be honest about what the watch WILL NOT see, too: you buy eyes on chosen blind spots, not the whole asset.
