The wrong valve nobody mentioned

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna. You have been given a cross-functional team for a plant upgrade - a senior technician, a graduate engineer, an experienced planner, a contractor supervisor - and you line-manage none of them.

You lead the way you were once led: firmly, clearly, the same for everyone. It feels like leadership.

Within two weeks the senior technician has gone quiet. The graduate is guessing rather than asking. The rattled planner is hiding. And nobody tells you the delivered valve is wrong for the duty until it is already installed. You are working harder than anyone on the team - and the team is performing worse than any of them would have alone.

I am leading everyone the same way and everyone is getting worse. How do I lead four different people - none of whom I command - so the team performs better than any of us could alone?

Before you read on, decide. Four people, none of whom report to you. A senior technician with twenty years. A graduate three months in. A planner who was blamed for something last year and has not recovered. You have led them firmly, clearly and identically — the same expectations, the same instructions, the same standard for everyone. It feels like leadership. Two weeks on: the technician has gone quiet, the graduate is guessing rather than asking, the planner is hiding. And nobody told you the delivered valve was wrong for the duty until it was already installed. You are working harder than anyone here, and the team is performing worse than any of them would alone. Write down how you would lead them — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why identical treatment is not fairness

• “Be consistent — treating people differently is unfair.” Consistency of treatment across people in genuinely different situations is not fairness; it is one style that happens to suit whoever matches it, and this one suits none of the three.

• “Be firmer — somebody should have flagged that valve.” Somebody knew. The question is why they did not say, and firmness is what taught them not to. Push harder and the next thing you do not hear about will be larger.

• “Escalate to their managers — I have no authority here.” Borrows authority to compel people whose cooperation you need for months, and confirms to all four that things go badly when they are raised with you.

• “Back off and let them get on with it.” Right for exactly one of them. The graduate will guess more confidently and the shaken planner will stay hidden — the same single style, set to a different position.

• “Do more of it myself.” What you are already doing, and it is why you are working harder than anyone while the team gets worse.

• Start with a diagnosis — and note that it is not of the person but of the person's relationship to the task in front of them, on two independent axes. How much can they actually do this job: not their reputation, not their years, their skill at this. And how confident and willing are they right now — a different axis entirely, because a highly capable person can be badly shaken and a novice can be fearless. That pair tells you what each needs, and the needs are opposite: direction for one is interference for another. Two safeguards. The reading is task-specific and temporary — your technician is an expert on the mechanical scope and a nervous beginner the first time he touches the new control system, and next year's graduate is the one you leave alone — so never write a label beside a name and file it. And it is a tool for adapting your own behaviour, not for sorting people. Then the thing all of it rests on: whether bad news can travel. People who fear the response stop reporting, and you lose exactly the information that lets you act early — which is how a wrong valve reaches installation. You cannot announce that safety; it is built by what happens the first time somebody brings you something unwelcome.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you lead and build a team - reading each person, matching your style, building the safety that makes them perform, and doing it all without authority.

1. Read each person's competence and confidence - on THIS task, not as a label

LEADERSHIP STARTS WITH A DIAGNOSIS, AND THE DIAGNOSIS IS NOT OF THE PERSON - IT IS OF THE PERSON'S RELATIONSHIP TO THE TASK IN FRONT OF THEM. For each person you are leading, on the SPECIFIC task, read two things - knowing that your reading is a judgment, not a measurement, and you will often have to revise it as you go. FIRST, COMPETENCE: how much can they actually do this particular job - not their reputation, not their years, but their skill at THIS. SECOND, COMMITMENT: how confident and willing are they right now - which is a different axis entirely, because a highly competent person can be badly shaken, and a complete novice can be fearless. HOLD THIS RULE ABOVE EVERYTHING ELSE IN THE UNIT: THIS IS A DIAGNOSTIC TOOL FOR ADAPTING YOUR OWN BEHAVIOUR, NOT A TOOL FOR LABELLING PEOPLE. THE DIAGNOSIS IS ALWAYS TEMPORARY AND TASK-SPECIFIC. AN EXPERT IN ONE TASK IS A NOVICE IN ANOTHER, AND EVERYONE MOVES. The senior technician who should be left alone on the mechanical scope is a nervous beginner the first time he touches the new control system, and next year the graduate you had to walk through every step will be the one you leave alone. So you never write 'novice' next to a name and file it; you read, freshly, for this task, this week. At Corathyl, Onalenna leads a cross-functional upgrade team she does not line-manage: a senior technician expert and confident on the mechanical work; a graduate engineer keen but new to commissioning; an experienced planner who has done this many times but is rattled after a recent failure was pinned on him; and a contractor supervisor she has no authority over at all. Four people, four different readings - and the same firm style applied to all four will fail three of them.

2. Match your style to what they need - direct, coach, support, or delegate

THERE IS NO SINGLE RIGHT WAY TO LEAD - THE RIGHT WAY DEPENDS ON THE READING FROM BOX 1. There are FOUR styles, set by how much DIRECTION (telling them what and how) and how much SUPPORT (encouraging, listening, sharing the reasoning) each person needs. DIRECTING - high direction, low support - for the WILLING NOVICE: the graduate engineer who is keen but cannot yet do it needs the steps spelled out, or she will freeze or guess. COACHING - high direction, high support - for the learner who has hit the HARD MIDDLE, where the first enthusiasm has worn off and the task is harder than it looked: still tell them how, but now explain the why and carry them through the discouragement. SUPPORTING - low direction, high support - for the CAPABLE PERSON WHO HAS LOST CONFIDENCE: the rattled planner has the competence; what he has lost is nerve, and directing him would only confirm his fear that he is no longer trusted, so you listen, back him, and let him rebuild it by doing. DELEGATING - low direction, low support - for the COMPETENT AND CONFIDENT: agree the outcome with the senior technician and get out of the way; directing him insults him and he will stop volunteering the very knowledge you most need. THE TWO COMMONEST FAILURES ARE DIRECTING THE EXPERT (who resents it and goes quiet) AND DELEGATING TO THE DROWNING NOVICE (who was too willing to say she was lost). AND HERE IS THE DIGNITY OF IT, WHICH IS THE WHOLE POINT: YOU ADAPT YOUR STYLE NOT TO MANAGE A PERSON, BUT TO SERVE THEIR SUCCESS ON THE TASK IN FRONT OF THEM. MEETING THEM WHERE THEY ARE IS AN ACT OF RESPECT. DIRECTING A NOVICE IS A GIFT OF CLARITY. DELEGATING TO AN EXPERT IS A GIFT OF TRUST. THE FAILURE IS NOT IN THE PERSON, BUT IN THE MISMATCH BETWEEN WHAT THEY NEED AND WHAT YOU GIVE.

3. Build the safety that makes a team perform - your response to bad news IS the culture

NOW ONE OF THE BIGGEST LEVERS YOU HAVE, AND IT IS NOT ABOUT ANY INDIVIDUAL - IT IS ABOUT THE AIR THE TEAM BREATHES. A HIGH-PERFORMANCE TEAM IS NOT MADE OF STARS. IT IS MADE OF SAFETY. A key predictor of whether a team performs is PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY - the shared belief that it is safe to speak up, admit a mistake, ask a question that might sound stupid, or flag a risk WITHOUT being punished or humiliated. A team of brilliant people who dare not tell you the bad news will lose to a modest team that does, because in asset management THE BAD NEWS YOU DO NOT HEAR IS THE FAILURE YOU DO NOT PREVENT. And here is the thing every leader gets wrong: SAFETY IS NOT MADE BY A POSTER OR A VALUE STATEMENT. IT IS MADE BY YOUR RESPONSE TO BAD NEWS. SAFETY IS NOT SOFTNESS. IT IS THE DISCIPLINE THAT MAKES IT POSSIBLE TO DISCUSS HARD THINGS. YOUR FIRST RESPONSE TO BAD NEWS DOES NOT JUST SOLVE A PROBLEM - IT TEACHES YOUR ENTIRE TEAM WHETHER THE TRUTH IS WELCOME HERE. THANKING THE MESSENGER WHO FLAGS A RISK BUYS YOU EVERY FUTURE WARNING. BLAMING THEM BUYS YOU SILENCE. Watch it happen at Corathyl. At the first project meeting a junior nervously says a delivered valve looks wrong for the duty. Onalenna has two responses available, and only two, and the team is watching which one she picks. She can THANK HER AND CHECK - in which case every person in that room has just learned it is safe to raise the next thing they are unsure about - or she can WAVE IT OFF because the schedule is tight, in which case they have learned to keep their doubts to themselves. Choose the second and the wrong valve goes in, and the failure it causes months later is the direct, delayed cost of the silence she bought in that meeting. You cannot order safety into being. You can only make it, one response at a time, and you are always either building it or spending it.

4. Give the team what performance actually needs - not a pep talk

SAFETY IS THE FOUNDATION, BUT A SAFE TEAM STILL NEEDS FOUR MORE THINGS TO PERFORM, AND NONE OF THEM IS A MOTIVATIONAL SPEECH. FIRST, STRUCTURE AND CLARITY: a clear goal, clear roles, and a clear plan - every person able to say what the team is trying to achieve and what THEIR part is. A surprising amount of team dysfunction is not conflict or weakness but simple fog: two people each assuming the other had it, a goal everyone would state differently. Clarity is a leadership job, not an administrative one, and it links straight to who is accountable for what. SECOND, DEPENDABILITY: people reliably do what they said they would, by when they said - because a team where commitments are soft spends its energy chasing and covering rather than delivering, and the reliable members quietly stop trusting the rest. The leader builds this by making commitments explicit and following up without punishing honesty about slippage. THIRD, MEANING: the work matters to each person for a reason of their own - which you find by knowing your people well enough to connect the task to something they actually care about, not by telling them it should matter. FOURTH, IMPACT: people can SEE that their work makes a difference - so show them the outcome, close the loop, tell them what happened downstream of what they did, because effort poured into a void quietly dies. Notice what these four have in common: THEY ARE CONDITIONS THE LEADER CREATES, NOT FEELINGS THE LEADER DEMANDS. You do not exhort a team into performing. You build the conditions in which performing is possible and then you get out of its way.

5. Lead without leaning on authority you do not have

HERE IS THE REALITY THAT MAKES ALL OF THE ABOVE NECESSARY RATHER THAN OPTIONAL: IN ASSET MANAGEMENT, YOU WILL LEAD PEOPLE YOU DO NOT COMMAND. YOUR AUTHORITY WILL NOT COME FROM AN ORG CHART. IT WILL COME FROM THE CLARITY OF YOUR REQUESTS, THE SAFETY YOU CREATE, AND THE TRUST YOU EARN BY MATCHING YOUR LEADERSHIP TO WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY NEED TO SUCCEED. Onalenna cannot order the contractor supervisor, cannot discipline the senior technician who reports elsewhere, cannot compel the planner who outranks her in years. And it does not matter, because NONE OF THE FOUR STYLES WAS EVER ABOUT AUTHORITY - they are about MATCHING, and matching is available to anyone, at any level, over anyone. The person who leads best the team they do not command is the one who gives each member exactly what they need to do good work and makes it safe to tell the truth, so that people WANT to deliver for them - which outlasts and outperforms compliance every time. AND THE LAST DISCIPLINE, WHICH NEVER ENDS: KEEP CHECKING WHETHER THE TEAM IS TELLING YOU WHAT IS TRUE OR WHAT YOU WANT TO HEAR. A quiet, agreeable, everything-is-fine team is not proof of good leadership - it is one of the two most dangerous states a team can be in, because the other is on fire and at least you can see that one. If nobody has brought you a problem this week, do not assume there are none; assume you have not yet made it safe enough to hear them, and go and ask. YOU ARE THE ARCHITECT OF THE CONDITIONS FOR SUCCESS. YOU ARE NOT THE HERO WHO DOES ALL THE WORK - and the day the team performs better than you could alone, without you in the middle of everything, is the day you have actually led.

Box 2 is where you stop leading everyone the same. Box 3 is one of the biggest levers you have. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Leadership is not a style you possess. It is the discipline of adapting your support to what each person needs, and making it safe for them to tell you the truth. Five questions:

  • Where is each person on this task? Competence and confidence, read fresh for the task - never a fixed label. An expert at one thing is a novice at another, and everyone moves.
  • What style does each one need? Direct, coach, support, or delegate. The two common failures: directing the expert, delegating to the drowning novice.
  • How do I make the team perform? Psychological safety - built by your response to bad news, not by a poster. Thank the messenger, or buy silence.
  • What else does performance need? Clarity, dependability, meaning, impact - conditions you create, not feelings you demand.
  • How do I lead people I don't command? Clarity, safety and matched support - not rank - and keep checking whether they tell you the truth or what you want to hear.

The four right styles, in one team. Onalenna's four people each need something different, on the same project. The senior technician, expert and confident on the mechanical scope: delegate - agree the outcome and step back; directing him insults him and he stops volunteering what he knows. The graduate engineer, keen but new: direct, then coach - spell out the steps, then the reasoning, or she freezes and guesses. The experienced planner, rattled after a failure was pinned on him: support - he has the competence; what he lost is confidence, and directing him only confirms his fear that he is no longer trusted. The contractor supervisor she cannot command at all: none of the four styles was ever about authority - they are about matching, and matching is available to anyone, over anyone. The same firm style applied to all four fails three of them.

Why safety is a key lever, and where it is made. A high-performance team is not made of stars; it is made of safety - the shared belief that you can speak up, admit a mistake, or flag a risk without being punished. And safety is not softness; it is the discipline that makes it possible to discuss hard things. It is not built by a value statement. It is built by your first response to bad news: thanking the messenger who flags a risk buys you every future warning; blaming them buys you silence. When the junior says the valve looks wrong, Onalenna has exactly two moves and the room is watching which she picks. Thank her and check, and everyone learns it is safe to raise the next doubt. Wave it off because the schedule is tight, and they learn to keep their doubts to themselves - and the wrong valve goes in, and the failure months later is the delayed cost of the silence she bought in that meeting. You are always either building safety or spending it.

And the dignity that runs through all of it. You adapt your style not to manage a person, but to serve their success on the task in front of them. Meeting them where they are is an act of respect. Directing a novice is a gift of clarity. Delegating to an expert is a gift of trust. The failure is not in the person, but in the mismatch between what they need and what you give.

One honest boundary. You are the architect of the conditions for success; you are not the hero who does all the work. You cannot order safety into being - you can only make it, one response at a time - and you cannot do your people's jobs for them without teaching them they cannot.

Does it scale? The same leadership style that respects an expert will abandon a novice. And the single action that builds a high-performing team is not a pep talk - it is thanking the person who brings you bad news. Everything else is downstream of those two truths.

T4: Your Turn

A different team, and it is struggling - not because the people are weak.

A maintenance improvement team of four, led by a section head, is three months in and going backwards. The people are all capable. Here is what you can see:

PersonOn their current taskHow the leader treats them
A veteran fitterRedesigning a lubrication route he has run for fifteen years - fully capable, quietly insulted to be on the team at allGiven step-by-step instructions and checked daily
A new plannerBuilding her first criticality analysis - willing, but genuinely does not know how to startTold "you're clever, you'll figure it out" and left alone
A reliability engineerCompetent, but was publicly corrected by the leader in the last meeting for a small errorNow says little and agrees with everything
The leaderWorking nights to hold it togetherBelieves the team is "just not stepping up"

At the last meeting, when the reliability engineer began to raise a concern about the schedule, the leader cut in with "let's stay positive, we haven't got time for problems today."

  1. Diagnose two mismatches. For the veteran fitter and the new planner, name their competence and confidence on their task, the style they are getting, and the style they need.
  2. Diagnose the safety. Name the one specific leader behaviour that is destroying psychological safety, and what it has taught the team.
  3. Prescribe the style. Write the single sentence the leader should say to the veteran fitter to delegate the work properly.
  4. Prescribe the safety. The new planner nervously says "I don't actually know where to begin, and I'm worried I'm holding everyone up." Write the leader's exact reply.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the two mismatches are the same error in opposite directions. The veteran fitter is high-competence, high-confidence and is being directed and checked daily - he needs delegating, and the current style is why he is quietly withdrawing. The new planner is genuine-novice, willing, and is being delegated to ("you'll figure it out") - she needs directing, and the current style is why she is stuck and afraid to say so. The leader has the two styles exactly swapped.

Second, the safety-destroying behaviour is public correction and, worse, shutting down a concern with "we haven't got time for problems today." That single sentence taught the whole team that raising a problem is unwelcome - so the reliability engineer went quiet and agreeable, which the leader has misread as the problem rather than the symptom. The team is not failing to step up; it has learned that stepping up is punished.

Third, the delegating sentence to the fitter should hand over the outcome and get out of the way - something like: "You know this route better than anyone here - take the redesign, tell me what you need from me, and bring it back when it's ready; I don't need to see the working." That is a gift of trust, and it will bring him back to the team.

Fourth, the reply to the new planner is the whole unit in one moment. It must make her honesty rewarded, not exposed: something like "Thank you for telling me that - that took courage, and it's exactly what I needed to hear. It's not you holding us up; I left you without the start you needed. Let's sit down this afternoon and I'll walk you through the first analysis, step by step." That single reply switches her from directed-badly to directed-well and tells the whole team that saying "I don't know" is safe here. The exact words matter less than the structure - thank them for their honesty, own your part in the problem, and offer a concrete path forward - and mean it, because a formula without the feeling is worse than silence.

T5: The Guardrails

Most struggling teams do not have weak people. They have a leader using one style on everyone.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• One style for everyone. Whether you default to directing or to supporting, it will fail everyone it does not happen to fit. Read each person, on each task.

• Directing the expert. Telling a capable, confident person how to do their job insults them, and they stop volunteering what they know - which is the very thing you needed.

• Delegating to the drowning novice. "You'll figure it out" abandons the willing beginner, who was too eager to admit they are lost.

• Mistaking a confidence problem for a competence problem. A rattled expert needs support, not instruction. Direct them and you confirm their fear that they are no longer trusted.

• Thinking safety is a poster. It is made by your response to bad news, every time, not by a value on a wall.

• Blaming the messenger. Punish the person who brings you a problem and you have bought silence on every problem after it.

• Leading by a rank you do not have. You will lead people you cannot command. Clarity, safety and matched support are the currency - not authority.

• Reading a quiet, agreeable team as a healthy one. If no one has brought you a problem this week, do not assume there are none - assume you have not yet made it safe to hear them, and go and ask.

• Labelling people. "Novice" and "expert" describe a person's relationship to a task today, not their worth or their ceiling. Everyone moves.

Onalenna was working harder than anyone and leading a team that performed worse than its parts. Read each person's competence and confidence on the task in front of them, freshly and without labels, because an expert at one thing is a novice at another; match your style to what they need - direct the willing novice, coach the discouraged learner, support the capable person who has lost their nerve, and delegate to the one who can and knows it; build the psychological safety that is the biggest lever any leader has, remembering that it is made by your response to bad news and not by anything you say about it; give the team the conditions performance actually needs - clarity, dependability, meaning and visible impact - rather than a speech; and do all of it without leaning on an authority you will rarely have. Leadership is not a style you possess. It is the discipline of giving each person what they need to succeed, and making it safe for them to tell you the truth. Take one person you are leading right now, on one specific task, and ask honestly: what is their real competence and confidence here, what style am I actually giving them, and when they last brought me something difficult, did I thank them or wave it away?
