Step 1 of 5

Change one thing without breaking another

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. A supplier offers a newer chlorine dosing pump - cheaper, and rated at exactly the same flow as your worn old one. It will save R15,000 a year. It is a simple, sensible swap. You approve it, the team fits it, and you feel good: a small win for the budget.

Two days later, the water-quality alarms go off.

You scramble. The chlorine is out of range - not dangerously, but enough to breach compliance, enough that you have to notify the municipality. You check the pump: it is working perfectly, pushing exactly the flow it promised. So what went wrong?

Slowly it comes clear. The old pump delivered its dose in smooth, steady pulses. The new one delivers the same average flow, but in sharper bursts - and downstream, in the mixing, that subtly changed how the chlorine blended into the water. Nobody was careless. The pump did its job. But the thing you actually changed was never really "the pump" - it was the dosing pattern, and that rippled into the mix, and the mix rippled into the quality. You changed one thing, and it broke another you never thought to check.

I made a careful, approved change - and it still caused a failure, in a place I never connected to it. How do I change something without setting off a consequence I didn't see coming?

Before you read on, decide. The dosing pump was worn. You replaced it with a newer model at the same rated flow, saving about R15,000 a year. It was specified correctly, approved, and fitted properly, and it has been delivering exactly the average dose it should. Water quality has drifted anyway. Nobody was careless and the pump is doing its job. Write down what you would do differently next time — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what was actually changed

• “Be more careful with approvals — tighten the sign-off.” It was approved, by people who were paying attention. More signatures on the same information produce the same decision more slowly.

• “Only ever fit identical replacements.” A rule that is unaffordable and, sooner or later, impossible — parts are discontinued. It also does not generalise: you cannot run a works by never changing anything.

• “Test the new pump before fitting it.” Very close, and it raises the question the whole thing turns on: test it for what? It was tested for flow, which is what it was specified on, and flow was never the problem.

• “Watch the water quality more closely after any change.” Sensible and reactive. It shortens the time before you notice harm rather than preventing it, and on some changes noticing late is the whole cost.

• “Put it back and take the R15,000 hit.” Possibly right today, and it teaches nothing for the next change, which will be different and will surprise you differently.

• Look again at what was actually changed. Not the pump — the pump was replaced like for like on the property it was specified by. What changed was the way it delivers, and something downstream depended on that: the mixing, which nobody had connected to the pump at all. So the discipline is not more care but a deliberate act done before the work: trace outward and ask what else touches this, what assumes something about it, and who would need to be told — the mixing, the quality alarm and its settings, the operators' checklist, the spares list, the register. Then, for each new risk that surfaces, name the control beside it, since a risk written down and unmitigated is only a prediction. Keep a way back until it is proven rather than until it is installed. And have it approved by somebody other than the person proposing it — because the proposer is exactly the person who cannot see the dependency they did not know existed.

There is a discipline for exactly this - a way to catch the ripple before it bites. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the one page that would have caught it - the same dosing-pump change, run through properly before a spanner was lifted. Read it, and watch where the danger actually hides.

1. The Change, the Why, and the Type

Replace the worn chlorine dosing pump with a newer, cheaper model rated at the same flow - saving R15,000 a year. Type: PERMANENT.

2. The Ripple Map - What Else It Touches

Trace everything it touches: the chemical mixing downstream (does this pump dose the same WAY?); the water-quality sensor and its alarm settings; the operators' daily checklist; the maintenance plan and the spares list; the asset register.

3. The New Risks It Brings - and the Controls

New risk: the new pump's different pulse pattern could shift the actual chemical mix even at the same average flow - a water-quality risk. Control: bench-test its dose pattern against the old pump first, then monitor quality hourly for the first week before trusting it.

4. The Way Back (back-out plan)

If quality drifts, the old pump stays on the shelf, wired-ready, so we can swap back within an hour. We do not scrap the old pump until the new one is proven over a full month.

5. Approval Before, and Review After

Approved BEFORE fitting by Operations and the Process Engineer together - never the proposer alone. Reviewed two weeks AFTER: did it hold water quality? any surprises? If clean, the change is closed; if not, we back out. (This after-check is the Post-Implementation Review.)

Look at box 2. The failure was never in box 1 - the pump itself was fine. It was in the ripple: the mixing, the quality sensor. This page forces you to trace those lines, name the new risk, plan a way back, and have someone else check you - all before the change is live. That is the difference between "I hope this works" and "we have assessed the risks, and we are ready". Now let us walk through it.

T3: The Blueprint

This is a Management of Change record - an MOC. It comes from ISO 55001, clause 6.3, and its one demand is simple and unbreakable: assess the risks of a change before you make it. Because, as Onalenna learned, the failure of a good change is almost never the thing you changed - it is the consequence nobody thought to trace.

The heart of it is one move - the ripple map. Put the change in the middle of the page, then draw a line to everything it touches: other assets and processes, the control system and its alarm settings, the documents and checklists, and the people who now need to know or be retrained. The danger is always in the lines you don't draw. Now each box, as the question it answers:

  • 1. The Change, the Why, and the Type - What are we changing, why, and what kind of change is it? Permanent, Temporary (which needs an expiry date), or Emergency (done fast to make safe, then signed off straight after). Naming the type sets how careful and how quick you must be.
  • 2. The Ripple Map - What else does this touch? The most important box, and the one everyone skips. Trace outward, upstream and downstream. The pump touched the mix, the sensor, the checklist, the spares list.
  • 3. The New Risks - and the Controls - What could now go wrong that couldn't before, and how do we hold it? The new risk usually lives in something the change touches, not the change itself. Name it, and name how you will control it.
  • 4. The Way Back - If it goes wrong, how do we undo it - safely, and fast? A change with no back-out plan is a gamble. Plan for failure before you start. (Keep the old pump, wired-ready.)
  • 5. Approval Before, and Review After - Who checks us before, and how do we confirm after? Sign-off comes from the people the change affects - never the proposer marking their own homework. And a review afterwards (the Post-Implementation Review) closes the loop: did it work, any surprises, is it now safely closed?

Does it scale? At Corathyl, this one page assesses the risk of changing one R40,000 pump to protect the community's water. At a refinery, the exact same procedure assesses the risk of changing a R40-million reactor catalyst to prevent a catastrophe that could level the plant. The stakes change; the discipline does not.

T4: Your Turn

Now you run one. Think of a real change you would actually like to make to something you know - your bike, your study setup, a family business's equipment, a society's gear (swap a part, change a routine, upgrade a tool). Then, before you would make it, run it through the five boxes - and give box 2, the ripple map, real effort.

The one box that matters most? Box 2. Push yourself: what else does this touch - and what does THAT touch? The risks you find there are the ones that would have surprised you.

T5: The Guardrails

A change record is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then strengthen any box that needs it.

The five deadly sins of change - and how to dodge them

• The "it's trivial" sin. Believing a change is too small or cheap to need this. Onalenna's pump was both - and it still breached compliance. Risk is not proportional to size or cost.

• The blinkers sin. Looking only at the thing you are changing, and never tracing the ripples around it. The ripple map (box 2) exists to break exactly this habit.

• The no-way-back sin. Making a change with no tested plan to undo it. If the new pump had failed completely, how would you have kept dosing? Always plan for failure first.

• The marking-your-own-homework sin. The person who wants the change being the only one who approves it. A real MOC needs an independent check from the people it affects - operations, maintenance, safety.

• The "permanent temporary" sin. A "quick temporary fix" with no expiry date and no plan to make it proper. Uncontrolled temporary changes quietly pile up and become a main cause of future failure. Every temporary change needs a date it dies.

The water-quality scare did not come from a bad pump - it came from a good change made without tracing where it rippled. Now you hold the tool that catches the ripple before it bites: trace what a change touches, name the new risk, keep a way back, and let someone else check you - all before you lift a spanner. Master it, and you can change what needs changing without breaking what didn't. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, bring your change record to your next session.
