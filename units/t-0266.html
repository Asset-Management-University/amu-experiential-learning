Step 1 of 5

One clause every eight months

T1: The Ache

You are Ntsika, and the certification audit has been booked.

The board decided it at the March meeting. External ISO 55001 certification, five months from now, because a large customer has begun asking for it in tenders. Everybody is pleased. There is a plan on a wall with green ticks on it.

You have been asked to "do a readiness check", which everybody expects will take an afternoon and confirm that things are broadly on track.

It takes an afternoon. It does not confirm that.

The internal audit programme has covered three clauses in twenty-four months. One clause every eight months. The certification audit covers the whole standard, and it is in five.

The management review has never been held. Not once. There is a procedure describing it.

And you are the most junior person who has looked at this, you are holding a number that nobody in that March meeting had, and the wall chart is green.

The date is booked, everybody is confident, and the arithmetic says otherwise. How do I produce a readiness review that is honest and still useful?

Before you read on, decide. The date is booked. The wall chart is green. Everybody in that March meeting was confident, and not one of them was lying. And you are the most junior person who has looked at this properly, holding a number nobody else in the room has: three clauses in twenty-four months, and the whole standard examined in five. Write down what you would put in a readiness review, and in what order, so that it is honest about that and still worth reading — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one costs you

• “List every gap, worst first.” The natural order, and it hides the thing that will actually sink you. Severity is what the eye reaches for; time is what the calendar reads. A serious gap you can close in a fortnight is not your problem. A modest one that needs nine months, with five available, has already decided the outcome — and sorted by severity it sits halfway down page three.

• “Do not lead with the arithmetic — it will read as an attack.” Understandable, and it costs you the one thing the review can settle. Left unsaid, the coverage rate becomes an argument that runs for a month; said early in a flat sentence with no adjectives, it is just a fact everybody now shares. The audit programme was resourced for what it was asked to do, and saying so in the same breath is not softening — it is accuracy.

• “Report the coverage: three clauses of the standard are done.” True, and it overstates what you hold. One of those three was examined for whether it works; two were readings of documents. Those are different examinations, and a count that adds them together tells your reader they have three of something when they have one of one thing and two of another.

• “Grade everything as important — it all matters.” It does all matter, and a review in which everything is critical gives the reader nothing to act on, so they act on none of it. You have to separate what would actually stop certification from what would not, and that separation is the review; without it you have written a list.

• “Recommend moving the date.” Possibly right, and it is a conclusion rather than a review — it will be received as defeatism by people who have committed publicly, and refused. The recommendation only becomes available once the plan is built backwards from the date and visibly does not fit. Offered now it is an opinion; offered then it is arithmetic, and it is far cheaper today than discovered by an assessor in five months.

Hold on to the one fact you brought into the room: at the rate this organisation has actually achieved, six-tenths of one further clause will be covered before they arrive. Everything below is built on saying that plainly and then making it useful.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the readiness review - what has actually been covered and at what rate, gaps sorted by time rather than severity, what would block certification separated from what would not, the plan built backwards, and a checklist somebody can work from.

1. Work out what has actually been audited - and at what rate you are covering the standard

<strong>Before any readiness judgement, establish the plainest fact available and it is usually the one nobody has computed.</strong> <strong>The internal audit programme has covered three clauses in twenty-four months. That is one clause every eight months.</strong> <strong>The certification audit is in five months and covers the whole standard.</strong> <strong>At the current rate, 0.6 of one further clause will be audited before they arrive.</strong> <strong>Say that sentence in the report, early, without adjectives.</strong> It is not a criticism of anybody - the audit programme was resourced for what it was asked to do - and it settles a question that would otherwise be argued about for a month. <strong>Then record the depth, not just the coverage.</strong> Of the three clauses covered, one was audited for effectiveness and two were document reviews. <strong>So the honest statement is that the organisation has one clause of effectiveness evidence and two of conformance evidence, against a standard that will be examined in full.</strong> <strong>A readiness review that begins with a percentage of clauses ticked will overstate the position every time</strong>, because a clause with a document review against it looks identical, in a tracker, to a clause that has genuinely been tested.

2. Sort the gaps by how long they take to close - not by how serious they are

<strong>Every readiness review sorts gaps by severity. That is the wrong axis at this stage, and it is why organisations arrive at certification with the important things fixed and the slow things unfinished.</strong> <strong>Sort by TIME TO CLOSE, because time is the constraint you cannot buy back in month four.</strong> <strong>Three kinds.</strong> <strong>Document gaps</strong> - a procedure that does not exist or does not say what the standard requires. Genuinely fast: days to weeks, and largely a matter of somebody sitting down. <strong>Implementation gaps</strong> - the procedure exists and the plant does not do it. Slower: weeks to months, because behaviour and systems have to change. <strong>Evidence gaps</strong> - and these are the ones that end certification attempts. <strong>The organisation may do the right thing from tomorrow morning and still have no RECORD of having done it for a period long enough to demonstrate</strong>, and no amount of effort compresses that. <strong>Worked.</strong> A management review that has never been held: writing the procedure takes a week, holding the first one takes a month, <strong>and demonstrating that it operates on a cycle takes two cycles, which is the binding constraint and it is nine months.</strong> <strong>Compute that first, for every gap, and the plan writes itself in reverse from the audit date.</strong>

3. Separate what would stop certification from what would be raised as a finding - and be honest about which is which

<strong>Not every gap stops a certificate, and treating them all as equally urgent wastes the five months you have.</strong> <strong>Separate them honestly, using the same test you would apply auditing somebody else.</strong> <strong>Likely to block:</strong> a required process that has never operated - no management review, no internal audit of most of the standard, no evidence of evaluating performance against objectives. <strong>These are systemic absences of things the standard requires to exist, and an assessor cannot certify a system whose required elements have never run.</strong> <strong>Likely to be raised and not to block:</strong> a criticality register three years out of date. <strong>It is a real nonconformity and it is a maintained system that has drifted, which is a different thing from a system that was never built</strong>, and it can be closed with a corrective action plan after certification. <strong>Likely to be an observation:</strong> handover notes inconsistent in format. <strong>Now say all of this plainly in the report, including the uncomfortable half.</strong> <strong>If the honest position is that the organisation is not ready for the date it has booked, that sentence belongs in the first paragraph</strong>, and it is the most valuable thing the review produces. <strong>A readiness review that tells an organisation what it hopes to hear costs it the fee, the assessor's time and its own credibility with a body it will have to face again.</strong>

4. Build the plan backwards from the audit date - and mark the point after which a gap can no longer be closed

<strong>Work backwards from the date the assessor arrives, because forwards planning always produces a plan that finishes conveniently on time.</strong> <strong>For each gap, subtract the time to close from the audit date to get the latest possible start - and then check today against it.</strong> <strong>Mine, from five months out.</strong> Procedure documentation: two weeks, latest start month four, <strong>fine</strong>. Reinstating the criticality register: six weeks, latest start month three, <strong>fine</strong>. Internal audit of the remaining clauses: at a realistic six hours per clause and a single part-time auditor, <strong>this needed to start eleven months ago and cannot be completed</strong>. Two management review cycles: <strong>nine months, needed to start four months ago</strong>. <strong>Two of the four are already past their latest start, and no amount of effort recovers them.</strong> <strong>Name that in the plan, in those words.</strong> <strong>An organisation that knows in month five that two gaps cannot be closed has two real options - postpone the audit, or proceed knowing it will take those findings - and both are manageable decisions.</strong> <strong>An organisation that finds out in month one of the audit has neither.</strong> <strong>The single most useful thing a readiness review produces is the list of things that can no longer be fixed, delivered while there is still time to decide what to do about that.</strong>

5. End with a checklist somebody can work from - owned, dated, and honest about the residual

<strong>The report ends as a working document, not a verdict.</strong> <strong>One row per action: what, who, by when, and - the column most readiness checklists omit - what evidence will exist to demonstrate it.</strong> <strong>That last column is what turns a task list into an audit-ready record</strong>, because the assessor will not ask whether you did it; they will ask to see it. <strong>Worked row:</strong> 'Populate criticality for the 41 assets commissioned since 2021 / asset information officer / by 14 March / evidence: register export showing 100 per cent populated, plus five sample work orders raised after that date carrying the value.' <strong>Then the honest close, and it is the paragraph that earns the report its authority.</strong> <strong>State what will still be open on the audit date, and what the organisation should expect as a result.</strong> Mine: 'Internal audit coverage will stand at four clauses of the standard, and one management review cycle will have been completed. Both are likely to be raised as nonconformities. The recommendation is to move the certification audit by six months, at which point both will be closed.' <strong>Recommending a delay is not a failure of the review. It is the review working.</strong> <strong>The organisation is free to proceed anyway, and now it proceeds knowing what it will meet rather than hoping.</strong>

Box 2 is the axis nobody uses, and box 4 is where the review earns its keep. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A readiness review is not a progress report. It is an attempt to find out, while there is still time to act, which gaps can no longer be closed before the date somebody has booked. Five moves:

  • Establish the rate. Three clauses in twenty-four months against a whole standard in five. Plainly, early, without adjectives.
  • Sort gaps by time to close, not severity. Document, implementation, evidence - and evidence gaps cannot be compressed by effort.
  • Separate what blocks from what is merely raised. A process that never ran is different from a maintained one that drifted.
  • Plan backwards from the audit date. Latest possible start for each gap - then check today against it.
  • End with a checklist, and state the residual. Including what will still be open on the day.

The arithmetic that settles the argument. Twenty-four months, three clauses: one clause every eight months. Five months remain, so 0.6 of one further clause will be audited before the assessor arrives. That sentence takes ten minutes to produce and it resolves a question that would otherwise be debated for a month. And record depth, not just coverage: of the three, one was audited for effectiveness and two were document reviews. A tracker cannot tell those apart, and a readiness review that counts ticks will overstate the position every time.

Why time is the right axis. Severity tells you what matters; time tells you what is still possible. Sorted by severity, an organisation fixes the important things and arrives with the slow things unfinished - which is the commonest way a certification attempt fails. Evidence gaps are the ones that end attempts. An organisation can begin doing the right thing tomorrow morning and still have no record of doing it for long enough to demonstrate. The management review is the worked case: the procedure takes a week, the first meeting takes a month, and showing it operates on a cycle takes two cycles - nine months, and nothing compresses it.

What planning backwards exposes. Four gaps, latest start dates computed, today checked against each: two are already past their latest start and cannot be closed. That is not a comfortable finding and it is the most valuable thing the review produces. An organisation that knows this in month five has two manageable options - postpone, or proceed knowingly and take the findings. An organisation that discovers it on day one of the audit has neither.

On recommending a delay. It is not a failure of the review. It is the review working. The organisation remains free to proceed, and now proceeds knowing what it will meet rather than hoping - which is the difference between a decision and a surprise.

An honest boundary. This assesses readiness and produces a plan. It does not close the gaps; that is months of other people's work. It is not a pre-assessment by the certification body, which is a different and paid engagement with a different standing. And its judgement of what would block certification is your professional opinion informed by the standard - a real assessor may weigh something differently, and the report should say so.

T4: Your Turn

A different organisation, and this time the numbers look much better. Certification audit in seven months.

ItemPosition today
Internal audit coverageEvery clause audited within the last 18 months. Programme running well
Management reviewHeld quarterly for three years without interruption. Minuted
Documented systemComplete and current
Performance evaluationMonthly reporting against objectives, twelve months of records
Corrective actionProcess operating. 34 actions raised in 12 months, 31 closed
The one thingThe organisation restructured 4 months ago. Maintenance and operations merged, 3 senior roles changed, the asset management policy still names the old structure and the old accountabilities
Their view"That's just a document update. We'll fix the policy before the audit."
  1. Their view is that this is a document update. Say why it is not - be specific about what else the restructure touches.
  2. Classify the gap by the three kinds. Which is it, and what does that tell you about the timing?
  3. The internal audits were all done within 18 months and are all pre-restructure. What is the problem, and what does it do to their coverage position?
  4. Plan backwards from seven months. What is the binding constraint here?
  5. Would it block certification? Give your answer and your reasoning.
  6. Write the opening paragraph of the readiness review for an organisation that is, genuinely, in good shape.

Work it before you turn over. The point of this one is that a well-run system can be made unready by a single event, and that the organisation will be the last to see it.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. Not a document update. A restructure that merges two functions and changes three senior roles moves accountabilities, and accountabilities are what most of the standard is about. It touches: who owns the asset management policy and objectives; the responsibilities in every procedure that names a role; the competence requirements for changed posts; the assurance map, because the lines have moved; and the risk ownership. The policy document is the visible corner of it. Updating the policy and stopping there produces a system whose top-level document is current and whose procedures name people who no longer hold those responsibilities.

2. Which kind of gap. The policy itself is a document gap - fast. But the real gap is an evidence gap: the new structure has operated for four months, and there is no evidence of the new accountabilities discharging their duties, because every record predates them. Evidence gaps cannot be compressed.

3. The audits. All the internal audits are complete and all of them audited an organisation that no longer exists. They are not worthless - the processes are largely the same - but an assessor will ask how the system is being assured under the current structure, and the honest answer is that it has not been audited since the merge. Their coverage position is not "every clause in 18 months". It is "every clause in 18 months, none since the restructure four months ago". Those are very different sentences and only the second is true.

4. Planning backwards. Policy and procedure updates: three weeks. A management review under the new structure: they are quarterly, so one will occur naturally - and two would need six months, which fits inside seven. The binding constraint is a post-restructure internal audit of at least the clauses most affected by changed accountabilities - realistically six to eight clauses, needing perhaps two months of a part-time auditor, and it must finish early enough that any findings can be corrected and the corrections evidenced before the assessor arrives. Latest start: about month two. They have time. They do not have much.

5. Would it block? Probably not, if they act now. This is a maintained system that has drifted for four months, not a required element that never existed - the distinction that separates a certificate from a postponement. But it could become blocking if they treat it as a document update, because by month seven they would have a current policy, no post-restructure audit evidence, and no way to demonstrate that the new accountabilities are discharged. The difference between "raised as a finding" and "blocks certification" is, in this case, entirely a function of what they do in the next four weeks.

6. The opening paragraph. "This organisation's asset management system is in good order and the certification date is achievable. The internal audit programme has covered every clause within eighteen months, the management review has run quarterly for three years without interruption, and there are twelve months of performance records against objectives. One risk requires attention now rather than later: the restructure four months ago changed accountabilities that most of the system's documentation and all of its audit evidence predate. This is not a documentation task, and the actions below are sequenced so that the post-restructure evidence exists before the audit rather than after it."

Notice what that paragraph does. It says the true thing first - they are in good shape, and they are - which means the risk that follows is read as information rather than as an attack on a system people are proud of. A readiness review is most likely to be acted on by an organisation that recognises itself in the first sentence.

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Reporting coverage without depth. A tracker that shows a clause was "reviewed" cannot distinguish a document check from an effectiveness audit - record the depth, not just the tick.

• Sorting gaps by how bad they look rather than by how long they take to close. An evidence gap cannot be compressed by effort - two audit cycles take two audit cycles however hard anyone works between now and the date.

• Treating a required element that never ran the same as a system that drifted. One blocks certification outright. The other is merely raised. Keep them apart or every gap looks equally urgent.

• Recommending the date holds because saying so is easier than recommending a delay. A recommended delay is the review working, not the review failing - it is the whole reason to run one while there is still time to act.

What you can do now that you could not before. Compute the rate and state it without adjectives. Sort gaps by time to close and identify the evidence gaps that cannot be compressed. Separate what would block certification from what would merely be raised. Plan backwards and name what is already past its latest start. And end with a checklist that says what evidence will exist, and what will still be open on the day.

Nobody in the March meeting was wrong to book the date. They did not have the number, and the number took an afternoon.

One clause every eight months is not a criticism of anybody. It is an arithmetic fact about a programme that was resourced for what it was asked to do - and it was never asked to do this.
