Step 1 of 5

Apply Nigerian data protection (NDPA 2023) and public-policy guidance (ISO 55011)

T1: The Ache

It is a wet Tuesday morning in Lekki, on the edge of Lagos. You are the data lead for the Lekki-Epe Water Board, standing in a hot server room behind the billing office. The fan rattles. The room smells of warm dust and old plastic.

In your hand is a one-page sheet from your engineering team: the rollout plan for 9,400 new smart water meters - small boxes that sit on each household pipe and send a reading over the mobile network every hour. The plan is exciting. It will cut leaks, end guesswork bills, and show the state which pipes are failing first.

But your Managing Director, Mrs Adeyemi, is leaning in the doorway with her arms folded. She read the same sheet last night. "Each meter is tied to a house," she says. "A house has a name, a phone number, an address, and now an hour-by-hour record of when people are home and when they are not. That is personal information about 9,400 families." She taps the sheet. "If this leaks, or if a regulator asks how we protect it, what do we say? We go live in six weeks. I need an answer I can defend."

Your stomach tightens. You know there is a Nigerian law on personal data, and you know the water sector has its own rulebook for big public assets. But you have only ever seen the law quoted in scary headlines and the asset rulebook treated as an engineering thing. Right now they are crashing into each other on one sheet of paper, and your name is on the project.

The easy move is to copy a privacy notice off another company's website, paste your logo on it, and call it done. But you can feel that this is bigger than a notice.

I need a reliable, repeatable way to check a public-infrastructure project against both the data-protection law and the asset-management policy rules - every time - so I can show my work and defend it. Not a lucky guess. A method.

Before you read on, decide. Names, phone numbers, addresses — and now an hour-by-hour record of when 9,400 families are home and when they are not. Six weeks to go live, and she wants an answer she can defend. Write down what you would do. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one would survive being asked about

• “Copy a privacy notice from another company and put our logo on it.” Fast, and it answers the smallest possible version of her question. A notice describes what you do with data; it does not change what you do with it — and what you are about to do is hold an occupancy record for nine thousand households.

• “Encrypt everything and lock the database down.” Necessary and it is a different question. Security asks whether the wrong person can reach the data. Neither of the two rulebooks on that sheet is satisfied by keeping data safely that you should not be holding at all.

• “Get consent from every customer at sign-up.” The instinct everybody reaches for, and it assumes consent is the reason you are allowed to hold each field. Some of it you hold because you must bill them; some because a leak in a public network has to be found. Ask for consent where you actually rely on something else, and you have promised a choice you cannot honour.

• “Ask the lawyers — it’s a legal question.” Sensible, and they will ask what data you hold, why you hold each item, and for how long — and if nobody has walked the meter’s data flow, the six weeks are spent assembling what you should have brought them.

• “It’s an engineering project — the asset rules are what apply.” They do apply, and so does the other rulebook, on the same sheet of paper. Treating one as the real one is precisely how the two ended up colliding six weeks before go-live with nobody holding both.

Notice the sentence she actually said, because it sets the bar: not “are we allowed”, but “what do we say if a regulator asks how we protect it” — which is a request to show your working.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the method run to completion, on your own case: the Lekki-Epe smart-meter pilot. The numbers stay consistent throughout - 9,400 meters, 6-week go-live, hourly readings.

Step 1 - Map the data. You walk the meter's data flow and list every personal item it touches: household name, service address, account holder phone number, meter ID (unique per house), hourly water-use reading (time-stamped), GPS location of the meter. Six items. You note straight away that "hourly reading with a time-stamp" quietly reveals occupancy - when a family is home - so it is more sensitive than it looks.

Step 2 - Name the lawful basis. For each item you ask: why am I allowed to hold this? For name, address, phone and meter ID, the basis is performance of the billing contract - you cannot bill a customer you cannot identify. Hourly readings have two possible reasons: billing (which only strictly needs a daily total) and leak detection / network management, a public-interest task. You write both down, because the law wants the basis named, not assumed.

Step 3 - Trim to need (minimisation). You challenge each field. Do you really need hourly granularity for billing? No - a daily total bills the customer perfectly. The hourly detail is only needed for leak detection, and leak detection does not need a name attached. So you decide: store hourly readings against the meter ID only, not the name, and keep the name-to-meter link in a separate, locked table. This single change cuts the occupancy-tracking risk sharply. You also drop a "household size" field engineering had added "just in case" - you do not need it.

Step 4 - Run a DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment). Because the project watches 9,400 homes hourly, it is exactly the kind of large, sustained monitoring that needs one. Three example rows from your table:

Risk to peopleHow likely / how badHow you reduce it
Hourly data reveals when a home is empty (burglary aid if leaked)Likely if names attached; high harmSeparate name from hourly data (Step 3); encrypt the database
A staff member browses a neighbour's usagePossible; medium harmRole-based access; every lookup logged and reviewed monthly
Phone numbers sold or misused by a contractorPossible; high harmContract clause banning reuse; contractor signs NDPA terms

You record the leftover risk as low once these controls are in place. The DPIA is now your evidence.

Step 5 - Secure and give rights. You set the safeguards named above: encryption at rest, role-based access, an access log reviewed monthly. For rights, you add a line to the customer portal: "See my data / correct my details / ask a question," routed to a named officer who must reply within the time the law expects. Now a household can see and fix its own record.

Step 6 - Frame the public value (whole-life). You switch rulebooks. Cheap-today thinking would buy the lowest-cost meter. Whole-life thinking asks about 15 years. The meters cost ₦1.6 billion to install, but cut non-revenue water (water lost to leaks and theft) from 38% to a target 22%, recovering an estimated ₦540 million a year. Over 15 years that is real public value, and it serves the state's stated goal of universal metered supply by 2035 - your line of sight to a public promise.

Step 7 - Check stakeholder fairness and transparency. You list the stakeholders: households (want fair bills and privacy), the Board (wants less loss), the state regulator (wants public trust). Bills become accurate, not higher by stealth, and privacy is protected by Steps 3-5. Then the radio test: could you explain this on Lagos Talks FM and survive? Yes - "we measure your pipe, not your private life; your name is never stored with your hourly use." That sentence is your transparency anchor.

Step 8 - Write the one-page defence. You combine both checks onto one page for Mrs Adeyemi: data mapped and minimised; lawful basis named per field; DPIA done with residual risk low; rights and security live; whole-life value of ₦540 million a year against a ₦1.6 billion build, with clear line of sight to the 2035 goal; fair and explainable. You now have an answer you can defend - to the MD today and the regulator tomorrow. You did not guess. You ran the method.

See what the method did. It turned a one-page sheet that made your stomach tighten into a one-page defence that survives a regulator's questions - and it did it by refusing to satisfy one duty loudly while quietly failing the other. The mission is in the method itself: a household's private life (when they are home, who lives there) is protected not by a slogan but by a structural choice - separating the name from the hourly reading - and the public's money (₦1.6 billion) is justified not by a glossy pitch but by an honest, whole-life account of the value it returns. Neither duty is allowed to hide behind the other.

T3: The Blueprint

Two rulebooks press on you here. Name each one, then see how they fit together, then walk the eight steps that ran in the example above.

What is the data-protection rulebook? It is the Nigeria Data Protection Act of 2023, usually shortened to the NDPA (an "Act" is a law passed by the national assembly). The NDPA is Nigeria's main law on how organisations may collect and use personal data - any information that can point to a living person: a name, a phone number, an address, a meter ID tied to a household, or a record of when a home uses water. It is enforced by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC). It rests on a few plain ideas, each the answer to a question you must ask of every piece of data you hold:

  • Lawful basis - why am I allowed to hold this?
  • Purpose limitation - am I using it only for what I said?
  • Data minimisation - do I really need this field?
  • Security - who could get in, and have I stopped them?
  • Rights of the person - can a person see and correct their own record?

The NDPA also expects a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) before a project that handles a lot of personal data or watches people in a sustained way - a structured "what could go wrong with this data, and how have I reduced it?" document.

What is the asset-policy rulebook? It is ISO 55011 (ISO is the International Organization for Standardization, the global body that writes agreed standards; the number is the catalogue number for this one). ISO 55011 is guidance for governments and regulators on how to encourage good management of public infrastructure assets - water pipes, roads, power lines - so the public gets lasting value, not just a cheap build that fails early. It sits alongside ISO 55001, the standard for managing assets inside one organisation; ISO 55011 is the public-policy cousin, speaking to the people who set the rules for a whole sector. It pushes a few plain ideas, again each a question:

  • Whole-life value - is this good value over 15 years, not just cheap today?
  • Line of sight - which public promise does this keep?
  • Stakeholder balance - who is affected, and is it fair to them?
  • Transparency and trust - would this survive being explained on the radio?

When this combined method fits - and when it does not. Use the two-rulebook check when a public-infrastructure project also handles personal data - smart meters, traffic cameras, IIoT sensors (the Industrial Internet of Things: the network of sensors and devices that report data from physical equipment). That is exactly the Lekki-Epe case. You would not need the NDPA check for a purely mechanical upgrade that touches no personal data, such as relining a pipe; and you would not lead with ISO 55011 for a private in-house tool with no public-policy angle. The skill is knowing that a project sits in the overlap, and checking both.

The eight steps, in order (this is exactly what you saw run in T2):

  1. Map the data - list every personal-data item the project touches.
  2. Name the lawful basis - write down why you are allowed to hold each item.
  3. Trim to need (minimisation) - cut any field you do not truly need.
  4. Run a DPIA - write down the risks to people and how you reduce each one.
  5. Secure and give rights - set safeguards; build a way for people to see and correct their record.
  6. Frame the public value (whole-life) - state the public goal and the value over the asset's whole life.
  7. Check stakeholder fairness and transparency - show the deal is fair and explainable.
  8. Write the one-page defence - combine both checks into a record you can hand to the MD and the regulator.

Now test the method against four situations. Decide your own answer to each before you open the reveal.

Task 1 (warm-up). A second pilot adds streetlight sensors that record only light level and a pole ID - no household, no name. A junior asks you to run a full DPIA "to be safe." Decide: does this need the personal-data treatment, and why?

What good looks like

You recognise there is no personal data here - a pole ID and light level point to no person. So the personal-data checks (lawful basis per person, DPIA for monitoring people, individual rights) do not apply. You would still apply whole-life thinking to the streetlight asset, but you correctly avoid burying a harmless project in privacy paperwork. Knowing when the method does not fit is part of the skill.

Task 2 (apply the steps). The billing team wants to keep hourly readings tied to the name because "it might be useful later." Use Steps 2 and 3 to give them a clear answer.

What good looks like

You name the lawful basis and find that billing only needs a daily total (Step 2), so hourly-with-name fails data minimisation (Step 3). "Might be useful later" is not a lawful basis - the law bans keeping data for vague future reasons (purpose limitation). Your answer: store hourly data against meter ID only, keep the name link separate. You give the reason, not just the refusal.

Task 3 (harder - both rulebooks at once). The state offers a grant if you share anonymised ward-level water-use data publicly to attract investors, and asks you to advise on policy. Advise using both rulebooks: what must you check before agreeing?

What good looks like

On the data-protection side, you check that "anonymised" is truly anonymous - ward-level totals are fine, but you must be sure no small ward (say, a handful of homes) can be re-identified; if it can, it is still personal data. On the public-value side, you weigh whole-life value and stakeholder trust: open data could attract investment (public value) but you advise transparency about what is shared and what is protected, so trust holds. A strong answer keeps both checks visible and recommends releasing only aggregated, non-re-identifiable data.

Task 4 (stretch - the trade-off). Go-live is in two weeks and the DPIA is not finished. The engineering lead says "ship now, document later." Make and defend a decision.

What good looks like

You hold the line that a DPIA is required before large-scale monitoring goes live - "document later" reverses the law's purpose, which is to find harm before it happens. But you are practical: you propose a phased go-live of, say, the first 500 meters once their DPIA section and security controls are signed, while the full DPIA completes. You protect people and the timeline. A weak answer either blocks everything rigidly or ships blind; a strong answer manages the real trade-off honestly.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the two-rulebook defence true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Copying a privacy notice and calling it compliance. Symptom: a polished notice on the website, but no data map, no DPIA, no named lawful basis. Fix: a notice is the last page, not the work. Run Steps 1-5 first; the notice should simply describe what you actually did.

• Treating "anonymised" as a magic word. Symptom: "it's anonymous" claimed for data that still ties to a single small household. Fix: test re-identification. If a person can be picked out - even from a small group - it is still personal data and the law still applies. Aggregate further or do not release it.

• Collecting data "just in case." Symptom: extra fields (household size, occupation) with no clear use. Fix: apply data minimisation (Step 3). If you cannot name the lawful basis and the need, delete the field. Every extra field is risk you carry for free.

• Leading with cheapest-today on a public asset. Symptom: the lowest-cost meter chosen with no 15-year view. Fix: apply whole-life value (Step 6). Compare build cost against losses recovered across the asset's whole life, and check the line of sight to a public goal.

• Running only one rulebook. Symptom: a strong DPIA but no public-value case, or a glossy value case with no privacy check. Fix: your project sits in the overlap. The one-page defence (Step 8) is only complete when both checks are on it.

• Doing the DPIA after go-live. Symptom: "we'll document it once it's running." Fix: a DPIA exists to catch harm before it reaches people. Do it first; if time is short, phase the rollout (Task 4) rather than skip the check.
