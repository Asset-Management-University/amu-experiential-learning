My single-probe scan of the nozzle weld read clean and the truck is waiting - but if a 1.5 mm crack sat in that root, would one fixed beam ever have reached it?

T1: The Ache

You are in the NDT bay at a busy fabrication shop, a report in your hand and a DN150 nozzle welded into a 25 mm carbon-steel shell on the bench in front of you. The bay smells of couplant gel and warm steel. This vessel goes into service at 19 bar, carrying hot process fluid a few metres from where people work - and your signature on the release is what lets it leave the shop.

Your single-probe UT has just swept the weld: one fixed angle, one beam, a tidy meter reading, clean. The foreman leans round the door - "Is it passed? The truck's waiting." It would be easy to sign on the reading you already have.

But you know this geometry. The sound from a fixed 60-degree probe has to reach the root through steel that changes thickness as the weld curves around the nozzle, and there are angles that beam simply never arrives at. A clean reading is not a statement about the weld. It is a statement about wherever the sound went - and you cannot tell, from the reading, which of those two you are holding.

My screen is clean and I do not know whether that means the weld is clean or that the sound never got there. How do I actually reach the whole volume - and size what I find, rather than just notice it?

Before you read on, decide whether you sign. One clean sweep, one fixed angle, a curving root, a truck at the door, and your name on the release of a vessel that will run at 19 bar near people. Write down what you would do and what you would rest it on. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four ways this gets decided, and what each one actually establishes

• "Sign it - the scan was clean and the procedure was followed." Defensible on paper, and it is what the paperwork is built to support. It rests entirely on an assumption nothing in the reading can confirm: that the beam reached the root.

• "Scan it again from another angle by hand." A real improvement, and much better than signing. It also relies on choosing the right extra angle by judgement, and produces no record of which parts of the volume were covered and which were not.

• "Send it for radiography." Genuinely finds things, and it is poor at exactly what matters here - a tight planar crack lying the wrong way to the beam can be nearly invisible, and it will not give you a through-wall height.

• "Reject it to be safe." Feels responsible, costs a fabrication, and is not a decision about this weld at all - it is what you do when you have no way to see into it.

• The gap under all four is the same. None of them can tell you where the sound went, and none of them returns a size. A disposition needs both: coverage you can show, and a height you can put against the code's number - because "there is something there" and "there is a 1.5 mm planar flaw in the root" lead to opposite decisions.

T2: The Tool in Action

Ultrasonic testing (UT) sends high-frequency sound into steel and listens for echoes - a flaw bounces sound back, and the echo time gives its depth. A single fixed beam is fine for a simple plate weld, but a complex geometry hides angles it never reaches. Phased Array UT steers a fan of angles electronically to see the whole weld as an image; the Total Focusing Method (TFM) focuses at every point for the sharpest picture, to size a flaw exactly. Watch both on the nozzle weld, and watch what the set-up has to do before either can be believed.

Step 1 - Plan the technique, pick the method (ASME V)

Reratile records the technique: carbon steel, 25 mm thick, a set-on DN150 nozzle, full weld-volume and heat-affected-zone coverage, acceptance to the construction code. The sound from a single fixed 60-degree probe cannot reach the root through steel that changes thickness as the weld curves around the nozzle. So she picks a 5 MHz, 64-element array on a 55-degree wedge, a sectorial sweep from 40 to 70 degrees, plus a TFM zone over the root - the region a single beam never reaches.

Step 2 - Calibrate on a representative block

She uses a 25 mm block of the same steel with side-drilled holes at 6, 12 and 19 mm depth and a notch. Velocity: about 5900 m/s longitudinal, 3230 m/s shear. Wedge delay: zeroed against a known reflector. TCG (Time-Corrected Gain): the 6, 12 and 19 mm holes equalised, so a deep flaw does not look smaller than a shallow one. Reference: the 12 mm hole peaks at 80% full screen height, and every later indication is reported relative to it (for example '+2 dB to reference').

Step 3 - Scan, then characterise (find AND size)

Encoded scan of the full nozzle circumference, S-scan (fan image) and C-scan (top-down map) watched, edges over-scanned. The TFM pass over the root shows a small bright spot at 18.5 mm depth. She characterises it: position - the root, 18.5 mm deep, at 210 mm around; length - it persists over 8 mm; through-wall height - by the 6 dB drop edges, 1.5 mm tall; type - sharp and planar at the root line with a tip echo, a root crack (lack of root fusion), not rounded porosity.

Step 4 - Judge against the code, then report

The construction code's flaw rule for 25 mm allows a planar root flaw no taller than 1.0 mm. The found flaw is 1.5 mm - rejectable. Reratile writes the ASME Section V report: technique sheet, calibration records, S/C/TFM images, an indication table (position, length, height, amplitude to reference, type), and the disposition - reject: grind out and re-weld, then re-inspect. The clean-looking single-probe sweep would have passed it.

See what the four steps did. The single-probe sweep read clean and would have let the truck leave; steering the beam into geometry one fixed angle could never reach found a root crack, and then did the thing that actually matters - sized it. 1.5 mm through-wall, against a code limit of 1.0 mm, at a named position. Not "an indication", not "it looked fine": a height, a length and a location, which is the difference between a beep and a disposition somebody can act on. A vessel at 19 bar a few metres from where people stand is no place for "the screen was clean", and a clean screen is only ever a statement about the angles the sound actually reached. The aim is not a passed report. It is a weld that has genuinely been looked into, and a release signed by somebody who can say exactly what they saw and how big it was.

T3: The Blueprint

Four steps, and the second is the one that decides whether the other three mean anything.

  • 1. Plan the technique, pick the method. The written ASME V procedure; phased array and TFM for the geometry.
  • 2. Calibrate on a representative block. Same steel and thickness; velocity, wedge delay, TCG, reference.
  • 3. Scan, then characterise. Encoded scan and images; find AND size the flaw - height, length, type.
  • 4. Judge against the code, then report. Accept or reject on the number; the full auditable record.

The ideas underneath. A weld can hide flaws you cannot see from outside - gas pockets, trapped slag, or a crack where the metal never bonded at all - and on a pressure part a missed one means a leak or a rupture. Phased Array UT uses many small elements fired with tiny delays to steer a fan of sound, so one probe reaches angles a fixed beam never arrives at, and the fan itself is what shows you which parts of the volume were actually covered. The Total Focusing Method goes further, using all the reflected data to build the sharpest image at every point - which is what lets you put a height on a small flaw rather than merely notice it. The work is governed by a formal written procedure (ASME Section V in this trade) and by a representative calibration block - the same steel, the same thickness, reflectors at known depths - because a bad calibration does not fail loudly, it reports confidently and wrongly.

It fits complex or thick geometry where one angle cannot cover the volume and you must characterise rather than merely find. It is over-engineering for a simple thin plate, and coarse-grained or austenitic material can defeat it. And it has one limit that no amount of skill closes: finding a flaw proves that this flaw was findable, and says nothing about the smallest flaw the procedure would reliably catch. That is a separate measurement, it is somebody's job, and a signature that implies it has been made when it has not is the most dangerous line in an inspection report.

Two disciplines keep the release honest. First, characterise, never just detect - and judge on the number, not the screen: a clean-looking sweep proves nothing about the angles the sound never reached, and "it looked fine" is not a disposition, while a through-wall height measured against the code's limit is. Second, calibrate on something that genuinely represents the part, and treat a bad calibration as worse than no inspection. An instrument calibrated on the wrong steel, the wrong thickness or the wrong reflector will report confidently and wrongly, and the report it produces is indistinguishable from a good one - which is exactly why the calibration record, not the images, is the first thing an honest reviewer asks for.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your inspection true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A clean screen is not a safe weld. One fixed beam misses the angles a complex geometry hides. If a single probe cannot reach the root, a clean reading proves nothing about it.

• The block must represent the part. Same steel, same thickness, the curvature where it matters. Calibrate on the wrong block and every confident number after it is wrong.

• Characterise, do not just detect. Find the flaw, then size it - through-wall height, length, type. The code judges a dimension, not an impression.

• Finding a flaw is not evidence that your method finds flaws. It shows this one was findable. What size your procedure reliably catches is a different question, answered by measuring the procedure itself - and never by a good day.

• Never hide the gap. If your reliable floor sits above what the code rejects, say so, and close it or own it. A signature must not paper over a flaw size your method can miss.

You came to the bench with a clean meter reading, a truck waiting, and the quiet knowledge that one fixed beam cannot follow a curving root. You can leave with a weld you have actually seen into: the beam steered where a single angle never reached, a root crack found at 18.5 mm depth, sized at 1.5 mm through-wall against a code limit of 1.0 mm, and written up so that anybody can check every step of how you got there. A clean screen is not a safe weld - it is a statement about the angles the sound reached. That is what a signature on a release should mean: not "it looked clean", but "I reached the whole volume, I sized what was there, and here is the record". (Working with an AMU facilitator?
