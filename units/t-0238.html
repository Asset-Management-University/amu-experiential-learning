What it lets you do

T1: The Ache

You watch the balance sheet drive the wrong decisions, again. The expensive plant carries a big number, so it gets the attention, the protection, the budget. The small components carry almost nothing, so they get none - including the cheap dosing control that is the only thing standing between the town and unsafe water. A costly, idle asset is guarded because it looks valuable; the small, critical part that keeps the whole system running is ignored because it does not.

You know, in your gut, that the asset base is failing the mission in quiet ways - a capability resting on something no one is watching, money tied up in things that serve nothing any more. But when you try to say so, all anyone can point to is the financial value, and by that measure everything looks fine. You lack the language to prove what you can plainly see.

Our asset base is valued by its book value, which drives the wrong decisions and hides where we are quietly failing the mission. How do I assess what the asset base actually contributes to our strategy?

Before you read on, decide. You can see it plainly. The town's entire chlorination capability depends on a dosing controller worth perhaps R40,000 — no spare, no support contract, and nobody watching it, because at that value it appears on no report anybody reads. Meanwhile R6 million of clarifier plant sits carrying full book value and serving a process the works stopped using four years ago. You raised both at the planning meeting and were shown the asset register, where the totals look entirely healthy, and the conversation moved on. You were not being dismissed; that register is the only measure in the room. Write down how you would assess what this asset base actually contributes — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two kinds of value

• “Revalue the assets properly.” Produces a more accurate financial number, and it is the same kind of number. A precisely valued controller is still a R40,000 item that nobody looks at, and precisely valued obsolete plant is still obsolete.

• “Write off the obsolete plant.” Cleans the balance sheet and addresses half of one of your two findings, while the capability sitting on a single unwatched controller is untouched.

• “Add the controller to the critical spares list.” Correct and specific, and you found it by walking around and noticing. What about the next one? Without a way of finding these, you are relying on somebody happening to see it.

• “Raise it as a risk.” Puts it on a register alongside hundreds of entries, where it will be scored and sit. It also frames as a hazard what is really a question about what the works can and cannot do.

• “They are not listening.” They are listening, and answering with the only measure anybody has. The gap is not attention — it is language.

• The root of it is that an asset base holds two very different kinds of value, and they are constantly confused. What it would cost to replace, or fetch to sell, is real and belongs on the balance sheet. But its strategic value is its contribution to the capabilities the organisation needs — and that is what planning turns on, because the whole reason these assets exist is to let the works do something. A pump has a rand value and a strategic value, and they are not the same number, nor even the same kind of number. So run the assessment top-down rather than bottom-up: do not start with the register and total it up — start with what this organisation exists to do, the capabilities that requires, and only then which assets deliver each one. Read that way, the register stops being an inventory and becomes a map of what you can and cannot do — and it produces exactly your two findings, which no financial view can: the trivial thing everything rests on, and the valuable thing that enables nothing.

You value it by a different question: not what it is worth, but what it lets you do. Let us learn the assessment.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you assess and value an asset base by its strategic contribution - starting from the objectives, mapping assets to the capabilities they enable, and surfacing what the balance sheet hides.

1. Two kinds of value

An asset base holds TWO VERY DIFFERENT KINDS OF VALUE, and confusing them is the root of the whole problem. Its FINANCIAL VALUE - what it would cost to replace, or fetch to sell - sits on the balance sheet, and it is real. But its STRATEGIC VALUE is its contribution to the CAPABILITIES the organisation needs to achieve its objectives - and THAT is the value strategic planning turns on, because the entire reason the assets exist is to let the organisation DO something: treat the town's water, keep the plant running, deliver safely and reliably. The discipline calls this THE SHIFT FROM MANAGING ASSETS TO MANAGING FOR THE VALUE THE ASSETS DELIVER. A pump has a rand value AND a strategic value, and they are NOT the same number - nor even the same kind of number. Strategic planning cares about the second.

2. Start from the objectives, not the register

Because strategic value flows from what the organisation is trying to achieve, you assess the base TOP-DOWN, NOT BOTTOM-UP. Do NOT begin with the register and add up what everything is worth; BEGIN WITH THE OBJECTIVES - what this organisation exists to do - and the CAPABILITIES those objectives require. Corathyl's objective is to reliably deliver safe drinking water to the town; that demands capabilities like 'disinfect the water to a safe standard', 'keep supplying during a power failure', 'detect a quality problem before it reaches a tap'. THIS IS THE LINE OF SIGHT FROM THE MISSION TO THE METAL, and it is the only order that works, because A CAPABILITY THE STRATEGY NEEDS IS THE UNIT OF VALUE - and you cannot see whether an asset matters until you know which capability it serves.

3. Map assets to capabilities, and assess how well

Now, CAPABILITY BY CAPABILITY, you map the base: which asset or assets PROVIDE OR ENABLE this capability, and - the real assessment - HOW WELL? For each required capability, give an honest verdict: is it ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED (a sound, sufficient asset stands behind it), AT RISK (it rests on something ageing, single, or stretched), or is there a GAP (no asset adequately provides it at all)? You are no longer tallying rands; YOU ARE ASKING, FOR EACH THING THE ORGANISATION MUST BE ABLE TO DO, WHETHER THE ASSET BASE CAN ACTUALLY DO IT. That capability-by-capability verdict IS the strategic value of the base - a picture of what the organisation can and cannot rely on itself to do. It is an assessment, not an inventory.

4. What the balance sheet hides - three findings

Value the base this way and THREE THINGS APPEAR THAT A FINANCIAL VALUE CAN NEVER SHOW. FIRST, THE UNDERVALUED CRITICAL ASSET: the cheap component a whole capability rests on - Corathyl's modestly-priced dosing-control unit, near-worthless on the books, yet the single thing that keeps the water safe; its financial value says trivial, its strategic value says priceless, and only the capability view sees the gap between them. SECOND, THE STRATEGIC GAP: a capability the objectives require that NO asset adequately provides - Corathyl's 'keep supplying during a power failure' resting on a standby generator too small for the plant. THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT FINDING OF ALL, because it is a risk to the MISSION, not merely to a machine, and it is INVISIBLE to a balance sheet, which only lists what you have, never what you critically LACK. THIRD, THE STRATEGIC SURPLUS: assets that cost money to hold, maintain and insure but serve NO current objective - a decommissioned clarifier line still on the books - candidates to dispose, repurpose or release. A purely financial view gets all three BACKWARDS: it protects the expensive idle clarifier and overlooks the cheap critical control.

5. The output: a guide to invest and divest

The strategic assessment is NOT an accounting exercise filed away; it is the INPUT TO THE STRATEGY AND THE INVESTMENT PLAN. It tells you where to INVEST - shore up the undervalued critical before it fails, close the strategic gap that threatens an objective - and where to DIVEST - release the surplus that serves nothing. THAT IS THE BRIDGE BETWEEN THE ASSET BASE AND THE STRATEGY: a clear, defensible line from what the organisation is trying to BE, through the capabilities it needs, to the assets it should build, keep, or let go. And it is mission-first: AN ORGANISATION'S OBJECTIVES ARE THE REASON IT EXISTS - TO PROVIDE A SERVICE, TO ENSURE SAFETY, TO CREATE A PUBLIC GOOD. THESE ARE HUMAN OUTCOMES. THEREFORE, ASSESSING AN ASSET BASE BY ITS CONTRIBUTION TO THOSE OBJECTIVES IS THE MOST HONEST WAY TO VALUE IT. IT MEASURES THE ASSETS BY THE MISSION THEY SERVE. To value assets by what they let the organisation DO is to value them by WHOM THEY SERVE.

One thing must be said plainly, because it is why this technique exists at all. An organisation's objectives are the reason it exists - to provide a service, to ensure safety, to create a public good. These are human outcomes. Therefore, assessing an asset base by its contribution to those objectives is the most honest way to value it. It measures the assets by the mission they serve. When you value Corathyl's dosing control by what it lets the plant do, you are really valuing it by the town that drinks safe water because of it - and that is why a purely financial view can quietly betray the mission, guarding an expensive idle asset while the humble thing that protects people goes unrecognised. Does it scale? This is how you value any set of resources against a purpose - a team's skills, a budget, your own time. Do not ask only what each is worth in the abstract; ask what capability the mission requires, which resource provides it, and how well - and you will find the same three things: the modest resource that quietly carries everything, the gap where a needed capability has nothing behind it, and the expensive thing you keep out of habit that serves no current purpose. Value by contribution to the mission, not by price tag, and you finally see what to protect, what to build, and what to let go.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. A community cold-storage facility has one objective: to provide reliable, affordable cold storage so local small-scale farmers do not lose their produce to spoilage. Its main assets:

  • A large main cold room - recently built, high book value, in good condition.
  • The single refrigeration compressor that cools the main room - mid-life, no backup, moderate book value.
  • A small standby generator - old, and rated for the lights and office only, not the compressor.
  • A second, older cold room - built for a cannery contract that ended two years ago; now empty, still powered, maintained and insured.
  • A delivery vehicle - reliable, in service.
  1. Undervalued critical. Which asset has a modest book value but carries a whole capability? Say why in one sentence.
  2. Strategic gap. Which capability the objective requires has no adequate asset behind it? One sentence.
  3. Strategic surplus. Which asset costs money but serves no current objective? One sentence.
  4. Recommend. Based on your assessment, what is your single most important one-sentence recommendation for the next investment cycle?

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the undervalued critical: the single refrigeration compressor - moderate on the books, but the entire "keep the produce cold" capability rests on it, and if it fails the whole facility fails its one purpose. The expensive main cold room is worth little without it.

Second, the strategic gap: "keep the produce cold through a power failure" - the objective demands reliability, but the standby generator cannot run the compressor, so a power cut spoils everything. A needed capability with no adequate asset behind it, invisible on the balance sheet.

Third, the strategic surplus: the second, empty cold room - built for a contract that ended, now serving no current objective yet still costing power, maintenance and insurance. A candidate to repurpose (extra capacity as farmers grow?) or release.

Fourth, the recommendation: something like "Before anything else, close the power-failure gap - fund a generator able to run the compressor (partly from releasing the idle second cold room), because a single power cut or compressor failure currently destroys the whole mission." The mark of a real answer is that it protects the capability the mission depends on, funded partly by the surplus - a decision the book value would never have surfaced.

T5: The Guardrails

A balance sheet tells you what your assets are worth; a strategic assessment tells you what they let your organisation be. Value the base by the capabilities it enables, not by its book value.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Valuing the base only by book or replacement value. That number drives you to protect the expensive and ignore the critical. Value by contribution to capability instead.

• Assessing bottom-up from the register. Adding up what everything is worth never tells you what the base lets you do. Start from the objectives and the capabilities they require.

• Ignoring the cheap critical asset. A low book value hides a high strategic value. Find the modest component a whole capability rests on and protect it accordingly.

• Missing the strategic gap. A balance sheet lists what you have, never what you critically lack. Name the capabilities with no adequate asset - they are risks to the mission.

• Keeping the strategic surplus because it looks like an asset. An idle thing that serves no objective is a cost, not a value. Repurpose or release it.

• Confusing one asset's criticality with the whole base's contribution. The assessment is the base against every required capability, not a single asset in isolation.

• Treating this as accounting, not strategy. The output is a decision about what to build, keep and let go - the input to the strategy, not a figure for the books.

• Forgetting who the assets serve. The mission is human - safe water, preserved food, safe workers. Value the base by the people it lets you serve, or you will value the wrong things.

The balance sheet was driving you to protect the costly and idle while the cheap and critical went unwatched, because it measures the wrong thing. So measure the right one: start from what the organisation exists to do, name the capabilities its objectives require, and assess the asset base by how well it delivers each - adequately, at risk, or not at all. That reveals what the books hide: the undervalued critical asset to protect, the strategic gap to close, and the strategic surplus to release, giving you a defensible plan for what to build, keep and let go. A balance sheet tells you what your assets are worth; a strategic assessment tells you what they let your organisation be - and an asset base's true value is not what it is worth, but what it lets you do for the people you serve.
