Step 1 of 5

Eight fitters, fourteen jobs

T1: The Ache

The four-day shutdown plan is approved. The critical path is clean, every task is sequenced, the bar chart on the wall is a beautiful staircase of work marching from Monday to Thursday. Everyone signed it off. It is, by every test anyone applied, a finished plan.

Then Tuesday morning arrives. Fourteen separate jobs need a fitter today. You have eight fitters standing in the yard. Six jobs cannot start, the critical path slips before the first spanner turns, and the overtime and the scramble begin. And on Thursday - you will see it later - half the crew stands around with almost nothing to do.

The plan did not fail on Tuesday. The plan was impossible on Monday, and every day before that, and nobody saw it - because the only picture anyone ever drew was tasks against time, and that picture cannot show you the one thing that just broke you: whether the people to do the work actually exist on the day the work is due.

My schedule is perfect on time and impossible in people. How do I see the resource problem before the shutdown, while I can still fix it - instead of discovering it at seven on Tuesday morning?

Before you read on, decide. The schedule was built properly, the dependencies are right, the critical path is sound, and it was checked and signed off. At seven on Tuesday morning it collapses: the day's tasks need fourteen fitters and you have eight. On Thursday and Friday half the crew stands around with almost nothing to do. Everybody on site experiences Tuesday as bad luck. It was not — the plan was impossible on Monday, and every day before that, and nobody saw it. Write down how you would have seen this while you could still fix it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question the schedule never asked

• “Check the schedule more carefully next time.” It was checked carefully. The overload is not an error in the schedule — it is invisible in that kind of picture, because Tuesday's demand is spread across a dozen bars in a dozen rows and nobody adds them up.

• “Bring in contract fitters for the peak.” The right remedy applied on the wrong day. Discovered on Tuesday it costs premium rates and induction time; discovered three weeks out it is a phone call.

• “Add float to the tasks.” Float addresses time risk. Tuesday was not late — Tuesday was overloaded, and a task with more float still needs the same number of people on the day it is done.

• “Ask the supervisors if it looks doable.” Worth doing and unreliable: each supervisor sees their own tasks and none of them sees the total. That is exactly how fourteen people got asked for without anybody noticing.

• “Work overtime on Tuesday.” Eight people working longer are still eight people, and the tasks that clashed were concurrent rather than sequential. It also has Thursday's idle crew paying for Tuesday's crush.

• Here is the trap in one line: a schedule answers when, and never whether you can. A critical-path schedule genuinely tells you the earliest the job can finish and which tasks cannot slip — and says nothing about whether the fitters, the crane or the certified welder exist on the days those tasks fall due. Time-feasible is not resource-feasible, and a plan can be perfect on time and impossible in people at the very same moment, looking complete and sign-off ready either way. That is why the scramble always feels like bad luck rather than something foreseeable. So draw the resource picture beside the time picture: for each constrained resource, the demand day by day, totalled — and Tuesday's fourteen against eight leaps off the page weeks in advance, while it can still be fixed cheaply. What you then do depends on which constraint is real. If the end date can move, spread the work until demand fits. If the date is fixed, rearrange within the float you already have. Either way you are fixing it in the office in September rather than in the yard at seven on Tuesday.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you turn a time-perfect schedule into a plan that is actually possible - seeing the hidden question, drawing the resource picture, levelling or smoothing for your real constraint, planning the geography, and holding an honest plan.

1. See the hidden question - a schedule says WHEN, not WHETHER YOU CAN

START WITH THE THING THAT MAKES THE TUESDAY DISASTER INVISIBLE UNTIL TUESDAY: A SCHEDULE ANSWERS ONE QUESTION, AND IT IS NOT THE QUESTION THAT BREAKS YOU. A critical-path schedule is a genuinely powerful thing, and it tells you exactly one thing - given how the tasks depend on each other, what is the fastest the job can finish and which tasks cannot slip. THAT IS THE 'WHEN'. But it says NOTHING about the 'WHETHER YOU CAN' - whether the fitters, the crane, the certified welder to do those tasks actually EXIST on the days the tasks fall due. So here is the trap in one line: TIME-FEASIBLE IS NOT RESOURCE-FEASIBLE, AND A PLAN CAN BE PERFECT ON TIME AND IMPOSSIBLE IN PEOPLE AT THE VERY SAME MOMENT - and it will look complete, sign-off ready, beautiful on the wall, either way. The bar chart of tasks against time is physically incapable of showing you an overload, because the resource total for a given day is spread across a dozen different bars in a dozen different rows; nobody adds them up, so nobody sees that Tuesday quietly asks for fourteen people when eight exist. This is why the scramble always feels like bad luck on the day and never like a mistake in the plan: everyone was reading the only picture they had, and it was the wrong picture for the question that mattered. THE WHOLE UNIT IS ABOUT DRAWING THE PICTURE THAT ANSWERS THE OTHER QUESTION - is this plan actually possible in the resources I have - AND DRAWING IT EARLY, on a quiet afternoon a fortnight out, while there is still time to make it possible instead of discovering it is not at seven on Tuesday morning with the plant already down. A SCHEDULE IS NOT A PLAN UNTIL IT IS POSSIBLE FOR THE PEOPLE WHO MUST DO THE WORK.

2. Draw the resource histogram - demand as bars, availability as a line

THE FIX IS ALMOST EMBARRASSINGLY SIMPLE: DRAW A DIFFERENT GRAPH. Instead of tasks down the side and time across the bottom, put TIME across the bottom and, going UP, the QUANTITY OF ONE RESOURCE the plan demands in each period - fourteen fitters Tuesday, eight Wednesday, two Thursday - as a bar for each day. Then draw ONE HORIZONTAL LINE straight across the whole chart: HOW MANY OF THAT RESOURCE YOU ACTUALLY HAVE. That line is the truth the schedule kept hiding. Now just look at the shape. EVERY BAR POKING ABOVE THE LINE IS A PROMISE THE PLAN CANNOT KEEP - AN OVERLOAD, a day demanding more people than exist. EVERY GAP BELOW THE LINE IS PEOPLE YOU ARE PAYING TO STAND IDLE - A TROUGH, wasted capacity you could have used to relieve a peak somewhere else. A well-built plan has bars that sit close under the line, day after day; a broken plan has a mountain on Tuesday and a desert on Thursday, and now you can SEE it. CRUCIAL POINT: DRAW A SEPARATE HISTOGRAM FOR EACH RESOURCE THAT IS GENUINELY SCARCE - the fitters, yes, but also the single mobile crane, the one certified welder, the confined-space team - because a plan can be perfectly comfortable in headcount and still be sunk by the one crane that three jobs need on the same afternoon. The plan fails on the resource you did not chart. AND THE POINT OF THE HISTOGRAM IS NOT THAT IT SOLVES THE PROBLEM - IT DOES SOMETHING MORE IMPORTANT FIRST: IT MAKES THE PROBLEM VISIBLE WHILE YOU CAN STILL ACT ON IT. An overload you find a fortnight out is a scheduling puzzle you solve at a desk. The same overload found on the morning is a crisis you survive. Same fact, wildly different cost, and the only difference is whether you drew the picture in time.

3. Level or smooth - and know which your constraint demands

ONCE THE OVERLOAD IS VISIBLE YOU HAVE TWO TOOLS, AND THE SINGLE MOST COMMON MISTAKE IS USING THE WRONG ONE, BECAUSE THEY ANSWER TO TWO DIFFERENT MASTERS. BEFORE YOU TOUCH THE PLAN, ASK ONE QUESTION: WHAT IS THE HARD CONSTRAINT HERE - THE PEOPLE, OR THE DATE? RESOURCE LEVELLING IS FOR WHEN THE RESOURCE IS THE HARD LIMIT. You cannot conjure more fitters, so you move work OUT until the plan fits the crew you genuinely have - and doing that MAY PUSH THE END DATE OUT, AND MAY EVEN CHANGE WHICH TASKS ARE CRITICAL, and you SAY SO PLAINLY, because the only alternative is a plan secretly built on people who do not exist. Levelling answers the honest question: with the crew I actually have, when does this REALLY finish? RESOURCE SMOOTHING IS FOR WHEN THE END DATE IS THE HARD LIMIT - a shutdown window that truly cannot move because the plant must restart Friday and every hour past it costs a fortune. Here you are FORBIDDEN to move the finish, so instead you shuffle jobs ONLY WITHIN THE FLOAT THEY ALREADY HAVE - the slack the critical path handed you for free - to shave the peaks and fill the troughs. Because you only ever move work inside its own float, SMOOTHING DOES NOT MOVE THE END DATE AND DOES NOT CHANGE THE CRITICAL PATH; it just makes the resource profile flatter and saner within the window you were always going to keep. THE DISCIPLINE: if the date is fixed, SMOOTH FIRST, using every scrap of real float. And here is the honest part - IF SMOOTHING WITHIN YOUR REAL FLOAT STILL LEAVES A BAR ABOVE THE LINE, THAT IS NOT A FAILURE OF THE PLAN. IT IS THE PLAN TELLING YOU THE TRUTH: this window needs more people or more days, and no amount of rearranging inside it will change that. The error to fear is the quiet one - smoothing PAST the float you actually have, dragging critical-path work around to flatten a peak on the chart while silently breaking the very end date you were protecting. Move only what truly has slack; when the slack runs out, the answer is resource or time, not pretending.

4. Plan the geography - sequence the work by area

THERE IS A RESOURCE THE HISTOGRAM DOES NOT SHOW AND THE SCHEDULE NEVER ASKS ABOUT, AND ON A COMPLEX JOB IT QUIETLY EATS A WHOLE SHIFT: PLACE. A plan can be perfect in time AND perfect in people and still bleed hours, because it sends the crew from a valve on the far west boundary to a pump on the far east and back again, four times a day - and EVERY ONE OF THOSE CROSSINGS IS REAL, PAID TIME: fifteen minutes each, multiplied by the whole crew, multiplied by every day of the job, all of it spent WALKING rather than WORKING. GEOGRAPHICAL, OR AREA, PLANNING IS THE ANSWER: SEQUENCE AND BATCH THE WORK BY LOCATION. Finish everything the crew can do in the west zone while they are standing in the west, THEN move them once to the east - one crossing in the day instead of four - and you have converted walking time back into wrench time without hiring a soul. Geography also kills a quieter waste that pure scheduling creates: TWO CREWS SENT TO THE SAME SMALL SPACE AT THE SAME TIME, where they do not double the pace at all - they queue for the same scaffold, trip over each other's isolation, and block each other, so the plan that looks twice as fast on paper runs at half speed on the ground. Scheduling space is as real as scheduling time. AND ALL OF THIS STARTS WITH A PHYSICAL WALKDOWN - actually going to the job and looking at it - BECAUSE THE DRAWING DOES NOT SHOW YOU THE SEIZED VALVE, THE ACCESS THAT IS NOT THERE, OR THE SCAFFOLD THAT HAS TO GO UP BEFORE ANYONE CAN EVEN START. The best duration and resource estimates come from someone who has stood in front of the actual asset, not from someone reading a plan of it. On a complex job, the plant's own geography is a constraint you schedule for on purpose, or one that ambushes you for free.

5. Hold an honest plan - the histogram is evidence, never a stick

NOW THE LINE THAT KEEPS THIS WHOLE TECHNIQUE HONEST, BECAUSE A RESOURCE HISTOGRAM CAN BE MISREAD AS A STICK - 'THE BAR SAYS THE CREW SHOULD BE DOING MORE' - AND IT MUST NOT BE. A PEAK ABOVE THE LINE IS NOT THE CREW FAILING. IT IS THE PLAN ASKING FOR PEOPLE WHO DO NOT EXIST. THE PLANNER'S FIRST DUTY IS TO MAKE THE PLAN MATCH REALITY, NOT TO FORCE THE CREW TO MATCH AN IMPOSSIBLE PLAN. When the histogram shows an overload you cannot smooth away, there are exactly three honest responses - CHANGE THE PLAN (level it), ADD RESOURCE (a contractor, a borrowed crew, one welder for one day), or MOVE THE END DATE - and exactly one dishonest one: ignore the bar, bank quietly on unpaid overtime and heroics, sign the plan off as finished, and blame the fitters when the heroics run out. AN HONEST PLAN THAT SAYS 'THIS WILL TAKE THREE MORE DAYS' OR 'THIS NEEDS FOUR MORE FITTERS' IS NOT A FAILURE TO BE HIDDEN. IT IS THE EVIDENCE A SOUND DECISION REQUIRES, AND PRODUCING IT IS THE CORE OF THE PLANNER'S JOB. A planner who buries an overload to make a plan look approvable has not saved anyone - they have simply moved the disaster from the meeting room, where it could still have been solved with a phone call and a budget, to the plant floor at two in the morning, where it cannot be solved at all, only survived. The histogram turns an argument about blame ('why is the crew so slow?') into a decision about resources ('the plan needs eight and we sent six'), and that is the most valuable thing it does. THE FINISHED PLAN IS THE ONE THAT IS POSSIBLE IN PEOPLE AND IN PLACE - and where it genuinely cannot be, the honest, evidenced statement of what it needs is not the planner failing at the job. It IS the job.

T3: The Blueprint

A schedule tells you when; it does not tell you whether you can. A schedule is not a plan until it is possible for the people who must do the work. Five questions:

  • Is it time-feasible, or resource-feasible? Different questions. The critical path optimises time and hides the resource question completely. Time-perfect and impossible-in-people can be the same plan.
  • Have I drawn the resource picture? The resource histogram - demand per day as bars, availability as a line. Peaks above the line are overloads; gaps below are idle troughs.
  • Do I level, or do I smooth? Level when people are the hard limit; smooth when the date is. Levelling moves the end date; smoothing only moves work within its float. Do the one your real constraint demands.
  • Have I planned the geography? Sequence the work by area. An hour spent walking across the plant is an hour not turning a spanner - and two crews in one small space just block each other.
  • Is the plan honest? A resource-feasible, area-sequenced plan - and where it will not fit, the histogram is the evidence to add people or move the date, never a stick for the crew.

The hidden question a schedule cannot answer. A critical-path schedule is a magnificent thing and it answers exactly one question: given how the tasks depend on each other, what is the fastest the job can finish and which tasks cannot slip? It says nothing - nothing - about whether the bodies to do those tasks exist on the days they fall due. So a schedule can be flawless on time and impossible in people at the same instant, and it will look complete either way. Time-feasible is not resource-feasible, and the bar chart of tasks against time is physically incapable of telling the two apart. This is why the Tuesday disaster is invisible right up until Tuesday: everyone was reading the only picture they had, and it was the wrong picture for the question that mattered.

Draw the second picture - the resource histogram. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: draw a different graph. Instead of tasks down the side and time across the bottom, put time across the bottom and, going up, the quantity of one resource the plan demands each period - fourteen fitters on Tuesday, eight on Wednesday, two on Thursday - as a bar per day. Then draw one horizontal line across the whole chart: how many of that resource you actually have. Now look. Every bar poking above the line is a promise the plan cannot keep - an overload. Every gap below the line is people you are paying to stand idle - a trough. Draw it for each resource that is genuinely scarce - the fitters, the single crane, the one certified welder - because the plan can be comfortable in everything except the one thing that sinks it. The histogram does not solve the problem; it does something more important first - it makes the problem visible while you can still act on it, on a quiet afternoon a fortnight out, instead of at seven on Tuesday morning with the plant down.

Level or smooth - and know which you are doing. Once the overload is visible you have two tools, and they are not interchangeable - they answer to two different masters. Ask first: what is the hard constraint here - the people, or the date? Resource levelling is for when the resource is the hard limit. You cannot conjure more fitters, so you move work out until the plan fits the crew you truly have - and that may push the end date out, and may even change which tasks are critical, and you say so plainly, because the alternative is a plan built on people who do not exist. Levelling answers: with the crew I actually have, when does this really finish? Resource smoothing is for when the end date is the hard limit - a shutdown window that genuinely cannot move because the plant must restart Friday. Here you may not move the finish, so you shuffle jobs only within the float they already have - the slack the critical path handed you free - to shave the peaks and fill the troughs without touching the end date or the critical path. The classic error is to reach for the wrong one: to quietly smooth past the float you actually have and silently break the date, or to level and slip the restart when a contractor for one day would have held it. Smooth first if the date is fixed; and if smoothing within the real float still leaves you over the line, that is not a failure of the plan - it is the plan telling you the truth, that the window needs more people or more days.

Plan the geography - the third axis nobody schedules. There is a resource the histogram does not show and the schedule never asks about: place. A plan can be perfect in time and in people and still bleed hours, because it sends the crew from a valve on the west boundary to a pump on the east and back again four times a day, and every one of those crossings is real time - fifteen minutes each, times the whole crew, times every day - paid to walk, not to work. Sequence and batch the work by area: finish the west-side jobs while the crew is in the west, then move once to the east - one crossing instead of four. Geography also stops the quieter waste of two crews sent to the same small space, where they queue for the same scaffold and block each other. And it starts with a physical walkdown - going and looking at the actual job - because the drawing does not show you the seized valve, the missing access, or the scaffold that has to go up first. Travel time and access are real time; a plan that ignores where the work is will lose a shift to the plant's own geography.

One honest boundary. The planner's job is to make the plan match reality, not to make reality match an impossible plan. A peak on the histogram is not the crew failing - it is the plan asking for people who do not exist, and the honest response is to change the plan, add resource, or move the date, never to bank on heroics and unpaid overtime and then blame the fitters when the heroics run out. An honest plan that says "this needs three more days" or "this needs four more fitters" is not a failure to hide - it is the evidence a sound decision requires, and producing it is the core of the job. A planner who hides an overload to make a plan look approvable has not saved anyone; they have moved the disaster from the meeting room, where it could have been solved, to the plant floor, where it cannot.

Does it scale? A student with four assignments due the same Friday has a time-feasible plan - all four can be done by Friday - and a resource-impossible one, because there are only so many hours in a night and they are the only resource. Draw the hours-per-day against the hours-you-have and the impossible Thursday appears; then either start earlier (smooth into the free days you were wasting) or accept one goes in late and say so early (level). The shutdown and the study week fail and succeed on exactly the same picture.

T4: Your Turn

Here is a real one. You are planning a four-day shutdown that must restart Friday - the end date cannot move. You have eight fitters available each day. The approved, time-feasible schedule demands this many fitters per day:

DayMonTueWedThu
Fitters needed71482
Fitters available8888

You are also told: three of Tuesday's jobs - worth 5 fitter-days between them - have enough float to move to Thursday. The rest of Tuesday's work is on the critical path and cannot move. Separately, the plan currently sends the crew back and forth across the plant, crossing between the west valves and the east pumps about four times a day.

  1. Read the histogram. Which day is the overload and by how much? Which day is the idle trough, and how many fitter-days are wasted there?
  2. Smooth or level? The date is fixed - name which tool that points you to, and why the other one is wrong here.
  3. Do the move. Shift the 5 floated fitter-days from Tuesday to Thursday and write the new four-day profile. Does it now fit under the line of 8 everywhere? If not, exactly where and by how much is it still over?
  4. The honest sentence. For whatever will not fit, write the one sentence you would say to management - using the histogram as the evidence - and name the two legitimate ways to close it.
  5. The geography. Re-sequence so the crew crosses the plant once a day instead of four times. If each avoided crossing saves about 15 minutes for each of 8 fitters, roughly how many fitter-hours does that recover across the four days?

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the overload is Tuesday: 14 needed against 8 available, a peak of 6 fitters over the line. The trough is Thursday: 2 against 8, so 6 fitter-days sit idle. The total work is 7 + 14 + 8 + 2 = 31 fitter-days, and it stays 31 no matter how you reshape it - you are moving work, not deleting it.

Second, smooth. The end date is the hard constraint, so you may not move the finish; smoothing shifts work only within its float and leaves the date and the critical path untouched, which is exactly what a fixed window allows. Levelling would move the restart - the one thing you have been told you cannot do - so it is the wrong tool for this constraint (it would be the right tool if the crew, not the date, were fixed).

Third, move the 5 floated fitter-days from Tuesday to Thursday: Mon 7, Tue 9, Wed 8, Thu 7 (and 7 + 9 + 8 + 7 = 31, conserved). It does not fully fit: Tuesday is still 1 fitter over the line of 8, because the last of Tuesday's work is on the critical path and has no float to move. Smoothing took you as far as the real float allows, and no further - honestly.

Fourth, the honest sentence is something like: "After smoothing every job that has float, Tuesday is still short by one fitter against the critical-path work - here is the histogram; we either bring in one contract fitter for Tuesday, or the restart moves to Saturday." The two legitimate closes are add the one resource (one contractor for one day) or move the date (level the restart by a day) - never assume the crew will absorb it in unpaid overtime and call the plan finished.

Fifth, the geography: you cut from four crossings a day to one, saving three crossings a day. Three crossings times 15 minutes is 45 minutes per fitter per day; across 8 fitters that is 6 fitter-hours a day; across 4 days, about 24 fitter-hours - roughly three fitter-days recovered, simply by finishing one area before crossing to the next. That is real capacity found without hiring anyone, and it is exactly the sort of slack that might have covered your leftover Tuesday fitter.

T5: The Guardrails

A schedule that looks finished and cannot be resourced is not a plan. It is a trap laid for the people who have to execute it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Trusting a time-only schedule. The critical path answers "how fast," never "with whom." A signed-off Gantt chart can still be impossible in people. Always draw the resource picture too.

• Never drawing the histogram. If you cannot see resource demand against availability, you cannot see the overload until the day it lands. The picture is fifteen minutes; the Tuesday scramble is a lost shift.

• Charting only the obvious resource. Draw the histogram for every scarce resource - the crane, the certified welder, the confined-space team - not just headcount. The plan sinks on the one you did not draw.

• Levelling when you should smooth, or the reverse. Ask what the hard constraint is. Fixed date means smooth within float; fixed crew means level and move the date. Using the wrong tool either breaks the date silently or slips it needlessly.

• Smoothing past the float you actually have. Shuffling critical-path work to flatten a peak does not smooth the plan - it quietly breaks the end date while the chart still looks fine. Only move work that genuinely has slack.

• Hiding an overload behind assumed overtime. Banking on heroics and unpaid hours is not a plan, it is a debt the crew pays. Show the overload; do not bury it.

• Blaming the crew for a peak the plan created. A bar above the line is the plan asking for people who do not exist - not the fitters going slow. Fix the plan, do not push the people.

• Ignoring geography. A plan perfect in time and people still bleeds a shift if the crew walks the plant four times a day. Sequence by area; move once, not constantly.

• Two crews, one space. Sending two teams to the same small area does not double the pace - they queue for the same scaffold and block each other. Geography schedules space as well as time.

• Skipping the walkdown. The drawing does not show the seized valve or the missing access. Go and look before you commit the plan; the job on the ground is never quite the job on paper.

The shutdown plan was perfect on time and a lie in people, and nobody saw it because the only picture anyone drew was tasks against time. Draw the second picture - the resource histogram, demand against availability - and the impossible Tuesday and the idle Thursday appear while you can still fix them; then choose the honest tool for your real constraint, smoothing within float when the date is fixed and levelling the date when the crew is; sequence the work by area so the crew turns spanners instead of walking the plant; and where the plan still will not fit, hold up the histogram as the evidence that the window needs more days or more people. The planner's job is to make the plan match reality, not to make reality match an impossible plan - and a plan that honestly says it needs more is the most useful thing you will ever hand a manager. Next time a schedule lands on your desk signed off and beautiful, draw the resource picture before you trust it - and find out, on a quiet afternoon, whether the plan is possible, while there is still time to make it so.
