The wrong mountain

T1: The Ache

You run a water utility, and by every operational measure your teams are excellent. The maintenance crews hit their schedules. The reactive jobs close on time. Costs are held. Everyone is working harder than ever, and every target on every list is green.

And the one thing that could sink the whole utility - the ageing bulk main that carries the town's entire supply, corroding quietly toward a failure that would leave the town dry for weeks - is not on anyone's list. It is nobody's target, because it is not this week's work, or this month's, and the lists are full of this week's work. Your organisation is climbing a mountain, hard and efficiently and with real skill.

You have a sinking feeling it is the wrong mountain.

We do everything on our list well, and the thing that actually matters goes unaddressed. How do I lift the organisation from working hard on its tasks to choosing the right direction in the first place?

Before you read on, decide. Every list in this works is full and every list is being cleared. The teams are skilled, the targets are met, the reporting is green. And the raw-water main that carries the town's entire supply — corroding quietly toward a failure that would leave people without water for weeks — is on nobody's list. Not refused, not deferred: never a target, because it is not this week's work or this month's, and the lists are full of this week's work. Meanwhile the catchment is changing and nobody has looked at that either. Write down how you would lift this organisation from working hard to choosing a direction — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the word that separates a strategy from a wish

• “Put the main on the list.” Fixes the one thing you happened to notice. Next year there will be another item nobody put on a list, discovered the same way — by somebody's sinking feeling rather than by any mechanism.

• “Write a strategy document.” Very likely produces a plan or a wish. A list of activities is a to-do list with a cover, and a statement about being a leading utility cannot be wrong and therefore cannot guide anything.

• “Set more ambitious targets.” Targets on the same axes make the organisation climb faster. If it is the wrong mountain, speed is not the friend you want.

• “Do a risk assessment of everything.” Genuinely useful and it produces a register, which is a list of things to worry about rather than a decision about where to go. The main would appear on it — alongside four hundred other entries.

• “People are working flat out — we cannot take on more.” True, and it names the real constraint: the lists are already full, so anything genuinely new can only arrive by displacing something. That is the decision nobody is making.

• Start by refusing the two things strategy is usually mistaken for. It is not a plan — a list of activities to get through — and it is not a wish, a statement of what you would like to be true. It is a set of deliberate choices about where to go and how to get there, made by reading the world outside and the capability inside. And the word that separates a strategy from a wish is choice: to have one is to decide you will do this and not that. Here is the part everybody avoids — a strategy that tries to do everything has chosen nothing, because choosing everything leaves you exactly where you are, with a to-do list instead of a direction. Two things follow. The choice comes from reading the environment — what is changing outside, like that catchment, and what you genuinely can and cannot do inside — rather than from preference. And it must connect downward to the work, in both directions: from the chosen direction to what somebody does on Monday, and from any item of work back to the direction it serves. A strategy that never reaches the lists is a document; lists that never connect upward are exactly the busy competence you have now.

Here is what strategy really is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you choose a direction and give it a line of sight to the work - seeing that strategy is choice, reading the environment, running the cycle and the golden thread, knowing the components of a plan, and telling strategy from operations.

1. Strategy is deliberate choice - not a plan, not a wish

START BY REFUSING THE TWO THINGS STRATEGY IS MOST OFTEN MISTAKEN FOR. IT IS NOT A PLAN - a list of activities to get done. AND IT IS NOT A WISH - a statement of what we would like to be true (to be the best, to grow, to be world-class). STRATEGY IS A SET OF DELIBERATE CHOICES ABOUT WHERE TO GO AND HOW TO GET THERE, MADE BY READING THE WORLD OUTSIDE AND THE CAPABILITY INSIDE, TO TURN WHAT THE ORGANISATION IS FOR INTO WHAT IT WILL DO. The heart of it, the word that separates a strategy from a wish, is CHOICE: to have a strategy is to decide we will do THIS and NOT THAT - and here is the hard part people avoid, A STRATEGY THAT TRIES TO DO EVERYTHING HAS CHOSEN NOTHING, because choosing everything is the same as choosing nothing and leaves you with a to-do list, not a direction. The principles of strategic management are a short, sturdy set that the rest of this unit unpacks. IT STARTS FROM PURPOSE - the mission and objectives, what the organisation exists to achieve. IT READS THE ENVIRONMENT - what is changing outside, and what the organisation can and cannot actually do inside. IT MAKES A REAL CHOICE - a this-not-that that commits and therefore gives something up. AND IT RUNS AS A CYCLE, not a document written once and filed and forgotten. Get those four right and you have a strategy; miss the choice and you have a wish; miss the environment and you have a gamble; miss the cycle and you have a fossil. THE SINGLE HARDEST TRUTH: STRATEGY IS NOT ABOUT CLIMBING THE MOUNTAIN EFFICIENTLY; IT IS ABOUT CHOOSING THE RIGHT MOUNTAIN TO CLIMB IN THE FIRST PLACE.

2. Read the environment - outside and inside

A CHOICE MADE BLIND IS A GAMBLE; A CHOICE MADE FROM AN HONEST READING OF THE SITUATION IS A STRATEGY - so before the choice comes the SITUATION ANALYSIS, and it has TWO HALVES that are equally necessary and neither of which can be skipped. THE EXTERNAL ENVIRONMENT: what is changing beyond your walls - the threats and the opportunities in the climate, the demand, the regulation, the technology, the economy - the forces that will help or hurt you whether or not you choose to notice them. You scan these because a strategy formed in ignorance of the outside world is planning for a world that does not exist. THE INTERNAL ENVIRONMENT: what your organisation can and cannot actually do - its genuine strengths and its honest weaknesses, its capacity, its money, its data, its people, its condition. A common and useful tool brings the two halves together into STRENGTHS, WEAKNESSES, OPPORTUNITIES AND THREATS, matching what you have against what the world is doing. AND HERE IS THE DISCIPLINE MOST OFTEN SKIPPED, AND THE ONE THE ASSET MANAGER GUARDS: AMBITION MUST BE CHECKED AGAINST CAPABILITY. It is easy and pleasant to set a bold direction; it is the internal reading that tells you whether you can actually do it. A strategy that assumes crews, budget, data and condition you do not have is not brave and it is not visionary - IT IS A WISH, and it will fail on first contact with the reality it never bothered to read. The external scan keeps you relevant; the internal scan keeps you honest; you need both, and a strategy built on only one of them is half-blind.

3. The cycle, and the golden thread

TWO IDEAS HERE, AND BOTH FIGHT THE INSTINCT TO TREAT STRATEGY AS A ONE-OFF DOCUMENT. FIRST, STRATEGIC MANAGEMENT IS A CYCLE, NOT A DOCUMENT WRITTEN ONCE AND FILED. The standard cycle has four activities: you SCAN the environment (box 2), you FORMULATE the strategy (make the choices), you IMPLEMENT it (align the structure, resources and processes to the choices and turn them into action), and you EVALUATE it against what actually happened - AND THEN YOU GO ROUND AGAIN, because what was true last year may not be true now, and the whole point of scanning and evaluating is to catch the moment the world has moved out from under your plan. A strategy you never revisit is not stable, it is stale. SECOND, THE GOLDEN THREAD - the way a strategy earns its keep in asset management specifically. It is the LINE OF SIGHT FROM THE TOP DOWN: the ORGANISATION objectives set the ASSET-MANAGEMENT objectives, which set the PLANS, which set the WORK - an unbroken thread from the boardroom purpose to the spanner in the crew's hand. Why it matters: it means EVERY TASK CAN BE TRACED BACK TO A CORPORATE PURPOSE, so no asset activity is an ORPHAN - work done out of habit or local preference for reasons no one can connect to why the organisation exists. When the thread is whole, a crew member can ask why am I doing this and get an answer that reaches all the way up; when it is broken, the organisation spends money and effort on assets in ways that serve no stated aim. BUILDING the asset-management strategy documents that carry this thread - the strategic asset-management plan, the asset-management plan - is its own craft with its own techniques; the PRINCIPLE they enact is this one: a living cycle at the top, connected by an unbroken thread all the way down to the work.

4. The components of a strategic plan

WHATEVER AN ORGANISATION CALLS ITS STRATEGY DOCUMENT, A REAL ONE HAS RECOGNISABLE COMPONENTS - and knowing them is what lets you COMPARE one plan against another and, more usefully, SPOT WHAT A PLAN IS MISSING. There are four groups. WHERE WE ARE GOING: the MISSION (the fundamental purpose, why the organisation exists), the VISION AND VALUES (what it aspires to become and the principles that guide how it behaves getting there), and the OBJECTIVES (the measurable targets that translate that purpose into outcomes you can actually check - not to be the best, but to reduce main failures by half in five years). WHERE WE ARE: the SITUATION ANALYSIS, the honest evidence base of the external environment and the internal capability from box 2 - the plan grounded in reality, not hope. HOW WE WILL GET THERE: the STRATEGIC CHOICES and priorities - the direction, the this-not-that, the actual strategy. AND HOW WE WILL DO AND MEASURE IT: the INITIATIVES and programmes, the RESOURCES committed to them, and the INDICATORS that will show whether it is working, so the plan can be steered rather than just admired. NOW THE DIAGNOSTIC POWER OF KNOWING THESE: THE COMMONEST AND MOST DANGEROUS KIND OF STRATEGIC PLAN IS ONE THAT IS ALL DESTINATION - full of mission and vision and inspiring objectives, but MISSING THE MIDDLE TWO: no honest look at where the organisation actually stands, and no real choices about how to get there. It reads beautifully in a boardroom and cannot be executed on a Monday, because it never touched the ground. When you are handed a strategy, run down the four components: if where-we-are and the real choices are thin or absent, you are holding a wish, however grand its language.

5. Strategic vs operational - direction versus execution

HERE IS THE SHARPEST IDEA IN THE UNIT, THE ONE THAT NAMES THE ACHE. STRATEGIC MANAGEMENT IS LONG-HORIZON, ABOUT DIRECTION AND CHOICE - IT IS DOING THE RIGHT THINGS (EFFECTIVENESS: ARE WE CLIMBING THE RIGHT MOUNTAIN?). BUSINESS OR OPERATIONAL PLANNING IS SHORTER-HORIZON, ABOUT EXECUTION - IT IS DOING THINGS RIGHT (EFFICIENCY: ARE WE CLIMBING WELL?). This is the distinction that decides whether an organisation thrives or works itself to exhaustion on the wrong thing, and it is the one people most often blur, because both feel like good management and both produce plans and targets. YOU NEED BOTH, and they are not the same skill or the same horizon: a brilliant strategy nobody executes is useless, and flawless execution of the wrong strategy is worse than useless because it gets you to the wrong place faster. THE TWO CLASSIC FAILURES: an OPERATIONAL TO-DO LIST DRESSED UP AS A STRATEGY - a pile of activities with no real choice or direction in it, a plan wearing a strategy's clothes; and a STRATEGY THAT IS NEVER OPERATIONALISED - a fine direction that nobody turns into work, a poster on a wall. The water utility in the ache is doing the second failure perfectly and ignoring the first entirely: OPTIMISING ITS WEEKLY SCHEDULES BEAUTIFULLY WHILE THE BULK MAIN QUIETLY FAILS. It is climbing, hard and skilfully and with every target green - up the wrong mountain. AND THE ROLE OF THE ASSET MANAGER SITS EXACTLY HERE: STRATEGY IS NOT THE PRESERVE OF THE EXECUTIVE SUITE. THE ASSET MANAGER'S VOICE, THE HONEST REALITY OF WHAT THE ASSETS CAN AND CANNOT DO, IS WHAT TURNS AN EXECUTIVE WISH INTO A WORKABLE STRATEGY. WITHOUT THAT VOICE, A STRATEGIC PLAN IS JUST A HOPE. And hold this without shame: A STRATEGY THAT CHANGES WHEN THE WORLD CHANGES IS NOT A FAILED STRATEGY; IT IS A LIVING ONE. THE ONLY FAILED STRATEGY IS THE ONE THAT REFUSES TO ADAPT TO A WORLD THAT WILL NOT STAND STILL. STRATEGY CHOOSES THE MOUNTAIN; OPERATIONS CLIMBS IT; AND AN ORGANISATION THAT ONLY DOES THE SECOND WILL CLIMB THE WRONG MOUNTAIN, VERY EFFICIENTLY.

Box 1 is what strategy actually is. Box 5 is the distinction that decides whether all that effort is aimed at the right thing. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Strategy is not about climbing the mountain efficiently; it is about choosing the right mountain to climb in the first place. Strategy chooses the mountain. Operations climbs it. Five questions:

  • What is strategy? A deliberate choice, not a plan or a wish. Where to go and how to get there - this, not that - turning what the organisation is for into what it will do.
  • What must I read? The environment - outside and inside. What is changing beyond you, and what you can and cannot actually do. Ambition checked against capability.
  • How does it stay alive? A cycle, and a golden thread. Scan, formulate, implement, evaluate - not a document filed once; and objectives to AM objectives to plans to work.
  • What is in a strategic plan? Where we are going, where we are, how we get there, how we do and measure it. Recognisable components you can compare.
  • Strategic or operational? Direction versus execution - the right things versus things right. You need both; confusing them climbs the wrong mountain very efficiently.

The principles are a short, sturdy set. It starts from purpose - the mission and the objectives, what the organisation exists to achieve. It reads the environment. It makes a real choice. And it runs as a cycle, not a document written once and filed. Everything else is detail on those four.

Read the environment - outside and inside. A choice made blind is a gamble; a choice made from an honest reading of the situation is a strategy. So before the choice comes the situation analysis, and it has two halves that are equally necessary. The external environment: what is changing beyond your walls - the threats and the opportunities, the shifts in climate, demand, regulation, technology, that will help or hurt you whether you notice them or not. The internal environment: what your organisation can and cannot actually do - its genuine strengths and its honest weaknesses, its capacity, its money, its data, its people. A common tool brings the two together into strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats. And here is the discipline most often skipped: ambition must be checked against capability. A strategy that ignores the internal reality - that assumes crews and budget and data you do not have - is not brave, it is a wish, and it will fail on contact with the world it never read.

The cycle, and the golden thread. A strategy is not a document you write once and file; the world keeps moving, so strategic management is a cycle: you scan the environment, formulate the strategy, implement it, and evaluate it against what actually happened - and then you go round again, because what was true last year may not be true now. And in asset management the strategy earns its keep through the golden thread, the line of sight from the top down: the organisation's objectives set the asset-management objectives, which set the plans, which set the work - so that every spanner turned can be traced back to a corporate purpose, and no asset activity is an orphan doing something for reasons no one can name. (Building those asset-management documents - the strategic asset-management plan, the asset-management plan - is its own craft, taught in its own techniques; here the point is the principle they put into practice: direction at the top, connected all the way down to the work.)

The components of a strategic plan. Whatever an organisation calls its strategy document, a real one has recognisable components, and knowing them lets you compare one plan against another and spot what is missing. Where we are going: the mission (the fundamental purpose), the vision and values, and the measurable objectives that turn purpose into targets you can check. Where we are: the situation analysis, the honest evidence base of the external environment and the internal capability. How we will get there: the strategic choices and priorities - the direction, the this-not-that. And how we will do and measure it: the initiatives, the resources committed, and the indicators that will show whether it is working. A "strategy" missing the middle two - all destination and no honest look at where you actually stand - is the commonest and most dangerous kind, because it sounds inspiring and cannot be executed.

One honest boundary - two of them, and both matter. First: strategy is not the preserve of the executive suite. The asset manager's voice, the honest reality of what the assets can and cannot do, is what turns an executive wish into a workable strategy. Without that voice, a strategic plan is just a hope. The people at the top can set a direction, but only the people who know the assets can say whether it is achievable, and a strategy set without that reality in the room is a wish with a letterhead. Second, on change: a strategy that changes when the world changes is not a failed strategy; it is a living one. The only failed strategy is the one that refuses to adapt to a world that will not stand still. Do not judge an organisation for revising its strategy when the drought worsened or the regulation shifted - that is the cycle working exactly as it should.

Strategic versus operational - the distinction that decides everything. Now the sharpest idea in the unit, the one that names the ache in the T1 story. Strategic management is long-horizon, about DIRECTION and CHOICE - it is doing the RIGHT THINGS (effectiveness: are we climbing the right mountain?). Business or operational planning is shorter-horizon, about EXECUTION - it is doing things RIGHT (efficiency: are we climbing well?). You need both - a brilliant direction nobody executes is useless, and flawless execution of the wrong direction is worse than useless. But they are different skills on different horizons, and confusing them is the classic organisational failure, in two forms: an operational to-do list dressed up as a strategy (a pile of activities with no real choice or direction in it), and a fine strategy that nobody ever turns into work. The water utility in the ache is doing the second thing perfectly and the first thing not at all: optimising its weekly schedules while the bulk main fails. It is climbing, hard and well - up the wrong mountain. Strategy chooses the mountain; operations climbs it; and an organisation that only does the second will climb the wrong mountain, very efficiently.

Does it scale? A person can be superbly organised - every day planned, every task done on time - and still spend a decade climbing a career, or a course of study, that was never right for them, because they optimised the execution of their life and never made the strategic choice about its direction. Being busy is not the same as being on the right path. Choosing the mountain, for a utility or a life, is the decision that all the efficient climbing in the world cannot make for you.

T4: Your Turn

Here is the water utility's situation, on one page.

PurposeDeliver safe water, reliably and affordably, to a growing town
OutsideA drying climate and a growing population; tightening water-quality regulation; one ageing bulk main carrying the whole town's supply, near the end of its life
InsideSkilled but stretched crews; a constrained capital budget that cannot do everything; good data on the treatment works, almost none on the buried network
  1. Situation analysis. In one sentence each, name the primary external threat and the primary internal weakness.
  2. The strategic choice. Name one genuine strategic choice this implies, framed as a trade-off: "We will prioritise X at the expense of Y, because..."
  3. The golden thread. Trace that choice down to one specific, measurable asset-management objective.
  4. Strategic or operational? Classify each of these three as primarily strategic or operational, and for one, name the "wrong mountain" risk: (i) negotiating a new bulk-water tariff with the region; (ii) rescheduling a maintenance crew to cover an absence; (iii) presenting a five-year capital-renewal plan to the board.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the analysis: the primary external threat is the ageing bulk main against a drying, growing demand - a single point of failure that could leave the town dry. The primary internal weakness is the near-total absence of data on the buried network (you cannot manage, or even see, what you have not recorded), compounded by a budget that cannot fix everything at once.

Second, the choice as a trade-off, for example: "We will prioritise securing the bulk-water supply and renewing the worst mains at the expense of the telemetry upgrade and some reactive-response speed, because a bulk-main failure is existential and the telemetry is not." The mark of a real answer is the phrase at the expense of - a choice that gives nothing up is not a strategic choice, it is a wish-list.

Third, the golden thread: that choice becomes a concrete asset-management objective - for instance, "renew 20 km of the highest-risk bulk and distribution main over five years, and complete a condition survey of the buried network within eighteen months" - which then becomes the plan and the work. The direction at the top is now connected, traceably, to a job a crew can do.

Fourth, the classification: (i) negotiating a bulk-water tariff is strategic (it shapes the utility's direction and viability for years); (ii) rescheduling a crew to cover an absence is operational (short-horizon execution); (iii) presenting a five-year capital-renewal plan is strategic (it is the direction itself). The wrong-mountain risk: if the utility pours all its attention into (ii) - running the daily schedule beautifully - while never doing (i) or (iii), it will be superbly efficient right up until the bulk main bursts and the whole town is dry, having climbed the wrong mountain faster than anyone.

T5: The Guardrails

Doing things right is not the same as doing the right things. The most efficient organisation in the world is in trouble if it is efficient at the wrong thing.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• A to-do list dressed up as a strategy. A pile of activities with no real choice or direction in it is not a strategy, however long it is. If it does not say "this, not that", it is a plan, not a strategy.

• A strategy nobody operationalises. A fine direction that is never turned into work is a wish. The golden thread must reach all the way down to a job a crew can do.

• Ambition with no capability check. A strategy that assumes crews, budget and data you do not have is a wish, not a plan. Read the internal environment as honestly as the external.

• A strategy written once and filed. The world keeps moving; a static strategy is slowly going wrong. Run the cycle - scan, formulate, implement, evaluate - and go round again.

• Optimising operations while the strategy is wrong. Climbing the wrong mountain efficiently is still climbing the wrong mountain. Check the direction before you celebrate the pace.

• Treating strategy as executive-only. The people who know the assets are the ones who can say whether a direction is achievable. A strategy without that voice is a hope with a letterhead.

• Punishing a strategy that adapted. A strategy that changed because the world changed is working, not failing. The only failed strategy is the one that refuses to move as the world does.

• A plan that is all destination. All vision and no honest look at where you actually stand is the most dangerous kind: it inspires and cannot be executed. Keep the situation analysis in.

The utility worked hard and well at everything on its list, and still the ageing bulk main went unaddressed, because there was a list of activities but never a real choice of direction. So understand strategy for what it is: a deliberate choice of where to go and how to get there - this, not that - made by reading the world outside and the capability inside, and run as a cycle rather than filed as a document; give it a golden thread from the organisation's objectives down through the asset-management objectives and the plans to the work, so nothing is an orphan; know the components of a strategic plan - where you are going, where you are, how you get there, how you do and measure it - so you can see what a plan is missing; and never confuse choosing the mountain with climbing it. Strategy chooses the mountain and operations climbs it - you need both, and the asset manager's honest account of what the assets can and cannot do is what keeps the chosen mountain a real one rather than a wish. The next time your organisation is busy and efficient and hitting every target, do not only ask whether you are climbing well - ask whether it is the right mountain, because all the efficient climbing in the world cannot fix a wrong direction.
