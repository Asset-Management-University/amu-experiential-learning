The whole life, not this quarter

T1: The Ache

Someone hands you a "business case" to cut maintenance. It is neat, it is quantified, it looks responsible - a consultant's proposal to trim the spend and focus on what matters. And your gut says it is a trap, but you cannot say exactly where, so you either sign it and hope, or reject it and look like the person who blocks every efficiency.

Or it is the opposite ache: you are drowning. You maintain everything to the same high standard - the trivial air-conditioner in the office and the safety-critical system that protects a town - and you are always behind, spending your scarce effort equally on what matters and what does not, because it all feels irresponsible to skip.

I am handed a "business-centred" case to cut maintenance and I cannot tell the wise part from the reckless part - how do I appraise business-centred maintenance instead of just obeying it or rejecting it?

Before you read on, decide. The case on your desk argues for cutting maintenance on business grounds, and parts of it are plainly right: it points out that you service the office air-conditioner to the same standard as the chlorine dosing system, which is true and indefensible. Other parts propose stretching intervals on the dosing system itself, and the justification counts only the direct cost of a failure — the repair, the callout, the lost hours. It does not count harm to anyone, a discharge breach, or what an extended run does to the plant's life. It is internally coherent. Your own practice is the mirror error: everything to the same high standard, always behind. Write down how you would appraise this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the one test that settles it

• “Reject it — you cannot cut maintenance on a safety system.” Defensible instinct, and it throws away the half that is correct. You are spending the same effort on an air-conditioner as on the dosing plant, and refusing the whole case keeps that error in place.

• “Accept it — finance has done the numbers.” The numbers are sound for what they count. That is exactly the problem, and it is invisible if you check the arithmetic rather than the boundary.

• “Split the difference — take half the cuts.” Applies an arbitrary discount to a mixture of the wise and the reckless, and half a reckless cut on a dosing system is still a reckless cut.

• “Escalate it to the board.” They will ask which parts you disagree with and why — which is the appraisal you have not yet done, and without it you will be arguing instinct against a coherent document.

• “Ask for more budget so nothing has to be cut.” Defends the practice that has you permanently behind, spending scarce effort equally on what matters and what does not.

• Understand the lens before judging it. It is not a technique prescribing tasks — it is the lens you look through to decide how much of which effort to spend where. Rather than asking what the perfect maintenance for an asset would be, it asks what a failure would actually cost, and whether the maintenance is worth that. And its real strength is that it refuses two opposite errors at once: maintaining everything to the highest standard regardless of whether failure matters — your error — and cutting without regard to what failure would do. So it is worth having and worth defending. But it is only as honest as its definition of the business, and that is the test. Where the consequences counted include harm to people, environmental and regulatory exposure and the asset's long-term life, the reasoning is sound. Where the business has quietly been narrowed to this quarter's direct cost — as it has here — the same logic produces something coherent and genuinely dangerous. So examine what has been counted, and you can embrace the wise part and refuse the reckless part with a stated reason rather than an instinct.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you appraise business-centred maintenance - seeing what the lens genuinely is, what it gets right, the one test that decides whether it is honest, and how to spot when it is being abused - so you can embrace the wise part and refuse the reckless part with a clear reason.

1. The lens, not a method - judge each decision by its consequence

The first thing to understand is what business-centred maintenance actually IS - and is not. It is NOT a technique that tells you to do preventive tasks, or condition monitoring, or an RCM analysis. IT IS THE LENS YOU LOOK THROUGH to decide how much of which effort to spend where. It says: do not ask 'what is the perfect maintenance for this asset?' Ask 'WHAT DOES A FAILURE OF THIS ASSET ACTUALLY COST THE BUSINESS, AND IS THE MAINTENANCE WORTH THAT?' The maintenance strategy is then matched to the failure - run-to-failure for the trivial and cheap-to-fix, condition-based or preventive for the consequential, deep rigour only where the consequence earns it. The technical engines that find the right task per failure mode - RCM, condition-based monitoring, PM optimisation - are the TOOLBOX; BCM is the judgement about where to reach for which tool.

2. The genuine strength - it refuses both errors at once

This is what makes BCM worth having, and worth defending. On one side is THE ENGINEER'S ERROR: maintain everything to the highest standard, regardless of whether the failure would matter, until you are drowning in work and spending as much care on the office air-conditioner as on the system that keeps a town safe. On the other side is THE ACCOUNTANT'S ERROR: cut maintenance everywhere by the same percentage, regardless of what each failure would cost, until something critical breaks. BCM REFUSES BOTH. It concentrates scarce effort where the consequence is real and releases it where the consequence is trivial. Done well, it is not less maintenance or more maintenance - it is RIGHT-PLACED maintenance.

3. The test that decides everything - how honest is 'the business'?

Here is the hinge on which the whole approach turns. 'THE BUSINESS CONSEQUENCE' OF A FAILURE CAN BE DEFINED HONESTLY OR DISHONESTLY, and BCM is only ever as good as that definition. Done honestly, 'the business consequence' is the WHOLE consequence over the WHOLE life: the safety of people, staff and public; the continuity of service to the communities and customers who depend on the asset; the long-term health and capability of the asset and the organisation; regulatory compliance and reputational integrity; and the environmental impact. WHOLE-LIFE, NOT THIS-QUARTER. THE PEOPLE THE ASSET SERVES ARE PART OF THE BUSINESS CASE. A maintenance strategy that ignores the human consequence of failure is not business-centred; it is a dereliction of duty.

4. The abuse to spot - pricing human and catastrophic risk at zero

Now the dark version, because you will be handed it. 'BUSINESS-CENTRED' GETS QUIETLY REDEFINED AS 'CHEAPEST THIS YEAR'. The whole-life consequence shrinks to the current budget cycle. And the classic move appears: a LOW-PROBABILITY, HIGH-CONSEQUENCE FAILURE - the kind that has not happened in fifteen years - is discounted to nothing, because 'look, no failures', and the maintenance that has been silently PREVENTING that failure is cut to hit a target. This is how safety-critical maintenance on the assets that serve the most vulnerable communities gets STARVED: not by a villain, but by a tidy business case that priced the catastrophe at zero. A BUSINESS CASE THAT PRICES HUMAN HARM OR CATASTROPHIC RISK AT ZERO IS NOT A BUSINESS CASE. IT IS A FAILURE OF JUDGEMENT. The tell is almost always the same: past performance ('it has not failed') used to justify discounting a future consequence, while the human and whole-life cost of that consequence is left out of the sum entirely.

5. The appraisal verdict - embrace where honest, refuse where abused

Appraising BCM is NOT saying yes to it or no to it. It is looking at a specific proposal through the lens and giving a REASONED VERDICT, MOVE BY MOVE. Where the lens is used honestly - releasing effort from genuinely trivial, low-consequence failures - you EMBRACE it, gratefully, because it frees your scarce capacity for what matters. Where it is abused - starving a safety-critical or community-serving system by pricing its rare, terrible failure at zero - you REFUSE it, and you refuse it with the reason spoken plainly: you REFRAME 'the business consequence' to include the people and the whole life, and you show that on an honest definition the maintenance pays for itself many times over. That reframing is the whole skill.

T3: The Blueprint

T4: Your Turn

A consultant has given Onalenna a two-part "short-term efficiency" proposal for Corathyl, both moves flying the flag of business-centred maintenance. Your job is to appraise each move honestly - embracing the wise one and refusing the reckless one with a reason.

Move 1. Shift the administrative block's air-conditioning units to a run-to-failure strategy: no scheduled servicing, just replace a unit when it dies. Cited reason: low operational impact, cheap and quick to replace.

Move 2. Reduce the inspection frequency on the raw-water intake screen's structural welds by 75 per cent. Cited reason: "no recorded failure in fifteen years", and the specialist inspection is expensive.

  1. Appraise each move through the lens. For Move 1 and Move 2, name the business consequence of the failure the maintenance protects against.
  2. Reframe the abusive one. For the move you would refuse, write out "the business consequence" of that failure honestly - the whole-life and human cost, not the repair bill.
  3. Write the verdict. One short paragraph for Onalenna: accept one move, reject the other, each with its reason grounded in an honest definition of "the business".
  4. Name the tell. State the general warning sign of an abused business case that this proposal shows.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the appraisal: Move 1 is BCM done right. An office air-conditioner failing is a comfort inconvenience, cheap and fast to fix, with no safety, service, or whole-life consequence. Run-to-failure is exactly correct - spending scheduled effort here would be the engineer's error. Move 2 is BCM abused. The intake screen's structural integrity is safety- and service-critical; its failure is low-probability but potentially catastrophic - loss of the plant's raw-water intake, a possible flood of debris or a structural collapse, and an interruption to the water a whole community drinks.

Second, the reframe of Move 2: "the business consequence" of a weld failure is not the cost of the inspection. It is the risk of losing the town's water supply for days or weeks; the safety of anyone near a structural failure; the regulatory and reputational cost of a preventable public-health incident; and the long-term capability lost if the intake must be rebuilt. Priced honestly, the rigorous inspection is cheap insurance against a whole-life catastrophe, not an expense to trim.

Third, the verdict: "Accept Move 1 - run-to-failure on the office air-conditioning is sound; the failure is trivial and this frees effort for what matters. Reject Move 2 - cutting the intake-screen weld inspections prices a rare but catastrophic, community-harming failure at zero. On an honest, whole-life definition of the business - the safety of the public and the continuity of the water supply - this maintenance is essential and stays. Business-centred means the whole life and everyone we serve, not this quarter's cost."

Fourth, the tell: the abused case uses past performance ("no failure in fifteen years") to discount a future catastrophic consequence to zero, while leaving the human and whole-life cost of that consequence out of the sum entirely. Whenever "it has not failed" is doing the work of justifying a cut to something safety-critical, appraise harder.

T5: The Guardrails

Business-centred maintenance is right when "the business" means the whole life and everyone the asset serves - and a dangerous lie when it means only this quarter's cost. Appraise it, do not obey it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Treating BCM as a fixed method. It is a lens, not a recipe. It tells you to judge by consequence, not which task to do - the tasks come from the toolbox (RCM, condition-based, preventive, run-to-failure).

• Equating "the business" with this quarter's cost. Whole-life, not this-quarter. Shrink the definition to the current budget and the whole approach turns dishonest.

• Pricing catastrophic or human risk at zero. A business case that prices human harm or a catastrophic failure at zero is not a business case; it is a failure of judgement. The people the asset serves are part of the business case.

• Using "it has not failed" to justify a cut. Past performance is the classic tell of an abused case - the maintenance may be exactly why it has not failed. When "no failures" justifies starving something critical, appraise harder.

• The engineer's error. Maintaining everything to perfection regardless of consequence drowns you and wastes scarce effort on the trivial. Right-place the effort; release it where the failure does not matter.

• Obeying a business case because it has numbers. A quantified case is an argument to appraise, not a truth to obey. Check what its definition of "worth it" includes - and what it leaves out.

• Giving a blanket yes or no. The skill is a move-by-move verdict: embrace the honest focusing of effort, refuse the discounting of harm, and say which and why.

You were handed a business case you could not read, or you were drowning in maintenance that treated the trivial and the critical alike. Now you can appraise.
