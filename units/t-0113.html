Two perfect halves

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and today you commission a brand-new treatment stage. Two teams built it. The mechanical team built the pump-and-pipe skid; the control team built the panel and the sensors. Each team tested their own half for weeks - both flawless, both signed off, both proud.

This morning, for the first time, the two halves meet. And nothing works.

The pump flange will not bolt to the pipe - the hole patterns do not match. The level sensor is sending its reading, but the control panel turns it into nonsense - the panel was expecting one kind of signal and the sensor speaks another, so "half full" shows as "empty". And there is no isolation valve anywhere: the mechanical team assumed the control team was supplying it, the control team assumed the mechanical team was. The whole stage stands dead on the floor - not because either half is bad (both are excellent) but because nobody ever agreed what happened at the line where they meet.

Two teams each built a perfect half, and together they made a useless whole - because every failure was at the boundary between them, where each had quietly assumed something different. How do I make sure separate parts will actually fit and work together, before the day they finally meet?

Before you read on, decide. Install day. The mechanical half is excellent. The control half is excellent. Together they are useless. The pipework will not mate. The panel is reading the level as nonsense, because each side scaled the signal to a different meaning. And the isolation valve is not here, because each team was certain the other was supplying it. Two good leads are standing in front of you remembering the same meeting differently, both in perfect good faith. Everyone is waiting and the crane is on hire. Write down what should have been done, months ago, to prevent this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the halves were actually joined by

• “More meetings between the teams.” They had the meeting. Both leads attended it, both left satisfied, and both are standing here now with different memories of it — which is precisely the failure, so more of it produces more of the same.

• “Put one person in charge of both halves.” Sometimes right, often impossible, and it relocates the problem into one head rather than removing it. That head still has to decide what happens at the join, and still has to tell both sides in a form that outlasts the telling.

• “Share the full drawings both ways.” Generous and counter-productive. Each side then holds hundreds of pages of the other's internal design, nearly all irrelevant, and the handful of facts that actually matter at the boundary are buried where nobody will find them.

• “Test-fit earlier.” This finds the mismatch sooner, which is worth a great deal, and it is still detection rather than prevention. And it would have caught the flange; it would probably not have caught two sides agreeing a signal's form while disagreeing about its meaning.

• “Somebody should have checked the scope for gaps.” True of the missing valve and not of the other two, and “somebody should have checked” is what gets said after every one of these — it names a diligence rather than a method.

• Look at what was actually holding the two halves together: two people's separate memories of one conversation. Memories diverge silently and get tested on the day it is most expensive. So the boundary itself has to be designed, agreed and written down before either side builds — and its discipline is restraint: it covers only what crosses the line, never how either side does its own work, which is what keeps it short enough to be read and stops it becoming an argument about somebody else's design. Three kinds of thing cross, and your three failures are one of each. What must physically fit, specified tightly enough to mate first time. What is exchanged — and, the part most often left out, what the exchanged thing means, since two sides can agree a signal perfectly and still disagree about what its values represent, giving you a system that works and is wrong. And who supplies what, item by item, which is where the valve nobody thought was theirs finally gets found. Then both sides agree it in a form that outlasts memory — because its whole job is to be the record on the day the two recollections differ.

There is one humble document whose entire job is to prevent exactly this. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the single page that would have saved the day - the agreement, made and signed long before install day, about everything that crosses the line between the two halves.

1. The Boundary - what two parts meet here?

The interface between System A (the mechanical pump skid, drawing SK-001) and System B (the control panel, drawing CP-001). Draw the line, name both sides - then define only what crosses it.

2. The Physical Interface - what must FIT?

What must physically mate: the pipe flange is DN80 PN16, with an 8 x M16 bolt pattern and a Type-K gasket. Both teams build to exactly this, so the parts bolt together first time - no surprises on install day.

3. The Data/Signal Interface - what crosses, and what does it MEAN?

The level transmitter LT-101 sends a 4-20 mA signal, scaled so 4 mA = 0 m and 20 mA = 5.0 m. Both sides agree the signal type, range, units AND what each value means - so the panel reads the real water level, not gibberish.

4. The Responsibility Matrix - who supplies and does what?

Who does which side: Mechanical supplies and installs the pump, pipework and flange; Control supplies and installs the panel, sensor and cabling - and (the one everyone forgot) the Control team supplies and fits the DN80 isolation valve. No gaps, no duplicates.

5. Authorisation - both sides agree and SIGN

Signed and dated by BOTH leads - the mechanical lead and the control lead. Now it is the single agreed truth, not two different memories of a meeting. Any change to it goes through change control (a Management of Change), or the agreement quietly breaks again.

Notice what it does: it draws a line down the middle, names the two sides, and then defines only what crosses the boundary - the physical fit, the meaning of the signal, who supplies what - and both leads sign it. Every one of this morning's failures is a box on this page that simply was never filled in. Two perfect halves do not make a working whole; an agreed boundary does. Now let us walk through it.

T3: The Blueprint

This is systems engineering in action - the discipline of getting a complex thing, built by many hands, to work as one. The pain you felt this morning has a name: an interface failure, and it is the single most common reason big, multi-team projects fail. That one page that prevents it is an Interface Control Document (an ICD), a core tool of the systems-engineering standard, ISO 15288. Each part answers a question:

  • What two parts meet, and where exactly is the line? The first, almost magical move: draw the boundary on a page and name the two sides. The instant the invisible "gap between two teams" becomes a real, drawn line, you can engineer it - and you list only what crosses it.
  • Will the parts physically fit? The physical interface - the flange standard, the bolt pattern, the connector. Both sides build to the same numbers, so they mate first time.
  • Do the signals mean the same thing on both sides? The data interface - the signal type, range, units, and what each value means. This is where "half full" became "empty": one side sent 4-20 mA, the other read it as 0-5 volts. Agree it, and the reading is true.
  • Who is responsible for what? The responsibility split - exactly who supplies and installs each thing at the boundary, so nothing (like that isolation valve) falls into the gap between "I thought you had it".
  • How do we prove we all agreed? Both leads sign it. Now it is one shared truth, not two different memories of a meeting.

Two ideas surround this and make it whole. First, every line on that ICD exists to meet a requirement - a stated need; good systems engineering traces every part back to a real need, so nothing is built for no reason. Second, the ICD is what lets you verify and validate the system: verification asks "did we build the thing right?" (does it meet the spec?), and validation asks "did we build the right thing?" (does it actually meet the need?) - t-0152 puts both to work on a real acceptance decision, proving each requirement proportionately - and you can only check those at the system level once the interfaces are defined.

Does it scale? The agreed flange standard and signal scaling on the Corathyl stage is the exact same discipline that lets a new engine bolt onto an aircraft wing, or a satellite's solar array correctly power its radio - thousands of people, hundreds of parts, held together by controlled interfaces.

T4: Your Turn

Now you control a boundary of your own. Your group is running an event, split between two teams: one handles the venue and seating, the other handles the audio-visual and catering. They have to meet - and that meeting point is where things go wrong.

Write a one-page ICD for the boundary between them, using the five boxes:

  1. Draw the line and name the two sides.
  2. Physical: where does the projector go? what size table for the food? where are the power sockets?
  3. Data/signal: what connects the laptop to the speakers? what power does the AV need, and is it there?
  4. Responsibility: who brings the extension lead? who clears up afterwards?
  5. Sign it - get both "team leads" to agree.

T5: The Guardrails

Interface control is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust your design will come together.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The "it's obvious" assumption. Believing the other side will naturally match you, because it seems obvious to you. It never is - "obvious" is exactly where the two teams quietly assumed different things. Write it down.

• The verbal handshake. Agreeing the interface in a meeting and never documenting it. Memories drift and differ; the signed page is the single source of truth, the spoken agreement is not.

• The silo. Perfectly defining and testing your own half, and never the join - until install day, when it is far too late and far too expensive. Test the integration early, even roughly.

• The quiet change. One side changes its half of the interface without telling the other. The signed agreement is now silently false. Any change to a signed ICD must go through change control (a Management of Change) - exactly so the other side is never blindsided.

Two flawless halves stood dead on the floor at Corathyl not because anyone built badly, but because the line where they met belonged to no one. Now you hold the tool that owns that line: draw the boundary, agree what crosses it - the fit, the meaning, the duties - and sign it, both sides. Do that, and the day the parts finally meet, they simply work - and you will have learned the truth that runs every great engineering project: the parts are rarely the problem; the boundaries between them always are.
