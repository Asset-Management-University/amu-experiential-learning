I can't say where my money goes

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, 21 years old. You work at your Uncle Yusuf's shop, earning a wage of R4,500 a month. You live with your parents and give them money for board, but you keep the rest. You promised yourself that this year, you would finally save up for a pair of sneakers you've been wanting, which cost R2,200. But every month seems to end the same way: with almost nothing left.

It's the last weekend of the month. Your cousin Khalid, who is always starting new side-hustles, messages you: "How's that saving going? You putting money away yet?"

You feel a familiar knot in your stomach. You want to say yes. You feel like you're careful. You don't make big, crazy purchases. But when you open your banking app to check, the number staring back at you is R40.

Forty rand. Until your next payday.

Your thumb scrolls up through the month's transactions. A taxi pass. Airtime. A few groceries here, a takeaway there. A pair of trainers from a sports shop. Nothing looks like a mistake. Each payment made sense at the time. You would probably make each one again.

But somewhere between your R4,500 wage landing and this moment, the money just... vanished. You can't say how. You only know it's gone, and your savings goal feels further away than ever.

You think: I just need to earn more. It's the easy thought. Ask Uncle Yusuf for a raise, find a weekend job. Khalid's message sits there, unread. Before you can honestly answer him, you first have to answer the question for yourself.

Where did my money go?

Look at the list. Really look. The answer is in there, but not in the way you think. Before moving on, have a real go at finding it.

Look at that long list of 14 payments. It's a jumble. How could you rearrange it to make it shorter and easier to understand?

Are these fourteen payments really fourteen different kinds of spending? Or do some of them belong together in the same pile? Try putting the ones that are alike into groups.

Now, add up the total for each group you made. Lay the totals next to each other. Does one group's total seem much bigger than you would have guessed? That's the one worth a closer look.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

So, what did you find?

If you started putting the payments into groups and adding them up, you did the exact thing required. There was no other way to turn that long list into a short, clear answer. That move - gathering scattered items into groups and totalling each one - is the core skill here. The name we give it matters less than the instinct that led you to it.

Where the month actually went

Here is one way to group the transactions. Your groups might be different, and that's okay. Any honest grouping reveals the same shape.

  • Board to parents: R1,500
  • Transport (taxi pass): R800
  • Airtime & data: R350
  • Personal (clothes, fun): R670 (R420 + R100 + R150)
  • Cash (untracked): R200
  • Food (groceries & takeaways): R940

Take a breath. This number isn't a judgement. It's a starting point.

The total money out was R4,460. With R4,500 coming in, the R40 left in the account makes perfect sense. There's no mystery, just maths.

But there is a surprise.

It wasn't the board or the taxi pass; you knew about those. The surprise was the R940 on food. Seven small, innocent-looking payments at the supermarket, a takeaway, the corner cafe, and others. Each one felt like nothing at the time. You had never seen them all added together before. They were a major leak, hiding in plain sight.

You made a prediction before you started. Did you expect one big purchase, or lots of small ones? Many people expect a single big mistake - here the truth was the opposite: seven small, ordinary food swipes that nobody had ever added up.

The tool that makes the invisible visible

This simple act - listing all money in and all money out over a period, sorted into groups - is called a money tracker (or a cash flow statement). It's not a plan for the future, not yet. It's an honest record of what already happened.

It's the only tool that can reliably answer the question, "Where does my money go?" You cannot manage what you have not measured.

The method: When you can't see where something goes, don't guess. Gather the scattered pieces, sort them into your own groups, and total each one. The answer is in the totals.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You found the move once. Now let's see if it travels. Here is a different world, a different scale, but the same deep problem. Read each part, do the work it asks, then click to reveal the insight.

Meet Fatima. She runs a tiny community soup kitchen from a church hall, funded by a single local donor. The donor sends a monthly donation of R2,000 by bank transfer into the kitchen's account. Fatima withdraws this as cash for a petty-cash tin to pay for daily needs. But for the second month in a row, the tin is running dry by the third week, and she has to go back to ask for more money to get through.

She is terrified. If she cannot show exactly where the R2,000 went, she is afraid the donor will think she is careless - or even dishonest, that she's stealing - and stop funding the kitchen the community depends on. This fear is the emotional driver.

Step 1: Why the slips are not the answer

Fatima has every single slip and note, so she can prove she didn't steal anything. But just laying out a pile of receipts for the donor doesn't answer the real question: Why does the tin keep running dry? The slips tell her where the rands went, but not what keeps draining the tin. This is exactly like you with your banking app: you could see every payment, but you still couldn't see the leak. The slips are not the answer; the insight is hidden.

Step 2: Find the hidden leak

Your turn: Before you read on, group and total those nine items for her. Which group total would surprise Fatima the way the R940 on food surprised you?

The slips and notes reconcile perfectly: they total R2,000. Her expected costs are all there: food and supplies total R1,580. But the three bakkie-hire payments - R150, R140 and R130 - add up to R420 on transport. That's the hidden drain.

This insight is everything. Now Fatima can go to the donor and explain honestly: "The food and supplies budget is right, but I was spending R420 on bakkie runs without realising it because the food kept running out." And better yet, she can fix it. She speaks to a church member with a bakkie, who agrees to do the big bulk runs as a volunteer. The leak is plugged, and the R2,000 will now last the whole month.

The deep lesson: The surface changes - a donor's tin, not a bank app - but the problem is identical. Money leaks away unseen when it is never grouped and totalled. The danger lives in the sum nobody ever adds up. And notice: nobody told you or Fatima to "group and total." You both figured out that was the move. That's the part that travels.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Your problem was never really about having only R40. It was about managing your money by feel, where every choice made sense on its own, but the total was a painful mystery. That problem lives everywhere. The move you just practised - gather, group, total - is yours to use now.

Remember that goal you set? You wanted to save R2,200.

Now that you can see the R940 leak that was hiding in small food purchases, how does that goal feel? A little more possible? The money tracker gives you the map to find that cash in your own spending.

Here is how to apply this in your own life:

  1. Catch your own hidden leak. Open your real banking app. Take last month's statement. Don't just scan it - get a piece of paper or open a spreadsheet and do exactly what you did for Muhammad. Group every transaction and total each group. It's the simplest grid: each group down one side, its total beside it - that's all a money tracker is.
  2. Find the surprise. Which group total genuinely surprises you? Is it takeaways? Subscriptions? Data? That's your R940. That's the number you've never seen before because you only ever saw the small pieces.
  3. Make one change. You don't have to fix everything at once. Now that you see the leak, what is one small, different choice you can make this month? That's how you start redirecting that leaked money towards your goal.

This is where the real work begins. Do it on your own statement, then make it stick: tell someone you trust what you found - what you expected, what the numbers actually said, and the one change you will make. Saying it out loud is how a plan becomes a promise. (And if you are working with an AMU facilitator, bring it there too.) The understanding you carry into your own life is the real prize - and in time, the wisdom you gain is what makes this path clearer for those who come after you.
