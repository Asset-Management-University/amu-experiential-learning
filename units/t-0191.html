A perfect map of a decaying territory

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna. The certification auditors arrive in a month, and their first request is the simplest one they will make all week.

Show us your asset management system.

You cannot. Not because it does not exist - it exists, and it works. But it exists as 41 documents across 6 folders, none of them listed in any map, so the only way to show it is to walk somebody through it from memory. A new engineer here takes about three and a half hours to find the governing document for a process, and finds it by asking people.

Then comes the second request. Show us your audit policy.

The works has run internal audits for years. There is no policy. Nothing states what gets audited, how often, against what, or by whom - and when you count, four people in a staff of thirty-four are competent to audit the system at all.

The system is real and nobody can see it whole, and we have been checking it for years without ever writing down how. How do I make it visible, and how do I make the checking honest when almost nobody here is independent of anything?

Before you read on, decide. They asked to see your management system. It is real — it works, people follow it, the plant runs on it — and it exists in scattered procedures, a shared drive, and a good deal of what is in your head and Thabo's. There is no document that shows it whole. Then the second request: show us your audit policy. This works has run internal audits for years and nothing states what gets audited, how often, against what, or by whom. And when you count, four people in a staff of thirty-four are competent to audit the system at all — and every one of them is connected to most of it. Write down how you would make it visible and the checking honest — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the half everybody skips

• “Gather every procedure into one folder.” Collection is not visibility. A folder of forty documents shows somebody forty documents; it does not show how they relate or what the system claims to cover.

• “Write the manual now and the audit policy later.” They are one thing. A description of what the system covers is exactly what an audit tests against, so writing them apart guarantees the second contradicts the first.

• “Say we audit everything annually, to be safe.” A promise four people cannot keep. Claiming coverage you cannot deliver produces audits that are performed rather than performed independently — and the first auditor to check the schedule finds the gap.

• “Buy in external auditors for everything.” Solves independence and cannot be afforded at that scale — and an entirely external programme leaves the organisation unable to check itself between visits.

• “Train more auditors.” Worth doing over a year, and it does not answer this week's request, nor the deeper problem: in a works of thirty-four, everybody is connected to almost everything, so competence is not the only constraint on independence.

• The manual has to state what is inside — and, the half people skip, what is deliberately outside and why. An unstated boundary is where every audit finding lives: either an auditor raises something against a system that never claimed it, or a real gap hides in the silence for years because everyone assumes it sits in somebody else's arrangement. Writing the exclusion is what makes the inclusion mean anything — the treatment works, the pump stations and the bulk mains are in; the offices, the fleet and the laboratory instruments are out, each for a stated reason and each named to the arrangement that does hold them. And state the boundary so somebody can test it: asset classes listed, with the register they live in, not “our critical assets”. A scope written in adjectives is a scope that will be argued about at the worst possible moment. Then describe the checking honestly rather than aspirationally: name the constraint you actually have, define what independence means here — nobody audits their own work — and write an explicit route for the cases four people cannot cover.

Here is the manual and the audit policy, built as one thing.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the pair - scope, the map, the programme, the honest treatment of independence, and the loop that keeps the map true.

1. State the scope and boundaries - what the system covers, and what it deliberately does not

A MANUAL BEGINS BY SAYING WHAT THE SYSTEM IS FOR AND WHERE IT STOPS. NAME WHAT IS INSIDE: which assets, which activities, which sites, which parts of the life cycle. Then - and this is the half people skip - NAME WHAT IS OUTSIDE, AND WHY. At Corathyl the system covers the treatment works, the pump stations and the bulk mains, and it EXCLUDES the office buildings, the vehicle fleet and the laboratory instruments, each for a stated reason: they are managed under a different arrangement, and that arrangement is named. AN UNSTATED BOUNDARY IS WHERE EVERY AUDIT FINDING LIVES. When the scope is silent, two things happen and both are bad. An auditor finds an asset nobody manages and raises it against a system that never claimed it. Or a real gap hides in the silence for years, because everyone assumes somebody else's system has it. WRITING THE EXCLUSION IS NOT AN ADMISSION OF WEAKNESS - IT IS THE ONLY WAY THE INCLUSION MEANS ANYTHING. And state the boundary in terms somebody can test: not 'our critical assets' but the asset classes, listed, with the register they are held in. A scope written in adjectives is a scope that will be argued about at the worst possible moment.

2. Map the processes and where each governing document lives

THIS IS THE PART THAT MAKES THE MANUAL A MAP RATHER THAN AN ESSAY, AND IT IS WHY THE DOCUMENT EXISTS AT ALL. THE SYSTEM AT CORATHYL IS REAL AND IT WORKS. IT IS ALSO 41 DOCUMENTS ACROSS 6 FOLDERS, NONE OF THEM LISTED IN ANY MAP - so a new engineer takes about three and a half hours to find the governing document for a given process, and finds it BY ASKING PEOPLE rather than by looking. That is not an information problem, it is a continuity problem: the system is held in the heads of the people who built it. SO MAP THREE THINGS. THE PROCESSES: what the system actually does - planning, acquisition, operation, maintenance, information, review - named as the organisation names them rather than as the standard names them, because a learner or an auditor is looking for the real thing. THE INTERACTIONS: which process feeds which, and where a handover happens, because handovers are where systems fail and where an audit should look first. AND THE GOVERNING DOCUMENT FOR EACH: title, owner, location, review date. NOTE WHAT THIS IS NOT. A FOLDER LISTING IS NOT A DOCUMENT MAP. A folder listing tells you what exists; a map tells you WHICH DOCUMENT GOVERNS WHICH PROCESS, which is the question people actually have. And keep the map itself under the register discipline - an owner, an entry rule and a review cadence - or within two years it will describe a system that has moved on.

3. Design the audit programme - objectives, scope, criteria, methods and a risk-based cycle

AN AUDIT PROGRAMME IS ARRANGEMENTS FOR AUDITS PLANNED FOR A SPECIFIC TIMEFRAME, WITH DEFINED OBJECTIVES, SCOPE, CRITERIA AND METHODS. Most organisations have audits and no programme - a cycle that repeats because it repeated last year. WRITE THE FOUR. OBJECTIVES: what the programme is for. Verifying conformity is one objective; evaluating whether the system is EFFECTIVE is a different and more useful one, and a programme that only ever checks conformity will report a healthy system in a plant that is degrading. SCOPE AND CRITERIA: what is audited and against what - the standard, the manual from box 2, the organisation's own procedures, or a licence condition. METHODS: interview, observation, document review, data analysis, and how evidence is recorded. THE CYCLE, AND HERE IS THE PART THAT SEPARATES A PROGRAMME FROM A ROTA: IT IS RISK-BASED, AND THE RISKS AND OPPORTUNITIES IN THE PROGRAMME MUST BE DETERMINED RATHER THAN MERELY CONSIDERED - which means reviewing evidence and reaching a conclusion, not gesturing at risk in a preamble. Prioritise high-risk processes and critical parameters. THEN TEST YOUR OWN PROGRAMME WITH ONE QUESTION: AM I AUDITING WHERE THE RISK IS, OR WHERE THE AUDITING IS EASY? Document review is easy and comfortable; the process with poor records, an unpopular manager and a history of near misses is neither, and it is where the programme earns its keep.

4. Competence and independence - in an organisation where nobody is fully independent

INDEPENDENCE IS THE BASIS FOR THE IMPARTIALITY OF THE AUDIT AND THE OBJECTIVITY OF ITS CONCLUSIONS - and in most real organisations it is arithmetically scarce, which the standards treat honestly: A LACK OF AUDITOR INDEPENDENCE OR IMPARTIALITY IS NAMED AS A RESOURCE RISK TO THE PROGRAMME. IT IS A RISK TO BE MANAGED AND DECLARED, NOT A VIRTUE TO BE ASSERTED. DO THE ARITHMETIC RATHER THAN THE ASSERTION. Corathyl has 34 STAFF, of whom FOUR are competent to audit the asset management system - and competence is real: audit principles and methods, the system requirements, the organisational processes and the relevant risks. Four auditors across five audit areas is 20 COMBINATIONS, OF WHICH 13 ARE CONFLICT-FREE - 65 PER CENT. But the aggregate is not the problem. TWO AREAS HAVE ONLY TWO ELIGIBLE AUDITORS, so on a three-year cycle the same person audits that area again and again, and WITHIN THREE CYCLES THEY ARE AUDITING WORK SHAPED BY FINDINGS THEY THEMSELVES RAISED. That is a slower, quieter loss of objectivity than a direct conflict - they are no longer auditing a system, they are reviewing their own past influence - and no rule against auditing your own department catches it. SO WRITE THE MECHANISMS: rotation even where no direct conflict exists, paired audits, a peer auditor borrowed from a neighbouring organisation, an external auditor for the thin areas, AND A REQUIREMENT TO STATE THE RESIDUAL CONFLICT IN THE AUDIT REPORT rather than pretend it away. AND WRITE THIS, WHICH IS NOT OPTIONAL: THE AUDIT POLICY MUST EXPLICITLY DEFINE A CONFIDENTIAL ESCALATION CHANNEL FOR AN AUDITOR WHO BELIEVES A FINDING CANNOT BE RAISED OR ADDRESSED THROUGH THE NORMAL PROCESS DUE TO A CONFLICT OF INTEREST, PRESSURE FROM THE AUDITEE, OR RISK OF REPRISAL. THIS CHANNEL MUST BYPASS THE CONFLICTED MANAGEMENT LAYER AND REPORT TO A DESIGNATED, INDEPENDENT AUTHORITY - a governance committee, a director from an unrelated function, or the head of the organisation. Without it you do not have an assurance mechanism. YOU HAVE A TOOL FOR ENFORCING COMPLIANCE ON JUNIOR STAFF WHILE SHIELDING MANAGEMENT, AND EVERYONE IN THE ORGANISATION WILL WORK THAT OUT FASTER THAN YOU DID.

5. Findings, closure and the loop back to the map

A FINDING IS ONLY WORTH RAISING IF THE POLICY SAYS WHAT HAPPENS TO IT. THREE THINGS. WHAT AUTHORITY IT CARRIES: the grading by risk, the response time each grade requires, and what a major finding obliges the organisation to do rather than merely consider. WHO MAY CLOSE IT, AND ON WHAT EVIDENCE - and the rule that matters most here: THE PERSON RESPONSIBLE FOR THE NONCONFORMITY MAY NOT BE THE PERSON WHO DECLARES IT CLOSED. A finding marked closed by the manager whose area it concerns, with no evidence attached, is not a closure - it is an opinion recorded in a system that will be believed. AND THIRD, THE LOOP BACK TO THE MAP, WHICH IS WHY THESE TWO DOCUMENTS BELONG IN ONE UNIT: WHERE A FINDING CHANGES THE SYSTEM, IT MUST CHANGE THE MANUAL. A new process, a moved responsibility, a retired procedure - if the map is not updated, the next audit tests the system against a document that no longer describes it, and it will report conformity or nonconformity against a fiction either way. STATE THE OBLIGATION EXPLICITLY: the corrective action is not complete until the manual reflects it. THIS IS THE WHOLE MECHANISM IN ONE SENTENCE. THE MANUAL MAKES THE SYSTEM VISIBLE, THE AUDIT PROGRAMME MAKES THE VIEW TRUSTWORTHY, AND THE LOOP BACK KEEPS THEM THE SAME THING. Break the loop and you get the worst outcome available: A PERFECT MAP OF A DECAYING TERRITORY, audited faithfully, year after year, against a description of an organisation that no longer exists.

T3: The Blueprint

A management system is only real if you can see it whole, and you can only trust the view if you have a stated, honest way of checking it. Five questions:

  • What is in, and what is deliberately out? Scope and boundaries, written so they can be tested. An unstated boundary is where every finding lives.
  • Where does everything live? The process map and document register. A folder listing tells you what exists; a map tells you which document governs which process.
  • What gets audited, and why that? The programme - objectives, scope, criteria, methods, and a cycle whose risks are determined rather than considered.
  • Who may audit, and how is impartiality achieved? Competence against independence, done as arithmetic rather than assertion.
  • What happens to a finding? Authority, closure by someone other than the responsible person, and the loop back to the map.

The arithmetic that turns a platitude into a design problem. Four competent auditors across five audit areas is twenty combinations, of which thirteen are conflict-free - 65 per cent. That figure sounds workable, and it hides the problem. Two areas have only two eligible auditors, so on a three-year cycle the same person returns to that area again and again, and within three cycles they are auditing work shaped by findings they themselves raised. They are no longer auditing a system; they are reviewing their own past influence. No rule forbidding you to audit your own department catches that, because no conflict of interest exists - only a slow, quiet loss of objectivity that the policy has to design against with rotation, pairing or a borrowed peer.

Why assertion fails here. Independence is the basis for the impartiality of an audit and the objectivity of its conclusions - and the standards are honest that a lack of it is a resource risk to the programme. That framing is the gift. It moves the problem from an undeclared moral failure to a declared, managed risk. An audit policy that simply states auditors shall be independent, in an organisation where four people must cover five areas, has said something everyone can see is untrue - and a policy visibly untrue in its first paragraph will not be obeyed in its fifth.

And the clause that is not optional. The policy must define a confidential escalation channel for an auditor who believes a finding cannot be raised or addressed through the normal process because of a conflict, pressure from the auditee, or risk of reprisal - a channel that bypasses the conflicted management layer and reaches a designated independent authority. Without it you do not have an assurance mechanism. You have a tool for enforcing compliance on junior staff while shielding management, and everyone in the organisation will work that out faster than you did.

One honest boundary. You write the manual and the policy. You do not conduct the audits under it, you do not implement the corrective actions, and you do not close your own findings.

Does it scale? A map of a coastline is only as good as the survey behind it and the date it was last checked. Sailors do not drown because the chart is beautiful; they drown because the sandbank moved and nobody re-surveyed. Your manual is the chart, your audit programme is the survey, and box 5 is the rule that a new sandbank gets drawn on.

T4: Your Turn

A regional transport authority has, by common agreement, an excellent asset management system manual - digital, hyperlinked, genuinely used by staff every day. And the system's performance is degrading.

What is workingDetail
The manualComplete, current, navigable, used daily
The audit programmeDetail
Who auditsTwo administrators with no audit training and no operational background
RotationThe same person has audited the same department for seven years
MethodDocument review against a checklist. No interviews, no observation, no data
SelectionEvery department audited once a year, in alphabetical order
Findings, last three years34 raised. 34 closed. All closed by the manager of the audited area
MeanwhileUnplanned outages up 40 per cent over the same three years
  1. Do not touch the manual. Say why that instruction matters before you start.
  2. Name the four failures. Map each to a box in the canvas.
  3. The closure statistic. Thirty-four raised, thirty-four closed. What does a 100 per cent closure rate tell you, and what would a healthier number look like?
  4. Redesign the selection. Alphabetical order is a rota. Make it a programme.
  5. Competence. The auditors cannot challenge an operational manager. What do you change, and what do you not?

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, on the closure rate. Thirty-four of thirty-four closed, every one signed off by the manager of the area concerned, is not evidence of a responsive organisation. It is evidence that closure is a formality. A healthy programme shows findings open past their due date, findings escalated, and findings disputed - because those are what a real corrective action looks like while it is happening. A hundred per cent closure rate with rising outages is the clearest possible signal that the audit and the plant have stopped being about the same thing.

Second, on competence. The instinct is to train the two administrators, and that is right but insufficient - audit training does not confer the operational knowledge needed to challenge a maintenance manager on a deferral decision. The honest answer pairs them with a competent operational auditor from an unrelated function, or borrows a peer, and says so in the policy. Notice also what you do not change: the administrators are not the failure. They were given a task without the competence the standard requires, by a policy that never specified any, and they did it faithfully for seven years.

Third, step 1 is the whole exercise in one line. The manual is excellent and irrelevant to this problem, and the reflex to improve the artefact you know how to improve is the most common failure in this work. A perfect map of a decaying territory is still a perfect map. The thing that has broken is the survey.

T5: The Guardrails

Manuals and audit policies fail in ways that look like diligence.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• A manual that restates the standard. Nobody needs your paraphrase of a clause. They need to know which of your documents governs which of your processes.

• No stated exclusions. Writing what is outside the system is the only thing that makes what is inside it mean anything.

• A folder listing called a document map. One tells you what exists; the other tells you what governs what.

• A scope written in adjectives. "Our critical assets" will be argued about at the worst possible moment. Name the classes and the register.

• An audit rota mistaken for a programme. Alphabetical order is not risk-based. The risks must be determined, which means evidence and a conclusion.

• Auditing where auditing is easy. Document review is comfortable. The process with poor records and a history of near misses is where the programme earns its keep.

• Asserting independence that does not exist. A policy visibly untrue in its first paragraph will not be obeyed in its fifth. Do the arithmetic and manage the residual.

• Missing the slow conflict. The same auditor returning for years has no conflict of interest and has stopped being objective. Rotate anyway.

• No confidential escalation route. Then the findings that matter most are the ones that never get raised, and the programme protects the people it should be testing.

• Findings closed by the people responsible for them. That is an opinion recorded in a system that will be believed.

• A hundred per cent closure rate. Not a triumph. A question.

• A system change that never reaches the manual. The next audit then tests a fiction, and reports conformity or nonconformity against it either way.

Onalenna had a system nobody could see and a decade of audits nobody had designed. State the scope and the exclusions in terms that can be tested; map the processes, their interactions and the document that governs each, and keep the map itself under a register discipline; design the audit programme with objectives, criteria, methods and a cycle whose risks are determined rather than gestured at; do the independence arithmetic honestly and manage what is left with rotation, pairing and a borrowed peer, declaring the residual conflict rather than asserting it away; and give the auditor a confidential route for the finding that cannot safely be raised, because without it the programme tests the junior and shields the senior. Then close the loop: a finding that changes the system must change the manual. The manual makes the system visible, the programme makes the view trustworthy, and the loop keeps them the same thing - and break that loop and you are left with a perfect map of a decaying territory. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, count how many people in your organisation are genuinely competent to audit it, and then count how many areas need auditing.
