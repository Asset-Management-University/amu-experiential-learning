Step 1 of 5

Every step in this process is fast and the whole thing takes eleven days - so where is the time actually going?

T1: The Ache

At Rietvlei Community Health Centre, a cervical screening result takes eleven days to reach the woman it belongs to. You are the operations manager, and the district has asked you to explain why.

You have spent a week asking, and here is what you have found: every single person in that chain does their part within a day, and most of them within an hour.

Sister Petersen takes the sample in ten minutes. The courier runs daily. The laboratory turns the test around in two hours and posts the result the same afternoon. Mr Marais in admin prints the day's results every morning. Dr Adams reads every result she is given, at the next clinic session. Admin telephones the patient.

Add up the actual work and it comes to about three and a half hours. The elapsed time is eleven days.

And every conversation you have had this week has gone the same way. Each person describes their own step, accurately, and it is fast. Each of them has a theory about which of the others is slow, and every one of those theories is wrong, because you have now checked all of them. Nobody in this building is slow. Nobody in this building can see the whole path either - they each see their own desk and the tray it arrives in.

There is a woman waiting eleven days for a result that took two hours to produce, and the district wants a name.

Every step is fast and the whole thing is slow. Where is the time actually going - and how do I see something that lives in the space between people rather than inside anyone's job?

Before you read on, say where you would look. Eleven days, three and a half hours of work, six people who are each quick and each certain somebody else is the problem, and a district asking you for an answer. Write down what you would do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five places this normally gets looked for, and what each one can find

• "Time each step and find the slow one." The obvious move, and it has already been done - that is where the three and a half hours came from. It is a complete measurement of every step and it accounts for about one per cent of the eleven days. The rest of the time is not inside any step.

• "Ask people what the problem is." Everyone has been asked, and everyone answered honestly about the part they can see. Six accurate accounts of six fast steps do not add up to a picture of eleven days, and the theories about each other cannot be adjudicated because nobody has the whole path.

• "Set a target - results within three days." This will produce something. What it usually produces is each person doing their own fast step slightly faster, and the eleven days barely moving, because the target lands on the steps and the time is not in the steps.

• "Buy a better system." Might genuinely help, and it is being proposed before anyone can say what it would need to do differently. A system bought against an unexamined process reproduces the process.

• "Find out who dropped it." The district's question, and the honest answer available today is that nobody did. Every person can show their part was done. If the answer stays "who", somebody competent will be blamed for something structural.

• What none of them looks at is the space between the steps - the moment a result stops being one person's job and is not yet the next person's. That space belongs to nobody, appears in no job description, and is where ten and a half of the eleven days are sitting. To see it you have to draw the process in a way that shows not only what happens, but who it belongs to at each moment, so the boundaries become visible objects rather than gaps.

T2: The Tool in Action

Draw the process as one line of steps from start to finish. Then give every person or team their own horizontal band, and put each step in the band of whoever actually does it. Every time the line crosses from one band to another, mark it - and put the waiting time on the crossing, not on the step. Watch it run at Rietvlei.

Step 1 - Write the steps end to end, in order, without owners yet. Sample taken. Sample collected. Sample tested. Result released. Result printed. Result read. And then the path divides, because about one result in twenty is not normal: a normal result means the patient is told and recalled in three years, while an abnormal one means an urgent referral for colposcopy. Seven steps and one place where the path splits. Nobody has ever written this list down before; it exists only as six overlapping fragments in six people's heads, and half an hour of asking produces it.

Step 2 - Give every person a band, and put each step in its owner's band. Five bands: Nurse, Courier, Laboratory, Admin, Doctor. Now the shape appears on the page - the line starts in Nurse, drops to Courier, drops to Laboratory, climbs back to Admin, climbs to Doctor, and drops to Admin again. Seven steps, and the line crosses between bands six times. The split after "result read" has to be drawn too, and drawing it exposes something nobody had said out loud: both branches leave the doctor's band and go back to Admin in exactly the same way, so the urgent referral travels by the same tray, at the same speed, as the routine recall.

Step 3 - Put the real elapsed time on the crossings, not on the steps. This is the move, and it takes an afternoon of asking "and then what happens to it?"

Three and a half hours of work, and roughly ten and a half days sitting in six gaps. Not one of those gaps is anybody's job. Every one of them is real, ordinary and invisible from inside any single band.

Step 4 - Attack the crossings, and count each saving once. Not "can each person go faster" - they cannot, meaningfully. The question at every crossing is: does this handoff need to exist at all, and if it does, what makes the next person know it has arrived? Three changes, and it is worth being strict about what each one actually buys, because it is easy to count the same day twice.

  • The laboratory system notifies Dr Adams directly when a result is released. That deletes the Laboratory-to-Admin crossing entirely: 1 day.
  • Being notified does not by itself make her read it sooner - she still reads at a clinic session. A five-minute review slot at the start of every session takes that wait from 3 days to 1: 2 days.
  • When she marks a result reviewed, the system puts it straight onto the recall list instead of the paper going back to Admin. That takes the Doctor-to-Admin crossing from 2 days to none: 2 days - and it does the same for the abnormal branch, which had been travelling at routine speed.

One day plus two plus two is five days saved. And one crossing deliberately does not move: the three days of telephone attempts to reach the patient. Nothing in this drawing fixes that, and pretending otherwise would be the easiest way to make the whole finding untrustworthy.

Step 5 - Redraw the target, and count the crossings again. Five crossings instead of six, and each surviving one now has something that makes the next person aware of it rather than a tray. Eleven days becomes six, and the work is still three and a half hours - because none of it was ever the problem. Then read the new drawing honestly: half of what is left is the three days of telephone calls, which is now the largest single thing in the process and is a different problem with a different answer. That is what a redrawn target is for - not to declare victory, but to show you what the biggest remaining thing is once the easy structural time is gone.

See what putting people into bands did. The line crossed between bands six times, and every one of those crossings turned out to hold a wait that lives in no job description, no timing study and nobody's account of their own work. That is why timing the steps found one per cent of the delay and asking people found six accurate answers that did not add up. And notice what the drawing does to the district's question. It arrived as "who dropped it", and the honest answer is nobody - the drawing replaces a name with a structure, which is both the true answer and the only one that changes anything. For the woman waiting, it is eleven days becoming six - and a clear-eyed view of what the next three days would take.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the third is where the time is found.

  • 1. List the steps end to end, in order, before assigning anyone to them.
  • 2. Give every person or team a band, and put each step in the band of whoever actually does it.
  • 3. Put the elapsed time on the crossings, not on the steps - by asking, at each one, "and then what happens to it?"
  • 4. Attack the crossings: can this handoff be removed, and if not, what makes the next person know it has arrived?
  • 5. Redraw and count the crossings again. Fewer crossings is the improvement; faster steps rarely is.

What you have drawn is a process model in the shape the world uses for it, and its vocabulary is only names for what you did. Each band is a swimlane, and it is the whole reason the method works: a flowchart without lanes shows what happens and hides who has it, which is exactly where the delay lives. A point where the line leaves one lane and enters another is a handoff, and the place where the path divided - normal result or not - is a gateway; every branch of one must reach an end, because a branch that stops on the page is a case that stops in the building, which is very nearly what the abnormal branch was doing at routine speed. The standard set of shapes for all this is BPMN - Business Process Model and Notation - and its only real virtue is that it is agreed, so a drawing made in one department can be read in another without translation.

It fits any work that passes between people or teams and takes longer than the sum of its parts - clinical pathways, order to delivery, permits and approvals, onboarding, claims, repairs, admissions. Two limits are worth stating. It draws the process as it actually runs, which is often not the process as written - and if you draw the written one you will find nothing, so this must be built by asking the people who do the work rather than from a procedure document. And it shows where time and handoffs are, never whether the process should exist at all: a beautifully optimised path through work nobody needs is still work nobody needs.

T4: Your Turn

Take a process you are responsible for that takes longer than it should - an order, an approval, a repair, a referral, a hire, a claim. List its steps end to end. Then give every person or team a band and put each step where it truly belongs. Then do the part that finds the answer: walk each crossing and ask the people on both sides what actually happens to the work in between, and write that time on the crossing.

Fill this in for your own processYour answer

Which crossings could be removed altogether?
For each survivor: what tells the next person it has arrived?

Two disciplines decide whether the drawing finds anything. First, build it from the people who do the work, never from the written procedure - the two are almost always different, and the gaps live in the difference. If your drawing matches the documented process exactly, you have drawn the document. Second, put the waiting time on the crossings and resist putting any of it on a person. The moment a wait is recorded as "admin is slow" rather than "nothing tells admin it has arrived", the finding becomes an accusation, the next person you interview becomes careful with you, and the drawing stops being true. The whole power of this method is that it locates delay in a structure rather than in somebody - and that is not diplomacy, it is where the delay actually is.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the drawing true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• No lanes, no finding. A flowchart without bands shows what happens and hides who holds it. The delay lives at the boundaries, so a drawing that cannot show a boundary cannot show the delay.

• Time the gaps, not the tasks. Timing every step is the natural instinct and it accounted for one per cent of eleven days here. Ask "and then what happens to it?" at every crossing instead.

• Draw what happens, not what is written. If your drawing matches the procedure document exactly, you have drawn the document. Build it by asking the people doing the work.

• Every branch must reach an end. A decision point with a path that simply stops is a real case, somewhere, that also stopped. Trace each branch to a finish.

• Fewer crossings beats faster steps. Removing a handoff removes its wait permanently; asking people to hurry removes it for as long as the attention lasts.

• Never let a wait be recorded against a person. "Nothing tells them it has arrived" and "they are slow" describe the same delay and lead to opposite actions - and only one of them is true here. It is also the difference between people telling you what really happens and people managing you.

• A fast process is not necessarily a needed one. This finds where the time goes inside work that exists. It cannot tell you whether the work should exist, and optimising something unnecessary is a tidier waste.

You went into that week with eleven days, three and a half hours of actual work, six people each doing their part quickly and each quietly certain about somebody else, and a district asking for a name. You can come out with a drawing: seven steps, five bands, six crossings, and ten and a half days sitting in gaps that belong to nobody - five of which can be removed by three changes at the boundaries, taking eleven days to six without asking a single person to work faster - and leaving the three days of telephone calls standing as the largest thing left and the next honest problem. This is how you see a problem that lives in the space between people, instead of blaming the people either side of it. For a woman waiting on a screening result, that is a week of her life.
