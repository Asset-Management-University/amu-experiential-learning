The owner wants the cheap generator because cash is tight - but I know 'cheaper to buy' is not 'cheaper to own', and the two machines do not even last the same number of years. How do I prove which one really costs less?

T1: The Ache

It is the end of a long week at a small bottling plant, and the air in the filling hall is sweet with warm syrup. You are the plant engineer. Out in the yard sits the problem: the old diesel generator that carries the whole line through load-shedding. It is fifteen years old, it tripped twice last week, and every trip stops the line and spoils a batch. The generator is not a luxury here - with the grid down for hours a day, it is the plant.

On your desk are two quotes the owner, Ntsako, dropped this morning. She runs the place tight and she has been burned before. Quote A, a new diesel genset: about R400,000 to buy, lasts roughly 8 years, burns around R320,000 a year in diesel and servicing, worth about R30,000 as scrap at the end. Quote B, a gas-engine genset off the nearby gas line: far dearer to buy at about R1,150,000, but it lasts roughly 14 years and burns only about R130,000 a year, worth about R110,000 at the end. Ntsako taps Quote A. "The diesel is less than half the price. Cash is tight. Buy the cheap one and let us move on." Her tone says she has half-decided.

You feel the pull to agree - the diesel genset is cheaper to buy, it is the easy yes that keeps her happy today. But something nags. The gas one lasts almost twice as long and costs a third as much to run. The two machines do not even live the same number of years - how do you fairly compare a R400,000 machine that lasts 8 years against a R1,150,000 machine that lasts 14? "Cheaper to buy" and "cheaper to own" are not the same sentence, and you know it.

The owner wants the cheap generator because cash is tight - but I know "cheaper to buy" is not "cheaper to own", and the two machines do not even last the same number of years. How do I prove which one really costs less?

Before you read on, decide. The diesel generator costs R400,000, runs at about R320,000 a year, lasts 8 years, and would fetch about R30,000 at the end. The gas machine costs R1,150,000, runs at about R130,000 a year, lasts 14 years, and would fetch about R110,000. Money costs the business roughly 13%. The owner wants the diesel because cash is tight. Write down which one is genuinely cheaper to own, and how you would prove it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the one that gets closest and is still wrong

• “The diesel — R400,000 against R1,150,000, and cash is tight.” This is the price, not the cost. It is also the answer the owner has already given, which is why you are being asked at all.

• “Add the running costs: eight years of R320,000 is R2.56m, fourteen of R130,000 is R1.82m — the gas wins.” Closer, and it ignores two things at once: that a rand in year twelve is not a rand today, and that these are not the same number of years.

• “Work out a cost per year for each.” Better again, and it spreads the purchase price as though a rand paid in year eight weighed the same as one paid on day one.

• “Bring every cost back to today’s money and total them.” Right — and it produces about R1.92m for the diesel against about R1.95m for the gas, which says the diesel is very slightly cheaper. That is the trap, and it is the most dangerous answer here because it is arrived at properly. It compares a cost over eight years with a cost over fourteen.

• “Buy the diesel now and the gas later when cash allows.” A real option, and a different question. It does not tell you which is cheaper to own.

• Both totals are honest. Neither is comparable, because they cover different lengths of time — and the fix is to convert each into the equal yearly amount that would pay for it across its own life. Do that and the answer turns over: about R401,000 a year for the diesel against about R309,000 for the gas — roughly R92,000 a year cheaper to own the expensive machine. The reason is plain once you see it: matching the gas machine’s fourteen years means buying the diesel nearly twice.

You need a way to put a defensible number on the real cost of owning each one - not the price on the quote, the whole-life cost - and to compare two machines with different lives fairly, on one page you can stand behind. Here is that tool, worked on these two quotes.

T2: The Tool in Action

The trap in the yard is treating the purchase price as the cost. The price is one payment, on day one. The cost is every rand the machine pulls out of you across its whole life - fuel, servicing, downtime - minus anything you get back at the end. Here is the four-step tool that turns "the diesel is cheaper" into the honest answer, worked on Ntsako's two quotes.

Box 1 - The discount rate (the real cost of your money)

We build it, we do not guess it: a real cost of money plus a premium for operating in a volatile economy. We use 13% (nominal) and write it on the page - a board can challenge it, and a too-low rate would quietly flatter the long-lived gas machine.

Box 2 - The whole-life cost of each option (what it costs to OWN, not to BUY)

Diesel: R400,000 to buy + R320,000/yr running for 8 years + R30,000 salvage -> about R1.92m in today's money. Gas: R1,150,000 to buy + R130,000/yr for 14 years + R110,000 salvage -> about R1.95m. The diesel total looks SLIGHTLY cheaper - just as the owner said. That is exactly the trap: you are comparing a cost over 8 years against a cost over 14.

Box 3 - EAC: the fair yearly cost (the great equaliser for unequal lives)

Capital-recovery factor at 13%: diesel 0.2084 over 8 years, gas 0.1587 over 14. Diesel EAC = R1.92m x 0.2084 ~ R401,000/yr. Gas EAC = R1.95m x 0.1587 ~ R309,000/yr. The truth flips: per fair year the gas is about R92,000 CHEAPER to own - because matching the gas machine's 14 years means buying the cheap diesel almost twice and paying its heavy fuel bill the whole time.

Box 4 - The decision, and the honest caveat

Winner on EAC: GAS, by about R92,000 a year. The honest caveat: the gas advantage rests on its low running cost; it would only lose if that cost jumped about 70%, to roughly R222,000/yr - well above today's gas price. So the recommendation is gas now, and watch the gas price - not 'gas costs R309,000', but 'gas beats diesel comfortably unless gas nearly doubles'.

Read the four boxes together and the answer flips in front of you. On whole-life total the diesel looks slightly cheaper (about R1.92m against gas's R1.95m) - which is exactly why the owner reached for it. But that compares 8 years against 14, which is not fair. EAC - the level yearly cost - is the equaliser: the diesel costs about R401,000 a year to own, the gas about R309,000, so the gas is roughly R92,000 a year cheaper. The "cheaper" machine is the dearer one. Why? Because to keep the line running for the gas machine's 14 years you would have to buy the cheap diesel almost twice over, and pay its heavy fuel bill the whole time. EAC saw what the sticker price hid.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the comparison in four steps, on real quotes, never guesses. Each is a plain question with a simple sum.

  • 1. Set the discount rate. A rand next year is worth less than a rand today, so you shrink future money back to its value today - that shrunk amount is its present value, and the rate you shrink by is roughly what money costs you. Build it from a base cost of money plus the risk of your economy, write it down, and be ready to defend it - a flattering, too-low rate makes every long-lived option look better than it is.
  • 2. Add up the whole-life cost of each option (LCCA). Discount every cost across the asset's life - buy, run, maintain, dispose - back to today and total it, minus salvage. This life-cycle cost is what turns "the diesel is cheaper" into "the diesel costs more to own".
  • 3. Convert to EAC - the fair yearly cost. You cannot fairly compare a total over 8 years against a total over 14. So spread each whole-life cost into a level yearly figure, like rent: multiply the present value by the capital-recovery factor for its own life at your rate. Lowest EAC wins. EAC is the great equaliser for unequal lives.
  • 4. Decide, and name the caveat. State the winner on EAC, then stress your biggest guess: which single number, if it moved, would flip the answer - and by how much? Give the verdict and the one risk that could change it.

That four-step chain - rate, whole-life cost, EAC, decision - is the engine. Three more tools round it out when the stakes are high, and the deeper toolkit is worth knowing by name. Monte Carlo simulation re-runs the sums thousands of times across sensible ranges for the uncertain inputs, turning "the answer is R309,000" into "almost certainly between R290,000 and R330,000, and it beats diesel about nine times in ten". NPV (net present value) is the same discounting used to judge a single yes/no spend - a positive NPV adds value - with payback (how long to earn it back) and IRR (the rate at which it breaks even) as quick sense-checks. MCDA folds in what is not money - reliability of supply, safety, local jobs - by scoring and weighting each factor, so a slightly dearer option can still win on the whole picture. And every estimate carries a trust class: a back-of-envelope guess and a firm vendor quote deserve different confidence, and you should say which you are leaning on.

Do not stop at Box 2 - the whole-life total is the trap, not the answer; it is Box 3, the EAC, that compares unequal lives fairly and often flips the "obvious" choice. And do not skip Box 4: a number with no caveat is a false promise. When you have it, you hold one defensible page - "the cheap one costs more to own, here is by how much, and here is the one thing that would change my mind" - the kind of page a tight-cash owner can take to the board and act on.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your comparison honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Price is not cost. The sticker is one day's payment; the cost is every rand the asset pulls out of you across its life, minus salvage. A low buy-price routinely hides a fortune in running costs - that is the whole reason the diesel "wins" on price and loses on ownership.

• Never compare totals across unequal lives. A cost watched for 14 years will always look bigger than one watched for 8 - that is not the same as dearer. Convert to EAC first; comparing raw whole-life totals across different lifespans is the single most common mistake.

• Defend your discount rate. The rate decides everything, and a too-low one flatters every long-dated option. Build it openly from a real cost of money plus your economy's risk, and write it on the page so it can be challenged - do not borrow a comfortable textbook number.

• A single answer is a false promise. Every figure is an estimate, so stress the biggest guess and say how far it would have to move to flip the decision. "Gas wins unless its fuel cost nearly doubles" tells a board far more than a lone confident number.

• EAC ranks cost, not everything. Reliability, safety and supply risk are real and not in rand; when they matter, score and weight them alongside the EAC rather than letting the cheapest yearly cost decide on its own.

The owner was not wrong to fear the price - she was reading the only number on the quote. What you can give her is the number that is not on the quote: the true yearly cost of owning each machine, fairly compared whatever its lifespan. Use EAC to see past the sticker price to the real cost of ownership, so a capital-tight decision is a smart one and not a slow financial drain. Get that right and "buy the cheap one" becomes "buy the one that is actually cheap" - a choice you can prove, defend, and stand behind. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
