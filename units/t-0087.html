A dewatering pump 1.4 km underground is getting rougher month by month - overhaul it now for R180,000, or run it on and risk a R1.2m flood? How do I lay out this repeating choice so the best policy can be worked out, not guessed?

T1: The Ache

You are in the pump chamber on the 38 level of Lentswe Deep, a gold mine, 1.4 km underground. The air is warm and wet, and you can hear water running somewhere behind the wall. In front of you sits the No. 3 dewatering pump, the one that lifts the water seeping into the workings up to surface. If it stops for too long, the level floods, and nobody mines until it is pumped dry again.

In your hand is the monthly vibration report. Last month the pump read 4.2 mm/s. This month it reads 6.8 mm/s. The maker's "act now" line is 7.1 mm/s. The pump is getting rougher, month by month.

The shift boss wants an answer today. "Do we pull it out and overhaul it now, or run it another month?" An overhaul takes the pump out for two days and costs R180,000. But if you wait and it fails in service, the flood and the emergency repair cost the mine about R1.2 million - and a flooded level is a safety problem, not just a money one. Pulling a pump that still had months of life left wastes good money. Leaving it too long risks the big one.

It would be easy to just go with your gut: it is over 6, that feels close, pull it. But "feels close" is not a method. Next month you face the same choice again, and the month after that. You are not making one decision - you are making the same decision over and over as the pump's condition drifts, and each choice changes what state you face next.

I am not making one decision - I am making the same overhaul-or-wait choice every month as the pump drifts. How do I lay this repeating choice out honestly - the conditions, the actions, the odds, the costs - so the best policy can be worked out, not guessed?

Before you read on, decide. The pump has drifted again. Pull it now and you may throw away months of good life; leave it and you risk the flooded level and the R1.2 million. Your gut says it is over 6, that feels close, pull it. Write down how you would decide — not just this month, but every month. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one leaves unsaid

• “It feels close — pull it.” It may even be right this month. It is not a method, so it cannot be checked, cannot be handed to the next planner, and cannot be argued with by anybody who thinks differently — including you, next month, when it feels slightly less close.

• “Set a fixed threshold — always pull at 6.” A real improvement and a big hidden assumption: that the right action depends only on the reading. It takes no account of what an overhaul costs against what a failure costs, or of how likely the pump is to reach next month at all.

• “Overhaul on a fixed schedule and stop agonising.” Peaceful, and it deliberately ignores the condition information you are already collecting. Some pumps get pulled with months left; others fail before the date arrives.

• “Work out this month’s best choice properly.” Closer, and it treats one decision as though it stood alone. Whatever you choose changes the state you face next month — pulling it resets the pump, leaving it lets the drift continue — so a choice that looks best in isolation may be poor as a rule.

• “Ask the OEM what they recommend.” Useful input and it cannot answer this. They do not know your failure cost, your access, or what a flooded level means on your mine.

Say the thing you already noticed out loud, because it changes the shape of the question: you are not making one decision. You are making the same decision every month, and each answer changes what you face next.

You need a reliable way to describe the whole repeating loop - so the best policy can be worked out instead of felt. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is to formulate the problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) - a clear, four-part description of a repeating decision under uncertainty. The name sounds heavy; the idea is plain. You write down four things - the states the asset can be in, the actions you can take, the probabilities of moving between states, and the costs of each - and that honest description is something a solver can later turn into a policy: the best action in every state. Watch the planner, Mapula, formulate the pump decision - not solve it, just write the four parts honestly.

Step 1 - Set the clock (the decision point and time step)

Mapula sets it: a decision is made once a month, right after the vibration reading. The time step is one month, so every part of the model - costs, probabilities, actions - is 'per month'. This month's reading of 6.8 mm/s is the starting point for the No. 3 dewatering pump.

Step 2 - Define the states (S), and check the Markov property

Mapula uses the vibration bands the team already trusts, plus a failed state: S1 Healthy (below 4.5 mm/s), S2 Worn (4.5-6.5), S3 Degraded (6.5-7.1, near the act-now line), S4 Failed. She notes the limit honestly - a freshly overhauled pump is a true S1, but one that limped at S3 for months may not be - and writes it down to revisit. The 6.8 mm/s reading puts the pump in S3 Degraded.

Step 3 - Define the actions (A)

A0 Do nothing (allowed in S1, S2, S3 - just monitor); A1 Minor service (S2, S3 - bearings and seals, buys some life); A2 Overhaul (S2, S3 - strip and rebuild, returns to as-new); A3 Repair after failure (S4 only - the forced response). In S1, doing nothing is the only sensible choice; in S4, repair is the only choice at all.

Step 4 - Write the transition probabilities (P), each row sums to 1

From three years of history: if you DO NOTHING - S1 stays 0.85 / slips 0.13 / 0.02 / 0.00; S2 0.00 / 0.70 / 0.25 / 0.05; S3 0.00 / 0.00 / 0.60 / 0.40. If you OVERHAUL - from S2 it returns to S1 with 0.98 (or only to Worn, S2, with 0.02); from S3 it returns to S1 with 0.95 (or only to Worn, S2, with 0.05) - never back to Degraded, because an overhaul rebuilds to as-new. Every row sums to 1.00. The honest danger line: in S3, doing nothing carries a 40% chance of failure next month - the number 'feels close' could never give you.

Step 5 - Write the rewards/costs (R), add gamma, assemble the tuple

Costs: do nothing R0, minor service R35,000, overhaul R180,000, repair after failure R1,200,000 (flood + emergency rebuild + lost production). So 'S3, do nothing' is R0 today but a 40% chance of R1.2m next month (an expected R480,000); 'S3, overhaul' is R180,000 today and an almost-certain healthy state after. Mapula sets gamma = 0.99 (the pump runs for years), writes the tuple and every assumption, and hands a solver a clean, checkable model - which can now PROVE the policy she can only sense by eye.

See what the formulation did. It took a fuzzy "it feels close, pull it" and turned it into a tidy, checkable model - and in doing so it surfaced the one number the gut could never see: in the Degraded state, doing nothing carries a 40% chance of failure next month. This is the mission in it, and it is two things at once. First, honesty: the method's whole demand is that you write down every state, every cost, and every probability, and state every assumption out loud - so a reviewer can challenge any one of them, because a wrong formulation gives a confident, wrong policy, and a confident wrong answer about a pump 1.4 km underground is far more dangerous than an honest "we are not sure yet". Second, people: the failure here is not a line in a budget, it is a flooded level where men are working - so the heavy cost on failure is really the weight of their safety, made explicit, forcing the model to take it seriously. The money - R180,000 against R1.2 million - is the measure of the trade-off; the safety of the people below is the motive; and the formulation is the humble, honest part that makes the optimisation worth trusting.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the formulation in five moves.

  • 1. Set the clock. The decision point and time step - the rhythm everything lines up to.
  • 2. Define the states (S). The conditions the asset can be in - and check the Markov property.
  • 3. Define the actions (A). What you can do in each state.
  • 4. Write the transition probabilities (P). The odds of each next state - every row sums to 1.
  • 5. Write the rewards/costs (R). The cost of each action and failure; add the discount and assemble the tuple.

The ideas underneath. This is a sequential decision: you decide, time passes, the asset changes, you decide again, and today's choice shapes tomorrow's situation - a loop a one-off tool cannot hold. A state is a snapshot of the asset's condition; the word "Markov" carries one rule, the Markov property - the future depends only on the state you are in now, not the whole history of how you got there - so your states must hold enough to decide (if the past matters in a way they miss, add it to the state). An action is a maintenance choice available in a state; a transition probability is the chance, for a state-and-action, of each next state (the row must sum to 1); a reward is the value, in maintenance usually the cost, of an action - failures dominate, and we write costs as negative rewards and seek the lowest total. The discount factor (gamma, between 0 and 1) shrinks future costs slightly so the maths behaves and the near term counts for more. Put States, Actions, Probabilities and Rewards together - the tuple (S, A, P, R), sometimes with gamma - and you have formulated the MDP; a solver (by value iteration or policy iteration, taught elsewhere) then finds the policy, the best action in every state, weighing cost against risk across the whole future. It fits when the decision repeats, the condition fits a few states, the uncertainty can be put in numbers, and the current state describes the future well enough; it does not fit a true one-off (use a decision tree), a case where you cannot even roughly estimate the probabilities (then the model is fiction), or a hidden condition behind noisy readings (you need a Partially Observable MDP).

Two disciplines keep the formulation honest. First, make every probability row sum to 1, and chase down why if it does not - a row that will not total 1 is not a rounding nuisance, it is the model telling you there is a next-state you have forgotten or a number you have guessed badly; the arithmetic is the cheapest honesty check you will ever run. Second, write the assumptions down beside the model, never in your head - which probabilities came from real history and which from judgement, where the Markov property is shaky, what the failure cost includes; a formulation whose assumptions are visible can be argued with and improved, while one whose assumptions are hidden produces a confident answer nobody can question, which is exactly how a wrong policy survives.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your formulation true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Keep the state list short and honest. The fewest bands that still drive different decisions. Too many states make the model unwieldy; too few hide the danger.

• Every probability row sums to 1. If it does not, you have missed a next-state or guessed a number badly. The arithmetic is the cheapest honesty check there is.

• Check the Markov property, and admit where it is shaky. If history matters in a way the state misses, fold it in - or write the assumption down to revisit. Do not pretend it holds when it does not.

• Let the failure cost carry the real weight. A failure underground is a flooded level, not just a number. Cost it high enough that the model takes the people seriously.

• A wrong formulation gives a confident, wrong policy. The formulation is the honest, humble work. Get it right, with assumptions visible, and the optimisation can be trusted.

You came to the pump chamber with a rising number and a gut that said "close". You can leave with a clean, checkable model: the clock set, the states defined, the actions listed, the probabilities summing to 1, the costs written, the discount chosen, and every assumption on the page where a reviewer can argue with it. A clean formulation of states, actions, odds and costs - so the best policy can be proven across every future month, not guessed from this one; a wrong formulation gives a confident, wrong policy. That is how a repeating, high-stakes choice stops being a monthly gamble and becomes a decision you can defend: not by trusting the feeling that it is close, but by laying the whole honest loop on the table so the right action can be worked out - and the people 1.4 km below kept safe.
