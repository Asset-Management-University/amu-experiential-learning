How do I prove a R420 million public water scheme is good value to a national treasury, when the people it serves are 12,000 households who will never pay its full cost - and the biggest benefit, two hours a day no longer spent walking for water, appears on no balance sheet?

T1: The Ache

It is 16:40 on a Thursday and you are still at your desk in the planning office of a rural district municipality. The window behind you looks out over a dry riverbed, where 12,000 households queue at four shared standpipes - a two-hour round trip for water, every day. On the desk is a single creased A4 page: "Lethabo Bulk Water Scheme - Funding Submission, due Friday 12:00". Someone has written across the top in red pen: "Council wants a number by tomorrow."

The number they want is whether this scheme is worth building. The pipeline and treatment works will cost R420 million to build and about R18 million a year to run, and serve those 12,000 households plus a small dairy co-operative. The national grant office will only release the money if you can show the scheme is good value for the public - not just that the pipes will hold water, but that the money spent returns more in benefit to society than it costs.

You feel the pull to do the easy thing: add up the construction cost, divide by households, and write "R35,000 per household - affordable, recommend approval." But you have seen that page come back. The grant office sent the last one back with three questions you could not answer: What is the scheme actually worth to people who walk two hours for water? How do you compare R420 million today against benefits arriving slowly over thirty years? Who carries the risk if the demand never comes? A cost-per-household figure answers none of them. The dairy manager, Phelo, phoned twice today - they have held off expanding for two years waiting on this water, trucking it in at R9,000 a load.

How do I prove this R420 million scheme is good value to a national treasury, when the people it serves will never pay its full cost - and the biggest benefit, two hours a day no longer lost to walking for water, appears on no balance sheet?

Before you read on, decide. The Lethabo scheme will serve 12,000 households of about five people each, who currently lose roughly two hours a day walking for water. It will cost R420 million to build and about R18 million a year to run for thirty years. A dairy nearby needs 400 m³ a day and is trucking water in at R9,000 a load. The households will never pay anything close to the full cost. Write down how you would prove to a national treasury that this is good value — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the benefit that appears on no balance sheet

• “Work out what the users will pay and set it against what it costs.” The ordinary business appraisal, and it fails on the first line: the people who benefit are not the people who pay. Judge every rural water scheme this way and almost none is ever built, which tells you the test is wrong rather than the schemes.

• “Count the dairy’s saving — that is the benefit you can actually measure.” Real, and small beside the thing that matters. Worse, it rests the whole case on the one beneficiary who can pay, which quietly decides who the scheme is for.

• “Argue that water is a right and should not need a number.” True, and unusable. A treasury is choosing between this and other schemes; refusing to offer a comparison does not stop the comparison happening, it just means the money goes where somebody did offer one.

• “Add up thirty years of costs and show they are affordable.” Half a sum. Affordable is not the same as worth it, and it cannot rank this scheme against anything else competing for the same rand.

• “Estimate the hours saved and put a value on them.” The right move, and the one people flinch from, because pricing an hour of somebody’s walk feels like pricing a person.

• The procedure does that flinching-from thing deliberately, and in a fixed order. First state what the asset must actually deliver, so benefits cannot be invented afterwards to suit the answer. Then price the benefits that appear on no balance sheet: about 730 hours a year per household at R12 an hour is R8,760, across 12,000 households roughly R105.12m a year — beside which the health gain (R6m) and the dairy (R5m) are the small items, not the large ones. Bring thirty years into today’s money and the case can finally be compared: against R420m to build and about R202.7m of running costs in present value, the benefits are around R1,307.5m — roughly R2.10 of social benefit for every rand spent. Only then does it ask who should carry which risk, and check the guardrail: whether the people who cannot pay are actually protected by how it is funded. The two hours a day were always the biggest number in the scheme. They were simply the one nobody had written down.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to appraise a public scheme - one that handles money arriving over decades, weighs benefits to people who never pay a cent, and says plainly who should carry which risk. Here is that procedure.

T2: The Tool in Action

The procedure is public-infrastructure appraisal: the structured way governments and development funders decide whether a public asset is worth building, and how to share its risks. It is not ordinary business appraisal, because the people who benefit are often not the people who pay, and effects on health, on the environment, and on the people with the least to fall back on must be counted even when no money changes hands. It runs in five parts. Watch them on the Lethabo scheme.

Step 1 - Levels of service (what the asset must deliver)

Lethabo Bulk Water Scheme: quantity 60 litres per person per day x 5 people x 12,000 households = 3,600 m3/day, plus 400 m3/day for the dairy = 4,000 m3/day; quality treated to SANS 241 drinking standard; reliability available at least 350 days a year. Now every benefit must trace back to delivering this - which stops you inventing benefits.

Step 2 - Social benefit-cost analysis (is it worth it to society?)

Costs: build R420m (year 0), opex R18m/yr for 30 years. Benefits (shadow-priced): time saved 730 h/household/yr x R12 = R8,760, x 12,000 = R105.12m/yr; health R6m/yr; dairy R5m/yr; total R116.12m/yr. At an 8% real social rate the 30-year factor is 11.26: PV benefits R116.12m x 11.26 = R1,307.5m; PV opex R18m x 11.26 = R202.7m; construction R420m. NPV = 1,307.5 - 202.7 - 420 = +R684.8m; BCR = 1,307.5 / 622.7 = 2.10 (R2.10 of social benefit per R1 spent). Worth building.

Step 3 - PPP risk allocation (who carries which risk?)

Garib Build-Operate will build the works and run them for 10 years. Construction cost over-run and delay -> the builder (they control the build; fixed price + penalties); demand below forecast -> the public (the public chose the service level; the builder cannot control uptake); water-quality failure in operation -> the builder; currency/interest on the loan -> the public (it borrows in rand); drought reducing dam yield -> shared (neither controls rainfall). Forcing the builder to carry demand risk it cannot control would either kill the deal or spike the price.

Step 4 - Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (what harm, to whom?)

Against national environmental law and the IFC Performance Standards: the pipeline crosses a wetland at km 9 - reroute 600 m to avoid it (adds R4m, already inside the R420m); 14 households farm on the route and must be compensated and resettled to IFC Standard 5 (involuntary resettlement) so no one ends up worse off (budget R1.2m, included). A strong BCR does not excuse harm - the ESIA is a guardrail, not a formality.

Step 5 - Political-economy check (will the decision hold?)

Who gains: the 12,000 households (time, health, dignity) and the dairy. Who loses or bears cost: the 14 resettled households (must be made genuinely whole, not merely paid off), and whoever loses an informal water-trucking income. Who decides: the council, the grant office, the ward councillors. Naming this openly - and bringing the losers' voices in early - is what stops a sound scheme dying in a council meeting.

See what the five parts did. They turned "R35,000 per household" - a number that answered nothing - into a defensible case: a service standard, a benefit-cost verdict (NPV +R684.8m, BCR 2.10), a fair sharing of risk, a guardrail that protects the vulnerable, and an honest map of who decides. And notice the quiet heart of it: shadow-pricing the two-hour walk at R12 an hour is not putting a price on a person - it is the opposite. It gives a burden the market had rendered invisible an economic voice loud enough for a treasury to hear. The household that never pays a cent finally counts. Money here is the measure of the good done, never the point of doing it.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the appraisal in five parts, in order.

  • 1. Levels of service. State what the asset delivers and to what standard (quantity, quality, reliability) - so every benefit traces back to a real service, not an invented one.
  • 2. Social benefit-cost analysis. Count all costs and benefits to society; shadow-price what the market ignores; discount the future at the social rate; read off NPV and the benefit-cost ratio.
  • 3. PPP risk allocation. If a private partner helps, a matrix puts each risk on whoever can best manage it.
  • 4. ESIA. Assess harm to the environment and to people against the law and the IFC standards - it can stop or reshape a scheme regardless of the ratio.
  • 5. Political-economy check. Ask honestly who gains, who loses, and who decides.

The two ideas that make part 2 "social": shadow pricing replaces a missing or wrong market price with the true value to society (a saved hour, an avoided illness), and the social discount rate shrinks future amounts to today's value but more patiently than a bank would (a lower rate, because a society values its citizens' future more than a lender values a return). NPV above zero and a ratio above 1 mean worth building. This fits any publicly- or grant-funded infrastructure - water, roads, clinics, electricity, schools; it does not fit a purely private investment serving paying customers (use ordinary financial appraisal), or a tiny job where the appraisal would cost more than the work.

Two disciplines keep it honest. First, count the people the market forgets, but count them conservatively - shadow-price saved time and health at modest, defensible rates (a fraction of a wage, real clinic costs), because an honest R2.10 of benefit per rand survives scrutiny where an inflated R8 collapses under the first hard question. Second, let the ESIA and the political-economy check have teeth - a glowing benefit-cost ratio never excuses a family left worse off or a voice never heard; if the appraisal cannot protect the 14 households on the pipeline route, it has not done its job, however large the NPV.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your appraisal honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Cost-per-household is not value. Dividing the build cost by the number of users answers none of the real questions. It is the number that gets a submission rejected. Appraise the benefits, do not just average the cost.

• Do not ignore the un-costed benefit. If a benefit has no market price - saved time, better health, dignity - shadow-price it, conservatively. Leaving it at zero is the most common way good schemes for the people who need them most get killed.

• Do not flatter the numbers. An honest, defensible BCR beats an inflated one that dies under the grant office's first question. Use modest shadow prices and the proper social discount rate; show your working.

• A good ratio does not excuse harm. The ESIA can stop or reshape a scheme no matter how high the NPV. No one on the route ends up worse off - that is a hard constraint, not a nicety.

• Do not skip the politics. A scheme sound on paper still dies if the losers are never heard or the powerful block it. Map who gains, loses and decides - early, and out loud.

You walked in with a creased page, a council wanting a number by morning, and 12,000 households whose daily two-hour walk shows up nowhere in a treasury spreadsheet. You can walk into that meeting with something far stronger than a cost-per-household guess: a service standard, a benefit-cost case that values the people the market forgot (NPV +R684.8m, a ratio of 2.10), a fair sharing of risk, a guardrail that protects the families on the route, and an honest map of the politics. That is the craft here: translate social and environmental value into the language of a treasury - and so prove a scheme's worth to society, and win the funds to build it.
