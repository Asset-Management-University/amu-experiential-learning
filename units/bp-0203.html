Step 1 of 5

How did that 'easy' R299 a month end up costing me R2,372 extra - and how do I never get caught like that again?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Asande. More than two years ago, you needed a new phone. The one you wanted was R6,000 cash - a huge amount to find all in one go. But the store had a deal: take it home today, for 'only R299 a month' on a store card. It felt like a brilliant solution. Almost free. You walked out with the phone that same afternoon.

This morning, you made the final payment. And for the first time, you actually added it all up: R299, month after month, for twenty-eight months. You expected the total to be around R6,000.

It is not R6,000. It is R8,372.

You paid an extra R2,372 - more than a third on top of the price - just for the privilege of paying slowly. And the phone itself? It is slow now, the battery barely lasts the morning, and its trade-in value is maybe R2,000. You spent more than two years handing over R8,372 for something that is now worth a fraction of that.

The strange thing is, it never once felt expensive. Each payment was small. Easy. You barely noticed it leave. And that, you are starting to realise, was exactly the point. A small debt quietly grew into a big cost, month by month, like something rolling and growing in the dark - and you never saw it coming.

How did that 'easy' R299 a month end up costing me R2,372 extra and take years to pay off - and how do I see this trap coming so I never get caught again?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Staring at that R8,372, three reactions tempt you - and every one keeps the trap working. "Next time I'll just pay the minimum each month - it's manageable." But the minimum payment IS the trap: most of it gets eaten by interest, so what you owe barely shrinks, and you stay caught for years. "It's fine - everyone buys things on credit like this." That 'take it home today' feeling is exactly what the trap is sold on; 'normal' does not make it cheap, it just hides the total, the rate, and the years. "If money gets tight, I'll get another easy-credit deal to cover this one." That is the spiral - trying to outrun one snowball by jumping in front of a bigger one. None of these lets you actually see what happened.

So before you read on: that 'small' R299 turned into R8,372. WHY does spreading a cost into easy monthly payments make it grow like that - and what should you have looked at before you ever signed?

Focus on the gap between the two numbers. The phone's cash price was R6,000. You handed over R8,372. Where did that extra R2,372 go - and why did spreading it over 'easy' monthly payments make it MORE expensive, not less?
Think about the snowball from the last unit - the one that quietly built Atlegang's wealth, growth piling on growth over time. Now picture that exact same snowball, but rolling the OTHER way: down a hill, on top of you. What happens to money you OWE when interest piles on it, month after month?
The trap has a name: negative compounding - the wealth snowball, reversed. Interest is charged on what you owe, then next month on the debt AND that interest, so it grows on itself against you. The escape is one test: does this purchase buy something that will GROW in value (good debt), or something that will SHRINK (bad debt)? And always ask the TOTAL and the rate - never just the monthly.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The 'easy' payment was not a kindness. It was the bait on a trap - and the trap runs on an engine you have already met. Five moves:

  • See the real total, not the monthly: The trap works by hiding a big total (R8,372) behind a small-sounding monthly payment (R299). 'Only R299 a month' feels almost free; 'R8,372 for a R6,000 phone' does not. The first move is to ignore the monthly figure and ask the only question that tells the truth: what is the TOTAL I will hand over by the end?
  • The engine - the snowball in reverse: Remember the wealth-building snowball from the last unit? This is the exact same engine, reversed. With bad debt, interest is charged on what you owe - and then next month, interest is charged on the loan AND last month's interest. The debt grows on itself, faster the longer it runs. That is negative compounding: growth-on-growth, but rolling against you instead of for you.
  • Spot the bait - 'easy monthly payments': 'Take it home today, only R299 a month' is not a kindness - it is the bait. It is engineered to make the cost feel painless and to keep your eyes off the two numbers that actually matter: the interest rate, and the total cost over the whole term. Whenever someone leads with the monthly payment, that is exactly when to ask for the rate and the total.
  • Draw the line - good debt vs bad debt: Not all borrowing is the enemy. GOOD debt buys something that GROWS - in value, or in your power to earn (a tool, a course that lifts your income, like the gift you grew two units ago). BAD debt buys something that SHRINKS - a phone, clothes, a takeaway. Your phone was worth R2,000 by the time you finished paying R8,372 for it: that gap is the signature of bad debt - the snowball eating you for a thing already losing value.
  • Refuse the trap - claim the agency: You were not foolish; you were sold a cleverly designed trap that is meant to feel painless. But now you can see how it is built - and what you can see, you can refuse. Run the good/bad test on anything you are about to buy on credit. Ask the total and the rate, not the monthly. And if you are already caught, the way out is to attack the highest-rate debt first - that is the biggest snowball, so stopping it first saves you the most.

The whole thing lives in one line: bad debt is the wealth snowball from the last unit, running in reverse - the same growth-on-growth, but rolling against you, for a thing that is already losing value. That is why a 'small' R299 became R8,372: interest piled on what you owed, then on that, month after month. The good news is the same as the bad news: it is just an engine, and now you can see it. You were not careless - this trap is engineered to feel painless, and it is sold to you on purpose. But a trap you can see is a trap you can refuse. Ask the total and the rate, never just the monthly. Run the good/bad test before you sign. And if a snowball is already rolling over you, attack the highest-rate one first to stop it. You still get to have the things you want - you just save the gap and buy them without the snowball eating you alive.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own life, see the line between debt that builds you and debt that eats you - drawn clean, with two people who started from the same R5,000 wish.

See how Lihle and Banele used the same R5,000 of debt

Lihle wants to learn graphic design so she can earn on the side. A good online course costs R5,000, which she does not have. She borrows it at a fair rate. Within six months she has finished the course and is earning about R1,500 a month from small freelance jobs. Her debt bought her an ASSET - a skill - that now earns far more than the loan ever cost her. The borrowing built her up. That is GOOD debt.

Banele wants a pair of limited-edition sneakers, also R5,000. He uses a 'buy now, pay later' deal. With interest and a couple of late fees, the repayments add up to about R6,200 over eighteen months. By the time he finishes paying, the sneakers are scuffed, worn, and long out of fashion - worth maybe R500. He paid R6,200 for something now worth R500. The borrowing just ate him, for a thing that was shrinking in value the whole time. That is BAD debt.

Both started with the same R5,000 wish. Lihle's debt grew her income; Banele's debt cost him over R5,700 in lost value. The difference was not the amount, and not the borrowing itself - it was what they bought: something that GROWS, or something that SHRINKS.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Now take the test into your own life, before the next 'easy' deal finds you:

  1. Ask the total and the rate, never just the monthly. The moment anyone leads with 'only R-something a month', that is your signal. Work out what you will hand over by the very end, and the interest rate behind it. Seeing the true total is what breaks the spell of the small payment.
  2. Run the good/bad test. Does this borrowing buy something that will GROW - in value, or in your power to earn? Or something that will SHRINK - a phone, clothes, a night out? Good debt can be worth it. Bad debt lets the snowball eat you for something already losing value.
  3. If a snowball is already rolling, attack the steepest one first. If you owe on more than one thing, find the one with the HIGHEST interest rate - that is the fastest-growing snowball - and throw every spare rand at it first while paying the minimums on the rest. That stops the most damage, fastest.

You do not have to be its victim. You can ask the total, run the test, and walk past the deal that would have quietly cost you years. That is you, refusing the trap and being the change in your own future - starting with the very next offer that tries to find you.
