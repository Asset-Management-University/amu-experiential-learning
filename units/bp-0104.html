I sent good people to a job that wasn't ready and we lost a whole morning - how do I make a job truly ready BEFORE I hand it over, when stopping to check feels like the one thing I don't have time for?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tumi, and you supervise the team that keeps the factory running. An urgent call comes in: the main conveyor has stopped, and production is grinding to a halt. Under pressure, you do what seems fastest - grab a job slip, write "fix conveyor motor", and send your three best people straight out to the floor.

They arrive, ready to work, and... they can't. The replacement motor they were told to grab is the wrong one - nobody checked the serial number on the actual machine. The special wrench to remove the old motor is locked in another store, and the person with the key is off-site. And the conveyor can't be switched off safely until the line manager gives permission, and she's stuck in a meeting for the next hour. So three of your most skilled people stand idle for a whole morning, the machine stays broken longer than it needed to, and everyone is frustrated. You look at the useless slip in your hand and see the truth: the job was never actually ready to be done. You just sent it.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Under pressure, the instinct is to act immediately. You might just send the crew faster, believing speed is everything - but that's the trap: you're not finding out by doing, you're finding out by failing, with the whole team already committed and the cost at its highest; speed into an un-ready job is the slowest path of all. Or you write a longer, more detailed slip from your desk - better than nothing, but still a guess from memory, because the real surprises (the wrong part number, the rusted bolt, the locked gate) live at the real job, not in your head. Or you blame the crew for not sorting it out on arrival - but it was never theirs to discover; by the time they're standing there, tools in hand and unable to work, the time and morale are already lost.

The cost of an un-ready job always lands at the worst possible moment. So before you read on: what small thing, done before you ever handed the job over, would have caught all three problems while they were still cheap to fix?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The most expensive moment in any job is skilled people assembled, ready, and blocked - so the work to prevent it must happen before the job is released, not be discovered during it. Five moves:

  • See the trap - the un-ready job and the blocked moment: a job handed over with too little information looks like a real task, but its true shape is hidden: the doer meets the wrong part, the locked store, the access nobody arranged only AFTER they've arrived and committed - the most expensive and most demoralising moment to learn it. You didn't ready the job; you just sent it, and the surprises were waiting at the far end.
  • Looking ahead feels like waste - it's the opposite: stopping to check before you start feels like losing time you don't have, especially under pressure - but that's an illusion. A small, certain cost up front beats a large, probable cost mid-task: a 30-minute look-ahead by one person the day before is a tiny price against three skilled people losing a 4-hour morning (twelve person-hours gone). The look-ahead is the fast path, not the slow one.
  • Go and look at the real job: the simple, powerful action at the heart of it: someone physically goes and looks at the real thing the job touches, in advance. You can't check a part number from your desk or see an access problem from a diagram - reality holds the surprises. See it with your own eyes while there's still time to fix what you find, not when the crew is already standing there.
  • Answer a fixed set of plain readiness questions: before you release any job, run the same short list every time, and solve every 'no' now, cheaply: PARTS (the right ones, confirmed and in hand - this one is so vital it has its own cousin), TOOLS (all the special ones, available), ACCESS (can you physically reach it), PERMISSIONS (everything to be switched off, locked out, or approved is sorted), PEOPLE (the right specialists free when needed), TIME and CONDITIONS (the window protected, the work safe). A ready job is simply one where every answer is 'yes'.
  • Make it a standing rule someone owns: don't leave readiness to good intentions on calm days - make the look-ahead a formal step a named person owns (a supervisor, a planner, a team lead), responsible for ensuring no job reaches the doer until it's ready. 'Un-ready jobs don't get sent' becomes simply how you work. This isn't adding paperwork - it's removing chaos, and it's an act of respect for everyone the job touches.

See how it would have saved Tumi's morning. The day before, one person spends thirty minutes at the real conveyor: they read the motor's serial and confirm the right replacement, find the wrench is in a locked store and arrange the key, and book the line manager's switch-off window in advance. Thirty minutes spent - and three skilled people walk straight into a job they can finish, instead of losing a twelve-person-hour morning standing idle. The small certain cost bought out the large probable one. The most expensive moment is everyone assembled, ready, and blocked - so don't send the job, ready it: go and look at the real thing (reality holds the surprises), answer a fixed set of plain readiness questions before you release it, and make that look-ahead a standing rule someone owns - because a small certain cost up front beats a large probable cost mid-task, every time.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about factory repairs - any work handed over un-ready hits the same wall. Watch a vague IT ticket: "the reporting website is slow."

See the same look-ahead on a software ticket

A developer is handed the ticket. They burn two hours just trying to work out which report is slow, for which user, under what conditions - and they don't even have a login that can see the system's performance. They're blocked, exactly like the crew at the conveyor. Now run the look-ahead first: before assigning it, a team lead spends fifteen minutes and calls the person who reported it.

• What exactly is slow? "The 'Monthly Sales' report for the North region."
• How do I make it happen? "Log in as a Regional Manager and run it for the last 90 days."
• Do I have the access? "No - I'll need a test account with those permissions." (arrange it now)
• Who else might I need? "The database administrator on standby."

The vague complaint becomes a ready job. The developer starts on the real problem immediately - the same parts, tools, access, people questions, just in a different world.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think about the work you hand to others, or the tasks you take on yourself - how often does work begin only to be blocked by a surprise that could have been found earlier? Resist the pull of false urgency, the instinct to "just send it":

  1. Go and look at the real job. Before you or your team commits, pause and see the real thing with your own eyes - reality holds the surprises a desk can't show you.
  2. Run the short list of readiness questions. Parts? Tools? Access? Permissions? People? Time? Conditions? Find every "no" while it's still cheap to fix - a ready job is one where each answer is "yes".
  3. Make the look-ahead a standing rule someone owns. Not heroics on good days - a named person responsible for it, so "un-ready jobs don't get sent" simply becomes how you work.

Sending an un-ready job under pressure was never laziness or poor management - it's the natural pull of urgency, where stopping to check feels like the one thing you can't afford. But the look-ahead isn't paperwork; it's the opposite of chaos. It is, at heart, an act of respect: for the skill of the people doing the work, who deserve to arrive and actually work rather than stand idle and deflated; and for the people waiting on the result, who get it sooner and right. Ready the job, and you honour them both - and the very urgency that tempted you to rush is the thing the look-ahead actually serves.
