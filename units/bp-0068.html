Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Nkululeko, an asset planner at a regional telecommunications network - exchanges, backbone fibre routes and masts with thirty- to forty-year lives, serving a city whose hunger for connection grows every year. The report on your desk feels heavier than paper: the core exchange that carries the hospital, the businesses and thousands of homes has failed. It is not a surprise. Not to you. The main switch was 42 years old, two years past its end-of-life date.

For five straight years you had put its R15 million replacement into the capital plan. And for five straight years, at the final budget meeting, it was deferred. It was an easy cut to make: the equipment was still working, and deferring it helped the managers hit their tight annual number. Each year, the saving was a clear R15 million win. The risk - a number buried in your asset register - just ticked a little higher.

Today, that risk became reality. The hospital is on backup, factories are offline, and thousands of homes have lost their signal. The emergency replacement will cost R25 million and take weeks you do not have. Everyone is asking how this could happen "out of nowhere". But you were there. You saw it coming, one budget year at a time.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

First you fall into the old habit: for the other ageing assets, you agree to "defer one more year" to free up cash for the emergency - using the very logic that caused the failure to respond to the failure, balancing this year's books by making the cliff edge five years out even steeper. Next, fuelled by the crisis, you try the opposite: you bundle every overdue project into one giant R300 million "catch-up plan" and take it to the board. They look at you as if you are mad - the money simply is not there - and they ask, "why was this not planned and funded steadily over the last ten years?" You have built an unaffordable wall of work that is instantly rejected. Finally you build a beautiful 20-year asset plan showing every asset, its health and its replacement date. But it has no formal power: each year, when the pressure is on for savings, managers raid your long-term plan for cuts. "That's a problem for year seven," they say, "I have a target to hit by Friday." A plan that can be ignored is not a plan; it is a wish list.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop fighting the annual budget and start translating between clocks. Your assets live on a 40-year clock; the demand for connection grows on a 20-year clock; the budget works on a one-year clock - and the shortest clock will always shout the loudest, unless you make the others speak its language by making the cost of time visible. Five moves:

  • See the mismatch - 40-year assets, a one-year ruler: the breakthrough begins by naming the real problem out loud: you are managing assets that live thirty or forty years, serving a demand that grows over decades, with a planning-and-budget clock that ticks once a year. That is not a budget that is too small - it is a clock that is wrong. When every decision is made by someone looking only at this year's number, the cheap deferral that wins the year quietly bankrupts the decade, and the failure that was visible years out arrives looking like a bolt from the blue. The asset and the demand keep their own long clocks no matter what your financial calendar says. Until you make the long view visible, the shortest clock will always shout the loudest and win.
  • Build a long-range plan across the asset's life: you cannot defend a future you have not drawn, so build a simple, visual long-range plan spanning the asset's whole life and the demand's horizon - a lifecycle map of your top assets showing, for each, its health, when it will need major renewal, and how the demand it serves will grow. It need not be elaborate; even a one-page timeline of the next twenty years puts the decades on the table where decisions are actually made. This is the long clock written down, the thing that turns 'I have a feeling that switch is getting old' into 'here is exactly when each major asset reaches the cliff, and what must be in place before it does'. Without it, the year is the only horizon anyone can see.
  • Make the decades visible in the annual decision: the long plan only bites if its consequences appear right next to the short-term choice. So when someone proposes deferring a renewal to balance this year, show both columns side by side: 'this year's gain: save R10 million' and 'year-four consequence: an unfunded R15 million emergency at eighty percent failure risk'. Now the deferral is no longer a free saving - it is a visible loan taken against a near, dated, expensive future. You are translating the long clock into the short clock's language (money and risk, this year versus that year) so the decision-maker can finally see what the year-only view hid. The future gets a voice in today's room.
  • Smooth and stage the big commitments: a decade of deferred renewals becomes an unaffordable wall, and an unaffordable wall gets rejected - so do not present a mountain, present a staircase. Break the lumpy R300 million backlog into a steady, predictable stream of, say, R30 million a year, sequenced by risk and lead-time, so the organisation funds the decades a manageable slice at a time and is never ambushed by a sudden cliff. Smoothing turns 'we cannot possibly afford that' into 'we can do this much every year', which is both fundable and far cheaper than the emergencies that lumpiness causes. The whole-life cost falls because nothing is left to fail and be replaced in a panic at premium price.
  • Protect the long plan from the annual raid: a long-term plan that is quietly cut every year for this year's number is not a plan, it is a wish list - so give it standing. Get the board to formally adopt the smoothed multi-year capital plan, so that cutting from it requires a deliberate, documented review rather than a casual deferral at the final budget meeting. When the long plan has a name, a place at the table and a rule that protects it, the short term can no longer silently eat the long term one easy cut at a time. This is the governance that holds the other four moves in place: it makes the long clock count, year after year, instead of being overruled the moment the annual pressure rises.

The surprises stop; the renewals happen before the failures; the network stays up for the people who depend on it. Your assets and the people who depend on them live on a decades-long clock. If you govern them by a one-year clock, the year will always win while the decade always loses. So plan to the life of the asset and the horizon of the need, not to the financial year: build the long-range plan, make the decades visible right next to this year's saving, smooth the big commitments into a steady stream, and give the long plan standing so the short term cannot quietly eat it. Make the long view visible, and fund the decades one slice at a time.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - think about a family's finances.

See the clocks in a household budget

A family can budget month-to-month for food, rent and petrol. But the car has a ten-year life, the roof a twenty-year life, and a child's education is a fifteen-year commitment. If they only ever balance this month's budget, they will never set aside anything for the new car or the new roof - and those big, predictable costs will arrive as a "surprise" crisis.

The fix is a long-term plan translated into this month: they work out they need to save, say, R500 a month for the car and R300 for the roof, and that R800 becomes a fixed line in every monthly budget. They are funding their decade-long needs with a small slice of every month - planning to the horizon of the need, not just to the next payday. The big costs stop being surprises, because the long clock now has a place in the short one.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own work. Where is a long-life commitment, asset or relationship being managed by a short-term clock - where are you winning the year but quietly losing the decade? The health of your machinery, the skills of your team, your relationship with a key supplier all have long lives. You can start to fix this today.

  1. Map the long horizon. For one critical asset or commitment, ask: what is its true lifespan? What will it need in year 5, 10, 20? Draw that timeline.
  2. Make the trade-off visible. Next time a decision is made for a short-term gain (like deferring maintenance), calculate the long-term cost or risk and put the two numbers side by side.
  3. Smooth the curve. Identify the big, lumpy investments on the horizon and ask: "how could we break this into smaller, steady steps starting now?"
  4. Give the long plan standing. Write a simple one-page summary of your long-term needs, give it a name, and ask for it to be formally reviewed - not quietly cut.
