It all sounded so certain - so how did I buy someone else's problem?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and a second bakkie would let you grow. A seller has one, and he is good - relaxed, certain, fluent: "Runs perfectly. One careful owner. Papers all in order. Honestly, another buyer is coming this afternoon, so you'd want to decide today." Everything he says sounds complete and true, and the clock is ticking. So you commit. You hand over R40,000 - a huge chunk of your savings.

A week later, the engine starts to knock. The mechanic's face says it before he does: a serious fault, R15,000 to fix - and that's if the papers even turn out clean, which now you're not sure of. The seller's phone is off. The problem that was his last week is, this morning, entirely yours.

It all sounded so certain - so how did I end up buying someone else's problem?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You reach for the usual lessons, and none quite holds. You tell yourself you should have trusted your gut - but your gut liked him; he was so sure, and sureness felt like proof. You vow to "be more careful next time" - but you were careful, and willpower melts the moment the next good deal has a deadline on it. So you swing the other way and decide to check absolutely everything about everything from now on - which is just a different way to freeze, and while you freeze, the clock wins and you check nothing.

So before you read on: when you made that call, whose information was it built on - and what, exactly, did the moment of paying hand over to you?

A confident seller, a great bakkie, a ticking clock - 'runs perfectly, one owner, papers clean, decide today'. Every fact came from him. I paid R40,000, and a week later the engine knocks - R15,000 to fix, and it's all mine now. It sounded so certain. How did I buy someone else's problem?
He was confident - but confident isn't the same as correct, and the moment I paid, his problem became mine. What if I'd checked the few things that would hurt most if false - using someone who doesn't gain from my yes?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The trap was mistaking confidence for correctness. The way through isn't to distrust people - it's a calm, professional habit you run before any big, hard-to-undo commitment. Five moves:

  • Spot the single self-interested source: notice where ALL your facts are coming from: the one person who gains if you say yes. 'Runs perfectly, one owner, papers clean' - every word from the seller. That isn't lying; it's just that you're deciding on information from someone with something to win. Once you SEE that, everything else follows.
  • Confidence is not correctness: he was fluent, certain, relaxed - and none of that makes a single claim true. We read confidence as proof, but they are different things. And here is the sting that changes everything: the moment you commit, the cost of any hidden problem stops being his and becomes entirely YOURS.
  • Name the few load-bearing claims: don't try to check everything - that's paralysis, and the clock wins. Pick the 2-3 claims that would hurt MOST if they turned out false. For a bakkie: the engine is sound; he really owns it and the papers are clean; it hasn't been in a big smash. Everything else is detail; these are load-bearing.
  • Verify each via a source that doesn't gain from your yes: for each load-bearing claim, find someone or something with nothing to win from your decision: an independent mechanic to look at the engine; the actual registration papers and an ownership check; a quick history check. An R600 inspection that saves you from a R15,000 hidden repair isn't a cost - it's the smartest R600 you'll ever spend.
  • Read the willingness to be checked: how the other side reacts to a fair, reasonable check is itself information. An honest seller with a good product welcomes it - it proves their quality. Strong resistance to a reasonable check is the one real red flag. Frame it as YOUR process, not suspicion of them: 'This looks great; because it's a big commitment, my standard step is to have my mechanic take a quick look - when suits you?'

See the trade plainly: an independent check might cost R600; the hidden fault cost R15,000 - so the R600 was never a cost, it was the smartest spend available, buying down a twenty-five-times-bigger risk. This is exactly the voice Ibraaheem would have been: "Brother, for R40,000, let's pay R600 to have someone look at the engine first - what if?" And hold the heart of it: you verify the commitment, not the person. Most sellers are honest; a fair check insults no one - an honest seller with a good thing welcomes it. You're not deciding whether to trust a human being; you're refusing to let one confident voice, with everything to gain, be your only source on a decision you can't undo.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This habit guards any commitment that's costly and hard to undo - watch it save a deposit.

See the same move when renting a place

You're about to sign a year's lease and hand over a big deposit. The agent is warm and rushing you: "The geyser's perfect, the place is secure, and yes, the owner's happy to rent - but someone else views it at four, so let's sign now." Every fact comes from the one person who gains if you say yes, and the clock is ticking - the bakkie all over again.

Same move, calmly:

• The few load-bearing claims: the hot water actually works; the place is genuinely secure; the person really has the right to rent it to you.

• Verify via a source that doesn't gain: run the taps and feel for hot water yourself; try the locks and look at the door; ask to see the lease and a proof of ownership (a title deed or a utility bill in the owner's name).

• Read the willingness to be checked: an honest landlord shows you gladly; someone who won't let you run a tap or see a single paper has just answered your biggest question.

A few minutes and a little awkwardness - against a lost deposit and a year in a place that isn't what you were promised.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of a costly, hard-to-undo commitment ahead of you - buying something big, signing something long, putting money in on someone's strong word. Before you commit, run the habit:

  1. Name the source who gains from your "yes". If most of your facts come from the one person who wins when you commit, that's your signal to check - not because they're dishonest, but because confidence isn't correctness.
  2. Pick the 2-3 claims that would hurt most if false. Not everything - just the load-bearing ones, the facts that would turn this into someone else's problem landing on you.
  3. Verify each via someone with nothing to gain, and watch the reaction. A neutral expert, the real papers, a quick independent check. Frame it as your standard process; a willing yes is reassuring, strong resistance is your answer.
R

Name your next big commitment and the one check you'll run before it. (Working with an AMU facilitator? List the load-bearing claims together.)

You will never have every fact, and you don't need to - certainty isn't the goal, and suspicion isn't either. The goal is simply this: never let a single voice with everything to gain be your only source on a decision you can't take back. Spend a little to check the few things that matter, treat a willingness to be checked as the good sign it is, and you keep the one thing the confident seller was quietly counting on you to give away - the few minutes it takes to find out whether the certainty is real.
