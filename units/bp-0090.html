Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sade, and you run a small community clinic. After months of waiting, a brand-new, high-tech blood-analysis machine arrives. The project team who bought it are thrilled: they run a test, it works perfectly, they get you to sign a form, and they leave. Everyone is congratulated. The machine is "done".

On Monday you try it on the first patient - an error code flashes, and there's no instruction manual anywhere. On Tuesday a small plastic clip snaps - there's no box of spares, and the helpline needs a contract number you don't have. On Wednesday the screen demands a "weekly cleaning cycle" no one on your team was ever shown. By Friday you try to report the fault to the hospital's maintenance team - but the machine isn't on their list of clinic equipment; as far as the system knows, your clinic doesn't even own it. The machine that cost a fortune to help your patients is now a big, useless box gathering dust.

It exists, it was signed off as finished, everyone's moved on - so why can't I actually run the thing I've been handed, and how did it arrive so un-ready?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You do what good people do: you try to make it work. You spend hours googling for a manual for that exact model; you tape the snapped clip back together; you guess at the cleaning cycle and hope you don't make things worse. But the patches don't hold - the machine is unreliable, you can't trust its results, so you go back to sending blood samples to the main hospital, adding a day's anxious delay for every patient. The very problem the machine was bought to solve is still here.

You feel like you've failed - but the problem isn't you; it was baked in long before the machine arrived. So before you read on: it exists and it worked in a demo - yet you can't run it. What was actually missing, and when was it really lost?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

A thing isn't "done" because it exists - it's done when the people who must live with it can actually run it and keep it running. The shiny machine working once in a demo is not the same as you being able to run it for years. Five moves:

  • See the trap - 'existing' is not 'runnable': a project team's job is often to make a thing exist and pass a test in ideal, day-zero conditions; YOUR job is to run it for years in real conditions - and real conditions need support the demo never showed. Don't confuse a successful demonstration with a service that can actually run; the shiny thing working once is not the same as you being able to keep it working.
  • A signature verifies readiness, it doesn't create it: the form you sign at handover isn't a magic wand that makes the thing ready - it's the moment you VERIFY it's ready. It's your one chance, before everyone moves on, to check that the support you'll need is actually there. Treat the signature as a checkpoint, not a celebration.
  • Run the five readiness checks: before you accept it, check the five things the people who run it need from day one: RECORDS (is it on our official list of what we own and must look after?); DOCUMENTS (simple instructions to use it, clean it, fix common faults?); SPARES & ACCESS (a starting set of spares, who to call, and can we physically reach the parts?); a ROUTINE OF UPKEEP (a clear schedule of checks and cleaning?); TRAINED PEOPLE (has someone been properly shown, and shown they can do it?).
  • Separate true blockers from safe follow-ups: you don't need everything at once. Ask: 'what stops us using this safely and correctly on day one?' A missing manual or no training is a TRUE BLOCKER - don't accept it as done. A plan for the first big service in six months is a SAFE FOLLOW-UP - accept it with a written plan for when it'll be sorted. Proportionate, not endless box-ticking.
  • The real fix is upstream - ask at the start: every missing thing was lost at the beginning, when the project was planned and nobody asked a person like you 'what will you need on day one?'. So next time you build or buy something for others to run, get the people who'll operate and maintain it into the room at the START - changes are nearly free while it's still a sketch, and ruinously dear once it's built.

See what really happened to Sade. The machine passed its day-zero demo, so everyone treated the signature as the finish line - but it should have been the moment to check. Run the five readiness things and every gap shows: no record (not on the equipment list), no documents (no manual), no spares or access (snapped clip, no contract number), no routine of upkeep (the unshown cleaning cycle), no trained people. The missing manual and the missing training were true blockers she should never have accepted; a six-month-service plan could have safely followed. And every one of those gaps was lost right at the start, when the project was planned and nobody asked her, "what will you need on day one?" A handover signature verifies readiness; it doesn't create it - so a thing is only finished when the people who must live with it can run and keep it running, and the real fix is to ask, at the very start, what they'll need.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about machines - the handover gap opens wherever something built is handed to someone else to run. Watch it with software.

See the same gap when a software team "ships" an app

A software team builds a new expenses app for your company and "ships" it on Friday. On Monday, nobody can even log in. Run the same five checks:

• Trained people? No - nobody was shown how to use it.
• Documents? No - there's no user guide.
• Routine of upkeep? No - no one was made an 'admin' to add staff or reset passwords.
• Spares & access? No - there's no support line for when it crashes.
• Records? No - IT doesn't even know it exists, so no one has checked it's secure.

The app "exists" but isn't runnable; the ship-it signature was mistaken for readiness. Same fix: check the five things at handover - and next time, get the people who'll use it (and the IT team who'll support it) in the room right from the start.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of the last thing you were handed and told was "finished" - a new process, a new tool, a "finished" renovation. Was it merely existing, or truly runnable from the moment you got it?

  1. Run it through the five checks. Were the records updated? Did you get the instructions? Did you know how to get spares and support? Was there a routine for its upkeep? Was anyone trained?
  2. Sort the gaps. Which were true blockers (no using it safely without them) and which were safe follow-ups (fine to accept with a dated plan)?
  3. Look forward - find the operator at the start. Next time you build, buy, or create something for someone else to run, find the person who'll have to live with it for years, bring them in while the plan is still a sketch, and ask them: "what will you need on day one to run this and keep it running?"

Involving the operators at the start might add a small cost or a few days to a plan - but a R100,000 machine sitting idle for six months for want of a R500 spares kit or a R5,000 training course isn't a saving; it's a quiet tragedy, and worst of all the patients it was bought to help waited anyway. The thing existing was never the point; the point was always the service it could give. So don't hand over a problem, and don't accept one in silence - hand over, and demand, readiness. That is how what we build actually reaches the people it was built for.
