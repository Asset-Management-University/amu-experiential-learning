T1: The Ache

You are standing on the gantry above Mill 3 at the Mkhulu Manganese beneficiation plant. It is a Tuesday, a little after seven. Below, the ball mill turns with that deep, steady grinding hum you have learned to read in your chest before you read it on a screen.

In your hand is the failure report for the Mill 3 main gearbox that seized eleven days ago. The repair bill was R2.4 million. The lost production while the spare gearbox was railed in from a depot hundreds of kilometres away was nine days - about R6.8 million of manganese concentrate that never left the plant. At the bottom, your maintenance manager, Sindi, has written one line in blue pen: "The bearing was screaming for three weeks. Nobody was listening."

She is next to you now, arms folded. "The board wants sensors on everything," she says. "Vibration, temperature, oil, the lot - Mill 3, Mill 4, the thickeners, the pumps. They want it warning us weeks ahead, not hours." She taps the printout. "But the last contractor who 'put sensors on everything' left us with a cupboard full of dead battery loggers nobody could read, and an invoice for R900,000."

You feel the pull to do the easy, visible thing: phone a vendor, ask for "a predictive maintenance package", and let them quote. That is exactly what got you the dead loggers. There are forty-odd machines in your head right now, each in a different place - some near power and a network point, some out at the tailings dam with no signal and no mains. The data has to get from a screaming bearing all the way to someone who can act, reliably, every day, for years, without being hacked. Buying parts piecemeal will not do that.

The board wants 'sensors on everything', and the last attempt was a cupboard of dead loggers. How do I design the whole path - from the sensor on the machine to the warning on someone's screen - securely and affordably, before I buy a single device?

Before you read on, decide. The board wants sensors on 42 assets. Eight are mill and conveyor drives with mains power and a network point. Twenty are thickeners and pumps near the plant, also on mains. Fourteen are tailings pumps 1.8 km out, with no mains and no cable — trenching alone was quoted at R310,000. The last attempt at this ended as a cupboard of dead loggers. Write down how you would design it before buying anything — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and which of the 42 actually decides the design

• “Choose the best sensor and buy forty-two of them.” This is the cupboard of dead loggers, repeated. It settles the easy thirty-eight and decides the hardest fourteen by not thinking about them, which is precisely how the last attempt ended up in a cupboard.

• “Trench a cable to the tailings dam so everything is the same.” Tidy, consistent, and R310,000 gone before a single sensor is bought. It treats the awkward constraint as an obstacle to remove rather than as the most useful piece of information on the page.

• “Start with a pilot on the mill and expand later.” Sounds prudent, and it pilots the eight assets nobody was worried about. It will succeed, and prove nothing about the part that killed the last attempt.

• “Buy a platform that does all of it and connect the sensors to that.” Choosing the far end first. The platform was never what stopped the loggers working.

• “Ask the vendor to design it.” They will — around what they sell, and their catalogue has no entry for a pump 1.8 km from the nearest wire.

• What the five have in common is that they buy a device when the thing that has to work is a path: from the bearing, through something that powers and carries the reading, to a person who can act on it — every day, for years. Decide that path on paper, layer by layer, and the layers constrain each other: what carries the data decides what power is available, which decides what sensor is even possible. So the fourteen pumps out at the tailings dam are not an awkward corner to solve last. They are the constraint that sizes the whole design, and the eight easy ones follow from it rather than the other way round. And the reason the last attempt died in a cupboard was not the sensors at all: nothing connected a reading to somebody whose job it was to do something about it.

You need a dependable way to design the whole path before a single device is bought. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

What you need is IIoT architecture design: deciding, on paper, the complete path your machine data will travel - from sensor, through a local gateway, up to the cloud - and how it stays secure and affordable along the way, before you buy anything. ("IIoT" is the Industrial Internet of Things: ordinary machines fitted with sensors that send their condition data onward so people can act.) It is five decisions made in order, each building on the last. Watch them run on the Mkhulu plant.

Step 1 - Lay out the layers (edge / platform / enterprise)

For Mkhulu's 42 assets: edge = the sensors plus one rugged edge gateway per plant area; platform = a cloud time-series database and analytics service (year one R140,000); enterprise = Sindi's dashboard plus a feed into the existing CMMS, so a warning becomes a work order. You decide on three edge gateways - one for the mill house, one for the thickener and pump bank, one out at the tailings pumps.

Step 2 - Specify the sensing against real constraints

Mills and conveyor drives (8 points, mains, high vibration/heat/dust) -> wired triaxial vibration + temperature, 8 x R9,800 = R78,400. Thickeners and near-plant pumps (20 points, mains nearby) -> wired vibration + temperature, 20 x R9,800 = R196,000. Tailings pumps (14, 1.8 km out, no mains and no cable - trenching was quoted at R310,000, alone enough to blow the budget) -> battery-and-small-solar wireless sensors over a low-power radio, 14 x R14,500 = R203,000, far cheaper and surviving a power cut. Sensing subtotal R477,400; three edge gateways at R42,000 = R126,000.

Step 3 - Choose the data pipeline (the right protocol per link)

Inside the plant -> OPC UA in its PubSub mode (rich self-description - 'I am Pump 14, my bearing-end vibration is 7.1 mm/s' - and the wired network can carry it). The 1.8 km tailings link, wireless and metered -> MQTT with the Sparkplug B convention: tiny messages, and its birth-and-death certificates mean the platform knows within seconds if the tailings gateway drops, instead of going silently blind. An MQTT broker on the plant server: R55,000.

Step 4 - Push intelligence to the edge where it pays (TinyML)

Raw vibration from 42 machines streamed continuously would be about 11 GB a day. Instead, each gateway runs a TinyML model trained on the early bearing-fault 'scream' Sindi's note described; it analyses the full-rate vibration locally and sends only a once-a-minute health score, plus a full burst only when the score crosses a threshold. The tailings link now carries about 40 MB a day, not gigabytes - and if the radio drops, each gateway keeps detecting on its own and raises the alert the moment the link returns. Model development: R180,000.

Step 5 - Secure it by design (IEC 62443 zones and conduits)

Zone A is plant control (the gateways and plant server, highest trust); Zone B is enterprise (dashboards and CMMS); Zone C is the cloud. Zone A reaches the cloud through one outbound, encrypted, certificate-authenticated channel - with NO inbound connections to the plant at all; the tailings link is its own encrypted conduit, each sensor holding its own credential so a stolen sensor cannot impersonate another. Security work: R120,000, plus R260,000 to install and commission the whole system. Add it up - sensors R477,400, gateways R126,000, broker R55,000, TinyML R180,000, cloud R140,000, security R120,000, installation R260,000 - and the full design totals R1,358,400, under the R1.6m ceiling with R241,600 spare; against a single avoided Mill 3 event (R9.2m), it pays for itself nearly seven times over.

See what the design did. It turned "sensors on everything" - the phrase that bought the last cupboard of dead loggers - into a whole, costed, defensible path from the bearing to Sindi's screen, for R1.36 million against a R1.6 million ceiling. And see the mission in it, because this is care made into engineering. The note on the report - "the bearing was screaming for three weeks, nobody was listening" - is the whole point: a machine was trying to warn its keepers and the message had nowhere to go. Designing the path is how you make sure someone IS listening, reliably, for years. But listening is not enough on its own. The design refuses three easy failures at once: it refuses the useless (matching every sensor to the real power and signal at its machine, so none ends up a dead logger in a cupboard); it refuses the unaffordable (wireless solar at the tailings dam instead of R310,000 of trenching, the alert sent instead of the gigabytes); and it refuses the unsafe (a plant full of connected sensors is a plant full of new doors, so security is designed in, not bolted on - a hacked sensor must not become a hacked plant). It even spends R120,000 on security that shows no dashboard at all, because the alternative is a new danger dressed as progress. The R9.2 million the seized gearbox cost is the measure of what was at stake; the aim is the next scream heard in time, and the plant kept safe in both senses - mechanical and digital.

T3: The Blueprint

You make five decisions, in order.

  • 1. Lay out the layers. Edge, platform, enterprise - the three-tier shape of the whole system.
  • 2. Specify the sensing against real constraints. Each sensor and gateway matched to power and environment.
  • 3. Choose the data pipeline. The right protocol for each link - rich inside, lightweight across weak links.
  • 4. Push intelligence to the edge. Decide what thinking happens on the machine versus in the cloud.
  • 5. Secure it by design. Zones and conduits, built in from the start, not bolted on.

The ideas underneath. The three tiers - the edge (sensor and the small computer beside the machine), the platform (cloud or central server that stores and analyses), and the enterprise (the dashboards and work-order systems people use) - are the spine of the IIC Reference Architecture, the industry's standard map. A protocol is the agreed messaging language: OPC UA (often its PubSub mode) suits the plant network, where machines describe themselves richly; MQTT with the Sparkplug B convention suits weak or metered links, being sparing with data and knowing within seconds when a device drops off. TinyML means running a small trained model on the tiny edge computer, so the fault is recognised on the spot and only the alert - not gigabytes of raw signal - travels onward, which also lets the edge keep working through a dropped link. And IEC 62443, the international industrial-cyber-security standard, builds protection in through zones and conduits: group devices that can trust each other into a zone, lock every connection between zones, and apply least-privilege so a dashboard may read but never command a machine. It fits whenever you put monitoring or automation across more than a handful of assets, in more than one place, that must run for years and carry data off-site; it is over-engineering for a single machine in one room watched by one local display.

Two disciplines keep the architecture honest. First, design the whole path before you buy any of it - the dead-logger cupboard is what happens when you start at the sensor (the visible, easy purchase) instead of at the path; a sensor with nowhere to send its data, or no power where it sits, is money already wasted, so the order is layers first, devices last. Second, treat security as a design layer, not a line item to trim - it is the easiest thing to cut when the total runs high and the cruellest thing to have cut when a breach takes the plant down; budget it from the start, accept that it buys no dashboard, and remember that connecting a plant to the outside world without it does not add a capability, it adds a door.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your architecture true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Layers first, devices last. Draw the edge-platform-enterprise path before you price a sensor. A device with nowhere to send its data is a dead logger waiting to happen.

• Match the kit to the place. Power and environment decide the sensor, not the brochure. A mains-only device at the tailings dam is wasted money.

• The right protocol per link, not one everywhere. Rich OPC UA inside the plant; lightweight MQTT across the weak, metered link. Forcing one onto both costs data or detail.

• Send the alert, not the gigabytes. Let the edge think (TinyML), so the link carries decisions, not raw data - and keeps detecting when the link drops.

• Security is a layer, not a line to trim. Zones, conduits, least-privilege, designed in. A connected plant without it has not gained a feature; it has gained a door.

You can leave with a whole path on paper: three tiers drawn, every sensor matched to its power and place, the right protocol on each link, the thinking pushed to the edge, the zones and conduits locked - all of it costed at R1.36 million, under the ceiling, and worth nearly seven times its price against one prevented failure. Design the whole path before you buy - so the next screaming bearing meets someone listening, not a cupboard of dead loggers. That is how a plant turns raw signals into warnings that arrive in time: not by buying the loudest vendor's box, but by drawing the path from the bearing to the person, end to end, useful and affordable and safe, before a single device is bought.
