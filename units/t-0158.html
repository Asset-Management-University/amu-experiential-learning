Twenty-seven years to do it properly

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and the instruction is reasonable: put every asset at Corathyl Water Treatment Works on a proper maintenance plan. All 1,200 of them.

You know how to do this properly. You can run a real RCM analysis - functions, failure modes, consequences, the task selected because the evidence supports it and not because somebody once wrote it down. You have done good ones. So you work out what doing it properly would take: about ten analyst-days per asset, two analysts, 1,200 assets.

Twenty-seven years.

Your rigorous, honest, textbook-correct approach would finish long after the assets it started on had been scrapped and replaced. And the only alternative anybody offers you is the thing you have watched fail at every works you have ever visited: one template, applied to everything, so that the raw-water pump that can leave the town dry gets the same three lines as the bench grinder in the workshop.

So you are stuck between a plan you cannot finish and a plan that protects nothing - and the quiet, awful part is that the second one will happen by default, because the first one cannot.

Doing it properly for every asset would take twenty-seven years, and doing it uniformly protects nothing. How do I plan a whole portfolio when rigour itself is the thing I am short of?

Before you read on, decide. 1,200 maintainable assets. A reliability team of two. A proper analysis takes about ten analyst-days per asset, and there are roughly 220 working days in a year each. You have done enough of that sum to know it does not land in months or even a few years. Meanwhile the plan actually in use is a three-line template applied uniformly — so the raw-water pump that can leave the town dry gets exactly what the bench grinder in the workshop gets. Write down how you would plan a whole portfolio when the thing you are short of is rigour itself — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the plan that wins by default

• “Ask for more analysts.” Do the arithmetic before the meeting. To finish this in a couple of years you would need a reliability department the size of an operations department — and even granted, you would be analysing the first assets again by the time you reached the last.

• “Start at the top of the register and work down.” An order, not a strategy. The register is not sorted by anything that matters, so you would spend your first three years on whatever happens to be alphabetically early.

• “Simplify the method so it fits.” This is what has already happened, and it is the trap: a method thin enough to apply everywhere is thin enough to protect nothing, and it hands the critical pump the same three lines as the grinder.

• “Buy software to speed up the analysis.” Tools help at the margin and do not change the order of magnitude. The scarce thing is judgement, and judgement is what takes the ten days.

• “Do the best we can and accept it will never be finished.” Honest about the constraint and silent on the decision — which means the uniform template continues by default, and a plan nobody chose becomes the plan. That is the quiet, awful part.

• The reframe: rigour is a budget, not a standard. Analysis costs time that exists in a fixed quantity, so the question was never how thoroughly but where the thoroughness goes. Price the methods honestly first, in the units you actually spend, using your own past studies rather than somebody's benchmark — full analysis at around ten days, a streamlined plan at about one, a deliberate run-to-failure decision at a fraction of one. Then do the impossible sum and keep it: 1,200 at ten days is 12,000 days against your 440 a year — better than twenty-seven years. That number is not a defeat, it is the evidence that the real decision was always allocation. Now band the portfolio by what happens when each thing fails — reaching a person, reaching the public, or merely costing money — and never by what it cost to buy, because a cheap relief valve standing between a vessel and a catastrophe outranks an expensive pump with a standby beside it. Deepest tier gets the full method, the middle a lighter one, and the rest an honest run-to-failure decision — which is a legitimate strategy when chosen and an accident when it merely happens.

The way out is to stop treating rigour as a standard and start treating it as a budget. Here it is, for the portfolio.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the tiered maintenance plan - the same 1,200 assets, with the thinking deliberately spent where it pays.

1. What does rigour actually cost? (price it in analyst-days)

Rigour is not free, so price it. Corathyl has 1,200 maintainable assets and a reliability team of two, which is 2 x 220 = 440 analyst-days a year. Measured on our own past studies, the benchmarks for THIS team are: a FULL RCM analysis 10 analyst-days per asset; a STREAMLINED plan (template plus PM optimisation) 1 analyst-day; a run-to-failure decision with a basic visual check 0.1 analyst-day. Now the sum nobody had done: 1,200 x 10 = 12,000 analyst-days = 27.3 YEARS. The honest, textbook-correct plan is not expensive, it is IMPOSSIBLE - it would still be unfinished when the assets it began with were scrapped. That number is not a defeat. It is the evidence that the real decision is not HOW to analyse, but WHERE.

2. Which assets deserve the thinking? (tier by consequence, never by cost)

Band the portfolio by what happens WHEN IT FAILS - safety, environment, supply to the town, and cost - and never by what the asset cost to buy. Criticality is a property of the consequence, not of the price tag: a cheap pressure relief valve standing between a vessel and a catastrophe outranks an expensive pump with an installed standby. Scoring Corathyl's 1,200 gives TIER 1: 60 assets whose failure reaches the public or a person; TIER 2: 240 whose failure costs real money or production but is contained; TIER 3: 900 whose failure is an inconvenience absorbed by a spare, a workaround or an afternoon. The tiers are not labels of importance to the people who run them - they are a statement about consequence, and they must survive being read aloud to the board.

3. What method does each tier get? (match depth to consequence)

Now spend the rigour where it earns. TIER 1 gets a FULL RCM analysis with condition-based triggers and tighter inspection frequencies - the deep, failure-mode-by-failure-mode work. TIER 2 gets a STREAMLINED plan from a template PLUS a PM OPTIMISATION pass, and note that this tier is where tasks get CUT, not just copied: a structured review commonly finds 30 to 40 percent of existing PM tasks have no technically justified basis, and removing those is the fastest saving available. TIER 3 gets RUN-TO-FAILURE plus a basic visual check. Run-to-failure is not neglect and not an admission of defeat - it is a legitimate engineered choice, one of the outcomes RCM's own decision logic produces alongside scheduled restoration, scheduled discard, condition monitoring and failure-finding. But it is a DECISION, so it gets recorded as one, with its spares and response implications faced.

4. Does the plan actually close? (prove the budget)

Prove it closes, or it is a wish. TIER 1: 60 x 10 = 600 analyst-days. TIER 2: 240 x 1 = 240. TIER 3: 900 x 0.1 = 90. Counts check: 60 + 240 + 900 = 1,200. Effort: 600 + 240 + 90 = 930 analyst-days, which against 440 a year is 2.1 YEARS. The same portfolio, the same two analysts: 27.3 years becomes 2.1, a 12.9-fold reduction in analytical effort. And here is the part that answers the objection you will hear - the 60 critical assets do not get LESS attention under this plan, they get MORE, because their 10 days each is real instead of being notionally promised and endlessly deferred behind 1,140 assets that never needed it. A finishable plan is the difference between a strategy and an intention.

5. What moves an asset, and what may never move? (escalation and the hard floor)

Tiers are a hypothesis about consequence, so write what CORRECTS them: a tier-3 failure that actually hurt or cost more than expected, a change in duty or in redundancy, a new hazard, a near-miss. An asset that bites gets re-tiered, and the register records why. THEN THE HARD FLOOR, which no budget argument may cross. The tiering of an asset is driven by the credible CONSEQUENCES of its failure, not its capital cost. An asset may be placed in run-to-failure IF AND ONLY IF its functional failure has NO significant safety or environmental consequence. Furthermore, any asset whose failure is HIDDEN from the operators in the normal course of their duties cannot be run-to-failure; it requires a calculated failure-finding task. A cheap pressure relief valve that prevents a catastrophic vessel failure is a TIER 1 asset. Mis-categorising it is not a cost saving; it is negligence.

Read box 4 twice. The objection you will meet is that tiering short-changes the assets at the bottom. The arithmetic says the opposite is what was happening: under the uniform plan the critical sixty were promised ten days each and would have waited years behind eleven hundred assets that never needed them. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A maintenance strategy for a whole works is not really about maintaining assets. It is about allocating a finite budget of analytical rigour - deciding where thinking hard pays the most. Five questions:

  • What does rigour actually cost? Price each planning method in analyst-days from your own past studies, count the portfolio, and do the sum. If the uniform answer is measured in decades, you have learned that your real decision was never how to analyse but where.
  • Which assets deserve the thinking? Criticality banding on the credible consequences of failure - safety, environment, supply, cost - and explicitly never on capital cost. Criticality is a property of the consequence, not of the price tag.
  • What method does each tier get? The top tier gets full analysis with condition-based triggers; the middle gets a streamlined plan plus a PM optimisation pass, which is where unjustified tasks get cut - a structured review commonly finds 30 to 40 per cent of existing PM tasks have no technically justified basis; the bottom gets run-to-failure with a basic visual check. Run-to-failure is a legitimate engineered choice, one of the outcomes RCM's own decision logic produces alongside scheduled restoration, scheduled discard, condition monitoring and failure-finding - it is a decision, recorded as one, with its spares and response implications faced.
  • Does the plan close? Sum the analyst-days and test them against the team you actually have. A plan that cannot be finished is not a strategy, it is an intention - and intentions are what leave critical assets uncovered while the paperwork says otherwise.
  • What moves an asset, and what may never move? Escalation rules - a failure that hurt, a change in duty or redundancy, a new hazard - because tiers are a hypothesis that reality is entitled to correct. And beneath them, the hard floor.

The hard floor, which no budget argument may cross. The tiering of an asset is driven by the credible consequences of its failure, not its capital cost. An asset may be placed in run-to-failure if, and only if, its functional failure has no significant safety or environmental consequence. Furthermore, any asset whose failure is hidden from the operators in the normal course of their duties cannot be run-to-failure; it requires a calculated failure-finding task. A cheap pressure relief valve that prevents a catastrophic vessel failure is a tier 1 asset. Mis-categorising it is not a cost saving; it is negligence.

One honest boundary. You are designing the thinking process for the whole works - deciding which sixty assets deserve the full ten-day study and which nine hundred are correctly managed by a recorded run-to-failure decision. You are not performing the RCM analysis itself, not writing an individual machine's plan, and not setting any single task's interval. Those are separate techniques, and this one decides how many of them you can afford to run and on what.

Does it scale? Think of yourself as running the casualty department of this plant. One patient has a cut finger, one a sprained ankle, and one is bleeding from an artery. "First come, first served" is not fairness - it is a death sentence for the arterial bleed. Your job is the triage nurse's: assess the consequence and put the most intensive resources on the most critical patient, not on the one who arrived first or shouted loudest. Uniform rigour treats the arterial bleed and the cut finger the same, and it is exactly how your most critical assets end up failing.

  2. Assign a method to each tier, and say what the tier-3 assets actually get.

The test of a real answer: one of these six is cheap, sits still almost all year, and will look to most people like the most obvious tier-3 asset on the list. Ask what its failure would cost - and then ask the harder question: would anyone know it had failed? A standby fire pump fails silently, and the failure is discovered at the only moment it matters. That is a hidden failure with a safety consequence, so it cannot be run-to-failure at any price; it belongs at the top, with a calculated failure-finding interval. If you tiered it by what it cost or by how often it runs, you have just found the exact mistake this technique exists to prevent.

T5: The Guardrails

Portfolio plans fail in a few reliable ways, and one of them can hurt somebody.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Tiering by capital cost. The most common error and the most dangerous. Expensive is not the same as critical, and cheap is not the same as harmless.

• Tiering by who complains. The noisy asset gets the attention and the silent high-consequence one waits quietly until it fails. Score the consequence, and be able to read the scoring aloud to the board.

• The uniform-rigour fantasy. Promising full analysis on everything sounds responsible and delivers nothing, because it never finishes. Do the arithmetic early enough that it changes the plan rather than excusing it.

• The opposite - one template on everything. It finishes, and it protects nothing. Both failures come from refusing to decide.

• Breaching the hard floor. No significant safety or environmental consequence, and no hidden failure, may sit in run-to-failure - whatever the asset cost, however rarely it runs.

• Treating run-to-failure as neglect. It is an engineering decision, and it carries obligations: the spares position, the response plan and the acceptance of the failure must all be recorded and faced.

• Freezing the tiers. An asset that bites you has told you your hypothesis was wrong. If nothing ever escalates, the tiering has stopped being a judgement and become a filing system.

• Forgetting that tier 2 is where tasks get cut. A streamlined plan is not a copied plan. The PM optimisation pass is the point of the tier - unjustified tasks removed, not inherited.

• Confusing the allocation with the analysis. This technique decides where rigour goes. It does not do the rigour, and it is not a substitute for it.

Onalenna could not do it properly for everything, and pretending otherwise would have quietly delivered the worst of both worlds. Price your rigour in analyst-days; band the portfolio by the consequences of failure and never by cost; match the depth of method to the tier; prove the budget closes against the team you really have; and set the rules that let reality re-tier an asset - beneath the floor that says no hidden failure and no safety consequence is ever run-to-failure. Uniform rigour is not fairness. It is starvation at the top and waste at the bottom. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take your own asset register and tier the first hundred - then do the analyst-day sum on what you were previously promising.
