Step 1 of 5

Everyone wants their street fixed first and the mayor wants the loud pothole done today - how do I spend a tiny roads budget where it does the most good for the whole town?

T1: The Ache

It is a hot Tuesday morning in Greenvale, a small municipality of about 90,000 people. You are the new roads engineer - three weeks in the job - and your whole network is 142 kilometres of tar streets. On your desk: one thin folder holding this year's repair budget, R6 million, and one fat problem - a stack of 37 complaint letters and a residents' group chat with 400 angry members. Everyone wants their street fixed first.

The mayor walks in without knocking, holding her phone. On the screen is a deep pothole on Acacia Street, a child's bicycle beside it for scale. "This one," she says, tapping the glass. "Fix this. Today. The councillor for that ward has called me twice."

Your face goes warm - because you walked Acacia Street yesterday, and yes, it has one bad pothole, but the road itself is sound. You also drove down Protea Avenue, where the whole surface is cracked into little blocks like a dried-up dam - the kind of failure that takes a road out along its entire length. Acacia's pothole is a R4,000 patch. Protea, left one more year, becomes a R2.4 million rebuild instead of a R180,000 reseal now. The tempting thing is to send the crew to Acacia this afternoon, photograph the filled pothole, and text the mayor - the calls stop, you look good. But you would be fixing the loudest street, not the worst one, and next year Protea will be gone.

How do I prove that the quietly collapsing road is a bigger crisis than the one loud pothole - and spend our tiny budget where it does the most good for the whole town?

Before you read on, decide. There is R6 million for the town’s roads. One street has a pothole and a resident phoning the mayor. Another — Protea — is quietly breaking up across its whole length, and nobody has called about it. You could fill the pothole this afternoon, photograph it, and the calls would stop. Write down how you would decide where the R6 million goes — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the quiet road is the emergency

• “Fix the pothole. The complaints are real and it is cheap.” It is cheap, the calls do stop, and you will have spent the afternoon on the loudest street rather than the worst one — which is how Protea gets to where it is.

• “Split the money evenly across all the roads.” Feels fair and buys nothing anywhere: too little to save the road that could still be saved, spread across roads that did not need it yet.

• “Fix the worst road first.” The instinct that sounds most responsible, and it can be the most wasteful. A road far enough gone needs rebuilding, and rebuilding one street can swallow the entire budget while five repairable roads slide past the point of rescue.

• “Drive around and rank them by eye.” Your eye is honest and it is not comparable — not between streets, not between years, and not to anybody who asks why Protea beat the pothole.

• “Ask residents which roads matter most.” That is the system you already have. It measures who complains, which is not the same as which road is failing.

• Two things have to be true before that money can be spent well. First, every road needs the same honest number, produced the same way, so a quiet street and a loud one can be compared at all — and so the comparison survives you, next year and the year after. Second, and this is the part that reverses the instinct: roads do not decay in a straight line. They hold up, then fall away steeply — so there is a stretch where a small spend buys many years, and a point past which nothing but a rebuild will do. Money spent just before the fall is worth several times the same money spent just after it, which is why Protea, quietly collapsing and still saveable, is a bigger emergency than a pothole anybody can see. That is an argument you can put to a mayor, and it is one the phone calls cannot make.

You cannot keep choosing roads by who shouts loudest. You need a reliable, repeatable way to measure how bad every road actually is, put an honest number on it, predict which will fail next, and send the R6 million where it earns the most. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is a Pavement Condition Index (PCI) survey. "Pavement" is the engineer's word for the built-up road and its layers; an "index" is a single score on a fixed scale. PCI turns the messy reality of a cracked road into one honest number from 0 (failed) to 100 (a perfect new road) - and it follows a written standard, ASTM D6433, so two different surveyors get nearly the same score. Watch it run on Protea Avenue.

Step 1 - Segment and sample the network

Protea Avenue is 800 m x 7 m = 5,600 m2; at about 225 m2 per sample unit that is roughly 25 units. You do not walk all 25 - you survey a representative 6, spread evenly down the road. (ASTM D6433 sets the method, so any two surveyors get nearly the same score.)

Step 2 - Survey the distress (the clipboard, not the gut)

On Sample Unit 4 you record exactly what you see: block cracking, medium, 90 m2; potholes, high, 3; patching, low, 12 m2. Severity by the written definition, never by eye - the clipboard, not the gut.

Step 3 - Score it: PCI = 100 - CDV

Each distress -> a density -> a deduct: block cracking 90/225 = 40% -> deduct 33; 3 high potholes -> deduct 40; patching 12/225 = 5.3% -> deduct 6. You cannot just add them: with q = 3 big deducts totalling 79, the correction curve gives CDV ~52, so PCI = 100 - 52 = 48 ('Poor'). The six sampled units (53, 49, 47, 48, 51, 46) average 294/6 = 49 - the segment PCI.

Step 4 - Model the decline (the ski-slope curve)

Protea was 58 ('Fair') last year and 49 ('Poor') now - a 9-point drop, and the ski-slope says the drops get steeper near the bottom. Forecast: ~38 next year, ~24, then ~11 ('Failed') in about three years. The model is shouting: roughly two years before a reseal becomes a rebuild.

Step 5 - Spend for the most good (the curve, not the noise)

Compare with numbers, not noise. Acacia Street is PCI 82 ('Satisfactory') with one isolated pothole - a sound road, a R4,000 patch. Protea is PCI 49 and two years from failure - reseal now R180,000, or rebuild later R2.4 million (acting on the number saves R2.4m - R180k = R2.22 million). The plan: patch Acacia's pothole today (cheap, real, quiets the mayor) AND reseal Protea this year - the loud job and the right job, every rand defensible with a score.

See what the five steps did. They replaced "this road feels bad" with a defensible number (PCI 49), replaced a hunch about the future with a forecast (two years to failure), and replaced "fix the loudest" with "spend where the curve pays". And notice the resolution: you do the loud job (Acacia's cheap, real pothole) and the right job (reselling Protea before it dies). The deepest lesson hides in the curve: a rand spent keeping a "Fair" road out of "Poor" saves many rands you would later spend rebuilding it from "Failed". You preserve the savable roads - you do not just chase the worst, or the loudest.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the assessment in five moves.

  • 1. Segment and sample. Break the network into sections, each into fixed ~225 m² sample units; survey a representative sample, not every metre (the standard gives the sample size).
  • 2. Survey the distress. Walk each unit and record every visible damage by type, severity (Low/Med/High by the written definition) and quantity - the clipboard, not the gut.
  • 3. Score it. Density → a deduct value off the chart → correct for overlap (the CDV step) → PCI = 100 - CDV, with a plain rating.
  • 4. Model the decline. Fit the ski-slope curve to PCI over time; predict next year; act before the cliff.
  • 5. Spend for the most good. Rank by the curve, preserve the savable, defend every rand with a score.

The ideas inside it: a distress is a visible damage type (about 19 are named for tar roads); a deduct value is the points subtracted from 100 for a given distress, severity and density (more and worse = a bigger deduct); you must correct the deducts rather than simply add them, because overlapping damage would be double-counted (the Corrected Deduct Value uses how many deducts are "big"); and the deterioration model is the curve that lets you act early. PCI fits a paved network you must rank and forecast on a tight budget; it does not see the hidden strength of the layers underneath (that needs coring or a falling-weight test), does not cover gravel roads (their own index), and does not measure skid or ride comfort (separate surveys) - PCI measures condition and decay.

Two disciplines keep it honest. First, measure with the clipboard, not the gut - severity by the written definition, quantities recorded as seen, deducts corrected not just added; the whole value of PCI is that it is repeatable, so do not let opinion creep in. Second, let the curve choose, not the noise - the loudest complaint is rarely the worst road, and the worst road is rarely the best place to spend; preserve the roads you can still save cheaply, and you protect the whole network for everyone, not just the ward that shouts.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your pavement survey honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not fix the loudest road. A complaint measures who has the councillor's number, not which road is failing. Answer it if it is cheap and real - but never let it set the whole budget.

• Do not just chase the worst road either. A road already "Failed" is the most expensive thing you can buy. Often the best rand preserves a "Fair" road before it slides - cheap now, ruinous later.

• Score by the definition, not the eye. Low/Medium/High severity each have written meanings. "It looks terrible" is not a measurement; the point of PCI is that two surveyors agree.

• Never add raw deducts. Overlapping damage double-counts. Use the correction (CDV) step, or you will condemn roads that are merely scuffed and miss roads that are quietly dying.

• Survey again, on a cycle. One survey is a photograph; the deterioration model needs at least two points in time. Re-survey on a fixed cycle, or you are forecasting from a single dot.

You walked in with a thin folder, 37 angry letters, and a mayor pointing at one pothole. You can walk into the council with a ranked list: every road scored the same honest way, the failing ones flagged before they fall off the cliff, and a plan that patches the loud pothole today and saves the road that would have cost the town millions. Rank priorities by the curve, not the complaint - and you can defend the right work against the loudest noise. That is how a tiny budget is made to serve a whole town fairly: not by silencing the shouting, but by answering it with a number. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
