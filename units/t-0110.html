Stop replacing the bearings

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. The big extraction fan is eating bearings. You replace the worn pair, run it up smooth - and within a few weeks the high-vibration alarm is screaming again, the new bearings already chewed to pieces. So you replace them again. And again.

You are trapped in a loop, and you know it. Replacing the bearings is treating the symptom. The real cause is hiding in the rotor itself: a fractional imbalance - a smear of build-up, a chipped blade-tip, a heavy spot you cannot see - that hammers every new bearing to death within weeks. And the obvious fix, sending the huge rotor away to a balancing shop, means weeks of downtime the town cannot afford.

You stand there, knowing the heavy spot is somewhere on that wheel, of some size - but you have no way to know where, or how much, or how to cancel it without pulling the whole machine apart.

My rotor is shaking itself to death from a heavy spot I cannot see, and I keep replacing the wrong part. How do I find exactly where it is heavy, and exactly how much weight to add to cancel it - without ever taking the machine out of service?

Before you read on, decide. This is the third set of bearings in eight months. The fan is running at 9 mm/s of vibration — a level that eats bearings — and you know the cause is a heavy spot somewhere on that wheel. You cannot see it, you do not know how far round it sits, and you do not know whether it weighs five grams or fifty. Sending the rotor away to be balanced means the works stands still for a week, which the town cannot afford. Write down how you would find out where the heavy spot is and how much it weighs, without taking the machine apart — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question the machine can actually answer

• “Fit better bearings and accept the vibration.” This is the fourth repeat of what has already failed three times, dressed as an upgrade. The bearings are not the fault; they are what the fault destroys.

• “Strip it and send the rotor for balancing.” The correct textbook answer and the one the situation has closed. A week of outage is not a cost you can spend, so an answer that requires it is not an answer.

• “Inspect the wheel — look for a lost blade or a build-up.” Worth doing, and it only helps in the case where the imbalance is large enough to see. Most are not, and if you find nothing you have learned nothing.

• “Add weight opposite where it seems worst, and see.” Closer than it looks, and as stated it is trial and error — you would be guessing at both quantities at once and could easily make it worse, since the right weight in the wrong place is worse than no weight at all.

• “Stiffen the mounts so it stops shaking.” This hides the reading and leaves the force exactly as it was. The shaking was the only evidence you had, and now you have none.

• The way out is to stop trying to see the fault and instead ask the machine a question it can answer. You already have one measurement: how it shakes as it stands. Now add a weight you choose — a known amount at a known place, temporary, and deliberately not the right answer — and measure again. The difference between the two runs tells you what a known weight in a known place does to this particular rotor, and once you know that, the weight needed to cancel the original shake is arithmetic rather than a guess. Two cautions, because both are easy to get wrong. Make the trial weight big enough to shift the reading appreciably, or its effect is lost in noise and the two runs tell you nothing. And treat where it goes as seriously as how much — the change in timing between the two runs is what locates the heavy spot, and mass in the wrong place makes the machine worse. Then one more run to confirm, and the rotor never left its bearings.

There is a beautiful, almost magical way to make the machine tell you the answer itself. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the worksheet that fixed the fan in an afternoon - without the rotor ever leaving its bearings. Watch how two simple spins turn a guess into a calculated answer.

1. Run 1 - how much is it shaking now? (and where?)

Run 1 (as found): measure the vibration - how big, and at what phase. Here: O = 9 mm/s (a bearing-killing level), with the heavy spot's direction marked from the phase reading.

2. Run 2 - add a known trial weight, see what it does

Run 2 (the trial): add a known 12 g trial weight at a marked spot, spin up, and measure. The change it caused - the trial's effect - is T = 6 mm/s. (Make the trial big enough to shift things by about 30%, the 30/30 rule, or you learn nothing.)

3. The calculation - the exact correction weight

The calculation: correction weight = trial x (original / trial-effect) = 12 g x (9 / 6) = 18 g. No guessing - the machine just told you exactly how much.

4. Where it goes - the position matters as much as the grams

Where it goes: opposite the heavy spot - and the exact angle comes from how the PHASE shifted between the two runs. The right weight in the wrong place makes it worse, so position matters as much as grams.

5. Trim run - check it worked

Trim run: spin up once more and re-measure. Vibration down from 9 to under 1.5 mm/s - smooth. The bearings will live now, and the rotor never left the works.

Look at what just happened. Onalenna never guessed where the heavy spot was. She measured the shake, poked the rotor with one known weight to see how it reacted, and the maths handed her the exact answer - 18 grams, in an exact spot. Guessing costs you bearings, money, and weeks of downtime; measuring costs you two spins of the rotor. Now let us walk through why it works.

T3: The Blueprint

This is in-situ (field) balancing by the influence-coefficient method - and its cleverness is that it gets the rotor to reveal its own secret. Each step answers a question:

  • How much is it shaking, and where? (Run 1) Measure the vibration - its size and the direction of the heavy spot, read from the phase. You cannot fix what you have not measured.
  • How does this rotor react to a known weight? (Run 2) Here is the genius move. You add one small, known trial weight at a marked spot and spin it up again. The way the vibration changes tells you how this exact rotor responds to weight - the change has a name: the influence coefficient. It is not abstract theory; it is the measured personality of that one rotor, telling you precisely how it answers a known push.
  • So what cancels the original shake? Once you know how it reacts to 12 grams, you can scale that to find the exact weight that cancels the original vibration: correction = trial x (original / trial-effect) - here, 12 x (9 / 6) = 18 grams. The formula is just that logic written in symbols.
  • Where does it go? Opposite the heavy spot - the exact angle coming from how the phase shifted between the two runs. The right weight in the wrong place makes it worse; position matters as much as grams.

One honest rule and one honest limit. The rule (the 30/30 rule): pick a trial weight big enough to change the amplitude by about 30% or the phase by about 30 degrees - too small, and the change is lost in the noise and you learn nothing. The limit: balancing only fixes imbalance. If your rotor is shaking from misalignment or a bent shaft, no amount of balancing will cure it - which is exactly why you confirm the cause first (root-cause thinking) before you reach for this tool.

Does it scale? Just as a garage adds small, precise weights to stop your car wheel wobbling at speed, an engineer on a power-station floor uses this exact logic to add a calculated weight to a multi-tonne turbine rotor - stopping it shaking without ever taking it apart.

Work out the correction weight with correction = trial x (original / trial-effect). Then say, in your own words, how you would find where to put it.

T5: The Guardrails

Field balancing is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust your weight.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Balancing the wrong fault. You balance it perfectly and it still shakes - because the cause was never imbalance, it was misalignment or a bent shaft. Balancing only fixes balance. Confirm the cause (root-cause thinking) before you reach for this tool.

• The useless trial weight. Too small to make a clear change (it breaks the 30/30 rule), so the effect is lost in the noise and your numbers are meaningless. Make the trial bold enough to see.

• Right weight, wrong place. A perfect 18 grams in the wrong position does not cancel the shake - it can double it. The angle, from the phase, matters every bit as much as the grams.

• Forgetting it is a live machine. This is a heavy thing spinning fast. Lock it out and make it safe before you ever put a hand or a weight near it. No measurement is worth a finger.
