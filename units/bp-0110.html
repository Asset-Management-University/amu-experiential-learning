Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and you've won a big, proud job: a new restaurant, The Golden Spoon, opens on Friday, and Tank You is installing its whole gas setup. A flagship customer. You're a professional - you plan it all. Stock checked: plenty of cylinders. Done. Sipho booked for Friday morning. Done. The bakkie reserved in the diary. Done. The special high-capacity regulator the restaurant needs - ordered from the supplier, "no problem, it'll be there this week." Done. Anisa, the owner, thrilled you'll arrive at 8am sharp. Done. Every piece arranged. You feel in control.

Friday morning: the bakkie's loaded, Sipho beside you, the newspaper already there for opening-day photos, Anisa buzzing. You go to collect the special regulator - and it isn't there. The supplier: "Oh, that one? We thought the opening was next week. Monday, earliest." Everything else is perfect - the gas, the bakkie, the installer, the customer - and all of it is useless. The Golden Spoon can't open. Anisa's biggest day collapses into tears and cancelled bookings, and your proudest job is now the reason her launch failed.

Every single piece was arranged and confirmed - so how did the whole thing still fall apart on the one day it mattered most?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is fury - blame the supplier, swear you'll never use them again, and promise Anisa you'll "double-check everything even harder next time." But that's a weak repair. The supplier was only one link; next time it'll be a flat tyre, or Sipho's child is sick, or the permit office closed early. "Checking harder" in your head isn't a system - it's just a promise to worry more. And notice: the supplier did confirm. Each piece was confirmed. Nobody was careless. The problem is deeper than any one link.

So before you read on: every piece was confirmed on its own. What was the one thing nobody ever checked?

Every piece was arranged - cylinders, the special regulator, Sipho, the bakkie - and each person said 'fine'. But nobody checked they'd ALL be ready at the same moment, and on opening morning the regulator hadn't come.
A job like this is a chain - it needs everything true at once, and it's only as strong as its weakest link. What if I tested the WHOLE chain together, before I promised the date?
One readiness gate before committing: list every link; each must be confirmed, reserved, and dated for THIS job - not 'should be fine'. One soft link blocks the date and gets an owner + a deadline. Line up the slowest item first.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

A job like this isn't a list of tasks - it's a chain, and a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Your mistake wasn't failing to check the links; it was checking each one in isolation. You had five strong-looking links, but you never once pulled on the whole chain to see if it would hold the weight of the actual day. The cure is a single checkpoint before you commit - a readiness gate. Five moves:

  • See it as a chain - only as strong as its weakest link: a job like this isn't a list of separate tasks - it's a CHAIN, where several things must ALL be true at the same moment. One broken link and the whole thing fails, no matter how strong the others. So the question is never 'is each piece arranged?' but 'will the whole chain hold the weight of the day?'.
  • List the links: for any job that depends on several things, write down every critical link the day needs: crew, parts, the vehicle, permits, the customer's readiness, a sign-off. Be brutally honest - what could make the day fail? You can't test a chain you haven't laid out.
  • Test each link: CONFIRMED, RESERVED, DATED: go down the list and demand a real answer for each: is it confirmed, reserved, AND dated for THIS job? 'It should be in stock' is not an answer - 'I've set cylinder 1137 aside for Friday' is. 'The supplier said it'll come' is not an answer - 'I have a tracking number and delivery confirmed for Thursday' is. Pinned to the day, or it doesn't count.
  • The gate stays shut until every link holds: if even ONE link is a 'should be' or a 'probably', the readiness gate is CLOSED: the job does not go on the schedule, and you do not promise the date to the customer. A date promised on a soft link isn't a plan - it's a hope, and hope collapses on the day.
  • Each open link gets an owner + a deadline (longest-lead first): turn every soft link into a hard one: give it a named owner and a deadline ('Muhammad to get the tracking number by 3pm today'), and always chase the longest-lead item first. The honest trade-off: this costs patience and chasing up front and won't make the job FASTER - but it makes it actually happen when you promised, instead of collapsing on the one day that mattered.

The heart of it: confirming each link separately is not the same as confirming the chain. The readiness gate is the one place you test the whole bundle together, for the single moment it must all be true - and you keep that gate shut, refusing to promise the date, until every link is a hard, confirmed "yes". You trade the small pain of chasing for the certainty of delivering.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about gas installs - it's any day that needs several things true at once. Picture a wedding.

See the readiness gate plan a wedding

A wedding on Saturday at 2pm needs a venue, a caterer, a marriage officer, and a band - all there, at the same time. You wouldn't just phone each one and accept "we should be free". You'd get a signed contract from the venue for that exact date, pay a deposit to lock in the caterer, and confirm the officer in writing.

And if the venue said "we can't confirm that date just yet"? You would not send out the invitations. You'd wait - because that's the readiness gate: make sure the whole chain holds before you commit the day. You don't do it because you're a pessimist; you do it because for the day to work, every link must hold. A wedding with a confirmed caterer but no venue isn't a wedding - it's an expensive meal in a car park.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your diary for one coming job that needs a few things to line up perfectly - a big install, a major service, a delivery to a tricky site, an event. Then build its gate:

  1. Draw the chain. Four or five links - in each, one thing that must be ready: a specific part, a specific person, the vehicle, a permit, site access.
  2. Test each link honestly. Is it a hard confirmed, reserved, dated fact - or a soft "should be fine"? One soft link means the chain is already broken.
  3. Make every soft link solid before you commit. Give each an owner (even if that's you) and a deadline, chase the slowest one first - and don't promise the final date until the whole chain holds.
R

Pick one upcoming job and run it through the gate this week - find the soft link before the day does. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Walk the chain together.)

Each piece looking fine on its own is exactly the trap - it feels like control while the real risk hides in the gaps between the pieces. A readiness gate is how you stop being the person apologising for a broken plan and become the person whose word holds: when you promise a day, it happens. That reputation - "if Muhammad says Friday, it's Friday" - is worth more than any single job.
