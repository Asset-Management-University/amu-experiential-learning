Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Wandile. Two weeks ago you were put in charge of the company's fleet of delivery vehicles - it felt good; now it just feels confusing. In one week, three people ask you the same simple question: "what are the vehicles worth?" The insurance broker, to renew your cover. The bank manager, because you're applying for a loan to expand. The company accountant, for the year-end records. Simple question, simple answer - you find a spreadsheet, it says R90,000 for the main delivery van, so you give that number to all three.

Every single one comes back unhappy. The broker: "this is far too low - a new van like that costs R320,000 to put on the road today; one bad crash and this cover won't get you back in business." The bank: "we can't use this - we need what we could sell it for quickly if the loan went bad; that's what we lend against." The accountant: "where does this come from? For the books we need what you paid, accounted for its age and use since - this number on its own means nothing." You're baffled: it's the same van - how can it be worth three different things at once, and which is the real one? Then you look closer: the spreadsheet still lists two vans sold last year, and the new one bought in January isn't on it at all.

It's the same set of things - so why does everyone need a different number, and how do I give each the right one without just guessing, when the list of what we own is half wrong to begin with?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to find the one, single, 'true' value and make everyone accept it. You defend the one number - go back with R90,000 and say "this is the figure we have" - and it's a disaster; you just look like you don't understand their jobs, because one number stretched across every job is too low to insure you, too unclear to borrow against, and has no basis for the accounts. So you hunt the 'real' value - chase second-hand adverts, phone a dealer, try to average it out - but the average works for no one: the insurer cares what it costs to replace today, the accountant about a consistent rule over time; you're chasing a single true worth that doesn't exist. So you value everything off the broken list - and calculate a perfect figure for a van sold a year ago while the new, uninsured van sits unprotected; a perfect number on a wrong list isn't useless, it's a dangerous lie.

Your problem is deeper than you thought. So before you read on: what if "what's it worth?" is really three different questions - and what would you have to fix first before you could answer any of them?

Stop hunting the one true value - there isn't one. The real skill is: WHICH value, for WHAT decision, on WHAT verified list? The same van has several honest worths at once: to INSURE = what it'd cost to put the same thing back today; to BORROW or SELL = what someone would actually pay now; to RECORD as it ages = what you paid, written down for wear and age; to KEEP-OR-REPLACE = repair cost vs replace cost. FIX THE LIST first (a perfect value on a phantom van is a lie). And STATE THE BASIS openly beside every figure - 'replace-today, current prices' / 'what a buyer'd pay' - so anyone can trust and check it. Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Stop hunting the one true value - there isn't one. The same van has several honest worths at once, because each person is making a different decision; the real skill is to ask which value, for what decision, on what verified list? Five moves:

  • See the trap - worth is not one number: the same van has several honest worths at the very same time, because each person asking is making a different decision. A single undefined number stretched across insure, borrow and record is wrong for all three at once - too low to cover a loss, too high to lend against, with no proper basis for the books. The question 'what's it worth?' is incomplete until you add the two words 'for what?'.
  • Match the value to the decision: to INSURE it, you need what it would cost to put the same thing back TODAY (so a loss can be made whole) - say R320,000 for the van. To BORROW against it or SELL it, what someone would actually PAY for it now (the real second-hand price) - say R140,000, the figure the bank trusts because it's what they'd recover. To RECORD it as it ages, what you PAID written down for wear and age - about R90,000 in the books. To KEEP-OR-REPLACE, the cost to repair against the cost to replace. Different decision, different kind of value.
  • Fix the list first: none of these values mean anything on a wrong list - so before valuing, verify what you actually own: go to the yard, tick off every vehicle, add the missing, cross out the gone, replace the placeholders. A perfect value on a phantom van (sold last year) is a lie, and meanwhile the real new van sits uninsured. Real decisions are already resting on this list, so an honest list is the first step to an honest answer. (This is the same world - records must match reality.)
  • State the basis openly beside every figure: this is the move that builds trust: a number without its story is a trap for the next person; a number with its basis stated is a tool they can check. So don't just write 'R320,000' - write 'R320,000 (basis: cost to replace with an equivalent new model today; for insurance)', 'R140,000 (basis: estimated current market price for a quick sale; for loan security)', 'R90,000 (basis: original cost written down for wear and age; for the accounts)'. Show your workings beside the number.
  • The honest goal - the right value, named, for the purpose: the goal was never to find the one true value - it was to give each decision the right value, labelled, on a list you've verified. Then the broker, the bank and the accountant each get a number that is correct FOR THEM, and you can defend every one because you can show the list it came from, the decision it's for, and how you worked it out. Not one magic number: many honest ones, each told truthfully.

See it resolve for Wandile. He fixes the list (the two sold vans gone, the new one added and now insured), then gives each asker the value their decision actually needs - R320,000 to the broker so a loss can be made whole, R140,000 to the bank because that's what they'd recover, about R90,000 to the accountant for the books - each one labelled with its basis and workings. Three numbers, all honest, all defensible, all correct for the person asking. Worth is not one number - it depends on the decision: so don't ask "what's it worth?", ask "which worth, for what decision?"; match each value to its job (replace-today to insure, what-a-buyer'd-pay to borrow or sell, paid-minus-wear to record); fix the list first, because a perfect value on a phantom is a lie; and state the basis beside every figure - the goal is never one true value, but the right value, named, for the purpose, on a list you can prove.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about vehicles - the same trap arrives with any set of valuable things. Watch a family inherit a grandmother's house and its contents.

See the same three worths on one inherited home

Three "what's it worth?" questions land at once, and each needs a different kind of value:

• For insurance: the cost to rebuild the house from scratch and replace the contents with new - the highest number, the one that protects you from a fire.
• For selling: what a buyer would pay for the house and contents as they are, in today's market - a different, probably lower number.
• For the estate: a formal value as at the day she passed, for tax and to divide the inheritance fairly - a third, specific kind again.

The craft is identical: first make a complete list of the house and its valuable contents (fix the list); then find the right kind of value for each decision (match the value); then write the basis beside each, so the insurer, the agent and the lawyer all get a number they can trust (state the basis).

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at the valuable things you're responsible for - your tools, your car, your home, your equipment, your stock. Someone, someday, will ask "what's it worth?" Your old reflex was to search for a single number; your new reflex is to pause and ask, "for what purpose?"

  1. Check the list. Do you have a clear, real list of what you actually own - nothing missing, nothing that's already gone?
  2. Match the need. Insuring against loss -> the 'replace-it-today' value; selling or borrowing -> the 'what-someone-would-pay-now' value; a formal record -> the 'paid, written down for wear and age' value.
  3. State your reason. Write the basis for the number right beside the figure, so anyone who uses it can trust it and check it.

Name the things, the worths people will ask of them, and which value each decision really needs. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build the verified list and the labelled values together.)

Reaching for one number was never ignorance - "what's it worth?" sounds for all the world like a question with a single answer, and giving the figure you found was the obvious, reasonable thing to do; this is a genuine hidden subtlety that trips almost everyone the first time. And telling the truth about numbers isn't about finding one magic value or massaging a figure to suit; it's about being honest about which value you're using and why. That honesty is a quiet act of care: it protects the people who depend on you - so a loss is made whole, a loan is sound, a record is true - and it lets everyone trust the decisions built on your numbers. Many honest values, each named for its purpose, on a list you can prove: that is what it really means to know what something is worth.
