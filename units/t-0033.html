A pump seized at 4 a.m. because the warning sat unread in a logbook. How do I build a programme that tells me which machines to watch, how to watch them, and exactly when to act?

T1: The Ache

It is 04:50 on a cold Tuesday. You are Nokwazi, standing in the dark next to Feed Pump 2, a 250 kW slurry pump that pushes ground ore to the flotation cells of a chrome concentrator. Under your boots the floor is still vibrating with a low, wrong hum - a sound the pump did not make last week.

In your hand is the night-shift logbook. The last line reads: "FP2 running warm, sounds rough, kept it going." Two hours later the bearing seized. The pump is dead, the plant has been down for forty minutes, and every hour of downtime costs about R180,000 in lost concentrate.

Sarel, the plant manager, arrives with his jaw tight. "Third unplanned stop this quarter. We spend about R2.1 million a year fixing things after they break. The board wants it stopped." He is not shouting; that is somehow worse. You already replace these bearings on a fixed 12-month schedule - sometimes throwing away good ones, sometimes, like today, losing a bearing at month nine that takes the whole machine with it. The calendar does not know what is happening inside the machine, and neither do you, not until it is too late. The pull is to do what you always do: swap the bearing, move on, promise Sarel you will "watch it more closely." But watching by ear at 4 a.m. is exactly what failed.

Before you read on, decide. Feed Pump 2 seized at 4 a.m. The warning had been written in a logbook and nobody read it. If that pump stops, the whole plant stops, at about R180,000 an hour. Its real failure modes are bearing wear, shaft misalignment, impeller wear and looseness. There is also a sump pump with a spare sitting on the shelf. Write down what you would build so this does not happen again — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the step that decides whether any of it survives

• “Swap the bearing and watch it more closely.” Watching more closely by ear at 4 a.m. is precisely what failed, and the promise costs nothing to make and everything to keep.

• “Put sensors on everything and monitor it all.” Generous and unaffordable, and it spends real money watching the sump pump, which has a spare on the shelf and should simply be allowed to fail.

• “Service it more often — go from annual to quarterly.” Trades a late failure for early replacement of healthy parts, and still nothing is watching between visits.

• “Buy a monitoring system and connect it to the pumps.” Starts at the shop rather than at the question. Which machines, watched for which faults, using what signal — none of that is answered by a purchase.

• “Make sure the logbook gets read every morning.” This is the closest to the actual failure and it is still a promise about human attention, competing with everything else at the start of a shift.

• A programme, not a purchase, and built in an order that keeps it affordable. First decide which machines deserve it: the pump that stops the plant scores at the top; the sump pump with a spare belongs on run-to-failure, and monitoring it would be waste. Then, for the machine that matters, list how it actually fails and ask what each of those failures sounds like — because once you do, something useful appears: all four of its failure modes announce themselves in vibration, so one well-placed sensor and an instrument you already have will cover the lot. Then learn what healthy looks like before you set any threshold, or the alarm is guesswork with a number on it. And the last step is the one this pump died for: the alarm has to raise a work order by itself. An alarm that arrives as a note for somebody to read is the logbook again, with a screen.

You do not need to invent anything clever. You need a reliable, repeatable programme that knows each machine's condition before it fails - built the same way every time, so it never again depends on one tired technician's ear. Here it is, worked on Feed Pump 2.

T2: The Tool in Action

You build a condition-based maintenance (CBM) programme: you act when the machine tells you it needs attention - not too early on a calendar, not too late after it breaks. Two standards give the recipe so everyone builds it the same way: ISO 17359 is the map for setting up the whole programme (the seven steps below), and ISO 13379-1 is the guide for linking a fault to the signal it gives off and the technique that can hear it. Watch the programme built on Feed Pump 2.

Step 1 - Decide what matters (criticality)

Score each machine likelihood x consequence (1-5). Feed Pump 2 = 4 x 5 = 20 - consequence is 5 because if it stops, the whole plant stops at about R180,000 an hour - top of the list, clearly worth CBM. A sump pump with a spare on the shelf = 2 x 2 = 4 - it stays run-to-failure; monitoring it would be a waste.

Step 2 - Failure modes + the fault-symptom matrix (ISO 13379-1)

FP2's real failure modes: bearing wear, shaft misalignment, impeller wear, looseness. For each, the symptom and the technique: bearing wear -> high-frequency vibration + heat -> vibration analysis (thermography backup); misalignment -> vibration at 2x shaft speed -> vibration; impeller wear -> falling discharge pressure -> pressure trend + vibration; looseness -> many vibration harmonics -> vibration. Vibration analysis alone covers all four modes - so one well-placed sensor plus the existing pressure transmitter does the work. The matrix shows what NOT to buy.

Step 3 - Collect a baseline (the machine's own normal)

Fit an accelerometer to the drive-end bearing and take a weekly reading for 8 weeks while FP2 is freshly overhauled and healthy: 2.8, 3.1, 2.9, 3.0, 3.2, 2.9, 3.1, 3.0 mm/s RMS. Its own normal: mean = 3.0 mm/s, standard deviation ~ 0.13 mm/s - a steady 3.0 heartbeat.

Step 4 - Set the alarm thresholds (Alert + Danger)

Absolute (ISO 20816 for this pump class): Alert ~4.5, Danger ~7.1 mm/s - fixed industry lines. Statistical (FP2's own normal): Alert = mean + 3 SD = 3.0 + 3(0.13) = 3.4; Danger = mean + 6 SD = 3.0 + 6(0.13) = 3.8. The statistical Alert (3.4) fires far earlier than the absolute (4.5) - catching FP2 drifting from its OWN normal - but 3.4 alone is twitchy, so set a layered limit: Alert = 4.0 (early, above the normal scatter, not crying wolf), Danger = 7.1 (the industry line). The seized bearing had read 5.2 mm/s for three weeks - well over a 4.0 Alert.

Step 5 - Wire the alarm to a work order, then review

Readings upload weekly. Cross Alert (4.0) -> the system auto-raises a Priority-3 inspection work order: investigate within 7 days. Cross Danger (7.1) -> a Priority-1 work order + an SMS to the engineer and the manager: plan a shutdown within 48 hours. Each order names the machine, the suspected failure mode, the reading and the threshold crossed - so the artisan knows what to look for. Review quarterly: a false alarm -> nudge the Alert up; a failure below Alert -> lower it. The thresholds are living, tuned by what happens.

See what the programme did. It put the sensor only where failure would hurt (criticality), chose the fewest techniques that cover every failure mode (one vibration sensor, not five technologies), learned the pump's own healthy heartbeat, set an Alert early enough to act and a Danger that means stop, and wired the alarm to a work order so a number can never again sit in a logbook. The hardest truth: the data to save this pump already existed - it had been reading 5.2 mm/s for three weeks, well over a sensible alarm. What was missing was not a sensor. It was a threshold and a workflow. That is what a programme gives you that "watch it closely" never can.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the programme in five moves (a tightening of the seven ISO 17359 steps).

  • 1. Decide what matters. Rank each machine by likelihood x consequence (1-5 each). The top scores earn CBM; the cheap, spare-on-the-shelf machines stay run-to-failure.
  • 2. Build the fault-symptom matrix. List how the machine breaks; for each mode write the early symptom and the technique that hears it (ISO 13379-1). Pick the fewest techniques that cover all the modes.
  • 3. Collect a baseline. Record the machine while healthy; find its mean and its spread (standard deviation) - its own normal.
  • 4. Set the thresholds. Alert and Danger, two ways: absolute (a fixed line from a standard) and statistical (the machine's own mean plus a few standard deviations); use a sensible layered limit.
  • 5. Wire the alarm to a work order, then review. Alert raises an inspection in days; Danger escalates a shutdown plan. Tune the limits by what actually happens.

Two ideas carry the weight. First, the matrix shows what NOT to buy: on FP2, vibration analysis alone catches bearing wear, misalignment, impeller wear and looseness, so one well-placed sensor plus the existing pressure gauge does the work - not five expensive technologies. Second, two kinds of threshold: the absolute line (e.g. the ISO 20816 vibration-severity chart for that pump class) never moves, but it is generic; the statistical line is set from the machine's own normal (Alert at mean + 3 standard deviations, Danger at mean + 6), so it catches a machine drifting away from itself long before it reaches the generic line. You usually set two levels - Alert ("investigate") and Danger ("act now / stop") - and take the more cautious of the two methods. CBM fits a critical machine that fails gradually, giving a measurable warning; it does not fit a part that fails instantly with no warning, or one so cheap that run-to-failure is simply cheaper - for those, keep run-to-failure or a fixed schedule.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your CBM programme honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not monitor everything. CBM is for the critical, gradually-failing machines. Put a programme on a cheap pump with a spare on the shelf and you have spent money to watch something you would happily let fail.

• Do not buy a technology per fault. Build the fault-symptom matrix first; usually one or two techniques cover most modes. The matrix's quiet gift is showing you what not to buy.

• A threshold with no workflow is decoration. Every alarm must raise a work order that names the machine, the suspected mode, the reading and the limit crossed - so a number becomes an action, not a note.

• Absolute-only misses the drift. A fixed industry line is a backstop, but a machine can be sick relative to its own normal while still "inside" the generic line. Set the statistical Alert too, and you catch it early.

• Do not over-tune. Thresholds are living, but adjust them on evidence - a real false alarm, a real miss - not on a hunch after every reading. Review on a cadence, not on a nerve.

You started the shift with a dead pump and the temptation to swap the bearing and promise to "watch it closer." You can build something better: a programme that ranks what matters, hears each failure with the cheapest sensor that works, knows every machine's own normal, alarms early enough to plan, and turns each alarm into a work order before the bearing dies. A CBM programme gives the machine a voice and the organisation a systematic way to listen - turning reactive chaos into proactive control. Do that and the R2.1 million reactive bill starts to fall, the 4 a.m. seizures stop, and no one carries the whole plant on their ear again. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
