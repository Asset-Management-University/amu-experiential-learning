Step 1 of 5

Skipping that service felt free every time - so how did it cost me so much?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and the bakkie is your business. Money's tight; the R2,000 monthly instalment is always a squeeze. The reminder pops up: routine service due - the small one, oil and filter and a quick once-over, about R800. It's boring, and the bakkie is running perfectly. So you skip it. "I'll catch it next time." You've just "saved" R800, and you feel clever. Nothing bad happens. Three months on, the reminder comes again - and again it still runs fine, so you skip it again. R1,600 "saved". Smart cash-flow management, you tell yourself.

Then comes the biggest delivery day of the quarter - a new, important client. Halfway through the route the engine makes a terrible grinding scream and falls silent. Seized. Sipho can only say, "Boss... it's finished." The diagnosis: oil starvation and sludge. The bill lands all at once, on the worst possible day - a new engine, a ruined day's deliveries, a customer let down.

Skipping that small service felt free every single time - so how did "saving" that money cost me so enormously?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first reaction is to blame yourself and vow to "be more careful, remember next time". But that's a weak defence: memory and willpower fail under the very same money-pressure that made you skip the service before. Or you make a worse error - because the bakkie ran six months without a service, you decide servicing is mostly a waste, mistaking the quiet stretch of growing risk for proof that the risk isn't real.

The cost didn't vanish when you skipped the service. It went quiet - and it moved. So before you read on: where did it go, and was that a saving at all?

The little service is boring and costs R800, and the bakkie runs perfectly - so I skipped it, twice, and felt clever for 'saving' R1,600. Then the engine seized mid-delivery on my biggest day. How did saving that money cost me so enormously?
Skipping felt free because nothing happened - but did the cost vanish, or just move to a worse day? Maybe the real choice was never 'spend vs nothing'.
The real choice is a small PLANNED spend on a day YOU choose vs a huge FORCED spend on a day chosen for you. 'Runs fine' isn't 'healthy' - good prevention is invisible when it works. Prioritise where failure hurts most, and automate the boring care.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The trap was a false comparison in your head. You thought the choice was "spend R800 now versus spend nothing now." That was never the choice. Five moves surface the truth:

  • The real choice (not spend-vs-nothing): you thought the choice was 'spend R800 now vs spend nothing now' - that's the lie. The honest choice is: a small, PLANNED spend now, on a day YOU choose - versus a huge, FORCED spend later, on a day the asset chooses, in a crisis. Prevention buys a smaller bill - but above all it buys CONTROL of the timing.
  • 'Runs fine' is not 'healthy': the most dangerous phrase is 'but it's still working'. An asset on worn-out oil or parts is like a person who feels fine but has dangerously high blood pressure. No visible problem is not proof of health. And good prevention is INVISIBLE when it works - the absence of disaster IS what you paid for, not a sign you wasted the money.
  • Prevention avoids the collateral damage: the cost of failure is never just the repair. The R800 service would have saved the R30,000 engine AND the R1,500 of lost income that day AND the let-down client and your good name. You don't just 'buy the breakdown' - you buy all the chaos it drags in with it.
  • Prioritise where failure hurts most: you can't prevent everything, so put your routine care where a failure would be most expensive or most dangerous - for a bakkie, the engine and the brakes; for a business, the backups and the key relationships. Let the torn seat cover slide; never skip the oil or the brakes.
  • Automate the boring: the moment you decide 'skip or spend' afresh under money-pressure, 'skip' wins. So remove the decision: build the service cost into the monthly budget, book it the day it's due (or the day you buy the asset), set a reminder. Make the boring, essential care happen WITHOUT fresh willpower every time.

See the false economy laid bare: the R800 service, skipped twice to "save" R1,600, bought you a R30,000 engine plus R1,500 of lost income that day plus a lost client - nearly twenty times the "saving", on a day you didn't choose. That's the whole lesson: the cost of prevention never disappears when you skip it - it just moves to a worse day and grows on the way. Good care is meant to be invisible; the quiet, uneventful months were the R800 doing its job. Pay small, on your day - or pay huge, on its.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't about bakkies - it's any boring upkeep whose harm is invisible until it isn't. Watch it with something everyone has.

See the same trap in your own teeth

A dental check-up and clean is small, planned, and boring - done on a Tuesday you choose, for a modest cost. Your teeth "feel fine", so you skip it for a few years to "save" the money, and the cost seems to vanish. But decay is silent. Then one night you wake with a massive, throbbing pain - and the choice is gone. You're forced into an emergency root canal: huge, painful, and expensive, on a day chosen by your tooth, not you.

The real choice was never "check-up versus nothing". It was a small, planned clean on your day versus a huge, forced root canal on its day. And "it doesn't hurt" was never the same as "it's healthy" - exactly like "it runs fine" was never "the engine is well".

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Name one thing you depend on whose small, boring upkeep you've been deferring because "it still runs fine" - a car, the roof, your teeth, your body, a backup you never test. Then take it out of the blind spot:

  1. Reframe the real choice. Write it down honestly: the small, planned spend on a day YOU choose - versus the huge, forced spend (plus all the damage it drags in) on a day IT chooses.
  2. Judge the stakes. Is this a "torn seat cover" (let it slide) or an "engine seizure" (never skip)? Put your care where a failure would hurt most.
  3. Automate the boring. Take one step in the next 48 hours to remove the skip-or-spend decision: book the appointment, set a recurring reminder, or start a small automatic set-aside to cover it.
R

Pick your one neglected thing and make its care automatic this week. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Reframe the real choice together.)

The most expensive things you'll ever buy are the disasters you could have prevented for almost nothing. "It still runs fine" is the sound a problem makes while it grows in the dark. Pay the small price on the day you choose - quietly, boringly, automatically - and you keep something worth far more than the money: the power to decide when, instead of being ambushed on the one day you could least afford it.
