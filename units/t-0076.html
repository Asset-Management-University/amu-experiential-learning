A conveyor keeps tripping and costing us a fortune, and everyone wants to swap the motor again - the R65,000 fix that already failed once. How do I prove what is really wrong before I spend the money?

T1: The Ache

You are the maintenance planner at a coal export terminal - a rail-to-ship loading yard. It is 06:40 on a Monday and you are on the gantry above Conveyor CV-3, the long belt that carries coal from the stockpile to the ship loader. In your hand is the night-shift handover sheet, and it tells you the same thing it told you last Monday: CV-3 tripped again, this time at 02:14, for 38 minutes.

The shift supervisor, Nontuthuzelo, is next to you with her arms folded. She does not raise her voice. "That is the fourth trip this month," she says. "Last month it was five. The ship demurrage clock does not stop for us." You know what she means: when a ship waits at the berth because the terminal cannot load fast enough, the terminal pays a penalty - about R18,000 for every hour the ship sits idle. You open the trip log. Over the last six months CV-3 has tripped 27 times, for about 19 hours of lost loading - roughly R342,000, gone. Your own bonus is tied to terminal availability, so this is not only the company's money on the line.

And here is the trap pulling at you. The easy move is what the team did last quarter: point at the most obvious part - the belt motor - swap it for a new one, and hope. They tried that. The trips kept coming. You have a strong hunch it is the motor again - but a hunch cost R65,000 last time and fixed nothing. You do not need another guess.

Before you read on, decide. Twenty-seven trips in six months, about nineteen hours of lost loading, roughly R342,000 gone. Last quarter the team swapped the belt motor for R65,000 and the trips kept coming. You have a strong hunch it is the motor again. Write down what you would do. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one would cost you

• “Replace the motor — properly this time, with a better one.” Your hunch, everybody’s hunch, and it has already been bought once for R65,000 and fixed nothing. The second purchase of a failed answer is the most expensive money in maintenance, because it also buys another quarter of the same trips.

• “Stand and watch it until it trips, then look at what happened.” Better than guessing and it gives you one event. You have twenty-seven already recorded — and one trip watched closely tells you about that trip, while twenty-seven sorted tells you which kind is actually costing you.

• “Fix everything the log mentions — belt, motor, chute, all of it.” Thorough, expensive, and it destroys the only thing that would have told you whether any of it worked. Change five things at once and a good result proves nothing, so the next time it drifts you are back to hunches.

• “Ask the operators — they know that conveyor.” Genuinely valuable and it is not a substitute for the log. Their attention is drawn by what is dramatic; the log is drawn by what is frequent, and those are rarely the same thing.

• “It’s only R342,000 — live with it and watch the penalty.” Defensible for a quarter, and it accepts a cost that recurs while a fix might not be expensive at all. You do not yet know what the fix costs, because you do not yet know what is broken.

The trap is not the motor. It is the order: a hunch cost R65,000 last time and fixed nothing, and the same hunch is now asking to spend again before anybody has counted what the twenty-seven trips were actually caused by.

You need a reliable, ordered way to find what is really causing the trips and to prove your fix works before you spend - and before Nontuthuzelo has to fold her arms again next Monday. Here is that method, worked on CV-3.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is DMAIC (say it "duh-MAY-ick") - a way of improving a process by reducing how much it varies and how often it fails. It is five steps done in order, and the order is the whole point: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. Watch them turn a R65,000 hunch into a R12,000 proven fix.

Define - the project charter

Charter: conveyor CV-3 tripped 27 times in 6 months = about 19 hours of lost loading and about R342,000 in ship demurrage (at R18,000 an hour the ship sits idle). Goal: cut trips by at least 75% in 3 months - from 4.5 a month to 1 or fewer. Scope IN: the CV-3 belt, drive, sensors and its feed; OUT: the ship loader and the reclaimer. One afternoon, and everyone agrees the same problem in the same numbers.

Measure - the honest baseline

Pull every trip over 6 months and sort by logged cause: belt mis-tracking / pull-cord 16 (59%), motor overload 6 (22%), blocked chute 4 (15%), other 1 (4%). The surprise: the motor everyone wanted to replace caused only 6 of 27. Replacing it again would have spent R65,000 to fix less than a quarter of the problem. The data, not the hunch, points at the belt.

Analyse - the root cause

Fishbone + five-whys: the belt trips because it wanders, because the load sits on the left, because the feed chute deposits coal off-centre, because its deflector liner is worn through on one side, because the liner is past its life and was never on the replacement schedule - the root cause. And the off-centre load was overloading the motor on bad days, so ONE root cause drove TWO of the symptoms.

Improve - the targeted fix

Change one thing: replace the worn chute deflector liner (R12,000 and one shift) so the coal lands centred. Over the next 8 weeks CV-3 trips once - a genuine blocked chute, unrelated - about 0.5 trips a month, down from 4.5, an 89% drop that beats the 75% goal. Because only one thing changed, you know it was the liner.

Control - lock in the gain

Add the liner to the planned-maintenance schedule (inspect every 8 weeks, replace at a measured wear limit), add a 30-second belt-tracking check to the operator's shift round, and put a control chart on the CV-3 board (trips per week, with a limit line) so the team acts the moment it drifts - not after another 27 trips. Six months on, the chart is still flat.

See what the order did. The hunch said "motor"; the Measure baseline said the motor caused only 6 of 27 trips, and the belt 16. Had you skipped to Improve, you would have spent R65,000 to fix less than a quarter of the problem - again. Instead the data sent you to Analyse, which traced the wandering belt to a single worn chute liner that was never on the maintenance schedule - one root cause quietly driving two of your symptoms. The fix cost R12,000 and cut trips by 89%. The discipline is simple to say and hard to follow: do not improve before you analyse, and do not analyse before you measure. That is exactly the discipline your hunch about the motor wanted you to break.

T3: The Blueprint

You run a stubborn, costly, unclear problem through five steps, in order. Each one earns the right to the next.

  • Define. Write the problem in numbers and say what "fixed" looks like - a one-page charter: the problem statement in numbers, a goal with a number and a date, the scope (in and out), and why it matters. Now everyone agrees the same problem.
  • Measure. Collect the honest baseline - how the process behaves now, captured before you change anything, sorted by cause. Without a baseline you can never prove you improved, and this is where the data overturns the hunch.
  • Analyse. Find the root cause, not the symptom: a fishbone diagram groups possible causes into families (Machine, Method, Material, People, Environment), and the five whys asks "why?" until you reach a cause you can actually fix. (t-0148 puts both shapes to work on a real failure, and shows how to pick the one the problem deserves.)
  • Improve. Change one thing, aimed at the proven cause, and check it against the baseline - one change at a time, so you know what worked.
  • Control. Lock the gain in so it cannot creep back: an updated standard or planned-maintenance task, and a control chart - a running graph with a limit line that shows the moment the process starts drifting again. (t-0052 is where you learn to read one honestly: which moves are real signals and which are only noise.)

DMAIC fits a real, repeating problem in an existing process where the cause is unclear and the cost of guessing is high - asset care is full of these: repeat trips, repeat failures, slow changeovers. It does not fit a one-off emergency you must fix right now (fix it, then come back), a brand-new process that does not exist yet (that is a design job), or a problem whose cause is already proven (just fix it). It is for the stubborn, costly, unclear ones - not for everything. And note the relatives: an OEE score is often what triggers a DMAIC project; RCM designs maintenance before failures, where DMAIC fixes a chronic failure you already have.

The hardest discipline is between Measure and Improve: the moment you see the baseline, every instinct will shout a fix - resist it until Analyse has named the root cause, because the loud, obvious cause is so often not the real one. And do not skip Control: a fix with no control chart is a problem waiting to come back quietly while everyone has moved on. Change one thing, prove it against the baseline, and lock it in.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your DMAIC honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not improve before you analyse, or analyse before you measure. This is the one rule the whole method exists to enforce. The order is the discipline; skip a step and you are back to guessing with extra paperwork.

• The loud cause is rarely the real one. The motor was the obvious, expensive suspect - and it was a symptom. Let the baseline data, sorted by cause, tell you where the problem actually lives before you spend a cent.

• No baseline, no proof. If you did not measure the "before", you cannot show the "after" was your doing rather than luck or the weather. Capture the baseline first, over a fixed window, every time.

• Change one thing at a time. Fix three things at once and you will never know which one worked - or which one to keep when budgets are cut. One change, measured against the baseline, then the next.

• Without Control, the win leaks away. Problems creep back the moment attention moves on. A control chart and an updated standard are what turn a one-time fix into a gain that stays - the least exciting step, and the one most often skipped.

The pressure on that gantry was to do something - swap the motor, look decisive, spend the R65,000 again. DMAIC gave you something better than motion: a way to be right before you spend, and to prove it afterwards. The data, not the hunch, names the cause. The baseline, not the feeling, proves the fix. Hold that order through the stubborn, costly problems, and you stop paying for the loud guess and start buying the quiet, proven one. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one repeating problem and its event log, and build the Define and Measure steps together.)
