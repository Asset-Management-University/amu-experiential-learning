Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Reza, and you install water pumps for homes and small farms. You're good at it - quality pumps, fitted perfectly, tested until the water runs clean and strong. You work hard to win each customer's trust, you give them a price, they agree, you do the work, and they pay you. Once.

And then... nothing. Your income is a bumpy road - a big payment, then a long stretch of nothing until the next job. You only hear from old customers when something breaks, and by then they're upset. You spend your quiet weeks chasing new sales, always hunting. Meanwhile competitors push cheaper, lower-quality pumps, so you find yourself arguing about the price of the box, not the quality of your work. And those pumps you installed? They have little lights that blink and gauges showing how hard they're working - real information, vanishing into thin air, because you never look at it.

I sell a great product but earn nothing between sales and compete only on price - is selling the box really all there is, or am I giving something valuable away?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

When the income feels unsteady, the first thought is to do more of the same, but harder. You sell more pumps - longer hours, further travel, more adverts - but that just runs you faster on the same treadmill: you only eat what you hunt, and you're always hunting. So you beat the competition on price - a cheaper pump, a thinner margin - a race to the bottom, selling the one thing anyone can copy, until your skill feels worthless. You even try bolting on a yearly maintenance check for a small fee - warmer! - but it's framed as an extra, a grudge cost on top of the box, and few customers take it.

Each attempt still treats the pump as the thing you sell. So before you read on: the customer never actually wanted a pump - what do they really want, and what is that installed pump quietly telling you that you're ignoring?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You've been selling the cheapest part of what you offer - the box - and giving the precious parts away for free: the guaranteed result, and the data. Five moves to put that right:

  • Separate the thing from the outcome: your customer never woke up wanting to OWN a water pump - they woke up wanting reliable, clean water, for years. The pump is just the tool; the outcome (the water that keeps flowing) is the real value. You've been selling the tool and treating the result - the thing they actually care about - as an afterthought.
  • See you earn nothing between sales: built around a single transaction, the moment the money lands the relationship goes cold. That gives you feast-or-famine income and means you only ever sell to a customer in a moment of need or panic - so you're always hunting the next one-off, never building something that pays while you sleep.
  • Spot the data you give away: that pump is quietly producing information every minute it runs - run-time, pressure, temperature. It's gold: it can tell you the pump is working too hard, a part is wearing, the water source is dropping - it can warn of a failure weeks early. Right now you bin it for free instead of using it to serve.
  • Redesign what you sell - the guaranteed result: stop selling the object, start selling the outcome. OLD: 'buy this pump for R8,000; if it breaks, call me.' NEW: 'I guarantee you have water - for a steady R400 a month you never worry about the pump again.' You keep the promise with the data: a sensor shows a pump running hot, you service it BEFORE it fails, and the customer - delighted - never even noticed. Income steadies; you compete on reliability, not price. (Make it honest value, genuinely worth more than the one-off, and cancellable - a real guarantee, never a lock-in trap.)
  • The honest tension - only promise what you can predict: guaranteeing the outcome moves the risk from the customer onto YOU: if it breaks, it's now your cost. So you can only promise what you can keep. Don't offer 'guaranteed water' to a factory running its pump flat-out 24/7 - too unpredictable. Start with your most predictable customers (the smallholder who waters two hours each morning), price in the risk, build the skill there first.

See it change Reza's whole business. He stops selling a pump for R8,000 once and starts offering "your water, guaranteed" for a steady R400 a month - and keeps that promise with the data: a sensor shows a pump creeping hotter than usual, he services it before it fails, and the customer, delighted, never even noticed a problem. His income steadies, he competes on reliability instead of price, and the information he used to bin now both serves the customer and could even be sold as insight. The catch he names honestly: the risk of breakdown is now his, so he offers it first to the smallholder whose two-hours-a-morning use he can predict, not the factory running flat-out around the clock. The customer never wanted the pump - they wanted the water; so sell the result, use the data to keep the promise, make it honest value rather than a trap, and take on only the risk you can predict.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

The same shift - from selling the thing to selling the result - works far beyond pumps. Watch a farmer and a tractor.

See the same move with a tractor

A farmer doesn't really want to own a R2,000,000 tractor - they want their fields ploughed.

Selling the thing: a company sells the farmer the tractor and is paid once. Now the farmer carries the fuel, the maintenance, the insurance, the repairs, and finding a skilled driver - and if it breaks down in the critical planting season, that's the farmer's disaster.

Selling the outcome: a different company says, "We'll plough your fields - you pay us per hectare ploughed." They own and maintain the tractors, whose data reports engine health, location and fuel use; they use it to service before breakdowns. The farmer gets exactly the outcome they wanted - ploughed fields - without the risk or headache of owning the machine, and the breakdown risk now sits with the company that's expert at managing it.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Now look at what you sell - or could sell:

  1. Separate the thing from the outcome. What's the physical thing you sell once - and what's the real result the customer truly wants from it? (You sell a security camera; they want a feeling of safety.)
  2. Spot the wasted data. What information does your product or service quietly create that's currently ignored? (The camera logs when it sees motion, but no one reviews the patterns.)
  3. Sketch the new offer. Instead of the thing for a one-off price, what would a steady payment for a guaranteed result look like, with rough illustrative numbers - honest value, not a lock-in? ("Instead of a R5,000 camera, 'Guaranteed Home Watch' for R250 a month - we monitor it and guarantee it's always working.")
  4. Be honest about the risk. If you guarantee the result, what new risk do you take on - and who's the most predictable first customer you'd try it with? Naming both makes the idea real.

Selling the object was never wrong - it's honest, skilled work, and it kept you going. It just leaves the best of what you offer on the table: the lasting result the customer actually wanted, and the quiet stream of data that could let you guarantee it. Pick up both, and you stop being a stranger who appears, installs, and vanishes - you become the one who quietly keeps the water flowing, year after year, catching the fault before it ever becomes their crisis. Selling the object is good work; selling the guaranteed outcome is deeper service - and the steady money you earn is simply the measure of the promise you keep.
