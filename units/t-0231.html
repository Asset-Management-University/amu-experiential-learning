Seen coming

T1: The Ache

There is a tight month that arrives every year, and yet somehow it always feels like a surprise. Too many bills land in the same few weeks - the school fees, the annual insurance, the thing you forgot renews - and the money that would cover them is somewhere else: still owed to you, or not earned yet because it is your quiet season.

And because it feels like a sudden emergency, it pushes you into your most expensive choices. The problem is here, now, and your options have narrowed to one: borrow fast and dear, pay the late fee, take the loan whose interest you will feel for months. You get through it - and next year the same month comes round and does the same thing.

Here is the quiet truth: you could have seen it coming. In fact, if you have a forecast, you already did - you were looking at this month, not far enough ahead to act while there was still time.

The same squeeze hits me every year and always feels like an emergency, forcing me into expensive borrowing - when I could have seen it coming. How do I plan for the tight months instead of reacting to them?

Before you read on, decide. Every February it happens. The annual insurance, the school fees and the quiet trading month land together, and you are short by about R18,000. This year you took the short-term loan again, and you will feel the interest for the next six months. You keep a cash-flow sheet — you look at it most weeks — and you have been reading it for this month's balance, not for what is coming. You get through it. And next February the same month comes round and does exactly the same thing. Write down how you would plan for the tight months instead of reacting to them — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the forecast was actually for

• “Save through the year so February is covered.” Genuinely one of the real answers, and it needs to be decided in May, not discovered in February — which is the whole point you are missing rather than a different one.

• “Get a cheaper loan lined up.” Reduces the price of the emergency and accepts it permanently. You would be optimising the response to a problem you could have removed.

• “Cut costs in February.” The month with the least room to cut anything, and the commitments landing then are mostly fixed — insurance and fees do not negotiate in the week they fall due.

• “Chase customers harder in January.” Worth doing, and it is a scramble a fortnight out, which is precisely when your options have already narrowed to the expensive ones.

• “It is just how this business is.” Partly true and it hides the actionable part: the shortfall may be structural, but the emergency is not — the emergency comes from the timing of when you looked.

So look for a specific shape: a stretch ahead where more is due to go out than is coming in. Your forecast exists for exactly that, which means reading it forward for the tight periods rather than for today's balance, and identifying the shortfall precisely — which month, how deep, how long — because everything you can do depends on seeing it early and clearly. The gift the forecast gives you is time, and time is the whole difference. A shortfall seen three months out is a problem you plan around with every option still open; the same shortfall found on the day is an emergency with only the expensive options left. That is the entire mechanism by which this manufactures its own cost — not the shortfall, but its lateness. And the responses divide usefully: some move the timing of what is already committed, and some change the structure so next February is shallower. The first gets you across this year; the second is what stops it arriving with the same force again.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you turn a cash-flow forecast from a picture into a plan - spotting the risk period ahead, and planning the actions that carry you across it before you get there.

1. The risk period - a stretch where more goes out than comes in

Start by naming what you are looking for: a cash-flow risk period, which is a stretch ahead where more is due to go out than you have coming in. It is the month the school fees, the insurance, and the slow-paying client all land together; or the quiet season when an irregular or seasonal income thins out while the bills carry on. Your cash-flow forecast exists for one purpose here: to make that stretch visible before you reach it. The forecast is an early-warning system, and the first move of the whole technique is to read it for the tight periods ahead - to find the risk period and see how deep it goes and how long it lasts. You are not waiting for the squeeze to announce itself when the bills arrive; you are finding it on the map while it is still months away. Identify it precisely - which month, how big the shortfall - because everything you can do about it depends on seeing it early and seeing it clearly.

2. The forecast's gift is time - plan in the calm

Here is why seeing it early matters so much: the gift the forecast gives you is time, and time is everything. A shortfall seen three months out is a problem you can calmly plan around, with your options still wide open. The same shortfall discovered on the day is a crisis, and a crisis narrows your options down to the worst ones: the fast, dear loan, the late fee, the penalty interest. Same shortfall, completely different situation, and the only variable is how early you looked. So the discipline has two halves: read the forecast for the tight periods ahead, and then plan the mitigation in the calm, while you still have room to move. A cash shortfall on the horizon is a signal to plan, not a crisis to fear. The month that used to ambush you was never really a surprise - it was waiting on a map you had not yet looked far enough ahead to read.

3. Levers 1 and 2: time the flows

The first two levers time the flows. Most risk periods are not about the totals for the year - they are about when money arrives and when it is due. So, you move the timing. Bring money in sooner (accelerate your inflows): bill or invoice earlier, chase what you are already owed, ask for a deposit or part-payment up front, offer a small discount for early payment. Push money out later, or trim it (delay or reduce outflows): reschedule a big payment out of the tight month, ask for more time on a bill, split an annual payment into instalments, or defer the non-essential so it does not land in the worst week. Between them, these two levers pull your inflows and outflows apart so they stop colliding. For example: your forecast shows your R3000 buffer dipping to R500 in three months (a R2500 shortfall). Seeing it early, you ask a client for a 50 percent deposit (brings in R900) and split an annual premium into two parts (pushes out R750). The R2500 gap shrinks to R850, small enough to cover calmly from your buffer, with no loan.

4. Lever 3: bridge the gap with a buffer arranged in advance

The third lever is to bridge the gap with a buffer arranged in advance. When timing the flows does not fully close the gap, you bridge the rest - but the whole art is that you arrange the bridge before you need it. The best option is a cash reserve built up in the easier months. The next best is a credit facility or overdraft negotiated in advance, in the calm, when you can still choose the terms. What you are avoiding is the trap: the worst time to ask for credit is when you are already desperate, because that is when only the most expensive money is on offer. That emergency, crunch-time borrowing is the most expensive money you will ever take, and the entire reason you plan ahead is to keep yourself out of its reach. A bridge chosen in the calm is cheap and steady; a bridge grabbed in a panic is ruinous. Plan the bridge while the risk period is still on the horizon, and you never have to meet the expensive lender at all.

5. The diagnosis: timing or structural

One diagnosis decides which lever to use - and whether a lever is even the right tool. Ask: is this a timing problem, or a structural one? A timing gap means the money is coming, just not lined up with the bills, and the three levers fix it. A structural gap is when you are short every month, a steady pattern. And here is what you must hold onto: you cannot bridge your way out of a structural gap. Borrowing to cover a shortfall that returns every month just digs the hole deeper and adds interest to it. That is structural, and it is not a personal failing - it is very often a fact about the world, that costs are high and an income is stretched. The honest answer is not a bigger overdraft; it is to face the underlying numbers and work the income or the spending side, or to seek real support. Remember, these levers are options, not requirements. You may have no receivables to chase or slack to trim, so start from what is possible in your world. This technique helps you see the truth of your situation, so you bring the right tool: the levers for a timing gap, and the honest income work for a structural one - never shame.

And two things must be said plainly, because they decide whether this technique reaches the people who need it most. First: a cash-flow forecast is not a tool reserved for people with "stable" incomes. For someone with an irregular or seasonal income - a gig worker, a freelancer, an informal trader - it is one of the most powerful tools there is, because it turns the uncertainty you live with into a visible map and gives you the same power to plan ahead that a salaried person takes for granted. That foresight is exactly how you stay in control and keep clear of the expensive, last-minute borrowing. Second: these levers are options, not requirements. You may have no one who owes you money to bring in sooner; your budget may have nothing left to trim; you may have no buffer yet to draw on. That is real, and it is where you start - by seeing what is possible in your world. Sometimes rescheduling a single payment is the one move that changes everything, and that alone is the technique working.

Does it scale? This is how you meet any predictable-but-uneven demand on a limited resource - your time before a busy season, your energy across a hard year, a small business's cash across its cycle. See the crunch coming while it is far off, and you can pull gentle levers early - shift the timing, build a reserve, arrange the help - instead of being forced into harsh, costly measures when it is on top of you. The forecast does not remove the tight months; it hands you the one thing that changes everything about them, which is time to prepare.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. Your month-by-month forecast keeps a safety buffer of R3000. It looks fine this month and next - but month 2 shows the buffer dropping to R200, because a R2200 school-fee payment and a R1600 once-a-year subscription both fall due then, while a R1500 payment owed to you usually arrives a month late.

  1. Identify the risk period. Which month is the risk period, and what is the size of the shortfall against your R3000 buffer, in RAND?
  2. Time the flows. Name one specific action to bring an inflow in sooner, and one to delay or trim an outflow, and say roughly how much each moves.
  3. Bridge the rest. Say how you would plan to cover the remaining gap without reaching for emergency credit.
  4. Diagnose. Is this a timing gap or a structural one - and, crucially, what does your answer mean for your next action?

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the risk period is month 2, and the buffer dropping from R3000 to R200 is a shortfall of R2800 against where you want to be. Naming the month and the number is the whole first move.

Second, timing the flows: an inflow action might be asking the person who owes you R1500 for part of it up front, or for on-time payment this once (bringing in, say, R750-R1500 sooner); an outflow action might be splitting the R1600 annual subscription into monthly instalments, or moving it to a different month (pushing out perhaps R1200-R1400). Together they might shrink an R2800 gap to a few hundred RAND. The mark of a real answer is that it moves both sides - not only cutting, but also pulling income forward.

Third, the bridge: cover the small remainder from a reserve built in the easier months, or from a facility arranged in advance in the calm - explicitly not a payday loan or a penalty-fee overdraft grabbed on the day. The mark of a real answer is that the bridge was arranged before the crunch, so it is cheap and chosen, not desperate.

Fourth, the diagnosis: this is a timing gap - a one-off collision of a few dated bills against a late payment - so the levers are exactly right, and the next action is to make those calls now, three months early. If instead the forecast had shown the buffer draining every month, it would be structural, and the honest next action would be different: not another bridge, but the income-or-spending work, or real support - because borrowing against a structural gap only deepens it.

T5: The Guardrails

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Ignoring the forecast until the day. The warning is worthless if you only read it when the bills arrive. Look months ahead, while your options are still wide.

• Reacting with emergency credit. The worst time to borrow is when you are desperate - that is when only the most expensive money is on offer. Plan the bridge in the calm instead.

• Using a bridge to cover a structural gap. Borrowing against a shortfall that returns every month digs the hole deeper. A structural gap needs the income or spending work, not more debt.

• Only cutting outflows. Bringing money in sooner is half the toolkit. Accelerate an inflow as well as delaying an outflow, and the gap closes from both sides.

• No buffer and no facility arranged in advance. Build a reserve in the good months, or set up a facility before you need it. A bridge chosen early is cheap; one grabbed in a panic is ruinous.

• Mistaking a structural problem for a timing one. One tight month is timing; every month is structural. Diagnose honestly, because the two need completely different answers.

• Assuming everyone has slack. Not everyone has receivables to chase or room to trim. Start from what is possible in your world - even rescheduling one payment can be the move that matters.

• Reading a squeeze as a personal failing. An irregular income and a structural gap are facts about circumstances, not character. See the truth clearly, then bring the right tool - never shame.
