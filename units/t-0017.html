The maintenance budget is cut 30% and I cannot fix everything - how do I find which failures truly matter, worst first, in an order I can defend, by Friday?

T1: The Ache

It is a wet Tuesday on the steel walkway above the Raw-Water Pump Station at the Klipdraai Water Reclamation Works, which lifts river water to a plant that supplies 45,000 households. The station has two identical pump sets, A and B - normally one runs and one rests, so either alone can carry the load - each a motor, a pump, a suction strainer and a discharge valve, all fed from one electrical supply panel. In your hand is the maintenance log for the last 18 months.

Mr Naicker, the senior fitter with twenty years on these pumps, is not happy. "Four breakdowns this year," he says. "The strainer blocked twice, the discharge valve on B jammed once, and the supply panel tripped once and took both pumps down at the same time. That panel one nearly cost us the town's water - we were six hours from empty reservoirs."

Boitumelo, the plant engineer who owns the budget, brings the news that lands on you: head office has cut maintenance by 30%. "I cannot fix everything. I need you to tell me which failures actually matter - in what order, with reasons I can defend upwards. In writing, by Friday." You look at the log: it is just a list of events, no order, no ranking. You are tempted to fix the thing that broke last - the strainer, fresh in everyone's mind - but "what broke last" is not "what matters most", and you know it.

I cannot fix everything on a budget cut by 30%. How do I find which failures truly matter - worst first, in an order I can defend - by Friday?

Before you read on, answer Boitumelo. From that log alone, name the one failure you would spend the remaining money on first, and write down the reason in a single line. Do it now - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from. What happens next in this unit is worth far more once your own answer is on paper.

Now compare - what happens when four people in that room each answer

• Mr Naicker says the strainer. It blocked twice; it is the freshest thing in everyone's memory and the most annoying to clear. He has twenty years on these pumps and he is not being careless - recency is genuinely persuasive.

• The maintenance planner says the discharge valve, because a jammed valve is the longest job on the list and blows the weekly schedule.

• Boitumelo says the panel, because it is the one that nearly emptied the reservoirs - though she cannot say whether it beats the strainer, only that it frightens her more.

• And you have a fourth answer, and no way to show that yours is better than theirs.

Notice what actually went wrong there. Four capable people, one log, four answers - and no way to settle it, because each person is weighing a different thing: how often, how bad, or how hard to see coming. Nobody is wrong; there is simply no shared ruler. That is the missing piece, and it is not more data. It is agreeing what the words mean before anyone scores anything.

T2: The Tool in Action

Score every failure the same way, on three things at once - how bad it is, how often it happens, and how likely you are to catch it before it bites - and let the combined score, not the freshest memory, set the order. Watch it run on the pump station.

Step 1 - Agree the scales first (before any scoring)

Busi writes the scales on the board. Severity: 1 = no real effect, 5 = one pump down (the other carries the load), 10 = both pumps down and the plant loses raw water. Occurrence: 1 = once in 20 years, 5 = about once a year, 10 = several times a year. Detection: 1 = an alarm tells us instantly, 5 = found on the weekly check, 10 = we only find out when the pump stops. Now the team scores the same way.

Step 2 - Score with FMECA (RPN = S x O x D)

Strainer blocks with debris (S5 O7 D4 = RPN 140); discharge valve jams shut (5 x 4 x 6 = 120); motor bearing seizes (6 x 3 x 7 = 126); the supply panel trips (S10, because it is the one part that downs BOTH pumps at once - a common-cause failure; O2; D8, nothing warns you = RPN 160); impeller wears (4 x 5 x 5 = 100).

Step 3 - Rank worst-first and turn it into an action list

Worst first: panel 160, strainer 140, motor 126, valve 120, impeller 100. The panel action - a busbar temperature alarm (Detection 8 -> 3) and a quarterly torque-check (Occurrence 2 -> 1) - re-scores it to 10 x 1 x 3 = 30. Owner Boitumelo, done by end of next month. The strainer (a differential-pressure alarm) and the motor (monthly vibration checks) get their own actions and owners.

See what the three steps did. They turned a flat 18-month log into a ranked action list with owners and dates - and the order is defensible to anyone who asks, because every number came from a ruler the whole room agreed on first. Look at what moved: the strainer, the answer everybody's memory volunteered, came second to the supply panel - the one part that takes down both pumps at once, and the one nobody had scored because it had only failed once. This is what the ranking is for: not to chase what broke last, but to spend a cut budget where it actually protects the 45,000 households who depend on this water. A feeling gets a budget cut; a ranked list with reasons gets the right work funded.

T3: The Blueprint

Three moves, in this order - and the order matters more than anything else here.

  • 1. Agree the scales. Define what 1, 5 and 10 mean for each of the three things you are about to score - before anyone scores anything - so everyone uses one ruler.
  • 2. Score every failure. For each part: how it fails, what that does, and why. Then the three scores, and their product.
  • 3. Rank and act. Worst first; an action list with owners and dates; re-score to prove the fix worked.

This is FMECA - Failure Mode, Effects and Criticality Analysis (IEC 60812) - and now that you have run it, its words are just names for what you did. Each way a part can fail is a failure mode; what it does to the system is the effect. The three things you scored are Severity (how bad the effect), Occurrence (how often the cause happens) and Detection (how likely you are to catch it before it bites - and this one runs backwards on purpose, because a high number means hard to catch, which is worse). Their product is the Risk Priority Number, and it is what put the panel above the strainer. A part that downs several others at once - Klipdraai's shared supply panel - is a common-cause failure, and it will almost always score high on Severity and be missed by memory, because it fails rarely.

The method fits whenever you must prioritise limited effort across many possible failures and defend the order upwards. It does not fit when your question is "what is the actual probability of one specific catastrophe" - the ranking tells you where to start, never how likely the worst outcome is; that is a different question, and a different unit.

T4: Your Turn

Take a system you are responsible for and rank its failures. Agree the three scales first - write down what 1, 5 and 10 mean, out loud, with whoever will argue with you later. Then score each part's failures, rank them worst first, and turn the top of that list into actions with an owner and a date.

Two disciplines keep the ranking honest. First, agree the scales before you score, and score from the log, not the gut - if two people mean different things by "Severity 7", the whole ranking is noise; the shared ruler and the real failure history are what make the order trustworthy, and they are the reason your answer beats the four in that room. Second, go looking for the part that several things depend on - the shared panel, the single supply, the one person who knows the system. It fails rarely, so memory will never nominate it, and it scores highest on Severity precisely because it takes everything down at once. The ranking's whole value is that it finds what recency hides.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your ranking true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not fix what broke last. Recency is not importance. The log tells you what happened; only the ranking tells you what matters. Let the score, not the freshest memory, set the order.

• Agree the scales before scoring. If "Occurrence 5" means different things to different people, every number after it is noise. Define 1, 5 and 10 together, first - it is the single step that saves the whole analysis.

• Detection is backwards on purpose. A high Detection score is bad - it means the failure is hard to catch before it bites. The cheapest wins are often an alarm that turns a 10 into a 3.

• Hunt the part that several things share. The one that downs everything at once - the shared panel, the single supply - has failed rarely, so nobody volunteers it. Score it anyway; it is usually your best fix.

• Re-score after you act. An action list with no new score is a wish. Show the number falling - the alarm dropped Detection from 8 to 3, the torque-check dropped Occurrence from 2 to 1, and the panel's score fell from 160 to 30 - or you have not proven anything.

• The score orders the work; it does not measure the danger. A Risk Priority Number is a ranking device, not a probability. If someone asks you how likely the catastrophe actually is, that is a fair question and this is not the tool that answers it.

You came onto that walkway with a flat log, a 30% cut, a Friday deadline and four people who each had a different answer. You can hand Boitumelo one page instead: every failure scored on one agreed ruler, ranked worst first, with the top items turned into actions that have an owner and a date - and the panel, which memory had never nominated, sitting where it belongs at the top. This is the ranked list that lets a cut budget still protect the town, and the reasons that let Boitumelo defend it upwards without flinching. Not what broke last; what matters most - for the 45,000 households who only ever notice this work when the water stops. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
