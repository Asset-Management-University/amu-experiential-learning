Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tshilidzi, the new Network Design Lead for a city's combined Water and Energy utility - a metro of about 1.2 million people spread across a high plateau. It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday and you are standing in the control room on the third floor of the old depot, holding a paper map that someone laminated in 2014. The map shows 1,840 km of water pipe and 47 electrical substations. The coffee in your hand has gone cold. On the wall in front of you are nine screens. Each belongs to a different team: the water team has one showing pressure at twelve big pumping stations; the electricity team has another showing load at the substations; a third, bought last year, shows GPS dots for the road-sweeper trucks. None of the screens talk to each other. Nobody designed them to.

Last night, two things happened at once, and nobody saw the link. At 02:10, a water main burst in Ward 9, an informal settlement on the east edge of the city, home to roughly 80,000 people. The pressure-drop alarm did fire - but on the water team's screen only, at 02:14. The duty officer logged it and went back to the substation alarms, which were louder. By 04:00, the burst had flooded a low-lying electrical kiosk. The kiosk tripped. When it tripped, it cut power to the very pump that could have raised pressure and slowed the leak. Each team did its job correctly. The system, as a whole, made the problem worse.

The acting manager, Khangela, slides the incident sheet across the desk without a word. His jaw is tight. He has been here twenty-two years and he does not like new people with new titles. "You were hired," he says, "to make this place SMART. We bought the sensors. We spent eleven million rand on sensors." He taps the map. "Tell me why eleven million rand of sensors let Ward 9 go dry and dark at the same time." The numbers sit in front of you: 6,200 sensors now installed across water and power, generating about 4 million readings a day, of which the teams actually look at maybe 200 values, on the nine screens. Ward 9 has the oldest pipe and the lowest-income households; its average water bill is a fifth of the leafy western suburbs', so historically it got the least attention. The council has given you R45 million over three years and one sentence of instruction: "Make the network observable and steerable."

Here is the part that pulls at you. The easy win is sitting right there. You could buy a tenth screen - a big, beautiful dashboard that pulls every one of those 4 million readings into one glorious wall display. The vendor who sold the sensors has already emailed a quote: R3.2 million for "a unified single pane of glass". It would look spectacular in the council chamber. It would make Khangela stop tapping the map. But something nags. Ward 9 did not go dark because no one had the reading. The reading FIRED. It went dark because nothing connected the water reading to the power decision, because no one could see how one part of the network would push on another, and because the people who could act were not watching the right screen. A bigger wall of numbers would not have changed last night. It would just be 4 million numbers instead of 200, and still nobody steering the WHOLE thing. Khangela is waiting. Ward 9's repair crews are still out there in the cold mud.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Khangela wants an answer and the vendor's quote is sitting in your inbox. "Buy the tenth screen - one beautiful dashboard with all 4 million readings." But Ward 9 did not fail for want of a reading; the reading fired and nobody connected it to the power decision, so a bigger view of the same parts changes nothing. "Add more sensors - clearly 6,200 was not enough." But the teams already ignore all but 200 of the 4 million readings; more numbers on parts that each worked is not the gap. "Steer the network automatically - let the system cut the kiosk itself next time." But a network that holds household data and can act at scale can also act wrongly at scale, and into people's homes - automation without a governed line between seeing and steering is its own disaster. So before you read on: name where last night's failure actually lived (in the parts, or in the joins between them?), and the one thing you would build before any dashboard.

Tell me why eleven million rand of sensors let Ward 9 go dry and dark at the same time.' There are 6,200 sensors generating 4 million readings a day; the teams look at maybe 200 of them. The temptation is the tenth screen - a glorious wall of all 4 million readings that would look spectacular in the council chamber. But Ward 9 did not go dark for want of a reading. The reading FIRED. It went dark because nothing connected the water reading to the power decision, because no one could see how one part of the network pushes on another, and because the people who could act were not watching the right screen. A bigger view of the parts would change nothing - it would just be 4 million numbers instead of 200, and still nobody steering the WHOLE. The network was not un-watched; it was un-MODELLED and un-GOVERNED. So stop treating each pump and substation as a thing to manage on its own, and start treating the RELATIONSHIPS between them - the couplings, the places where water touches power touches transport - as the asset. Build not a dashboard that shows the present but a digital twin that can be asked about the future ('if this kiosk floods, which pump do I lose, and how fast does Ward 9 drop?'), and test it cheaply by replaying last night's recorded data before you trust it with the city's money. Then govern it: decide who may SEE the data (privacy) and who may STEER the network (a human confirms before the network acts), and write a rule so the least-watched ward is not the one left to fail unseen. The real question is not 'which dashboard?' but 'where do the value and the danger really live - and who is allowed to see and to steer the whole?'
Five moves turn nine disconnected screens into a steerable, governed network. (1) REFUSE THE TENTH SCREEN: write it on the whiteboard - 'Ward 9 failed for want of a connection, not a reading'; the network is un-modelled and un-governed, not un-watched; a bigger wall of numbers changes nothing. (2) NAME THE NETWORK AS THE ASSET: draw the network in the centre, water/power/transport as coupled sub-systems; map where one pushes on another - 38 coupling points (the flooded kiosk feeding a pump); these 38, not the 6,200 sensors, are the centre of gravity. (3) BUILD A TWIN THAT STEERS, NOT A SCREEN THAT WATCHES: a living model of the couplings that answers questions about the future; TEST it by replaying last night against a success line set first ('light the full cascade - burst, flood, kiosk trip, pump loss - at least 10 minutes before the real one completed'). THE SURPRISE: the first replay FAILED - the twin missed the pump loss because the kiosk-to-pump coupling was hidden, not among the 38 - so hunt the hidden joins by walking the plant (38 -> 44), re-run, and now it lights the chain 11 minutes ahead, clearing the line; the map is always incomplete. (4) GOVERN WHO SEES AND WHO STEERS: the twin holds data that reveals when a home is empty or who cannot pay, so it works on AGGREGATED data (household-identified only for the named crew, 48 hours); and it RECOMMENDS while a human CONFIRMS (never steer itself into harm). (5) BAKE IN THE EQUITY RULE: because Ward 9 was always watched least, make alerting BLIND TO THE BILL - risk to 80,000 people ranks the same everywhere - with a measurable target (max time-to-detect equal across wards within 10%; it was 3.5x longer in Ward 9). Budget: R18m twin + R9m the sensors that matter + R6m governance and two new roles + R12m held back for the design you know is still wrong = R45m.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is what the team actually did. It took four months of arguing before they spent the first rand, and the argument was the most valuable part. Five moves:

  • Refuse the tenth screen - a connection, not a reading: it took the team four months of arguing before they spent the first rand, and the argument was the most valuable part. The Network Design Lead - you, in this story - wrote one sentence on the whiteboard: 'Ward 9 did not fail for want of a reading. It failed for want of a connection.' That reframed everything. The problem was never that the network was un-watched - the alarm fired, on time. It was that the network was un-MODELLED and un-GOVERNED: nobody owned the whole, and nothing represented the whole. The vendor's R3.2 million 'single pane of glass' would have shown 4 million readings instead of 200, beautifully, and changed nothing about last night, because the failure did not live in a missing number - it lived in the un-owned join between the water reading and the power decision, and in the fact that the person who could act was watching the wrong screen. So you refuse the tenth screen, and instead design three things, in order, because the order matters: a system-of-systems architecture, a connected digital twin, and network-level governance.
  • Name the network as the asset - model the couplings: the team drew a single diagram with the NETWORK in the centre, and water, power and transport as connected sub-systems hanging off it. The key design move: they stopped treating each substation and pump as a thing to be managed on its own, and started treating the RELATIONSHIPS between them as the asset. They mapped where water touches power (the flooded kiosk), where power touches transport (the signals on the main road), where each could push the others over - and found 38 such COUPLING POINTS across the network. These 38 points, not the 6,200 sensors, became the design's centre of gravity. This is the heart of a system-of-systems view: in a connected network the value and the risk do not live inside any single asset - each pump and substation can be perfectly healthy - they live at the level of the whole, in the couplings, in the places where one correct local fact collides with another and tips the whole over. You model the joins, not the parts.
  • Build a twin that steers, not a screen that watches: a dashboard SHOWS you the present; a digital twin is a living model of the network that lets you ASK QUESTIONS OF THE FUTURE - 'if this kiosk floods, which pump do I lose, and how fast does Ward 9 drop?' The team built the twin around the 38 coupling points first, ingesting the readings that mattered to a coupling and ignoring the rest. Then - before betting the council's money - they tested the bet against reality, cheaply: they did not wait for a live emergency, because last night's data was already recorded, a free, safe, repeatable trial sitting in the logs. They set a success line in advance, in writing: 'The model is good enough to fund only if, replaying last night's recorded readings, the twin lights the full cascade - burst, flood, kiosk trip, pump loss - at least ten minutes before the real cascade completed. Ten minutes is the time a crew needs to move a generator and isolate a kiosk. Less, and the model is a toy.' THE SURPRISE: the first replay FAILED - the twin lit the burst and the flood, then went quiet; it did not predict the pump loss. Their assumption that they had found the 38 couplings had a hole: the kiosk-to-pump link was not one of them - it lived in a wiring detail nobody had drawn, the flooded kiosk feeding the exact pump that fights the burst. The replay, not their cleverness, found the missing join. So they hunted for HIDDEN couplings, walking the physical plant rather than the diagrams, found six more, and lifted the map from 38 to 44. The re-run lit the full chain eleven minutes before the real cascade completed - clearing the line, justifying the whole programme to the council against last night's own recorded reality, and baking in the lesson that the map will always be incomplete. The twin cost R18 million, far more than the R3.2 million screen, and worth every rand, because it could steer, not just display.
  • Govern who may see and who may steer: this is the part most teams skip, and it is where the design either earns trust or loses it - so the team wrote the governance rules before the twin went live. WHO MAY SEE: the twin holds household-level water and power data, which can reveal when a home is empty, when someone is ill, who can and cannot pay - so it works on AGGREGATED data by ward and street, never household-identified, except to the named repair crew for the specific fault they are fixing, and only for 48 hours. Privacy was a design constraint, not an afterthought. WHO MAY STEER: they defined exactly which decisions a human MUST make and which the twin may recommend but never execute alone - cutting power to a flooded kiosk is twin-recommends, human-confirms, because the network must never steer itself into harm. The cost of that line is real (a human-in-the-loop adds maybe ninety seconds, and in a fast fire that could matter), and they paid it on purpose: a connected network that can act is a network that can act wrongly at scale, so the authority to steer the whole is held by a named person, not the machine.
  • Bake in the equity rule - make detection blind to the bill: and here is the constitutional core. Because Ward 9 had always been watched least - its average bill a fifth of the western suburbs', so historically it got the least attention - they wrote an explicit rule that the twin's alerting must be BLIND TO THE BILL: risk to 80,000 people in Ward 9 ranks the same as risk to 80,000 people anywhere. They made it measurable: the maximum time-to-detect a major fault must be equal across all wards, within 10%. Before, Ward 9's faults took on average 3.5 times longer to be noticed than the western suburbs'; the governance rule turned that gap into a number someone is now accountable for. The rest of the R45 million followed honestly: R9 million on the 44 coupling-point sensors actually needed (switching off 1,400 old sensors that watched nothing that mattered), R6 million on governance, training and two new roles - a Network Operations owner and a Data Steward who answers for privacy and equity - and R12 million held back for year three, deliberately unspent, because the missing six couplings had already proved the design would be wrong in places. Six months on, a second kiosk began to flood in Ward 12; the twin saw the coupling, recommended isolating the kiosk and rerouting the pump, a human confirmed in forty seconds, and nobody lost water or power. Smartness did not make the work smaller; it made it connected, and connection is a responsibility someone now owns. (Getting the build-time interfaces between work packages right is the sibling problem of bp-0070; keeping a single asset's twin faithful to its real condition is bp-0072 - here the twin is of the whole network, and the new work is the governance and the equity.)

See where it lands. Not a tenth screen, but a governed, steerable model of the whole - and six months later a second flooding kiosk in Ward 12 was caught, isolated and rerouted in forty seconds, with nobody losing water or power. When you are responsible for a connected system, the value and the danger do not live inside the parts - each part can be perfectly fine - they live in the un-owned COUPLINGS between the parts, and they bite hardest at the LEAST-WATCHED part. A bigger view of the parts never fixes that. What does is naming the network itself as the asset (a system-of-systems), building a steerable MODEL of the joins - a digital twin you test by replaying a past failure, not a screen you watch - drawing a governed line between who may SEE and who may STEER, and writing a fair rule, as a number someone owns, so the part that gets forgotten is not the one left to fail unseen. Smartness does not make the work smaller; it makes it connected, and connection is a responsibility.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You have seen the clean version - a whole city's network, neat couplings, a clear Ward 9. Real life rarely arrives that tidy. So take one guided rep, with a twist that makes the same problem harder to spot. No flooded kiosk, no metro, no rands by the million.

See the household where every account is "fine" and the month still ends short

Mokgadi runs a household, not a utility. She has a current account, a savings pocket, two store cards, a car loan, and a small side income from selling baked goods some weekends. Every single account, looked at on its own, is fine: the current account never goes negative on payday, the store cards are paid each month, the savings pocket has a little in it. Yet most months end short, and twice this year a payment bounced and cost her R185 in fees - on a month where, on paper, she had enough. She is thinking of buying a budgeting app that shows all her accounts on one screen. This looks nothing like a flooded kiosk. That is the point of the twist.

Notice what Mokgadi is about to do - buy one screen that shows all her accounts at once. Where have we seen that move before? It is the tenth screen. It is the vendor's "single pane of glass", shrunk to a phone. It would show her more of the parts, beautifully - but would seeing all five accounts at once have stopped the R185 fee? Each account was already fine on its own, so a bigger view of the parts changes nothing, exactly as it changed nothing for Ward 9. Now, the bounced payment is her cascade. Each part did its job: the current account was fine on payday, the store card was due, the baking income was a bit late that month. Nothing failed INSIDE a part. So where did the failure live? In the COUPLING - in the gap between when the store-card payment goes out and when the irregular baking income comes in. One correct local fact (the card is due now) collided with another correct local fact (the income lands a week later), and the whole tipped over, even though no single account was in trouble. That is the un-owned join: nobody was watching the timing BETWEEN parts, because everyone was watching the parts. And which money-stream is her Ward 9 - watched worst, least steady, easiest to forget? The irregular baking income: cash, lumpy, late and uneven, so it is the hardest to see and the first thing a tidy app will round away - and it is exactly the part whose timing causes the bounce. The least-watched part, doing the damage unseen, the same shape as Ward 9. So what does she build instead of the screen? The same three moves, scaled down. A tiny MODEL, not a dashboard: a one-page cash-flow timeline that asks "if this card goes out on the 25th and the baking money lands on the 2nd, what happens in between?" - a coupling model, watching the joins and the worst-watched stream first. A line between seeing and steering: the model may flag "you will be short on the 27th", but Mokgadi decides whether to move money or delay the card - never an automatic transfer that could itself trip a fee. And one small equity-style rule for the part that gets ignored: the lumpy income gets a named buffer before any of it is spent. Her smallest first rand is not the app - it is a R600 timing buffer that bridges the one coupling that keeps biting. A city utility and a household budget look utterly different and are the same problem: the value and the danger live in the un-owned couplings between parts and bite hardest at the least-watched one, whether the budget is R45 million or R600.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The same problem-type is almost certainly sitting in your world right now: you are responsible for something whose real value and danger live at the level of the whole connected system, but your tools, your data and your authority are all stuck at the level of the single part - so you can watch the pieces but not steer the whole, and the gaps between parts are where it bites you. It shows up in a hospital where each ward has its own staffing board and nobody steers patient flow across the building; a logistics firm where each depot is optimised and the network between them is nobody's job; a household with five accounts and no view of the money. Find it where you are:

  1. Name your network, and find your Ward 9. Write one sentence: "the value and risk in my world live in the connections between ___ and ___" - the whole you are really responsible for, not the parts you watch today. Then: which part is watched least, served last, or measured worst, and who pays in human terms if a cascade reaches it?
  2. Find your coupling points. Last time something went wrong, did one correct local decision make the whole worse? List two or three places where one part pushes on another and nobody owns the join. These are where your "twin" should start - not everywhere.
  3. Separate seeing from steering. Of the things you wish you could see, which would change a decision? And of those decisions, which must stay with a human, and why? Draw the line on purpose, before anyone builds anything.
  4. Write one governance rule, as a number, and spend the smallest first rand. Pick the rule about who may see or who may steer that, written before your last failure, would have changed the outcome - measurable, a number someone can be held to. Then: before any dashboard, what is the single smallest thing that connects two parts currently blind to each other?
R

Run the smallest test this week: take the one coupling that worries you most, and replay your LAST cross-system failure through it on paper - write down the lead time you would have needed to act, then check whether watching that one join would have raised the flag in time. You will know it worked when you can say "on the day it went wrong, this join would have warned me ___ minutes before the point of no return". If the answer is "not in time", you have found your smallest first rand. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build your Network Steering Map together.)
