If I died tomorrow, my family wouldn't know what I own or owe, and the law would decide

Movement 1 - The Simulation

Before you begin - a note of care. This unit sits with a death in the family and the difficult days afterwards: a funeral, a frozen account, relatives disagreeing over what is left. If you have lost someone recently, this may land close - go gently, and stop whenever you need to. You do not have to carry it alone: SADAG's free helpline is there to talk, 24 hours a day, on 0800 567 567.

It is a Sunday afternoon in your flat in Kwa-Thema, Springs, and the house is too quiet. You are fifty-eight. You have just come back from your cousin Bra Solly's place, where the family has been sitting since he passed three weeks ago - sitting and arguing. In your hand is the thing that started the argument: a brown envelope of Solly's papers that his widow, MaDlamini, pushed at you because "you are the one who reads things." You are turning it over and over on the kitchen table, and the paper has gone soft at the corners from everyone's worried hands.

Solly died without a will. Nobody knew what he had. There was a funeral policy somewhere - MaDlamini swears she remembers him paying it - but no one can find the papers, and the call centre won't talk to her because she cannot prove she is the right person to claim. His bank account is frozen: the bank locked it the day they brought the death certificate, and the R14 000 sitting in it cannot pay for anything, not even the groceries for the people who came to mourn. His brother in Daveyton has shown up saying half the house is his by rights. There was a stokvel - Solly was the treasurer - and now nobody knows whether the group owes the family money or the family owes the group. And there is a car still being paid off; the dealership has phoned twice about arrears nobody knew existed.

You can smell the candle MaDlamini keeps burning in the lounge, and under it, faint, the dust of papers that have been shoved in a drawer for years and never sorted. You hear her in the next room, on the phone again, voice tight, being passed from department to department.

And then the thought lands in your chest like a cold stone: if I died tomorrow, this is my family. Your own life is no tidier than Solly's. You have a pension somewhere from a job you left in 2009. There is a funeral policy - you think two, actually, but you'd have to dig. The bond on this flat has maybe R180 000 still owing. There is a bank account, an old post-office savings book, the burial society you pay R150 into every month, and a brother-in-law who borrowed R8 000 two years ago and has never been asked for it back. Your daughter Puleng, who is twenty-six and the only one who could carry this, has never seen any of it. She does not even know which bank.

Your first instinct is loud and simple: go buy a bigger funeral policy. More cover. A bigger payout. That is what the radio adverts say, that is what the man at the taxi rank is selling. Get more cover so the family is "sorted."

You sit with the soft brown envelope under your fingers. Solly had cover too - somewhere. The problem in this house was never that there wasn't enough money. The problem is that nobody could find it, prove it, reach it, or knew who it was meant for. And right now your own family is one Tuesday away from sitting at this same table, holding your envelope.

Where do you start? What is the real problem here, and what would you need to look at before you could fix it?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Before you read how Puleng and her father worked it through, stop and make your own best attempt. Write a few lines. Don't aim for perfect - aim for honest.

Use these to focus your thinking:

  1. Walk through Solly's mess one piece at a time - the frozen account, the lost policy, the brother claiming the house, the stokvel, the car arrears. For each one, ask: was the problem that the money/thing didn't exist, or that something else was missing? What was that something?
  2. Buying a bigger funeral policy feels like the answer. If Solly had bought double the cover, which of his family's five problems would it actually have solved? Which would still be exactly the same?
  3. Imagine your own family the day after you die. They are not short of money first - they are short of information and permission. Make two lists: everything they would need to know exists, and everything that tells them who gets what. Are those the same list?
  4. Look back at your answers. They keep circling the same gap - not a missing rand, but a missing something that would have told the family what is there and what you wanted done. Say it in one plain sentence: what is the single thing Solly never created, that you also have not created? Once it has a name, the move to make is easier to see.

Before you read on, set your own yardstick. You cannot "test" dying - but you can test whether your plan would actually work the day after. So write two short lines about your own situation, and keep them where you can see them:

  • My idea is fitting if I see ___ (for example: "if I see that the person I trust could, today, list everything I own and reach the cash without my help").
  • I would change my mind if ___ (for example: "if I find even one account, policy or fund that has no beneficiary named, or no written word saying who it is for").
Do not start with the papers. Start with the person who would have to find them. Picture your daughter at this table on the Tuesday after, with the tin in front of her and no one to ask. Go through the tin one item at a time and ask only this of each: could she find it, and would she know what it is for? Two different questions, and most of the tin fails the second one.
It is tempting to fix this by getting more - another policy, a bigger burial society, more saved. Test that: Bra Solly's family is not arguing because he had too little. They are arguing because nobody could say what he had, where it was, or what he wanted. More of the same thing, undocumented, would have made the argument bigger. So the missing thing is not money and it is not a product.
Everything in that tin exists. What does not exist is the one thing that would tell someone else what is there and what you wanted done with it - and it is the only item on the list you can create this afternoon, for nothing. Name it in one plain sentence: what did Bra Solly never write down, that you have not written down either?

Write your two lines now. The worked solution that follows is not a verdict on your answer - it is something to hold your own pre-stated lines against.

Make your attempt now. Then read on.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Puleng said it first, sitting next to her father at that same kitchen table the following weekend, the brown envelope between them: "Papa, the money isn't the problem. We don't even know what the money is. And we don't know what you want."

Here is how the two of them worked it through.

First, they separated two questions that everyone had been mixing up. Question one: what is here? - what does Papa own, and what does he owe. Question two: who is it for, and who decides? - when Papa is gone, who gets the flat, who gets the pension, who pays the bond, and who has the authority to act. Solly's family had drowned because both questions had no answer. They wrote the two questions at the top of two columns and stopped treating it as one big fog.

Then they answered question one - they built a register. They went through the flat drawer by drawer, and Puleng wrote down every single thing, in two parts. The owns side: the flat (worth maybe R650 000), the pension from 2009 (Papa thinks around R210 000 - they noted "must phone the fund to confirm"), the bank account, the old post-office savings book, the burial society (R150/month, the contact is "Sis Joyce, group 4"), two funeral policies (one whose insurer they could name straight off the debit order, one they could only half-remember - they wrote "FIND THIS"), and the R8 000 the brother-in-law owes. The owes side: the bond (R180 000 left), the clothing account at a chain store, the funeral society arrears if any. For each line they wrote three things: what it is, where the proof lives (which drawer, which app, which person to phone), and the rough rand value. It took one Saturday. When it was done, the fog was gone. There was nothing mysterious left - just a list.

Then they answered question two - and they hit the wall Solly hit. A list of what you own does not say who gets it. Puleng asked the hard question: "Papa, if you die, who gets the flat?" And Papa realised he had always assumed it would go to Puleng - but he had never written that anywhere. He learned, when they asked around, what had frozen Solly's family: when you die without a will, you die intestate, and then the state's intestate formula decides - a fixed legal rule splits everything between spouse and children in set shares, on the state's timetable, not yours. The brother in Daveyton claiming half of Solly's house was not being greedy; he was claiming what the formula might actually give him, because Solly never wrote down a different wish. And the frozen account, the call centre that wouldn't talk to MaDlamini - that was because no one had been named to act: no executor, no nominated beneficiary, no proof of who was allowed.

So Papa did the second half, and here he learned that two different tools do two different jobs. First he wrote a simple will: a clear, signed, witnessed document that says who gets what from his estate - the things the law gathers up when you die, like the flat and the money in his bank account (the flat to Puleng; the savings split between Puleng and her brother; the R8 000 debt forgiven) - and who is in charge of carrying it out (Puleng as executor). A will stops the state's formula from deciding for the family. But an estate still takes an executor time to wind up. So on the policies and the pension Papa did the second, quiet thing Solly never did - he phoned each provider and named a beneficiary directly. This matters because a named beneficiary is paid out straight to that person: the money goes around the estate and around the freeze, and reaches the family fastest, when they need it most. A will directs the estate; a named beneficiary delivers the cash. Papa did both.

Then - and this is the part Solly never reached - they tested the plan against reality before the event, because you cannot test it after. Papa set a plain success line first, the night before: "My plan works if, the day I die, Puleng can list everything I own and reach the cash without me - and the law cannot override my wishes." He could not run his own death, so he ran the next-cheapest honest check: he tested the reasoning against the people who actually hold the rules. He sat with Puleng and they did three things in one week. They asked, "if you die tomorrow, can you reach the money?" and worked the answer out line by line from the register. They confirmed the will's wording against a free standard will template and a one-off appointment at a legal-aid clinic, to be sure a homemade will would actually stand. And - the real test - they phoned every provider on the list and asked one question each: "Whose name is on this as beneficiary right now?"

That phone-around is where the surprise came, and it was not a small one. Papa had assumed that naming a beneficiary and having a will were the same safety net - that the will would catch everything. The policy they could trace had Puleng named, good. But the pension from the 2009 job came back with a beneficiary nomination Papa had filled in seventeen years ago, leaving the whole R210 000 to a sister who has since passed away. Worse, the fund explained the part that overturned his thinking: a pension pays out on its own nomination form and a fund trustee's decision, and a will cannot override it. His shiny new will, the thing he thought protected everything, would not have touched the biggest single number on the page. If he had died that month, R210 000 would have gone nowhere clean - straight into exactly the kind of frozen, fought-over delay that killed Solly's family.

So Papa changed his plan, not just confirmed it. He did not need more cover and he did not need a grander will. He needed to update one stale form - he asked the fund for a fresh beneficiary nomination and named Puleng that week - and to write into his register a note next to every pension and policy: "beneficiary confirmed, date checked." His success line was now true: tested against the real rule-holders, the plan would let the family reach the money and would hold up the wishes. He kept the will and the register; he corrected the one thing reality showed was broken.

What Papa and Puleng had really discovered is a single idea: value can exist and still be lost, because no instrument carries the owner's intent. Solly had money, a house, a policy, a stokvel - real value. What he never made was the two instruments that direct that value: a record of what exists (so the family can find it) and a will that says who it is for (so the law doesn't decide instead) - and he never checked that those instruments actually pointed where he thought. Without those, the value doesn't disappear - it just gets frozen, fought over, and handed out by a formula that never knew him. More cover would have added more value to the same broken machine. The fix was never more value. The fix was building the instrument that carries your wishes - and confirming, with the people who hold the rules, that it really does.

The honest trade-offs: a will costs a little money or at least an afternoon, and it forces you to make decisions you'd rather not face - who gets the house, what happens if a child dies first, whether to forgive a debt. A register must be kept up to date or it slowly goes stale - as Papa's seventeen-year-old pension form proved. And both ask you to sit with your own death on an ordinary Saturday, which nobody wants to do. That discomfort is exactly why most families only build these after a funeral, in the worst possible week, when it is already too late for the one who died.

Do this yourself - the "what I own, what I owe, and who it's for" register. When your family would be left guessing, you do not need a bigger policy, you need two things on paper: a map of what exists, and a will that directs it. Start with the map. Fill one row for every single thing you own or owe - leave nothing in your head.

Method & Finding

  • The Method (the lesson - reusable): For a one-way decision you cannot rehearse, set a plain success line first ("the day I die, can they reach it and is my wish protected?"), then test the reasoning against the people who hold the real rules - phone each provider and ask "whose name is on this right now?", and confirm a will's wording against a free template or legal-aid clinic - before the event, not after.
  • The Finding (in this story only): When Papa phoned around, the will was fine but the biggest asset - a R210 000 pension - still carried a seventeen-year-old beneficiary form naming a sister who had died, and a will cannot override a pension nomination; he fixed the one stale form rather than buying more cover.
  • Your Check (how you will know in your world): Phone every fund and policy provider this week and ask, for each, "whose name is on this as beneficiary today, and would a will override it?" - write the answer and the date next to that line in your register.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own life, try one in-between case - a little harder than Papa's, because here the danger is wearing a business suit and the word "death" never comes up.

Sibongile runs a spaza-turned-mini-market in Tembisa, and she runs it entirely from her own head. Turnover is good - about R90 000 a month. She knows which of her forty customers buy on credit and how much each owes (roughly R11 000 out there at any time). She knows the cash-and-carry gives her thirty days. She knows the cooldrink rep's cell number, the password to the card machine, and which supplier still owes her a credit note for spoiled stock. None of it is written down. "It's all up here," she taps her temple, and she is proud of it. Then she is hospitalised for two weeks with pneumonia, and her sister Nomsa tries to keep the shop open. Work it with me before you read each answer.

Hint 1 - what does Nomsa actually have, and what is she missing?

The stock is on the shelves. The cash float is in the drawer. The value is all there. So why can Nomsa not run the shop? Because the value exists but the information that directs it does not. Who owes what. Who to phone. What the passwords are. Which supplier owes a credit. None of it is on paper; it is all in a head that is now in a hospital bed.

Hint 2 - apply the test from the worked solution

Strip away that this is a shop, not a death, and that Sibongile is alive, not gone. What is the shape? Real value, no instrument carrying the knowledge and intent that makes the value usable by anyone but the owner. It is the same deep structure as Papa's house: value that cannot be reached because nothing written down says what exists or what to do with it. The disguise is that here the owner is only temporarily absent - but the family's helplessness is identical.

Hint 3 - now use the register idea

What would Sibongile have to write down so that anyone could run the shop for two weeks without her? Notice it is the same two instruments: a register (debtors, suppliers, passwords, who-owes-what - "what exists and where the proof is") and a statement of intent (how she wants credit handled, which suppliers to pay first - the living version of "who gets what"). The map of what exists, and the wishes that direct it.

The lesson of this step: the danger isn't only death - it's the owner being unreachable for any reason, with all the directing knowledge locked inside one head. Whenever value depends on a single person's undocumented memory and intent, the value is fragile, alive or not. Hold that lens as you turn to your own world now.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Solly's family, and Sibongile's shop, had the same problem: real value that no written instrument could direct, so the moment the one person was gone, nobody could find it, prove it, reach it, or knew what it was for. That shape hides in far more than a death. Find it where you are.

  1. Make the register the worked solution made. List everything you own and everything you owe - every account, policy, fund, stokvel, debt owed to you and by you. For each, write where the proof lives and the rough value. How long is the list - and how much of it is only in your head?
  2. Now ask Puleng's hard question of every line: who is it for, and who decides? Where you have not written it down, the law's intestate formula will decide for you. Which of your things would the state divide, and would that match what you actually want?
  3. Find the frozen-account trap before it finds you. For each policy and pension, have you named a beneficiary directly with the provider? Phone one this week and check. A named beneficiary skips the freeze entirely.
  4. Picture the person who would have to carry it - your Puleng, your Nomsa. Could they, today, with what you have written down, find everything and act? If not, the gap is not money. It is the missing instrument.
  5. Choose the one first step within reach this week: build the register, draft a simple will, or name one beneficiary. Name it and do it before the next weekend.
  6. Notice where else this shape hides: the business that lives in one person's head, the password no one else knows, the supplier relationship only you can call, the savings account your partner has never seen. Where else does real value have no instrument carrying it - so that if you vanished tomorrow, it would be frozen, fought over, or simply lost?

This is the same problem-type - value that exists but has no instrument to carry the owner's intent - in your own world.

Run the smallest test on your Test Card this week: pick the single biggest number on your register - your pension or your largest policy - phone the provider, and ask "whose name is on this as beneficiary today?" You will know it worked when you have a written answer and a date next to that line, and the name is the one you actually want. Then, if you wish, close the loop out loud the Ubuntu way: "I predicted ___, the provider told me ___, so I now think ___" - because here, reality, not AMU, is the teacher.
