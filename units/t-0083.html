Tank You is about to spend its savings on a plot for a proper depot - but the title is in a dead man's name and the only road in crosses the neighbour's yard. How do I check who really owns it, what comes with it, and whether we can lawfully use it, before we sign?

T1: The Ache

You are standing on a dusty plot on the edge of town, where Tank You wants to build a proper depot and cylinder-storage yard - room to hold stock, park the trucks, and grow past working out of the back yard. It is mid-morning; the smell of diesel hangs off the road. In your hand is a single sheet: a printed advert. "Erf 412, 2,000 m2, R680,000, owner motivated."

Muhammad is already pacing the boundary, working out where the cylinders will stack. Ibraaheem is on the phone to the bank about a loan. Everyone is excited. Tank You has R150,000 saved and a bank that says it will lend the rest if the deal is clean.

Then the seller's agent, a friendly man in a golf shirt, says something that makes your stomach drop. "Just so you know," he says, "the only way a delivery truck gets in and out is across the neighbour's yard next door. There's been a sort of agreement for years. And the title's still in the old owner's late father's name, but don't worry about that - it's basically sorted." You look at the gate. He is right: the only road touches the neighbour's land, not Erf 412. If the neighbour ever closes that gate, your trucks are trapped. And the title is in a dead person's name.

You feel the pull to just sign and sort the details later. Everyone wants this plot, the price is good, the bank is waiting. But a quiet voice says: if I get this wrong, we pour our savings into land we cannot use.

Who really owns this land, what exactly comes with it, and can we lawfully drive our trucks in and out? We have to know - before we sign, not after.

Before you read on, decide. The only road in crosses the neighbour’s land on a handshake that has held for years. The title is in a dead man’s name. The agent says not to worry, it is basically sorted — and the price is good, and the bank is waiting. Write down what you would do before signing. Your own answer, in your own words, not a choice from a list.

Now compare — the four answers, and what each one would cost you

• “Sign now and sort the details afterwards.” The pull is real — everyone wants this plot. And notice which way the leverage runs: before you sign, the seller needs you. Afterwards, you need him, and every problem you deferred is now yours to fix at his price, or the neighbour’s.

• “Get the neighbour to put the road agreement in writing.” Much better than a handshake, and ask the harder question: what happens when he sells? A promise between two people ends with those two people, and your trucks need to get in and out for as long as you own the depot — not for as long as you and he stay friendly.

• “The agent is a professional — he would tell me if there were a problem.” He has told you two problems already, in the same breath as telling you not to worry about them. He is paid when the sale goes through, and that is not dishonesty, it is simply not the same job as protecting your savings.

• “Ask a lawyer to check the paperwork.” The right instinct, and it is not the whole of it. A lawyer will read what you bring; somebody still has to ask what exactly comes with this land — whether there are debts sitting on it, what rights the neighbours hold over it, and whether you may lawfully drive in. Those are questions, not documents.

Hold on to the quiet voice you already heard: if this is wrong, the family’s savings go into land you cannot use. That is not a reason to be frightened of the deal. It is a reason to ask three questions before the money moves rather than after.

You need a reliable way to check, before you sign, who really owns this land, exactly what you are buying, and whether your trucks have a legal right to come and go - not a gut feeling, but a method you can repeat on every plot, store and depot you ever buy. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is a property-rights review: three plain questions, run in order before any money changes hands. Who really owns it? (title) What exactly comes with it? (the bundle of rights) Can we lawfully get to it and use it? (access). A useful picture: a property is a bundle of sticks, each stick one right - to use it, to keep others out, to sell it, to let someone cross it - and when you "buy", what you really buy is which sticks come with it. Watch the three questions run on Erf 412.

Question 1 - Who really owns it? (title)

You order a deeds search (about R75, a day). It returns the owner as 'Estate Late J. Khwela' - a deceased estate. So the friendly agent cannot sell you this land; only the executor, with a court's Letters of Executorship, can sign. You note: owner = deceased estate; see valid Letters of Executorship before any signing. You have already avoided the worst trap - paying a deposit to someone with no right to take it.

Question 2 - What comes with it? (the bundle of rights)

The title deed shows two burdens: a mortgage bond of R210,000 (a loan secured on the land, to be paid from the sale price) and rates in arrears of R18,400 (the municipality issues no transfer clearance until it is settled). That is R228,400 of debt sitting on the land. Neither kills the deal, but both must come off the top before the estate keeps a cent - and your conveyancer must confirm them cleared before transfer.

Question 3 - Can we lawfully get to it and use it? (access + tenure)

The only road in touches the neighbour's land, on nothing but 'a sort of agreement for years' - weak permission. You check the title for a registered right-of-way servitude: there is none. So you set a hard condition - the sale proceeds only if a 3.5 m right-of-way servitude is registered across the neighbour's land into both title deeds, forever. The neighbour agrees for a one-off R40,000, plus about R12,000 in registration and legal fees.

Step 4 - Roll up the TRUE cost to own and use it

Erf 412: price R680,000 + servitude R40,000 + registration R12,000 + transfer R23,000 = R755,000 true cost to own and use. The advert said R680,000. Your savings (R150,000) plus the bank loan (R530,000) cover R680,000 - so at the true cost you are R75,000 short. The plot you can actually run delivery trucks into costs R75,000 more than the sign promised.

Step 5 - Decide and condition the offer

You offer R605,000, subject to all three: valid Letters of Executorship; the bond and rates cleared before transfer; a registered right-of-way servitude. The estate, needing to settle the bond, accepts R620,000; with the servitude and fees the all-in cost lands at R695,000, funded by R150,000 saved and a R545,000 loan the bank grants once it sees a clean, access-secured title. Tank You buys land it can OWN (clean title, debts cleared), HOLD (freehold, forever) and USE (a right of way no neighbour can ever close).

See what the review did. It turned a hopeful guess and a friendly agent's reassurances into three hard facts - a dead owner who cannot sign, R228,400 of debt sitting on the land, and trucks with no legal way in - and then into a clean offer that protects every rand. This is the mission underneath the legal words: a family's hard-won savings should never be poured into land it cannot lawfully own, hold and use. Secure ownership is not paperwork for its own sake; it is the difference between building on solid ground and building on a handshake that the next angry neighbour can pull away. The same ache lives in a family with a home and no title deed - everything they have, resting on proof they do not hold. The R75,000 the review uncovered is the measure of what "looks like a bargain" was really going to cost; the point is a hold so secure the depot can stand on it for thirty years.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the review in five moves - three questions, a true-cost roll-up, and a conditioned decision.

  • 1. Who really owns it? Title: a deeds search; is the seller the actual, living, entitled owner?
  • 2. What comes with it? The bundle: read the title for limits - bonds, arrears, what is held back.
  • 3. Can we lawfully get to and use it? Access and tenure: a registered servitude, or just a handshake?
  • 4. Roll up the true cost. Price plus servitude, fees, transfer and any debt you carry.
  • 5. Decide and condition the offer. Every finding becomes a written "subject to"; use the gap to negotiate.

The ideas underneath. Property law is just the rules for who may hold, use and pass on a thing. Ownership means holding the strongest set of sticks the law allows; title is the proof (for land, the title deed at the Deeds Office). Tenure is the kind of hold you have: freehold (own outright, the strongest), leasehold (rent the use for a fixed time), or a licence (mere permission, the weakest - it can be withdrawn). A servitude is a registered, lasting right to use part of someone else's land for a set purpose - most often a right of way - and because it is written into the title deed it stays with the land even if the neighbour sells; a handshake does not. The same bundle matters at the end of an asset's life: disposal means lawfully passing it on, and you can only dispose of sticks you actually hold (a loan or a right someone else holds blocks a clean sale). Use this review whenever you acquire or dispose of a physical asset - land, a building, a depot, a vehicle, plant - or depend on access across land you do not own. You do not need the full review for a small movable cash item with no access question; and where a conveyancer (the legal specialist who transfers land) is already running the transfer, your job is to ask the three questions and read their answers, not to play lawyer.

T4: Your Turn

Take an asset you are about to buy, hold, or sell - land, a building, a vehicle, a machine - and run the review. Ask who really owns it; read the title for what comes with it; check whether you can lawfully get to and use it; roll up the true cost; then turn every finding into a written condition.

Two disciplines keep the review honest. First, demand the proof, never the word - "it's basically sorted", "there's been an agreement for years", "don't worry about the title" are exactly the reassurances that hide the trap; a deeds search, a registered servitude, a clearance certificate are proof, and only proof goes into your decision. Second, turn every finding into a written "subject to" condition before you sign - a risk you have spotted but not written down still costs you the money; the conditioned offer ("subject to valid Letters of Executorship", "subject to a registered right of way") is what turns a thing you noticed into a thing you are protected from.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your property review true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Buy the sticks, not the thing. "Owning" a property means holding its rights. A right already given to someone else - or a debt secured on it - is not in your bundle, however motivated the seller.

• Proof, not promises. "Basically sorted" is not a fact. A deeds search, a title deed, a clearance certificate are. Check the record yourself, every time.

• A handshake is not access. Weak permission to cross a neighbour's land vanishes the day they sell or fall out. Only a registered servitude, written into the title, lasts.

• The advert is never the true cost. Add the servitude, the fees, the transfer, the debts you would carry. The gap to the asking price is your leverage to negotiate.

• Write it down as a condition. A risk you noticed but did not make a "subject to" clause is a risk you have agreed to pay for. Condition the offer, then sign.

You came to a dusty plot with a good price, an excited family, and a waiting bank - and an agent waving away a dead man's title and a handshake gate. You can answer with three hard facts instead: only the executor may sign, R228,400 of debt sits on the land, and the trucks have no legal way in - each one now a written condition, and the R75,000 gap a reason to bargain. Tank You ends up with land it can own, hold and use, on solid ground no neighbour can pull away. Own it, hold it, use it - the three questions that turn a plot of ground into a secure foundation. That is what it means to protect a family's savings: not to trust the friendly word, but to check the record before you sign. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
