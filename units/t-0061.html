Step 1 of 5

My customers do not want a heavy steel cylinder - they want hot water that never runs out, and they say they would pay a monthly fee for it. How do I redesign the business to sell never-running-out, priced so it pays, without it falling over the first month?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning at the Tank You depot. You are standing at the steel rack where the full 9 kg gas cylinders wait. Ibraaheem is on the phone; Muhammad is reading the order book out loud. You sell gas the way everyone sells gas: a customer's cylinder runs empty, they message you on a messaging app, you drive out, swap the empty for a full one, break the seal in front of them, take the payment, and leave. You charge R260 a cylinder - the same as anyone - and your only edge is that you turn up on time.

In your hand is last month's printout. 612 cylinders delivered. Good. But the column beside it: 47 were emergencies - a customer ran dry on a cold night, a baby in the house, no hot water, begging you to come now. You went. You always go. But each emergency drive cost an extra hour and an extra tank of petrol, and you charged the same R260.

Ibraaheem hangs up and rubs his eyes. "That was the guesthouse," he says. "Ran out during breakfast. Six rooms, no hot water, angry guests." The kettle on the depot's own gas ring starts to whistle behind you - the only warm sound in a cold morning. And there it is, the thing that has been nagging you: your customers do not want a heavy steel object. They want hot water that never stops. They want to never think about gas again. What they would gladly pay more for is the certainty that the gas is simply always there.

Muhammad says it plainly: "If we could promise they will never run out, we could charge a monthly fee. People pay for peace of mind. But how do we build a business like that without it falling over the first month?"

The guesthouse owner was polite but firm: six rooms, no hot water for the morning showers, guests who think we are amateurs. Why can't we just make sure it never runs out - and could we charge for that?

Before you read on, decide. Six rooms, no hot water for the morning showers, and a guesthouse owner who was polite but firm. What she actually wants is never to think about gas again — and she would pay more for that. Muhammad’s question is the honest one: how do we build a business like that without it falling over in the first month? Write down how you would design it. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one really promises

• “Charge a monthly fee for unlimited gas.” The instinct, and it is a promise with no bottom. Unlimited means you carry every customer’s heaviest possible month, and the one guesthouse that fills every room every night is the one that empties the margin.

• “Just deliver more often to everyone — they’ll never run out.” It would work and it would cost you the business. Every extra visit is fuel, a driver and a slot on a fleet that already has a limit, spent on customers who did not need it that week.

• “Put a spare cylinder at every customer and swap it when it’s used.” Genuinely good, and notice what it is: you have bought the customer’s peace of mind with your own stock, sitting in their yard, unpaid for. That may be the right trade — but it is a cost you must price, not a clever way to avoid one.

• “Offer it to everyone — more customers on a plan is better.” The pensioner who has never run out in her life will pay you a premium for a worry she does not have, and will leave the moment she notices. The people who should say yes are the ones for whom running out is expensive, and they are a smaller list than you want.

• “Work out what the promise costs us, then price above it.” The right shape, and the hard half is the first clause: the cost of a promise is not the cost of the gas. It is the gas, plus the deliveries you will make to be safe, plus what happens on the month somebody uses double.

Keep the customer’s sentence in front of you, because it is the whole design brief: she does not want a heavy steel object. She wants never to think about this again.

You do not need to invent the idea - you can feel it is right. What you need is a reliable way to design it: a repeatable method to turn "we sell cylinders" into "we sell never-running-out", priced so it pays, with the risks named before they bite. Here is that method, worked on Tank You's real numbers.

T2: The Tool in Action

You are selling a thing (a cylinder); your customer wants the result the thing gives (endless hot water, zero worry). Redesigning a business to sell the result instead of the thing is called designing a Product-Service System (PSS), part of the bigger shift called servitisation - moving from selling products to selling the outcome they provide. Watch it work on Tank You.

Step 1 - Name the outcome the customer truly buys

Strip the product and finish the sentence: 'Our customer doesn't want the 9 kg cylinder - they want hot water that never runs out, and to never have to think about gas.' That is the outcome. Not a refill, not delivery - never running out. The guesthouse owner said it for you.

Step 2 - Pick your rung, and redraw who owns and does what

Product-oriented is today: R260 a cylinder, the customer carries the worry. Result-oriented (guarantee they never run out, ever, for a flat fee) is the dream but you carry the FULL risk - too much for month one. Choose USE-oriented: the customer subscribes to guaranteed access, Tank You keeps owning the cylinders and delivers before they run dry. Redraw ownership: owning the cylinders, deciding when to refill, monitoring the level and carrying the run-out risk all move from the customer to Tank You - you take on the worry the customer wanted gone.

Step 3 - Choose the charge, and price it

A per-refill charge would punish heavy users and reward you for letting them run dry - wrong incentive. Choose a monthly subscription, the 'Always Warm' plan: R660/month, up to 2.2 cylinders' worth (a typical home uses ~2 x 9 kg, plus a 0.2 buffer), delivered before empty. Cost it: gas 2.2 x R205 = R451; planned-route delivery R65; cylinder ownership + requalification R24; an emergency allowance (1 per ~10 customers) R30 - total R570. Price R660, cost R570, margin R90 (13.6%). Today the customer pays 2 x R260 = R520 and carries the worry; the plan is R140 more for never running out. The guesthouse says yes at once; a careful pensioner who never runs out says no - and that is fine.

Step 4 - Name the risks you took on, and the guardrails

Misjudging a customer's burn rate -> monitor usage and keep the 0.2-cylinder buffer. A cold snap spikes demand across all customers at once -> hold safety stock and stagger non-urgent deliveries. A broken 'never empty' promise costs trust far more than a late refill -> never sell the plan beyond what your supply can guarantee, and offer it only to those who feel the run-out pain. And be honest about the margin: at a thin ~R55 commodity contribution the servitised margin (~R90) barely beats the product margin (~R110 on two cylinders) - the real prizes are predictable recurring income, customer retention and route efficiency, not a fat markup.

See what the design did. It turned "we deliver cylinders" into "we guarantee you never run out" - and priced it honestly: R660 a month against a R570 cost to guarantee, a R140 premium over what the customer spends today for the worry to simply disappear. But notice the sober truth the numbers tell: at a thin ~R55 commodity contribution, the servitised margin (~R90) barely beats the old product margin (~R110 on two cylinders). Servitising a low-margin commodity is not a markup explosion - its real prizes are predictable recurring income, customer retention, and the route efficiency of planned deliveries. And it is honestly not for everyone: the guesthouse, where running out means angry guests, says yes at once; the careful pensioner who never runs out should stay on the product model - and that is exactly right.

T3: The Blueprint

You design a servitised model in four moves.

  • 1. Name the outcome the customer truly buys. Strip the product: "they don't want the asset - they want ______." That sentence is the business you are really in.
  • 2. Pick your rung, and redraw who owns and does what. The ladder runs product-oriented (sell the thing) → use-oriented (sell guaranteed access, you keep the asset) → result-oriented (sell the outcome for a flat fee). Higher rungs earn more and bind the customer closer, but pile more risk on you. Then list every task and asset: today vs servitised. Servitising moves ownership, tasks and risk onto you.
  • 3. Choose the charge, and price it. Match the charge to the outcome (a per-unit price often rewards the wrong thing); then cost what guaranteeing it takes per customer per month, add margin, and check it against what customers will pay. If it does not close, stop.
  • 4. Name the risks you took on, and the guardrails. The customer's old risks are now yours - list each, and write the guardrail that stops it sinking you.

PSS design fits when the customer cares about an outcome not the product, when the product needs ongoing service anyway (so you are already in their life), and when you can measure the outcome you promise. It is the wrong move when the customer genuinely wants to own the thing, when you cannot yet reliably deliver the outcome (promising "never empty" on a shaky supply is a promise you will break - and a broken outcome promise is far worse than a late delivery), or when the numbers do not close. Note the dependencies: you cannot honestly do move 3 without the costing discipline of a bottom-up budget and engineering economics, and you cannot keep the promise in move 1 without supply-chain reliability behind it. PSS design decides what business you are in; those tools run it.

Two disciplines keep this honest. First, the numbers must close on real costs before you promise anything: a per-customer monthly cost to guarantee the outcome, a margin, and a price a real customer will pay - if those do not line up, the model is a wish, not a business. Second, sell it only to the people who feel the pain you are removing; pushing an outcome subscription onto someone who is happy owning the thing is not service, it is a squeeze. The model earns its keep by transferring a worry someone genuinely wants gone - not by trapping someone who was fine.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep a servitised model honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The promise is the product now. When you sell "never run out", you are no longer selling a cylinder they can blame - you are selling a guarantee they will hold you to. Never sell the plan beyond what your supply can actually deliver.

• A broken outcome promise is worse than a late delivery. A late refill is an annoyance; a broken "never empty" is a betrayal of the exact thing they paid for. If your supply is shaky, fix that before you sell certainty.

• Make the numbers close on real costs. Cost the outcome per customer per month - gas, delivery, the asset, the risk buffer - add margin, and check it against what people will pay. If the fee that customers accept is below your cost to guarantee, the plan is a wish. Stop.

• It is not for everyone - and that is right. Sell it only to those who feel the run-out pain. Pushing a subscription onto a happy product customer is not service. Respecting their choice is.

• Do not oversell the margin. On a thin-margin commodity the servitised markup is modest; the real returns are predictable income, retention and efficient planned routes. Promise yourself the honest prize, not a fantasy one.

You started the morning selling a heavy steel object and eating the cost of every emergency drive. You can end it selling the one thing the guesthouse, and the family with the cold baby, actually want: the certainty they will never run out - honestly priced, offered only to those it truly serves, and built on a promise you can keep. When you sell an outcome, the promise becomes sacred: you are no longer selling a product the customer can blame, but a guarantee they will hold you to - so a broken promise is not one bad delivery, it is the failure of your whole system. Build the model on costs that close and a promise your supply can keep, and you stop selling cylinders and start selling peace of mind - which is what they were really paying for all along.
