The ladder is not the goal

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and you have just watched a supplier demonstrate a prescriptive optimisation platform to the finance committee. It was flawless. It looked at a network and told you which valve to close. The committee wants it.

You know it is a mistake, and you cannot say why. "We are not ready" is an opinion, and an opinion loses to a demonstration.

So you go back to your desk and open the work-order history. Five years, 12,400 assets, 1,850 corrective work orders.

Only 34 per cent of them carry a valid failure code. That is 629 labelled events, spread across 27 failure modes - 23 examples per mode.

Nothing can be predicted from 23 examples. And nothing can be prescribed from a prediction that does not exist.

Your objection just stopped being an instinct.

The committee wants to buy the top of the ladder and I have to show them where we actually stand on it. How do I design a multi-year analytics roadmap that earns its way up instead of buying its way up?

Before you read on, decide. The committee wants to buy the top of the ladder — prescriptive analytics, this financial year, money available and enthusiasm genuine. Your instinct says the organisation is nowhere near ready, and an instinct is worth nothing in that room. So you go and look. Last year produced 1,850 corrective work orders. Only 34 per cent carry a valid failure code — 629 labelled events, spread across 27 failure modes. That is about 23 examples per mode. Write down what you would put to the committee, and how you would plan this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what those 23 examples settle

• “Buy it — we will improve the data alongside.” Buys something that cannot function and starts its clock. Within a year the tool will be blamed, the vendor will be blamed, and the actual constraint will still be untouched.

• “Tell them we are not ready.” True and unpersuasive as an assertion — it is your judgement against their enthusiasm, and enthusiasm with a budget usually wins. It is also no longer necessary, because you now have 23 examples per mode, and nothing can be predicted from that. Your objection has just stopped being an instinct.

• “Buy a smaller version of the same thing.” A cheaper purchase of a capability the data cannot support. The constraint is not the size of the tool.

• “Fix all the data first, then revisit in three years.” Right in order and wrong in shape — an open-ended data programme with no visible return will be cancelled in year two, and rightly, because nobody can see what it is buying.

• “Run a proof of concept to show it will not work.” Spends months proving something the arithmetic already settles, and risks producing a demonstration that flatters on a hand-picked subset.

• Two structural points, and the first will surprise the committee. The levels are not a ladder everyone should climb — they answer four different questions: what happened, why it happened, what will happen, what should we do. Match them to the actual work and they separate cleanly: statutory reporting needs an accurate account of what happened and always will, since a regulator is not answered by a forecast, so climbing further there is waste; tariff and capital planning needs to know why costs moved, which a prediction cannot be interrogated for; scheduling on the critical sets genuinely needs a forecast; and one candidate — energy optimisation against a tariff structure — genuinely wants the top. Four families at four different rungs is the correct answer rather than a compromise. Second, each rung has preconditions the one below supplies, so the order is dependency rather than ambition — which is why the data work is not a delay before the interesting part but the thing that earns the next rung. A roadmap that marches everybody to the top has mistaken the ladder for the goal.

Here is the roadmap.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the roadmap - the decisions, the data gate, the rungs in precondition order, the plan across five years, and what it refuses.

1. Name the decisions the analytics must serve - and the rung each one actually needs

START WITH THE DECISIONS, NOT THE TECHNOLOGY, AND ACCEPT THAT THEY DO NOT ALL NEED THE SAME THING. The four rungs answer four different questions: DESCRIPTIVE asks WHAT HAPPENED, DIAGNOSTIC asks WHY IT HAPPENED, PREDICTIVE asks WHAT WILL HAPPEN, and PRESCRIPTIVE asks WHAT SHOULD WE DO. Now match them to the work. STATUTORY COMPLIANCE REPORTING needs DESCRIPTIVE and always will - the regulator wants an accurate account of what happened, and a prediction is not an answer to that question, so climbing further is waste. TARIFF AND CAPITAL PLANNING needs DIAGNOSTIC - the committee needs to know why costs moved, not a forecast it cannot interrogate. MAINTENANCE SCHEDULING on the critical pump sets needs PREDICTIVE, because knowing which set is drifting is what buys the outage window. And ENERGY OPTIMISATION across the pumping schedule is the one genuine PRESCRIPTIVE candidate, because there is a real optimisation to run against a tariff structure and it is worth running. FOUR FAMILIES, FOUR DIFFERENT RUNGS, AND THAT IS THE CORRECT ANSWER RATHER THAN A COMPROMISE. A roadmap that marches the whole organisation to prescriptive has mistaken the ladder for the goal.

2. Test what the data can actually support - and put a number on the gap

THE RUNG YOU CAN OCCUPY IS SET BY YOUR DATA, NOT BY YOUR BUDGET, AND THIS IS THE BOX THAT TURNS AN INSTINCT INTO AN ARGUMENT. A predictive model learns from LABELLED FAILURES, so count them. The works has 12,400 ASSETS and five years of history containing 1,850 CORRECTIVE WORK ORDERS. ONLY 34 PER CENT CARRY A VALID FAILURE CODE - 629 LABELLED EVENTS. Spread across 27 DISTINCT FAILURE MODES that is 23.3 EXAMPLES PER MODE. Take 200 examples per mode as a working target and you need 5,400 labelled events - 8.6 TIMES the labelled history that exists. BOUND THAT NUMBER HONESTLY WHEN YOU PRESENT IT: 200 IS AN ILLUSTRATIVE RULE OF THUMB, NOT A LAW. The real requirement depends on how complex the phenomenon is and which modelling technique you choose, and it ranges from dozens to thousands. THE POINT IS NOT THE THRESHOLD - IT IS THAT YOU CAN COMPUTE THE GAP AT ALL, and that a number survives a finance committee where an opinion does not. AND NOTICE WHAT THE GAP IS MADE OF. It is not money and it is not software. It is DISCIPLINED FAILURE CODING ON WORK ORDERS, starting now and accumulating for years. No purchase shortens it.

3. Lay the rungs in precondition order - and say what each one demands of the one below

THE RUNGS ARE NOT A MENU. EACH ONE STANDS ON THE ONE BELOW IT, and EACH STAGE REQUIRES DIFFERENT TOOLS, DIFFERENT SKILLS AND DIFFERENT ORGANISATIONAL STRUCTURES - WHICH IS WHY YOU CANNOT SKIP A STAGE BY BUYING BETTER SOFTWARE. DESCRIPTIVE (what happened): needs clean, integrated data from the systems that matter, and reporting people trust; it depends on the DATA FOUNDATION, which is not a phase to rush past. DIAGNOSTIC (why it happened): needs the descriptive base PLUS the context that explains variation - asset age, duty, environment, configuration - and people who can interrogate rather than admire a chart. PREDICTIVE (what will happen): needs the diagnostic understanding PLUS labelled outcomes to learn from and someone who can tell a model that works from one that agrees with you. PRESCRIPTIVE (what should we do): needs a trustworthy prediction PLUS an explicit objective, real constraints, and - the part most often missing - AN ORGANISATION WILLING TO ACT ON A RECOMMENDATION IT DID NOT AUTHOR. WRITE THE DEPENDENCY DOWN FOR EACH RUNG, because that is what makes the sequence a finding rather than a preference, and it is what you will be asked to defend.

4. Sequence it across the years - and let the climb NARROW

NOW LAY IT OUT IN YEARS, WITH MONEY AGAINST IT, AND MAKE THE SHAPE OF THE CLIMB VISIBLE. YEARS 1-2, DATA FOUNDATION AND DESCRIPTIVE, R2.4 MILLION (37 PER CENT) - failure coding made a required field with a controlled list, the asset register completed, reporting that the plant actually uses; every decision family is served here. YEAR 3, DIAGNOSTIC, R1.1 MILLION (17 PER CENT) - context attributes joined to the failure history so variation can be explained; compliance reporting STOPS HERE, deliberately. YEAR 4, PREDICTIVE, R1.9 MILLION (30 PER CENT) - TWO decision families only, because by now roughly three years of disciplined coding has accumulated and only those two have the labels to justify it. YEAR 5, PRESCRIPTIVE, R1.0 MILLION (16 PER CENT) - ONE family, the pumping schedule against the tariff. TOTAL R6.4 MILLION. LOOK AT THE SHAPE: FOUR FAMILIES, THEN THREE, THEN TWO, THEN ONE. A CLIMB THAT NARROWS IS A ROADMAP; A CLIMB THAT KEEPS EVERYTHING ABOARD IS A WISH. And note that the largest single share is the foundation, which no supplier will ever demonstrate to a committee, because there is nothing to show.

5. State what the roadmap refuses - and cost the detour if you are overruled

A ROADMAP THAT ONLY SAYS WHAT YOU WILL DO IS HALF A DOCUMENT. WRITE THE REFUSALS DOWN, IN THE PLAN, WHERE THEY CAN BE ARGUED WITH. THIS ROADMAP REFUSES: to buy the prescriptive optimisation platform now; to take every decision family to prescriptive; and to call the foundation work finished before failure-code completeness is measured and reported. NOW COST THE DETOUR, BECAUSE THAT IS THE ARGUMENT A FINANCE COMMITTEE UNDERSTANDS. The supplier's platform is R4.2 MILLION of capital plus R780,000 A YEAR, which over the same five years is R8.10 MILLION - R1.70 MILLION MORE THAN THE ENTIRE ROADMAP, for a tool whose inputs are 34 per cent labelled. And the money is the smaller loss. Organisations that try to skip stages PAY FOR THE DETOUR WITH INTEREST: THEY LOSE BUDGET, THEY LOSE TWELVE TO EIGHTEEN MONTHS, AND THEY LOSE THE EXECUTIVE SPONSORSHIP - and sponsorship, once spent on something that did not work, is the hardest of the three to raise again. SAY ALL OF THAT PLAINLY AND WITHOUT TRIUMPH. You may still be overruled, and if you are, the roadmap you wrote is what lets the organisation recognise what happened and recover quickly - which is worth more than being proved right.

Box 1 is where most roadmaps go wrong before any arithmetic starts, and box 2 is what turns the argument from taste into evidence. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

An analytics roadmap is not a shopping list. It is a capability sequence, gated by data readiness and targeted by decision value. Five questions:

  • Which decisions, and how far must each one climb? Decision-led fidelity. Compliance stops at descriptive and that is correct, not a compromise. Four families, four different rungs.
  • Can the data carry the next rung? The readiness gate. 629 labelled events across 27 modes is 23 per mode against a working target of 200 - a gap of 8.6 times, made of failure coding rather than money.
  • What does each rung stand on? Precondition ordering. Descriptive asks what happened, diagnostic why, predictive what will happen, prescriptive what to do - and each needs different tools, skills and organisational structures, which is why you cannot skip a stage by buying better software.
  • What does the plan look like across years? The climb narrows. Four families, then three, then two, then one. Largest budget share to the foundation, which no supplier will ever demo.
  • What are you refusing, and what does being overruled cost? Declared refusals and the priced detour. R8.10m for the platform against R6.4m for the whole roadmap - and the money is the smaller loss.

The arithmetic that makes the argument. 1,850 corrective work orders at 34 per cent coded gives 629 labelled events; across 27 failure modes that is 23.3 per mode; reaching 200 per mode needs 5,400 events, which is 8.6 times the labelled history in existence. Be honest about that threshold when you present it: 200 is an illustrative rule of thumb, not a law - the real requirement depends on how complex the phenomenon is and which technique you choose, and it ranges from dozens to thousands. The number is not the point. Being able to compute the gap at all is the point, because a number survives a committee where an instinct does not.

And the half of this that matters more than the sequence. The ladder is real, and the ladder is not the goal. A statutory compliance report needs descriptive analytics and always will; a regulator asking what happened is not helped by a forecast. Climbing that decision further is not ambition, it is waste. So the roadmap is not one line for the organisation - it is a line per decision family, and it says which decisions climb, how far, and where the climb deliberately stops. A plan that marches everything to prescriptive has confused technical capability with value, and it will spend most of its money proving that.

The thing that is never in the budget. The prescriptive rung needs a trustworthy prediction, an explicit objective and real constraints - and then it needs an organisation willing to act on a recommendation it did not author. That last one is not a software requirement and no supplier will raise it. Many prescriptive systems are installed, correct, and quietly ignored, and the roadmap should say how yours will avoid that before it funds the rung.

One honest boundary. You are designing the roadmap: the decision families, the readiness gate, the sequence, the money and the refusals. You are not building the models, not procuring the platform, and not approving the budget. Your document is what the committee decides on and what the delivery team works to.

Does it scale? You cannot teach calculus to someone who has not met algebra by buying a better textbook. The textbook is excellent and the learner is not ready, and the only thing that closes that gap is the work, done in order, over time. The supplier is selling an excellent textbook.

T4: Your Turn

A district municipality asks you to design a four-year analytics roadmap for its vehicle fleet. The data profile is nothing like Corathyl's.

ItemFigure
Fleet640 vehicles - refuse trucks, water tankers, light utility, plant
Corrective work orders, last 4 years6,200
Failure coding93 per cent carry a valid failure code, from a controlled list
Distinct failure modes26
Asset registerEvery vehicle has make, model and year. 247 of 640 have a recorded duty class and operating environment; the rest have neither, and never did
Decisions wantedReplacement timing, workshop scheduling, fuel and tyre budgeting, statutory roadworthy compliance
Budget envelopeR3.8 million over four years
PressureThe fleet manager has read about predictive maintenance and wants it in year one
  1. Decision portfolio. Rank the four decision families and state the rung each genuinely needs.
  2. Readiness gate. Run the label arithmetic. Does it pass?
  3. Find the real gate. Something else is blocking the climb. Name it, quantify it, and say which rung it blocks and why.
  4. Sequence. Lay out four years with the budget, showing the climb narrowing.
  5. Refusals and detour. State what you refuse, and what the fleet manager's year-one predictive project would cost in money and in time.

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, step 2 passes, and it is meant to. 6,200 work orders at 93 per cent is 5,766 labelled events across 26 modes - 222 per mode, comfortably above the working target. If you stopped there you would conclude the fleet is ready for predictive maintenance in year one, and you would be wrong.

Second, step 3 is the exercise. The block is context, not labels. A model can only learn why something fails if it can see what distinguishes one vehicle from another, and duty class and operating environment are recorded for 247 of 640 vehicles - 39 per cent. Restrict training to vehicles with usable context and - assuming failures are evenly distributed - 5,766 events becomes about 2,225, or 86 per mode. Worse, a refuse truck doing 40 km a day on gravel and a light utility doing 120 km a day on tar look identical to the model, so the failures appear random when they are not. That blocks the diagnostic rung, and everything above it. The lesson generalises: the gate moves. At Corathyl it was labels; here it is covariates; somewhere else it will be timestamps or asset hierarchy. Find the gate, do not assume you already know it.

Third, in step 3 notice what the fix costs. Duty class cannot be back-filled from records that never existed - it has to be captured going forward, or reconstructed from telemetry and route data if any survives. That is a programme with a lead time, and the lead time is the honest reason predictive cannot happen in year one.

T5: The Guardrails

Analytics roadmaps fail in ways that are visible from the first page, once you know what to look for.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Buying the top rung. Each stage needs different tools, skills and organisational structures. Software does not carry you past a stage; it only lets you arrive there unprepared and expensively.

• Treating the ladder as a destination for everyone. Compliance reporting needs descriptive and always will. A plan that climbs it further is spending money to answer a question nobody asked.

• A foundation phase with no exit criterion. "Improve data quality" ends when someone gets tired. "Failure-code completeness above 85 per cent, measured monthly" ends when it is true.

• Assuming more data means better labels. Volume and labelling are different problems. Five years of uncoded work orders is five years of nothing to learn from.

• Assuming the gate is always labels. It moves. Missing context, missing timestamps, a broken asset hierarchy - each blocks a different rung. Diagnose it before you fund the fix.

• Quoting a training-data threshold as if it were a law. Dozens to thousands, depending on the phenomenon and the technique. Present it as a working target and show the method, not the number.

• Costing the roadmap but not the detour. The comparison is what persuades. Lost budget, twelve to eighteen months, and the executive sponsorship - which is the hardest of the three to raise a second time.

• Letting a supplier demonstration set the sequence. A demo shows the rung that photographs best. Nobody has ever demonstrated a data foundation.

• Forgetting that climbing narrows. If the same number of decision families appears at every stage, the plan has not chosen anything.

• Funding prescriptive without funding the willingness to act on it. A correct recommendation that nobody follows is the most expensive thing on the roadmap.

• Presenting refusals as defeats. The refusals are the professional content of the plan. Write them where they can be argued with, not in a footnote.

Onalenna could not out-argue a demonstration until she had counted her labels. Name the decision families and the rung each genuinely needs, accepting that some correctly stop low; test whether the data can carry the next rung and compute the gap in a number a committee can check; lay the rungs in precondition order and write down what each demands of the one below, because that is what makes the sequence a finding rather than a preference; plan it across the years with money attached and let the climb narrow; and state your refusals in the document, with the cost of the detour beside them. An analytics roadmap is not a shopping list. It is a capability sequence, gated by what your data can support and pointed at the decisions worth the climb. The ladder has rungs, and the ladder is not the goal.
