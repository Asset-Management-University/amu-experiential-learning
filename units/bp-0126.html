My budget is just last year with a bit added - how do I build one that knows what we need?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and Tank You now runs a small fleet of bakkies. It's budget time, and for the maintenance plan you do what you always do: take last year's total - say R100,000 - and add a bit for inflation. "Make it R110,000," you decide. Five minutes, done.

But that number is blind. It's just a copy of history. It doesn't know that Bakkie 4 has done double the kilometres and its brakes are feeling soft, or that Bakkie 2 sat off the road for a month and barely needs a thing. It's just a pot of money. Then a big customer pays late, cash gets tight, and you have to trim. Where do you cut? Not the fuel. The new branded door wraps are bright and customers notice them - those stay. But Bakkie 4's major service? That's a quiet cost; the bakkie still runs; nobody will notice if it slips a few months. So you cut the service - you cut the quiet, essential thing because it has the weakest voice.

A month later Sipho, at the wheel, calls you with a shake in his voice: coming down a long hill fully loaded, the brakes faded to almost nothing. He stopped, just. The starved essential nearly bit, hard.

My budget is just a copy of last year with a bit added - it has no eyes. How do I build a number that actually KNOWS what my bakkies need?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to fix the blind number. Maybe you were just too stingy - next year add 20% instead of 10%! But a bigger pot is still a blind pot: you'll overspend on bakkies that don't need it and maybe still starve the one that does. Or, when you must cut, you try to be "fair" and shave the same 15% off every line - which treats a critical brake job exactly like a cracked mirror, starving the vital safety work as much as the cosmetic fix.

Both fail for the same reason: you're still treating the budget as one number handed down to the assets, from the top. So where should the number come from instead?

My budget is last year + 10%, done in five minutes - a number with no eyes. It can't see that one bakkie's brakes are worn and another's barely driven. So when cash got tight I cut the quiet brake service, not the visible stuff - and nearly caused a crash.
What if the number came FROM the assets, not from history? For each thing, two simple questions: how bad if it fails, and what state is it in right now?
Build the budget bottom-up: high-consequence + poor-condition = fund first; minor + fine = wait. Cut where need is lowest, never where resistance is weakest. Then every rand is tied to a named item - a number you can defend.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Reverse the flow of information: the things you own must tell you the number, instead of the number being handed down to the things. Don't start from last year's R100,000 - start from a blank page and a list of your bakkies, and build up. Five moves:

  • Reverse the flow - the things tell you the number: stop starting from last year's total and tweaking it - that number is blind; it can't see that one asset is worn and another barely used. Start instead from a blank page and a list of your actual things. The whole shift: the things you own tell you the number, instead of a number being handed down to the things.
  • Ask: how BAD if it fails? (consequence): for each item, judge what would happen if it failed. Brakes failing = a possible crash (very high). A gearbox = a stranded bakkie and a big bill (high). The air-con = a grumpy driver (low). The radio = a small annoyance (very low). This is the item's CONSEQUENCE - and it has nothing to do with how loudly anyone defends it.
  • Ask: what STATE is it in now? (condition): walk around with your mechanic and judge each item's CONDITION today: Bakkie 4's brakes feel soft (poor); Bakkie 1's tyres are near the limit (poor); Bakkie 2's air-con works fine (good). Real eyes on the real things, not a guess from behind a desk.
  • Fund by need - every rand tied to a named item: the highest need is where HIGH consequence meets POOR condition: Bakkie 4's brakes and Bakkie 1's tyres get funded first and fully. Low-consequence-and-good-condition (the working air-con) gets little or nothing this year. Some items get more, some honestly less - and every rand you ask for now points at a named item and a named consequence.
  • Cut where need is lowest - and defend the number: if cuts must come, cut where NEED is lowest, never where RESISTANCE is weakest - protect the silent essentials (the brake service nobody shouts about). Yes, it's more work than copying last year, and the total may even rise - but now the judgement is visible and you have a number you can DEFEND, item by item: 'we fund the brakes because they're a safety risk; we skip the air-con because it works.'

Watch it work. You go down the fleet with two questions. How bad if it fails? - brakes: a crash (very high); gearbox: a stranded bakkie and a big bill (high); air-con: a grumpy driver (low). What state now? - Bakkie 4's brakes are soft (poor); Bakkie 1's tyres near the limit (poor); Bakkie 2's air-con fine (good). High-and-poor wins the money: Bakkie 4's brakes and Bakkie 1's tyres get funded first and fully; the working air-con gets nothing this year. You build it up, item by item, to an honest R125,000 - more than last year, but every rand has a reason. And when Ibraaheem asks why it's higher, you don't shrug - you show him the list: "R125,000 because Bakkie 4's brakes are a safety risk and Bakkie 1's tyres are nearly illegal; we save by skipping the air-con, but we cannot skip the brakes." A number you can defend.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a fleet - it's about any budget you set by copying last year. Watch it in a household.

See the same reversal rescue a household budget

The default at home is "last year's spending, plus a bit". And when money's tight, what gets cut first? Usually the quiet, non-urgent essentials: the small amount into savings, the home or funeral cover, the bit set aside for the geyser that will one day burst. They don't shout. The loud things - a new phone, takeaways, a weekend away - often survive, because the wanting is felt right now. The silent essentials get starved exactly like the brakes.

Rebuild one part from need:

• List the real "assets": your health, the home (roof, geyser), the car, your future (savings), time with people.

• How bad if it fails? No savings when work dries up = very high; the geyser bursts in winter = high; missing one night out = low.

• What state now? Savings near zero = poor; the geyser old and groaning = poor.

• Fund by need: high-and-poor (savings, the geyser) get protected first; the loud wants wait. Your budget stops being a thing of guilt and becomes a tool for security.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at a budget you're responsible for - a department, a team, a club, your own family's spending. Then rebuild one slice from need:

  1. Find the "copy-forward" number. One recurring figure set by taking last year's and tweaking it - maintenance, supplies, training, marketing.
  2. Ignore the old number; list the real things. The actual assets, people, or goals the budget is meant to serve.
  3. Ask the two questions and build up. For each: how bad if it fails (consequence)? what state is it in now (condition)? Fund the high-and-poor first; be honest about what gets less or nothing - and protect the silent essentials from the easy cut.

Rebuild one budget slice from need this week, and write the one-line reason beside each number. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build the list together.)

A budget copied forward is a guess wearing a suit - it looks official and sees nothing. When you build the number from the things themselves, two things change: your money finally lands where the real need is, not where the loudest voice is; and you walk into any room with a number you can defend, rand by rand, because every one of them is tied to something real.
