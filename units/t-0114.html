Ten days became twenty-five

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. The works needs a major shutdown - the whole plant stopped, opened up, overhauled, and restarted. You plan it carefully: ten days, every job listed, every crew booked. Ten days, and the town is back on full supply.

It runs to twenty-five.

And when you look back, the most unsettling thing is that nothing went badly wrong. There was no flood, no broken part, no disaster. There was just... a steady drip. "While we are in there, let us also overhaul that second pump." "While the line is open, let us replace that old valve." "While we have the crane, let us repaint that section." Each one sounded completely reasonable. Each one was a small yes. Nobody ever refused a single one - and you never froze the list. A hundred small, reasonable yeses tangled the schedule, trebled the cost, and kept the plant down for over three weeks instead of ten days, with a lost-production bill that made your stomach drop.

My shutdown more than doubled - not from one big disaster, but from a hundred small "while we are in there" jobs nobody refused. How do I run a planned overhaul so it finishes on time, when every reasonable-sounding addition quietly destroys it?

Before you read on, decide. The window was ten days. Every day the plant is down costs R500,000 in lost production. Nothing went wrong — no disaster, no failure, no incompetence. What happened was about a hundred requests, each one sensible: while we are in there, could we also do this valve, that gearbox, this bit of pipework nobody can reach when we are running. Every one came from somebody reasonable with a good argument, and you refused none of them. It ran to twenty-five days. Write down how you would run the next one so this cannot happen — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why none of the hundred yeses was wrong

• “Be firmer — start saying no.” Say it to whom? Each request came with a real reason and a person standing in front of you, and “no” from a manager on the day is an argument you have to win a hundred separate times, under pressure, while the plant is down. Willpower is not a control.

• “Pad the plan — call it twenty days next time.” Then the additions fill twenty, because nothing about the mechanism has changed. You will also have doubled the cost you plan for and still overrun it.

• “Put more people and hours on it.” Expensive, and mostly wasted — as the next paragraph shows, effort spread evenly across every job is effort aimed almost entirely at jobs that cannot shorten the outage by an hour.

• “Plan the work in far more detail up front.” Necessary and insufficient. Your plan was not wrong; it was overtaken. A better plan with an open door at the end of it still ends up at twenty-five days.

• “Track the additions so I can see them accumulating.” Real progress, and it is measurement rather than control — you would have watched the overrun arrive with excellent visibility and no mechanism to stop it.

• The thing to see is that you never made one bad decision — you made one decision a hundred times, badly structured. Each was taken in the moment, socially, by whoever was asked, with the cost invisible to the person requesting. So the fix is not to be tougher but to make refusal a system rather than an argument. Challenge every job against a hard question — must this happen inside this window at all, or could it wait, or could it be done while running? Then freeze the list on a date, which is what turns an endless negotiation into a decision. Find and protect the chain of dependent jobs that actually sets the ten days, because speeding up anything off that chain cannot save you an hour, and that is where most well-meant help goes. And let anything proposed after the freeze meet a gate that says not “no” but “prove it” — how many days, how much money, why it cannot wait. Most additions cannot answer, and now nobody has to be the person who refused them.

There is a discipline whose whole purpose is to win exactly this battle. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the one-page dashboard that keeps a shutdown on its ten days - the thing Onalenna will never run an overhaul without again. Watch how it makes saying "no" a system, not an argument.

1. The fixed window - and what every extra day costs

The window is FIXED: 10 days, and every day the plant is down costs R500,000 in lost production. So every extra day is not a small thing - it is half a million rand. That cost is exactly why scope discipline matters.

2. The challenged worklist - then FROZEN

The worklist is challenged, then FROZEN by a deadline. Every job earns a code: M-NOW (must be done, in THIS shutdown), C-WAIT (could wait for the next one), or R-OP (can be done while running). Only the M-NOW jobs make the frozen list; the rest come off.

3. The critical path - the chain to protect

The critical path: the few dependent jobs whose chain sets the 10 days. PROTECT and resource this chain - speeding up any job NOT on it is wasted effort, because it cannot shorten the shutdown by a single day.

4. The change gate - 'prove it', not a friendly nod

The change gate: any job proposed AFTER the freeze must pass a formal review, not a friendly nod. Is it safety- or integrity-critical? How many days does it add? How much cost? Why can it not wait? The gate does not say 'no' - it says 'prove it'. Most additions cannot, and are deferred to the next shutdown. (Approved changes go through Management of Change.)

5. The key risks - plan them, do not discover them

Two risks to plan, not discover: SIMOPS (simultaneous operations - many crews, cranes and hot work crammed into one space and time is a serious safety hazard), and a phased, careful START-UP (a rushed restart trips the plant and undoes a perfect shutdown).

Look at what it does. It puts a hard rand cost on every extra day, so nobody treats "just one more job" as free. It challenges every job and then freezes the list. And it builds a gate that meets every late addition not with a person's "no", but with a system's "prove it". The twenty-five-day disaster was simply a list that was never frozen and a gate that never existed. Now let us walk through it.

T3: The Blueprint

This is turnaround management (running a planned shutdown, or "TAR"). And the enemy has a name: scope creep - the silent killer of every big shutdown. It is different from your routine maintenance planning, because here the whole plant is stopped and open, the clock is brutal, and the temptation to do "just one more thing while we are in there" is everywhere. Each part of the dashboard answers a question:

  • Does this job HAVE to be done now, in THIS shutdown? The challenged worklist. You interrogate every job: must it be done now (M-NOW), could it wait for the next shutdown (C-WAIT), or could it be done while the plant is still running (R-OP)? Only the must-do-nows survive. Then you freeze the list by a deadline.
  • What is the chain we must protect? The critical path - the longest chain of jobs that must happen one after another, which sets the minimum number of days. Pour your resources into protecting this chain; speeding up a job that is not on it is wasted effort, because it cannot make the shutdown a single day shorter.
  • Does this late idea justify what it costs us? The change gate. After the freeze, any new job must pass a formal review - not a quiet word in someone's ear. Here is the key: the gate does not say "no", it says "prove it". Is it safety- or integrity-critical? Exactly how many days does it add? How much cost? Why can it not wait for next time? Turning "no" into "prove it" makes saying no a professional discipline, not a personal refusal - and most additions cannot prove it, so they are deferred. (The ones that pass go through proper change control - a Management of Change; bp-0132 shows what a plant becomes when that discipline quietly lapses.)

And two risks you plan for, never discover: SIMOPS (simultaneous operations - cramming many crews, cranes and hot work into one space and time is a serious safety hazard that must be managed, not stumbled into), and a phased, careful start-up - because a rushed restart can trip the plant and throw away a perfectly run shutdown.

Does it scale? The challenge of freezing the Corathyl worklist is the exact same discipline a manager uses to control a refinery turnaround, an aircraft's heavy maintenance check, or a power-station outage - where a single un-frozen scope can turn a planned outage into a runaway one costing millions a day.

T4: Your Turn

Now run your own "shutdown". You have one weekend to move house - it starts Friday at 18:00, and you must be running (sleeping in your made-up bed in the new place) by Sunday at 22:00. A hard window, just like a plant.

  1. List the jobs - pack, clean, move the big items, set up the bed, connect the internet, unpack the kitchen, hang the pictures, and so on.
  2. Challenge the list: mark each one M-NOW (must be this weekend) or C-WAIT (can wait until next week).
  3. Freeze your worklist - the M-NOWs only.
  4. The gate: a friend says, "while we have the van, let us also fetch that wardrobe from my cousin's." Write the one gate question you will ask them to decide if it gets in.

T5: The Guardrails

Turnaround discipline is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you plan your next shutdown.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• No freeze. Treating the worklist as a suggestion instead of a locked commitment - the open door through which a hundred "while we are in there" jobs walk. Freeze it by a date, and defend the date.

• No challenge. Assuming every requested job is necessary, so the scope is bloated before you even start. Interrogate each one: must it be now, in this shutdown? Many can wait, or be done while running.

• An informal gate. A "gate" that is really just one tired person who can be worn down into a yes. It must be a formal, data-driven review that asks "prove it" - cost, days, criticality - the same for everyone.

• Crashing the wrong jobs. Pouring overtime into speeding up a job that is not on the critical path. It will not shorten the shutdown by a minute - find the critical chain and protect that.

• SIMOPS blindness, and a rushed start-up. Letting too many crews and cranes and hot work pile into one space unmanaged, or rushing the restart so the plant trips - and undoes a perfect shutdown in an hour. Both are planned for, never discovered.

Onalenna's shutdown did not double because her team was lazy or unlucky - it doubled because every reasonable little "yes" was free, and no system ever made one of them prove its worth. Now you hold that system: cost the days, challenge every job, freeze the list, and meet every latecomer with "prove it". Do that, and your overhaul finishes when you said it would - and you will have learned the quiet discipline behind every shutdown that comes back on time: saying no, fairly, to a hundred good ideas, so the right ones can be done well.
