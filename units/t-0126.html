The station that no longer existed

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have a digital twin of the pump station that everybody loves. It was built properly. It was validated against real measurements on the day it was commissioned. For a year and a half it has answered questions, settled arguments and saved trips to site.

So when you need to plan a duty change, you plan it on the twin. The model says the new arrangement will hold pressure comfortably. You schedule it.

It fails within minutes - in exactly the way the twin said was impossible.

Afterwards you sit down and list what has actually happened to that station since commissioning. A pump was replaced after a failure, with a different model. An impeller was trimmed. A control setpoint was changed - twice. A strainer was added. Every one of those was a sound engineering decision, properly executed, correctly documented on the plant. Not one of them ever reached the twin.

The model was never lying. It has been faithfully, accurately describing the pump station as it existed eighteen months ago - a station that stopped existing, piece by piece, while everyone went on trusting the screen. And here is the part that keeps you awake: because the twin still looked exactly as authoritative as it did on day one, nobody thought to check. Had you had no twin at all, you would have walked down there and looked.

My twin was built well and validated properly, and it has quietly become fiction - because the real plant changed and the model did not. How do I keep a digital twin TRUE across the life of the asset?

Before you read on, decide. The twin was built well and validated properly. Since then a pump was swapped, an impeller was trimmed, a strainer was fitted and a setpoint was altered — every one of those a proper job, done correctly, signed off. None of them reached the model. You ran the station off the twin, it told you a station that no longer exists, and you have paid for that. What keeps you awake is that the screen looked exactly as authoritative the whole way through, so nobody thought to check — and had there been no twin at all, you would have walked down there and looked. Write down how you would keep a twin true across an asset's life — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why a stale model is worse than none

• “Rebuild and revalidate it.” Gives you a correct twin on one day and starts the same decay from that day. In two years you are back here, having spent the money twice.

• “Review it annually.” Better than nothing, and it means the twin is wrong for up to a year at a time while looking right — and every decision taken in that year is taken on a document nobody has qualified.

• “Get a better modelling tool.” Your model was not deficient; it was overtaken. A more sophisticated model of a station that no longer exists is a more sophisticated fiction.

• “Train people not to trust it blindly.” Asks everybody to hold a permanent suspicion of a tool that shows no sign of being wrong, which nobody can sustain — and if they genuinely did distrust it they would stop using it, and you would have paid for a twin nobody uses.

• “Stop using twins — go and look at the plant.” Take this one seriously, because it is nearly the finding. A stale twin genuinely did leave you worse off than none: without it, somebody walks down there. That is not an argument against twins; it is the reason maintaining one is the condition of it being safe to have.

• What you need is not a modelling technique at all but a control system over the relationship between the model and the metal, and it is the one engineers have always used for drawings. Say precisely what the twin is and what it claims to represent, versioned — and stamp it with the date it was last checked against the physical asset, because without that date nobody can judge how far to trust it. Bind the change: no physical alteration is signed off as complete until the twin has been updated and checked, so the update is part of the job rather than a follow-up somebody may get to. Keep an honest ledger of what changed, and — this is the part that makes it usable — of known departures not yet built in, so anyone opening it sees how true it currently is instead of assuming perfection. And watch for drift by comparing what it predicts against what the plant actually does.

There is a one-page discipline for this, borrowed from how engineers have always controlled drawings. Let us show you it - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the plan that keeps a twin honest - not a modelling technique, but a control system for the relationship between the model and the metal.

1. The baseline - what IS the twin, and which asset does it claim to be?

Configuration identification. The twin is not 'the model' - it is a named set: hydraulic model v2.3, pump curves for the units actually installed, the current control setpoints, and the asset configuration it claims to represent. Stamped on it: LAST VERIFIED AGAINST THE PHYSICAL ASSET - 14 March. Without that date, nobody can judge how far to trust it.

2. The change gate - no physical change without its twin update

Bind the digital to the physical. Every Management of Change for this station carries a required line: 'twin update and verification'. Swapping a pump, trimming an impeller or altering a setpoint cannot be signed off as complete until the twin has been updated and checked. The twin update is part of the job, not a follow-up someone may get to.

3. Status accounting - the running record of what is and is not current

The honest ledger: what changed, when, by whom, and which twin version now holds it - plus every KNOWN departure not yet built in ('new strainer fitted 2 May, not yet in the model'). Anyone opening the twin can see exactly how true it currently is, rather than assuming it is perfect.

4. Drift watch - two different ways it goes wrong

Compare what the twin predicts against what the plant actually did, on a schedule. Two distinct failures: CONFIGURATION DRIFT (the asset changed and the model did not - a process failure, fixed by the change gate) and MODEL DRIFT (nothing physical changed, but the model itself has degraded as conditions evolved - fixed by recalibration). A persistent divergence is a signal, not noise.

5. The audit and the declaration - go and look, then say so

Walk the plant against the model - the configuration audit exists precisely to confirm that the asset is accurately reflected by its documents. Then declare it: every screen and report carries 'last verified against the asset on <date>'. A twin whose age is visible can be trusted appropriately; a twin whose age is hidden will be trusted absolutely, which is far more dangerous.

Look at the second box, because that is the one that actually does the work. The twin update is not a task someone will get to; it is written into the change itself, so the physical job cannot be signed off until the model has caught up. And look at the last box: the twin's age is published, not hidden. Now let us name the discipline.

T3: The Blueprint

Start with a reframe that changes everything: a digital twin is not a thing you build - it is a claim you maintain. It claims "this is what the real asset is, and how it behaves". Claims decay. The standards say as much: a digital twin is meant to be a living model, one that keeps changing as its physical counterpart changes - and "living" means maintained, not merely born.

There are two quite different ways the claim goes false, and they need different answers:

  • Configuration drift. The physical asset changed - a pump swapped, an impeller trimmed, a setpoint altered - and the model did not follow. This is a process failure, and no amount of clever modelling fixes it. It is fixed by controlling change.
  • Model drift. Nothing physical changed at all, but the model's own accuracy has degraded as operating conditions evolved. This is a failure of the model's continued validity, and it is fixed by recalibrating against fresh measurements.

The cure for the first is the oldest discipline in engineering documentation - configuration management - applied to the twin exactly as it is applied to drawings. Each part answers a question:

  • What exactly is the twin, and which asset state does it claim? Configuration identification: name the model versions, parameter sets and the asset configuration it represents - and separate as-built (how it left the project) from as-maintained (how it stands today, after every repair and modification). A twin that is still as-built is already out of date.
  • How does a physical change reach it? Change control - and this is the decisive move: bind the twin update into your Management of Change process as a required deliverable. The change is not complete until the twin is updated and verified. Not a follow-up. Part of the job.
  • Where is the record? Status accounting: the formal record of what changed, when, and the status of every change - including departures from the baseline that are known but not yet built in. This is what lets someone judge the twin instead of guessing.
  • When did anyone last check it against the real thing? Configuration audit - whose entire purpose is to confirm that the asset is accurately reflected by its documents. Walk the plant against the model, then publish the date.

And carry this last idea carefully, because it is the reason the whole unit exists: an out-of-date twin can be more dangerous than no twin at all. Without a twin, an engineer's instinct is caution - they go and look. With a confident, authoritative, drifted twin, their instinct is trust. It is the confidence that hurts you.

Does it scale? A beautiful, trusted map that does not show the road closed six months ago will drive you straight into the barrier - and you will follow it, precisely because you trust it. Your twin must be managed the way plant engineers manage as-maintained drawings rather than the original as-built set: verifiably reflecting every change since day one.

T4: Your Turn

Now put something you rely on under configuration control. Choose a model or document whose accuracy people trust - a spreadsheet that calculates something, a set of drawings, a room-booking system, a shared procedure, even a recipe scaled up for a big kitchen.

  1. Name the baseline. What exactly is it, what version, and what real-world state does it claim to describe?
  2. Design the change gate. When the real thing changes, what forces the update to happen? Make it part of the change, not a reminder.
  3. Write the record. Where would someone look to see what is current and what is known to be out of date?
  4. Set a drift check. How would you notice it had quietly stopped matching reality?
  5. Add the declaration. Write the "last verified on ___" line, and say where it will be visible.

The test of a good answer: if the real thing changed tomorrow and the person who changed it went on leave, would your model still get updated? If the answer depends on somebody remembering, you have written a hope, not a gate.

T5: The Guardrails

Twins fail in service far more often than they fail in construction. Glance through these before you hand one over.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Treating the twin as a project deliverable. It is handed over, celebrated, and then owned by nobody. A twin is an asset with a maintenance requirement, not a report that is finished.

• Letting physical changes through without a twin update. The single biggest cause of drift, and exactly what happened at Corathyl. If the twin update is not written into the change, it will not happen - not through negligence, but because everyone is busy.

• Assuming an unchanged asset means an accurate model. Model drift needs no physical change at all: a data-driven model can quietly lose accuracy as conditions evolve. Recalibrate against fresh measurement, do not just check the parts list.

• No status accounting. If nobody records what has and has not been updated, the twin becomes an article of faith. The record is what lets an engineer judge how much weight to put on it today.

• Never walking the plant. Comparing a model to other documents is not verification. At some point somebody must physically check that the asset matches the claim.

• Hiding the last-verified date. A twin whose age is visible gets trusted appropriately; a twin whose age is invisible gets trusted absolutely. Publish it on every screen and report.

• Trusting it because it looks impressive. Fidelity and currency are different things. A beautifully rendered, physics-rich model of last year's plant is still a picture of last year's plant.

Onalenna's twin never told her a lie - it faithfully described a pump station that had quietly ceased to exist, while looking every bit as confident as the day it was built. Now you hold the discipline that prevents that: know exactly what your twin claims and which asset state it claims it for, bind its update into every physical change, keep an honest record of what is current, watch for both kinds of drift, and go and check against the real thing - then publish the date so everyone can see how far to trust it. Do that, and a twin stays what it was meant to be: not an impressive artefact, but a true and living reflection of something real.
