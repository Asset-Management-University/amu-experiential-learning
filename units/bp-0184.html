Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Ntombizanele, and as of three weeks ago you are the new operations manager for the town's water network. It is just after seven on a cold Tuesday morning at the municipal water depot, and you are standing in the yard with a clipboard you do not really want to be holding. The job came with a title, a bakkie, and a problem nobody could explain to you in plain words.

In your hand is a single sheet from the treatment plant: last month it produced and pumped out 1,000 megalitres of clean water - that is a billion litres. On the desk inside is the billing report from finance. Last month the town BILLED customers for about 600 megalitres. So four hundred million litres of treated, pumped, chemically dosed water left the plant and was never paid for by anyone. On the sheet someone has scrawled in pen: "40% loss - same as last year, and the year before."

Mthokozisi, the old foreman who has worked this network for nineteen years, is leaning on the bakkie watching you read it. He has the look of a man who has watched three managers before you arrive, frown at this same page, and leave. "Everyone gets upset about the number," he says, not unkindly. "Then they go and dig up one street, and the number doesn't move." You can hear it before you see it. Two roads over there is a soft, constant hiss and the smell of wet concrete that never dries - a main has been weeping under the tar for months; the patch of road there is always dark. But Mthokozisi also takes you past a phase where a whole row of new backyard rooms have thin black pipes running over the wall from the municipal line, no meters, no accounts. People are washing cars with a hose. And the meters that DO exist? The reader has not been into half the town since March. Some accounts have not been billed in a year.

The numbers sit on you. The plant costs roughly R2.80 per kilolitre to treat and pump - so those four hundred missing megalitres cost the town somewhere near R1.1 million every month to produce, and not one cent of it comes back. Your budget for repairs this year is R6 million. The mayor's office wants the loss "fixed" before the next council meeting. Your name is now on this. Your first instinct is loud and simple: get a team and start digging up the leaks. Water is pouring out of the ground where everyone can see it - fix the visible thing, show the council a photo of a repaired pipe, prove you are doing something. A billion litres in. Six hundred million billed. Four hundred million gone - to leaks, to illegal pipes, to meters nobody reads - and R6 million to spend, once.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The mayor wants a photo and water is visibly pouring from the ground. "Send a team and dig up the leaks everyone can see." But Mthokozisi has watched three managers do exactly that and the 40% never moved - the visible leak may be the smaller half, and you would spend your one big budget aiming at it blind. "Bake more - produce extra to cover the 400 megalitres lost." But making more of something you already fail to capture just grows the loss; you would treat and pump more water and lose 40% of THAT too. "Cut off every unmetered connection tomorrow." But some of those are households who genuinely cannot pay, and a blanket disconnection answers a fairness question with a spanner - the wrong tool and the wrong ethics. So before you read on: name the TWO different kinds of loss hiding inside that one 40% number, and the one thing you must do before you send a single team out.

I am Ntombizanele, three weeks into being the new operations manager for the town's water network. The job came with a title, a bakkie, and a problem nobody could explain in plain words. In my hand is one sheet: last month the treatment plant produced and pumped 1,000 megalitres of clean water - a billion litres - but finance billed customers for only about 600 megalitres. So 400 million litres of treated, pumped, chemically dosed water left the plant and was never paid for by anyone. Someone scrawled '40% loss - same as last year, and the year before.' Mthokozisi, the foreman of nineteen years, has watched three managers frown at this same page and leave: 'Everyone gets upset about the number. Then they go and dig up one street, and the number doesn't move.' I can HEAR part of the loss - two roads over, a main weeping under the tar for months. But Mthokozisi also walks me past a row of new backyard rooms with thin black pipes run over the wall from the municipal line, no meters, no accounts; and the meters that DO exist, the reader hasn't been to half the town since March. The water costs about R2.80 a kilolitre to treat and pump, so the 400 missing megalitres cost about R1.1 million every month to make, and not a cent comes back. My repair budget is R6 million, once. My instinct is loud: get a team, dig up the visible leaks, show the council a photo. Where do I start?
Mthokozisi's warning is the clue: managers dig one street and the number doesn't move, which means the visible leaks are not the whole story. '40% loss' is ONE number, but it is hiding TWO completely different problems with two completely different cures. Some of that water PHYSICALLY ESCAPES the pipes and never reaches anyone - the weeping main, bursts, overflowing reservoirs: gone into the ground, recovered by FIXING INFRASTRUCTURE (a spanner). But some of it DOES reach people and IS used, and is simply never measured or billed - the illegal connections over the wall, the dead and unread meters, the accounts never invoiced: delivered but not captured, recovered not with a spanner but with METERS, METER-READING and BILLING. A leak team can do nothing about an illegal connection; a meter reader can do nothing about a burst main. So before you send anyone out, you cannot fix what you have not SIZED: build the balance (produced minus billed = the gap), split the gap into physical versus commercial, and MEASURE the split cheaply (a minimum-night-flow test gives the leakage share) instead of guessing it - then aim your one R6 million spend at the biggest, most-recoverable half. The real question is not 'which leak do I dig?' but 'how much of this gap is water escaping versus water used-but-unbilled - and which half recovers the most per rand?'
Five moves turn a paralysing 40% into a ranked plan. (1) BUILD THE BALANCE: write the two honest numbers - produced (1,000 Ml) and billed (600 Ml) - and name the gap (400 Ml, 40%): water that left the plant and was never paid for. (2) SPLIT THE GAP INTO TWO LOSSES: PHYSICAL (water escapes the pipes - leaks, bursts; fix infrastructure) and COMMERCIAL (water used but never measured or billed - illegal connections, dead meters, unbilled accounts; fix metering and billing) - same number, opposite cures, and splitting it is the step the three managers before you skipped. (3) MEASURE THE SPLIT, DON'T GUESS: do the cheap detective work - at night when nobody uses water, the minimum night flow into a sealed zone is leakage, which sizes the physical half; subtract it and the rest is commercial. The split comes out ~150 Ml physical and ~250 Ml commercial - and the SURPRISE lands: the visible, dramatic leaks your instinct screamed to fix are the SMALLER half; the bigger half is invisible water past dead meters and through illegal pipes. (4) TARGET BY RECOVERY-PER-RAND, biggest and cheapest first: rank fixes by rands recovered per rand spent, not by which is loudest - a meter-reading-and-billing blitz attacks the larger commercial half and is almost pure profit (the water is already delivered), so it goes first; illegal-connection metering next; a TARGETED leak repair only on the worst-measured physical zone, not a blind dig-everywhere. (5) HOLD THE TRADE-OFFS AND THE FAIRNESS: measuring looks like doing nothing while the council wants a photo, and some 'illegal' connections are poor households who genuinely cannot pay - a fairness question a meter alone does not answer. A big loss is two problems wearing one number; build the balance, split it, size each half, aim at the biggest recoverable part first.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Ntombizanele did not send a team out that first week. She did something that felt, to the mayor's office, like doing nothing - and it was the most important thing she did all year. Five moves:

  • Build the balance - produced versus billed: Ntombizanele did not send a team out that first week. She did something that felt, to the mayor's office, like doing nothing - and it was the most important thing she did all year. She wrote two numbers at the top of a page: water PRODUCED (1,000 Ml) and water BILLED (600 Ml). The gap between them - 400 Ml - is the thing everyone calls 'the loss', and putting it as a balance made it legible: this is not a vague crisis, it is one quantity, water that left the plant and was never paid for, 40% of everything they treated. You cannot manage a number you have not written down honestly, and the balance converts a year of frustrated outrage ('40% loss - same as last year') into a single, sized quantity you can now take apart. Before any team, any dig, any spend - the balance is the page everything else is built on.
  • Split the gap into two losses - physical versus commercial: then she split the gap into two kinds of loss, because they are not the same problem and do not have the same cure - and this was the step the three managers before her had skipped. PHYSICAL losses are water that physically escapes the pipes and never reaches a customer at all: the weeping main, bursts, overflowing reservoirs - water gone into the ground, recovered by fixing infrastructure. COMMERCIAL losses are water that DOES reach people and IS used, but is never measured or billed: illegal connections, broken or unread meters, accounts that exist but are never invoiced - water delivered but not captured, recovered not with a spanner but with meters, meter-reading and billing. The two look like one number on the finance report; they are completely different fixes. A leak team cannot do anything about an illegal connection; a meter reader cannot do anything about a burst main. So 'fix the loss' is not one job - it is two jobs, and until you separate them you will keep sending the wrong team at the wrong half.
  • Measure the split, don't guess it: then she measured the split instead of guessing it - because she could not fix what she had not sized. So she did the cheap detective work first: at night, when nobody is using water, she had the team measure the flow into one pressurised zone - the minimum night flow. In a sealed network with no use, that flow should be near zero; whatever is still running is leakage, and that gave her the physical-loss share. She subtracted it from the total gap, and what was left was the commercial loss - water being used but not billed. The split came out roughly: of the 400 Ml gap, about 150 Ml was physical (real leaks in the ground) and about 250 Ml was commercial (illegal connections, dead meters, unbilled accounts). That single cheap measurement turned the whole problem around - here is the SURPRISE: the visible, dramatic leaks, the thing her instinct screamed to fix first, were the SMALLER half. The bigger half was invisible - water flowing through illegal pipes and past dead meters, perfectly usable, simply never charged for. The thing you can hear hissing under the road is rarely the thing that is costing you most.
  • Target by recovery-per-rand, biggest and cheapest first: then she targeted the biggest, most-recoverable loss first. She had R6 million, once, so she ranked the fixes not by which was loudest but by rands recovered per rand spent. Digging up the weeping main: real, but expensive per litre, and it only addresses part of the smaller physical half. A meter-reading and billing blitz - read every meter, reconnect the dead ones, invoice the accounts that exist but were never billed: cheap, fast, and it attacks the larger commercial half directly, almost pure profit since the water is already being delivered. A drive on the worst illegal-connection zone - fit bulk meters and proper metered, billed connections (you cannot bill what you cannot measure): medium cost, and it attacks the largest single chunk of the commercial loss. So the decision was not 'fix the leaks the council can photograph.' It was: recover the most water per rand by going at the commercial half first - the billing blitz immediately, the illegal-connection metering next, and a TARGETED leak repair only on the worst-measured physical zone, not a blind dig-everywhere. Digging a street felt like action, but it was aiming the one big spend at the smaller half of the problem with your eyes closed. (Setting the tariff high enough to cover the cost of the water you DO bill is the sibling problem of bp-0183; aiming effort at the system's true constraint rather than the loudest part is bp-0097.)
  • Hold the honest trade-offs and the fairness question: and she held the honest trade-offs, because none of this is free or clean. Building the balance and measuring the split takes weeks while the council wants a photo NOW - you spend political capital looking like you are doing nothing, which is exactly why the easy thing, year after year, is to dig one street and watch the number not move. Chasing illegal connections is unpopular: people who have had free water will be angry - and, the part a meter cannot answer, some 'illegal' connections are poor households who genuinely cannot pay, which is a fairness question, not a metering question; the honest answer there is a subsidised lifeline tariff or a flow limiter, not a simple disconnection, because the mission is to serve the people the water is for, not just the balance sheet. And every kilolitre you start billing for is a kilolitre someone now has to find the money for. Measuring before fixing is always the slower, less photogenic road - but it is the road that actually moves the number, spends the public's R6 million where it recovers the most, and faces the affordability question squarely instead of pretending a spanner can solve it.

See where it lands. She spends her first weeks measuring, not digging, then aims the R6 million at the commercial half first - the billing blitz that is almost pure profit, then the illegal-connection metering, then one targeted leak repair where the night-flow test actually found the water. A large share of what you produce is never paid for, and that loss is two different problems wearing one number - water that escapes (physical loss, fixed with infrastructure) and water that is used but never captured (commercial loss, fixed with meters and billing). You cannot cut it by digging the loudest leak. Build the balance (produced minus billed), split it, MEASURE each half instead of guessing, and aim your one big spend at the biggest, most-recoverable part first - usually the commercial half, near-pure profit because the product is already delivered. Counting what you produce is not the test; measuring the gap between produced and paid, splitting it, and facing the fairness inside it - that is the work.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own world, try one in-between case - a little harder than Ntombizanele's, because here the missing product is not water, and the loss does not hiss at you from under the road. It hides in plain sight.

See the bakery that bakes 600 loaves and only rings up 400

Nkosinathi runs a busy bakery, and he is working flat out and still going backwards. Each day his ovens turn out 600 loaves - he knows that, because he counts the trays. At the till, the day's slips add up to about 400 loaves sold. So 200 loaves a day - a third of everything he bakes - produces no money, and he cannot understand why he is so tired and so broke at the same time. His instinct is the same as Ntombizanele's first instinct: bake MORE, work HARDER, push 600 up to 700.

Work it with him. First, build the balance: the only two numbers he needs at the top of the page are loaves PRODUCED (600) and loaves PAID FOR (400), and the gap is 200 a day - about a third, the same shape as the town's 40%: a large share of what he makes is never paid for. Now split the gap, the way Ntombizanele split hers. Some of those 200 loaves never reach a paying customer at all: loaves that burn, fall, go stale unsold, or get binned - product destroyed before anyone buys it, his version of the leaks in the ground (physical loss). And some reach customers but the money is never captured: loaves that ARE eaten or carried out but never ring up at the till - staff eating stock, "freebies" to friends, a till that isn't recording, simple theft - his version of the illegal connections and dead meters (commercial loss). Two different problems, one missing number. Now size each half: he counts the bin at closing and finds about 40 loaves thrown away (physical). That leaves 160 loaves a day that left the shop but never rang up (commercial) - four times bigger, and far cheaper to recover, because the loaves are already baked and gone, so every one he starts capturing is almost pure profit. So he fixes the till discipline and the staff-and-freebie leak first; the stale-and-burnt 40 is a smaller, second job. Baking 700 instead of 600 would just grow the loss. The loss-balance shape is not about water and it is not about bread - it is about any product where a large share of what you make is never paid for: build the balance, split physical from commercial, size each half, and aim the first fix at the biggest, most-recoverable part. Counting what you produce is not the test; measuring the gap between produced and paid - and splitting it - is.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Ntombizanele's problem was not "a leaking pipe." It was a large share of what we produce never being paid for - a gap that is really two different losses hiding inside one number. That same shape shows up far beyond a water network. Find it where you are:

  1. Write your own balance. How much do you produce or buy in, and how much do you actually get paid for? Put the gap between them in numbers. If the gap is big, you have this problem - and you almost certainly cannot yet say why.
  2. Split your gap into the two kinds, then size them. Which part is PHYSICAL loss - product that escapes, spoils, breaks, or is destroyed before anyone uses it? Which part is COMMERCIAL loss - product that IS used but never measured, recorded, or billed? Most people guess this split and most guesses are wrong - so do the cheap detective work (a closing stock count, a quiet-hour check, a meter read) that tells you how big each half really is.
  3. Resist the loud fix. What is your "dig up the visible leak" - the dramatic, photogenic action that feels like progress but only attacks the smaller half? Name it, and notice the pull toward it.
  4. Rank by recovery-per-rand, and face the fairness. Rank your fixes by recovery per rand spent, biggest and most-recoverable first - usually capturing the commercial loss, since the product is already delivered. Choose the one you can start this week. And where the loss is people who genuinely cannot pay, remember the answer is fairness (a lifeline price), not just a meter.
