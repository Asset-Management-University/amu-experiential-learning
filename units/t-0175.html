T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and you did this properly.

The viscosity grades were selected for the duty. The cleanliness targets were set against ISO 4406. You have the reports, and they are good reports.

And the gearboxes are still failing.

So you stop reading and follow one drum, from the gate to the machine.

It arrived three weeks ago and nobody measured it. It was stored on its side in the yard, where the drum tops collect rainwater. It was decanted with a jug that was last used for something nobody can now name. It went into a gearbox that breathes through an open vent in a dusty room. And the top-up was recorded on a whiteboard that gets wiped every Friday.

Here is what stops you. Every one of those steps is somebody doing their job exactly as they were taught. The storeman put the drum where drums go. The artisan used the jug that lives by the gearbox. Nobody was careless and nobody was lazy.

The lubricant was right. Nothing else was.

I selected the right lubricant and set the right cleanliness targets, and my machines are still dying of contamination. How do I design the programme around the lubricant - the system that actually gets it to the machine clean?

Before you read on, decide. You did the specification work properly — the right product for each duty, cleanliness targets set — and the machines are still dying of contamination. So you followed it. The drum stands outside where drums go, breathing damp air through its bung every night. It is decanted into the jug that lives beside the gearbox, the one with no label that has held three different products this year. The top-up goes in through an open filler. The gearbox breathes the pit air through a vent that is simply a hole. There are eleven lubricants in the store, several near-duplicates inherited from different suppliers and different decades. And every one of those steps is somebody doing their job exactly as they were taught. Write down how you would fix this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why nobody is breaking a rule

• “Train everyone on lubrication best practice.” The instinctive answer and it has nothing to bite on. The storeman put the drum where drums go; the artisan used the jug that lives by the gearbox. There is no rule being broken, so there is nothing for training to correct.

• “Write procedures for handling and storage.” Closer, and a procedure that assumes an outdoor drum, an unlabelled jug and an open vent is a procedure for doing the same thing carefully. The physical arrangements have to change, not just their description.

• “Filter the oil before it goes in.” One good link in a chain that is failing at five points, and it does nothing about the wrong product reaching the wrong machine.

• “Buy a better grade of oil.” The specification was already right. A premium product delivered through this chain arrives just as contaminated, at more cost.

• “Discipline is slipping — supervise it more closely.” Nobody is careless and nobody is lazy. Supervision aimed at people doing their jobs correctly is how you lose their goodwill and keep the contamination.

• The specification governs a substance; everything that then happens to it is a chain of handling nobody designed. So design the chain — and start with the move that pays for itself before a single procedure is written: cut the number of products. Eleven rationalise to about four, and the prize is not the stock saving — it is that every additional product is another route by which the wrong one reaches a machine, and consolidation closes those routes permanently. One caution: never merge two products into one that suits neither; the selection work stands, this removes only the duplicates it never needed. Then design each link — how product is received and stored, what it is moved in, how a container is identified, and what the machine breathes while it runs. And set the cleanliness standards by criticality rather than as one number for the plant: cleanliness costs money, and only the consequence of failure justifies spending it, so a fine-clearance system gets what a coarse one does not.

You stop thinking about the oil and start designing the chain. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the lubrication programme - the system that delivers the right lubricant, clean, every time.

1. Survey every point, then CONSOLIDATE the products

Start by surveying the plant area: every LUBRICATION POINT, what it takes, how much, and why. This area has 40 POINTS. Then do the step that pays for itself before anything else - CONSOLIDATE. There are 11 DIFFERENT LUBRICANTS in use here, several of which are near-duplicates inherited from different suppliers and different decades, and they rationalise to 4. That is 7 fewer products, a 64 PER CENT reduction in stock lines and shelf-life exposure - and the real prize, which is not the stock saving: EVERY ADDITIONAL LUBRICANT IS ANOTHER WAY FOR THE WRONG ONE TO REACH A MACHINE. Consolidation removes those routes permanently, before a single procedure is written. Note what it does NOT mean: never consolidate two products into one that is wrong for either duty. The selection work stands; this removes the duplicates it never needed.

2. Set cleanliness targets BY CRITICALITY, not one number for the plant

ISO 4406 expresses fluid cleanliness as a THREE-NUMBER CODE from particle counts at 4, 6 AND 14 MICRONS. The discipline is that targets are set PER APPLICATION ACCORDING TO CRITICALITY, never as one number for the whole plant - because cleanliness costs money, and criticality is what justifies spending it. Here: CRITICAL HYDRAULICS 16/14/11, where tight clearances and a servo valve make particles fatal; GEARBOXES 18/16/13; GENERAL PURPOSE 20/18/15. And understand the scale before you use it, because it is not linear: EACH RANGE NUMBER IS A DOUBLING of the permitted particle count, so the difference between 18 and 21 is not three of something, it is EIGHT TIMES the particles. A target chosen carelessly one number to the loose side is a decision to accept twice the contamination.

3. Control the whole chain - starting at the gate

Now the chain from supplier to component, where programmes actually fail. THE DELIVERY: NEW OIL IS NOT CLEAN OIL. Measure incoming cleanliness on arrival - this delivery reads 21/19/16, which against the 18/16/13 gearbox target is THREE CODE STEPS on every count, meaning the oil as delivered carries EIGHT TIMES the permitted particle count. ALL LUBRICANTS MUST BE FILTERED TO THE AGREED LEVEL BEFORE USE. STORAGE: indoors, sealed, upright, FIFO for shelf life. IDENTIFICATION: colour codes, symbols and viscosity labels on every container AND every fill point, so matching the right lubricant to the right machine requires no memory. TRANSFER: dedicated, labelled, sealed equipment per product - a shared jug undoes everything upstream of it. And BREATHERS: a reservoir with an open vent inhales the room all day, so fit desiccant breathers and stop the machine drinking its own environment.

4. The schedule - what, how much, how often, by whom

The deliverable. A line per point across all 40, each giving: the LUBRICATION POINT and its machine; the LUBRICANT (from the consolidated four); the QUANTITY, because both over- and under-lubrication cause failures - excess grease generates heat and churning losses while too little allows metal-to-metal contact; the METHOD; the RE-LUBRICATION INTERVAL; and the OWNER by name. Derive the intervals from DUTY, SPEED, TEMPERATURE AND ENVIRONMENT rather than inheriting them - a bearing running hot, fast, wet or dirty needs relubrication sooner than the same bearing in a clean dry room, and an interval copied from another plant encodes that plant's conditions rather than yours. Where the schedule and the CMMS meet, put the schedule IN the CMMS: lubrication that lives on a whiteboard is lubrication that stops during the first busy fortnight.

5. Verify against the targets, and act on the trend

A TARGET NOBODY MEASURES IS A WISH. Sample on a stated frequency, report against the box-2 targets by application, and TREND the results rather than reading them one at a time - a single sample tells you today, while a trend tells you whether the system is holding. Then act on what the trend means: A RISING PARTICLE COUNT IS EVIDENCE OF INGRESS SOMEWHERE IN THE CHAIN YOU JUST BUILT, and the chain is short enough to walk - the delivery, the store, the transfer equipment, the breather, the seal. Do not simply change the oil, which treats the symptom and guarantees a return visit. And one honest note about people: THE INVESTIGATION REVEALED NOT A SINGLE PERSON AT FAULT, BUT A SYSTEM THAT FAILED EVERY ONE OF THEM. THE GOAL OF A LUBRICATION PROGRAMME IS TO BUILD A RELIABLE SYSTEM WHERE THE CORRECT, CLEAN LUBRICANT IS THE EASIEST AND MOST OBVIOUS ONE TO USE, EVERY TIME.

Box 3 is where the drum's journey gets fixed, and the number in it is the one people find hardest to believe: the new oil, straight from the supplier, arrives carrying eight times the particles the gearbox target allows. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Selecting the right lubricant is necessary and insufficient. A machine's reliability depends on the integrity of the entire contamination-control system that delivers that lubricant from the supplier to the component. Five questions:

  • What do we actually lubricate, and with how many products? Survey every point, then consolidate - every additional lubricant is another route by which the wrong one reaches a machine.
  • How clean does each application need to be? ISO 4406 targets set by criticality, not one number for the plant. And remember the scale is logarithmic: each range number is a doubling, so three steps is eight times the particles.
  • Where does contamination get in? The whole chain: verify and filter on delivery (new oil is not clean oil), sealed indoor storage with FIFO, identification by colour, symbol and viscosity label on containers and fill points, dedicated transfer equipment, and desiccant breathers so the machine stops inhaling the room.
  • Who does what, when? The schedule: point, lubricant, quantity, method, re-lubrication interval and owner - with intervals derived from duty, speed, temperature and environment rather than inherited.
  • Is it working? Sample and trend against the targets, and read a rising count as ingress somewhere in the chain rather than as a reason to change the oil again.

And the thing T1 was really about. The investigation revealed not a single person at fault, but a system that failed every one of them. The goal of a lubrication programme is to build a reliable system where the correct, clean lubricant is the easiest and most obvious one to use, every time. If your programme depends on people remembering which jug is which, it is not a programme - it is a hope with a procedure number.

One honest boundary. Two other techniques feed this one and are not repeated here: selecting the lubricant for a duty gives you the specification, and contamination-control theory gives you the target code. This unit takes both as inputs and produces the programme - the schedule, procedures and controls that get that specification to that machine at or better than that target, for the whole of its service life. It is the management system, not the chemistry.

Does it scale? A hospital does not achieve sterility by buying sterile instruments. It achieves it with a chain - packing, autoclaving, sealed storage, dated indicators, a scrub protocol, and a single-use discipline - and any one broken link contaminates the patient no matter how good the instrument was when it was bought. Your drum arrived sterile in exactly the same sense, and then travelled through your plant.

T4: Your Turn

You are designing the lubrication programme for a sludge dewatering building containing:

  • Two centrifuge drives - high speed, tight clearances, hydraulically actuated back-drive. Failure stops dewatering for the site.
  • Four screw conveyor gearboxes - slow, heavily loaded, wet and gritty environment.
  • Six pump and motor sets - grease-lubricated bearings, general duty, a standby available for each.
  • One hydraulic power pack serving the centrifuge back-drives.

The store currently holds nine different lubricants. A delivery of gear oil has just arrived and samples at 22/20/17.

  1. Consolidate. Explain how you would decide which of the nine to keep, and state the rule you would not break while consolidating.
  2. Target. Assign an ISO 4406 target to each of the four equipment groups and justify each on criticality and clearance - not one number for the building.
  3. The delivery. Against the gearbox target you set, how many code steps out is the 22/20/17 delivery, and how many times the permitted particle count is that? State what you do with the drum.
  4. The chain. Name the three points in this building where contamination is most likely to enter, and the control for each.
  5. Schedule. Write two schedule lines - one for a screw conveyor gearbox and one for a pump bearing - with every field.

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, in step 2, the hydraulic power pack should be the tightest target in the building and the pump bearings the loosest, and it is worth being able to say why in one sentence about clearances and consequence. If you gave everything the same target you have designed an expensive programme that still fails the centrifuges.

Second, step 3 is the arithmetic that changes behaviour. Work it out properly - the answer is larger than people expect, and "it is brand new oil from a reputable supplier" is not a cleanliness measurement.

Third, in step 4, resist naming three exotic failure modes. The likely answers are mundane and that is the point: the open drum in the yard, the shared jug, and the gearbox vent breathing a room full of dried sludge dust. Programmes are lost at the boring links, which is precisely why they need designing rather than assuming.

T5: The Guardrails

Lubrication programmes fail at the links nobody photographs.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Assuming new oil is clean oil. The single most common and most surprising failure. Measure it on arrival and filter it to target before it goes anywhere.

• One cleanliness target for the whole plant. Either too loose for the hydraulics or too expensive for everything else, and usually both at once.

• Forgetting the scale is logarithmic. Letting a target slip by one range number is accepting twice the particles, not a little more.

• Too many products. Every extra lubricant is another route to the wrong machine - though never consolidate two products into one that suits neither duty.

• Open storage and shared transfer containers. A shared jug quietly undoes every control upstream of it.

• No identification. If matching lubricant to machine depends on memory, it will fail on the day the usual person is away.

• No breathers. An open vent means the machine inhales the room for its whole life, and no filtration at delivery survives that.

• Intervals inherited rather than derived. An interval copied from another plant encodes that plant's duty, speed, temperature and dirt, not yours.

• Ignoring quantity. Both over- and under-lubrication cause failures; more grease is not more protection.

• A schedule that lives outside the maintenance system. Whiteboard lubrication stops during the first busy fortnight and nobody notices for a year.

• Targets nobody samples against. Then they are decoration, and the first evidence of ingress is a failed gearbox.

• Blaming the person at the end of the chain. The artisan used the jug that was there. Fix the chain.

Onalenna selected the right oil and set the right targets, and her gearboxes died anyway - because the programme around the lubricant did not exist. Survey every point and consolidate the products, since each extra one is a route to the wrong machine; set ISO 4406 targets by criticality rather than one number for the plant, remembering that every range number is a doubling; control the whole chain from the delivery inwards, because new oil is not clean oil; write the schedule with lubricant, quantity, method, a derived interval and a named owner, and put it where the work actually gets called; then sample, trend, and read a rising count as ingress rather than as a reason to change the oil again. And build it so the correct, clean lubricant is the easiest thing to reach for - because a programme that depends on people remembering is not a programme.
