I bought safety valves cheap from a trader I do not know, and now I cannot tell if they are real - and I realise I have no idea where ANY part in my store came from. How do I keep fakes off my line?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning at a Tank You regional filling plant - the place where empty LPG cylinders are pressure-tested and refilled. Tank You has grown a long way from two brothers and a borrowed car; there are depots, a fleet of delivery bakkies, and this plant. You run the maintenance store here - the small warehouse of spare parts that keeps the filling line, the compressors and the test rigs running.

In your hand is a delivery note for a box of pressure-relief valves - the safety part: if a cylinder is over-filled, the valve must vent before the cylinder can burst. You ordered twelve; the note says twelve. But something is off. The price was R180 a valve; your usual approved supplier charges R520. An unknown trader on a messaging app offered them cheap and quick, and last month - short on stock, a line down, the boss breathing on your neck - you said yes. Now the box is open on the bench. The valves look right: same brass colour, same shape. But the stamped part number is slightly shallow, the maker's logo is off-centre, the valve feels a touch light in your hand, and there is a faint chemical smell of fresh paint.

Your stomach drops. If these are fakes - counterfeit parts dressed up to look genuine - and one fails on a cylinder being filled, someone on your line could be hurt. And the honest truth lands harder than the box: you have no idea where these valves came from - and no idea where any part in your store came from. A stack of delivery notes and a gut feeling, not a system.

Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and which one is about this box

• “Fit one and test it — that will settle whether they’re real.” No. A safety valve that passes one test may still be the wrong material at the wrong pressure on the wrong day, and the test you are proposing is a person standing next to a cylinder being filled.

• “Send them back and get a refund.” Right, and it is about this box only. Tomorrow another box arrives from another trader and meets the same defence — your eye, your hands, and whether you happen to be having a careful morning.

• “Only buy from the manufacturer from now on.” The strongest of the five, and it is a rule with no check behind it. Every counterfeit in the world arrives claiming to be genuine, and a policy that depends on a trader’s word about their own source is the position you are already in.

• “Get them tested by a lab before we use them.” Sound for these valves and unaffordable as a system — you cannot lab-test every part you buy, so something has to decide which parts get that scrutiny and at which moment.

• “Keep them, but only use them on the low-pressure jobs.” Nobody says this out loud and it is what happens when a box that cost money sits on a shelf. A part you suspect is fake and did not quarantine is a part that will quietly be fitted by somebody who does not know what you know.

Notice which of your two problems the first four answers solve. The valves in your hand are the small one. The large one is that a stack of delivery notes and a gut feeling is the whole of your defence — and it will be again next week, at seven in the morning, under pressure, with money on the other side.

You cannot keep buying safety parts on a hunch and a low price. You need a way to see your whole supply chain end to end, and to build checks into it that stop fakes getting near your line in the first place. Here is that tool, worked on the valves in your hand.

T2: The Tool in Action

Two methods do the job. SCOR (the Supply Chain Operations Reference model, said like "score") gives you one shared map - five standard boxes every chain uses: Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return - so you can see the flow. SAE AS6174 is a published anti-counterfeit standard that tells you the checks to stop fakes. The whole technique in one line: draw the chain with SCOR, then place an AS6174 control on every step where a fake could get in. Watch it on the valves.

Step 1 - Draw the chain with SCOR

Notice the line is low + decide how many = PLAN; order + the box arrives + you sign = SOURCE; MAKE = not used (you do not assemble valves); the technician fits one on the line = DELIVER; a failed valve binned = RETURN. Six honest steps, no checks anywhere - and now a clear five-box map instead of a vague worry.

Step 2 - Find where a fake gets in

Plan - LOW (deciding how many lets no fakes in). SOURCE - HIGH: the open door - an unknown trader, no paper trail, no inspection. Deliver - MEDIUM (a fake past Source gets fitted blind). Return - useful, but only if you record which supplier the failed part came from. Most of your defence belongs at Source, with a backstop at Deliver and a feedback loop from Return.

Step 3 - Place the AS6174 controls

Source: buy only from the maker or an authorised seller (no approved source, no order); require a Certificate of Conformity and full traceability on the order (no CoC, no order); inspect on arrival - stamp depth, logo position, weight vs a genuine sample, and the CoC matched to the part numbers. Deliver: issue only parts that passed receiving (a green 'accepted' tag). Return: record the supplier of every failure.

Step 4 - Write the control plan

Run the plan on the suspect box: trusted source? No - an unknown trader on a messaging app. CoC? None. Inspection? Stamp shallow, logo off-centre, 18 g against the genuine sample's 31 g, a fresh-paint smell - three fails. The plan does not say 'use your judgement'; it already says quarantine, do not shelve, red-tag 'suspect counterfeit', log the supplier. Twelve safety valves of unknown origin never reach a cylinder being filled.

See what the map did. A vague dread - "are these real?" - became a five-box picture with one box lit up red: Source, the door where an outside part first crosses into your control. That is where almost all your defence belongs. And when you run the written plan on the suspect box, the most important thing happens: the dangerous decision is already made. You are not weighing a R340-a-valve saving against a vague safety worry at 7 a.m. under pressure - the plan, written in the calm, simply says three fails: quarantine, do not shelve. SCOR showed you where the risk lived; AS6174 told you what to do there. The next box that arrives meets a system, not a gut feeling.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the defence in four steps - map first, then control.

  • 1. Draw the chain with SCOR. List the steps as they really happen today, then drop each into a box - Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return. Name any box you do not use; do not silently skip it. A SCOR map is just your real process drawn into those five boxes, with the people and documents shown.
  • 2. Find where a fake gets in. At each box ask: could a counterfeit slip through here? The highest risk is almost always Source - the box where an outside item first crosses into your control.
  • 3. Place the AS6174 controls. The standard is three plain ideas: buy from a trusted source (the maker or an authorised seller); demand traceability (an unbroken paper trail back to the maker, plus a Certificate of Conformity - the supplier's signed "this is genuine"); and inspect on arrival (catch the off-centre logo and shallow stamp at the door).
  • 4. Write the control plan. For each risky step: what you check, who checks it, what happens if it fails - decided now, in the calm.

SCOR fits whenever the flow itself is what you need to understand and control, and a problem could be hiding at any step; it is overkill for one tiny task in isolation. The deep idea is the pairing: SCOR shows you WHERE the risk lives; AS6174 tells you WHAT control to place there. And close the loop - the Return box is a warning system only if you record which supplier each failed part came from, so a pattern (say, three fails from one source) feeds back to strike them off your approved list.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your supply chain honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The cheap safety part is the dear one. A counterfeit is cheap and looks right - that is exactly what makes it dangerous. On a safety part, an unknown source offering a price too good to be true is a warning, not a bargain.

• Most fakes enter at Source - guard the door. The box where an outside part first crosses into your control carries almost all the risk. Put your strongest checks there: trusted source, a paper trail, and inspection before it ever reaches the shelf.

• No paper trail, no trust. Traceability and a Certificate of Conformity are not bureaucracy - they are how you know a part is what it claims to be. If a supplier cannot say where a part came from, treat the part as if it came from nowhere.

• Decide the failure action in the calm. The whole point of a written control plan is that the hard call - reject it, quarantine it - is made in advance, not improvised under pressure by whoever happens to be on shift. Pre-made courage beats on-the-spot courage every time.

• Close the Return loop, or you learn nothing. A failed part is free intelligence - but only if you record its supplier. Without that, the same bad source sells you the same fake next month, and the month after.

This morning you had a stack of delivery notes and a feeling. Now you have a map of every door a part can come through, and a written guard on each one. Draw the chain to see every door a fake could use, then place a control on each one - so the next suspicious box meets a system, not a guess, because the dangerous decision was already made in the calm. That is how a low-price gamble on a safety part stops being a risk you carry alone, and becomes a line in a plan that protects the person on the filling line and the customer who carries the cylinder home.
