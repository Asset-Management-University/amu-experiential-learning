Partners in a shared future

T1: The Ache

You are told to "do CSI". The easy path is right there: a cheque, a banner, a photograph, a box ticked. And you know it is hollow. A performance of generosity that leaves people exactly where they were - the money spent, the image bought, the community unchanged - and something about it feels like a quiet indignity.

You do not want to perform generosity. You want to invest in people. But every path offered to you seems to be about how the company looks, not about whether anyone's life is actually better - and you cannot see how to do it differently.

I am told to "do CSI", and every easy option is a cheque and a photo that changes nothing - how do I invest in my community in a way that is real, and that respects the people it is meant to serve?

Before you read on, decide. You have been told to “do CSI” and given a budget. Every option that has reached your desk is the same shape: a cheque to a cause, a banner at an event, a photograph for the annual report. Meanwhile this works takes its water from this catchment and eight of your eleven artisans grew up in this town — and your workshop holds a lathe, a test bench and four people who could teach somebody to use them, which nobody outside these gates has access to. You do not want to perform generosity. You want somebody's life to be genuinely better, and every path offered is about how the company looks. Write down how you would invest here — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and where this work actually starts

• “Write the cheque — at least the money helps.” It may help, and it is disconnected from anything you are good at, sustains nothing after it is spent, and — the part worth sitting with — treats people as an audience for a good impression rather than as neighbours.

• “Pick a cause that photographs well and do it properly.” Lets the report decide what the community needs. The selection criterion is still appearance, however sincerely the thing is then delivered.

• “Ask head office what other sites do.” Imports somebody else's answer to somebody else's town. Whatever fits there was fitted to their strengths and their neighbours' needs.

• “Decide what would help most and fund that.” Well meant and it is still a diagnosis made on people's behalf, which produces a gift rather than a partnership — and gifts, however generous, do not require anybody's agreement.

• “Refuse — it is all window dressing.” Understandable given what you have been offered, and it gives up something real. The alternative to performance is not abstention.

• Two versions of this work exist and they are worlds apart. Money handed out, disconnected from what you do and from what people actually need, chosen for how it reads in a report, is a cost to be justified, hard to sustain, and quietly disrespectful. Genuine investment puts resources where your real strength meets a real need, and builds both a stronger community and a more capable organisation. The truth underneath is that a person is a person through other people: an organisation embedded in a community it draws from and does not invest in is quietly undermining its own foundations — and you take your water and most of your artisans from here. So start by listening to the need as the community itself defines it, not with your idea of what would help. Then put the investment where your genuine strength meets that stated need — your lathe, your bench and four people who can teach are worth more than their cash equivalent and are far harder for anybody else to supply. Build it to last rather than to be announced, because a project that ends when attention moves on teaches people not to rely on the next one. And measure whether anybody's life is actually better, which is harder and is the only question you were asking.

Genuine community investment treats the community as a partner in a shared future, because the wellbeing of the organisation and the wellbeing of its home are inseparable. Let us learn to do it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you do corporate social investment as a real investment in shared flourishing - hearing the community's own definition of what it needs, investing where your organisation's strength meets that need, building it as a partnership over time, and measuring whether lives actually improved rather than how it all looked.

1. Investment in shared flourishing, not charity for image

The first thing to un-learn is that corporate social investment is a reputation cost, a form of marketing, or an act of disconnected charity whose goal is to LOOK good. There are TWO VERSIONS of this work, and they are worlds apart. CHEQUEBOOK CHARITY: money handed out, disconnected from what the organisation does and from what the community actually needs, chosen for how it reads in a report - a cost to be justified, hard to sustain, and quietly disrespectful, because it treats people as an audience for a good impression. GENUINE INVESTMENT: resources put where your organisation's real strength meets the community's real need, building both a stronger community and a more secure, more capable organisation over time. The truth underneath is UBUNTU: A PERSON IS A PERSON THROUGH OTHER PEOPLE. An organisation embedded in a community it draws from but does not invest in is quietly undermining its own foundations.

2. Listen to the real, self-defined need

So you do NOT begin with your idea of what would help, or with what would look generous. YOU BEGIN BY LISTENING - properly, using real engagement - to WHAT THE COMMUNITY ITSELF SAYS IT NEEDS. This matters because the community holds the knowledge of its own life that no outsider has, and because being asked, and heard, is the first mark of a PARTNERSHIP rather than a hand-out. Beware the pull toward the photogenic: THE PROJECT THAT MAKES THE BEST PICTURE IS RARELY THE ONE THAT MEETS THE DEEPEST NEED. Hear the real need, in the community's own words - its self-defined need, not your assumption of it.

3. Invest where your strength meets their need

Then you invest WHERE YOUR ORGANISATION'S GENUINE CAPABILITY MEETS THAT NEED - because that is where your contribution is largest and most durable, and where the value flows both ways. A water utility's real strength is water and the skills around it; a strength like that, aimed at a real community need, DOES FAR MORE THAN THE SAME MONEY HANDED OUT AS CASH. This is the heart of SHARED VALUE: the investment strengthens the community AND the organisation - a skilled local pool, a lighter demand on the plant, a safer place to operate - so it is worth sustaining, which pure charity, disconnected from the business, rarely is.

4. Partner and sustain - and hold the dignity of it

You build it WITH the community, over time, as PARTNERS IN A SHARED FUTURE - not as a benefactor bestowing a one-off gift that leaves nothing behind. Here is the guard that matters most on this topic. THE COMMUNITY IS A CAPABLE PARTNER, NOT A PASSIVE RECIPIENT; the people are the holders of their own knowledge and the authors of their own future, NOT A PROBLEM TO BE SOLVED OR AN AUDIENCE FOR AN IMAGE. Describe a barrier as what it is - A COST NOT YET MET, a path not yet open - NEVER as a failing of the people who face it, and never with pity. The language of 'giving back' and 'generosity' centres the organisation as a kind benefactor; the truer words are INVESTMENT and PARTNERSHIP, and the return is a thriving community, which is also the organisation's own foundation.

5. Measure the outcome, not the optics

Finally, you judge it by WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGED IN PEOPLE'S LIVES - did young people get real work, did households genuinely gain, did the community grow stronger - and NOT by the money spent or the photographs taken. And one honesty to hold: CSI is an important part of an organisation's social role, but IT IS NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR THE BASICS - running an ethical core business, treating your own people fairly, paying what you owe. IT IS ONE EXPRESSION OF UBUNTU, NOT THE ONLY ONE, and it cannot, by itself, solve deep structural problems. Done honestly and measured truly, though, it is real - an investment in a shared future that leaves both the community and the organisation better than it found them.

T3: The Blueprint

Invest in the community as a partner in a shared future, not as a recipient of a cheque. The wellbeing of the organisation and the wellbeing of its home are inseparable - they rise or fall together. Five questions:

  • What is CSI, really? Investment in shared flourishing, not charity for image. Not a cheque for a good photo, but resources put where they build a stronger community and a more durable organisation at once.
  • Where do I start? Listen to the real, self-defined need. Ask the community what it actually needs; do not assume, and do not pick the project that photographs well.
  • Where do I invest? Where your strength meets their need. Put your organisation's genuine capability to work on a real need, connected to what you do - not cash scattered widely.
  • How do I do it? Partner and sustain. Build it with the community, over time, as equals - not a one-off cheque that leaves nothing behind.
  • How do I know it worked? Measure the outcome, not the optics. Did lives actually improve? Count that, not the rands spent or the photographs taken.

T4: Your Turn

You are the asset manager at Corathyl, and your brief is to "improve community relations" through a CSI programme. The marketing team has a ready-made idea: sponsor a local football tournament, hand out branded kits, host a celebratory event. It will produce excellent photographs for the annual report. Your job is to do something real instead.

  1. Find the real need. Using genuine engagement, how would you discover the community's real, self-defined needs rather than assume them?
  2. Design an investment, not a donation. Design a CSI investment where Corathyl's core strengths - water treatment, water science, technical skills - meet a real need you might discover.
  3. Measure the outcome. How would you measure the real outcome of your investment, beyond rands spent or photos taken?
  4. Name the indignity. Explain precisely why the football-tournament sponsorship, despite its appearance of generosity, is a quiet indignity.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, finding the need: you go and ask, properly - sit with community leaders, households, and young people, and hear in their own words what stands in their way. You listen for the real need, not the one that would photograph well, and you treat what they tell you as knowledge you did not have, because you did not.

Second, an investment where your strength meets the need: for example, if you hear that young people have no path into technical work, a water-treatment apprenticeship-and-mentoring pipeline that brings local young people into real skills - which also builds Corathyl's own future workforce; or if you hear that households struggle to use scarce water safely, a water-wise programme designed and run with local leaders - which also eases demand on the plant. The value flows both ways, which is why it can be sustained.

Third, measuring the outcome: count what changed in lives - how many young people gained real, lasting work; whether households actually saved water or fell ill less often - and track it over time. Not the rands disbursed, not the event attendance, not the photographs. The spend is an input; the changed life is the outcome.

Fourth, the indignity: the football sponsorship spends money to buy the organisation an image while leaving the community exactly where it was. It treats people as an audience for a good impression rather than as partners whose real needs matter - a performance of generosity that centres the giver and changes nothing for the given. It is disrespectful precisely because it looks kind while doing nothing real, and people see that clearly.

T5: The Guardrails

Invest in the community as a partner in a shared future, not a recipient of a cheque - because the wellbeing of the organisation and the wellbeing of its home are inseparable.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Chequebook charity for an image. Money spent to look generous, disconnected from real need, is hollow and unsustainable. Invest for a real outcome, not a good photograph.

• Assuming the need instead of asking. The community holds the knowledge of its own life. Listen to its self-defined need; do not decide from the office what would help.

• The photogenic over the useful. The best picture is rarely the deepest need. Choose the investment that changes lives, even if it never makes the annual report.

• Scattering cash instead of investing strength. Your largest, most durable contribution is where your organisation's real capability meets a real need. Put your strength to work, not just your money.

• A one-off cheque. A single gift leaves little behind. Partner and sustain - build it with the community over time, as equals.

• Treating the community as recipients, or as an audience. They are partners and authors of their own future, not a problem to solve or a stage for your image. Describe a barrier as a cost not yet met - never pity the people who face it.

• Measuring spend, not outcome. Rands and photos are inputs. Count whether lives actually improved - that is the only honest measure.

• Over-claiming. CSI is not a substitute for an ethical core business, fair treatment of your own people, or paying what you owe. It is one expression of Ubuntu, not the whole of it.
