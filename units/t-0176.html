What happened last Tuesday

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and you did everything the change literature told you to do.

There is a reliability vision on the wall. There was a launch event, with catering. The leadership team signed a charter and meant it. There are new dashboards, and they are good dashboards.

Eleven months later, nothing has changed.

Not because anyone opposed it. Nobody argued, nobody sabotaged anything, everybody attended. But when a pump failed last Tuesday, the same thing happened that has always happened: the shift got it running again by lunchtime, nobody wrote down why it had failed, and the artisan who suggested taking it out of service for a proper repair was told - reasonably, apologetically - that production needed it.

You changed everything visible about this organisation and nothing about what it actually believes.

And what it believes, without ever having said so, is that maintenance exists to restore production quickly, and that stopping a machine on purpose is a kind of failure.

I have the vision, the charter, the launch and the dashboards, and last Tuesday went exactly as it always has. How do I diagnose what my organisation actually believes - and write a plan that changes it?

Before you read on, decide. You did all of it. The vision, the charter, the launch, the dashboards on the wall. Then last Tuesday the clarifier drive started running hot. The planner had a shutdown slot booked; production asked for two more days; the supervisor agreed, because he always does and because nobody has ever thanked him for stopping a machine. The condition report went in and nobody read it. It failed on Thursday. Everybody behaved reasonably by their own lights, and the day went exactly as it always has. Write down how you would find out what this organisation actually believes, and change it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and where a belief is actually legible

• “Communicate the values again — people have not understood.” They understood. The supervisor could recite the policy and still gave production the two days, because he knows what actually gets rewarded here. Repetition addresses comprehension, and comprehension was never the problem.

• “Run a culture survey and find out what people think.” Asks people to report a belief nobody has ever articulated — and they will answer with the values on the wall, sincerely. An unspoken assumption cannot be found by asking.

• “Hold the supervisor to account for overriding the plan.” He did what this organisation has taught him to do, and disciplining him teaches everyone that the new programme is a way of getting blamed. You would lose the reporting you have left.

• “Put better measures on the dashboard.” More of the visible layer, which is precisely the layer that changed and made no difference. The dashboards were up before Tuesday.

• “These things take time — keep going.” Sometimes true, and as a diagnosis it is a way of not having one. Time spent repeating an intervention aimed at the wrong layer produces the same Tuesday next year.

• So stop looking at what the organisation says and look at what it does under pressure — Tuesday, not the policy — because that is the only place an unstated belief is legible. Two disciplines make the diagnosis honest. A summary score is a lie: assess across several dimensions and you get an average that reads reassuringly while most dimensions sit lower — and since the organisation moves at the pace of its weakest dimension, report the lowest rather than the mean, because the mean conceals exactly what needs work. And hold the scoring as a structured judgement rather than a measurement; its value is in forcing honesty, not in producing a number. Then notice why your visible changes achieved nothing: what can be seen and heard is only the surface; beneath it sit the things people say they value, and beneath those the assumptions nobody examines — and yours says maintenance exists to restore production quickly, so stopping a machine on purpose is a kind of failure. Sequence the plan by dependency, deepest constraint first, and let the posters come last, as evidence that something changed rather than first as the thing meant to cause it.

You stop looking at what the organisation says and start looking at what it does under pressure. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the culture change plan - diagnosed from evidence, sequenced by dependency.

1. Diagnose the rung with EVIDENCE, not aspiration

The CULTURE LADDER has five rungs in order: PATHOLOGICAL (little or no investment in improving behaviour), REACTIVE (improvements only after something goes wrong), CALCULATIVE (attention is paid and the rules that matter have been determined), PROACTIVE (high priority, awareness worked on, improvements constantly made) and GENERATIVE (fully integrated into operations and instilled in behaviour). Score dimensions against it from OBSERVED BEHAVIOUR - what happened last Tuesday, not what the policy says. Here: leadership commitment 3, reporting of problems 2, blame versus learning 2, planning versus firefighting 2, measurement and data 3, investment in competence 2. THE MEAN IS 2.33, which reads as nearly calculative and is the number a report would lead with. THE MEAN IS A LIE. FOUR OF THE SIX DIMENSIONS SIT AT REACTIVE, and CULTURE MOVES AT THE PACE OF ITS WEAKEST DIMENSION. Report the lowest score as the organisation's true state. And hold this honestly: THIS IS A DIAGNOSTIC MODEL, NOT A MEASUREMENT - the scores are structured judgements from evidence, and their value is in forcing an honest assessment rather than in producing a number.

2. Read the three levels to find what is actually in the way

Culture has THREE LEVELS, from most visible to least. ARTEFACTS - what you can see, hear and feel: the physical environment, the language, the rituals and ceremonies, the published values, the processes. ESPOUSED VALUES AND BELIEFS - what the organisation states: the mission, the core values, the policies, what leaders say. And BASIC UNDERLYING ASSUMPTIONS - deeply held, usually unspoken, taken for granted, forming the foundation and guiding behaviour and perception WITHOUT EVER BEING STATED. Here is the test that finds them: WHERE THE ESPOUSED VALUE AND THE OBSERVED BEHAVIOUR DISAGREE, THE ASSUMPTION IS WHAT YOU ARE LOOKING AT. The wall says reliability is our priority; last Tuesday the machine went back into service unrepaired because production needed it. The assumption is that maintenance exists to restore production quickly and that stopping a machine on purpose is a failure. And this is why culture programmes fail: THEY ADDRESS ONLY THE VISIBLE ASPECTS WITHOUT TOUCHING THE DEEPER LEVELS THAT RESIST CHANGE.

3. You cannot skip a rung - so sequence by dependency

The brief says move from REACTIVE to PROACTIVE. That is TWO RUNGS, because CALCULATIVE sits between them, and THE LADDER IS ORDINAL - the rungs are not equal in effort and NONE OF THEM CAN BE SKIPPED. Now trace the dependency, which is the whole of this box. Calculative requires MEASUREMENT of the things that matter. Measurement requires honest REPORTING of problems. And AN ORGANISATION THAT BLAMES CANNOT PRODUCE REPORTING - people who expect to be punished report less, and report later, and report selectively. So blame is not one of six equal problems: IT IS THE BLOCKING DIMENSION, and every attractive proactive initiative built on top of it will stall regardless of effort or funding, because it rests on data the culture is preventing. SEQUENCE BY WHAT UNBLOCKS WHAT, not by what is most appealing to launch. The lowest-scoring dimension is almost always the one preventing progress in the others.

4. Change the experiences, not the posters

Assumptions are not formed by communication. THEY ARE FORMED BY WHAT PEOPLE REPEATEDLY EXPERIENCE AS CONSEQUENCES, which means the only interventions that move them are changes to consequence. What gets REWARDED - is the shift that restored production praised more than the one that stopped a machine early and prevented a failure? What gets ASKED in the morning meeting - availability only, or also what we learned? What happens WHEN SOMEBODY STOPS A MACHINE on integrity grounds - are they supported, publicly, the first time it is expensive? And above all, WHAT A LEADER DOES WHEN PRODUCTION AND INTEGRITY CONFLICT, because that single decision teaches the organisation more than any campaign, and everyone will hear about it by lunchtime. Then change the ARTEFACTS LAST - the posters, the vision statement, the new dashboard - as EVIDENCE of a change that has already happened rather than as its cause. Artefacts first is how eleven months pass with nothing different.

5. Measure the culture, not the activity - and be honest about the horizon

Most culture programmes report ACTIVITY - training hours delivered, briefings held, posters displayed - which measures the programme rather than the culture, and can rise to 100 per cent while nothing changes. Measure the ASSUMPTION SHIFTING instead, with leading indicators: PROBLEM REPORTS RAISED per month (does reporting rise as blame falls?); JOBS DEFERRED WITH A STATED REASON rather than silently dropped; NEAR-MISSES VOLUNTEERED, especially by people who could have stayed quiet; the proportion of work that is PLANNED rather than reactive; and repeat scoring of the ladder dimensions on the same evidence basis. Then be honest about time. RUNGS TAKE YEARS. A plan promising a transformed culture within six months is itself an artefact, and everybody in the organisation will recognise it as one - which damages the next attempt as much as this one. AND THE PRINCIPLE UNDER ALL OF IT: THE ASSUMPTIONS AND BEHAVIOURS OF THE WORKFORCE ARE NOT THE PROBLEM; THEY ARE A RATIONAL ADAPTATION TO THE SYSTEM'S TRUE REWARDS AND CONSEQUENCES. A CULTURE CHANGE PLAN THAT TREATS PEOPLE AS DEFECTS TO BE FIXED WILL FAIL. THE PLAN MUST TARGET THE SYSTEM THAT CREATES THE EXPERIENCES, NOT THE PEOPLE WHO RATIONALLY RESPOND TO THEM.

Box 1 contains the number that would have hidden the problem, and box 3 explains why the plan has to start somewhere unglamorous. Notice that the posters come last, and only as evidence. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Culture is not what an organisation says or displays. It is what its people assume without being told - and those assumptions run the plant whatever is on the wall. Five questions:

  • Which rung are we actually on? Score the dimensions against the five-rung ladder - pathological, reactive, calculative, proactive, generative - from observed behaviour. Read the weakest dimension, not the average.
  • What is in the way? The three levels: artefacts (what you can see), espoused values (what is stated), and basic underlying assumptions (unspoken, taken for granted, guiding behaviour without ever being said). Where the espoused value and the observed behaviour disagree, the assumption is what you are looking at.
  • What has to come first? You cannot skip a rung, and the lowest-scoring dimension is usually the one blocking the others. Sequence by dependency, not by appeal.
  • What actually moves an assumption? Repeated experience of consequences - what is rewarded, what is asked, and what a leader does when production and integrity conflict. Artefacts change last, as evidence rather than as cause.
  • How would we know? Leading cultural indicators - reports raised, deferrals stated, near-misses volunteered - rather than training hours delivered. And an honest horizon measured in years.

Four honest caveats about the scoring, because a number attached to culture is dangerous. It is a model, not a measurement - the scores are structured judgements from evidence, and their value is in forcing an honest assessment of observable behaviour. The mean is a lie - the average is a fiction that hides the real problem, so report the lowest score as the organisation's true cultural state. The blocking dimension is the key - the lowest score is almost always what prevents progress in the others. And the rungs are not equal - the ladder is ordinal, the effort differs at each step, and none can be skipped.

And the principle beneath the whole technique. The assumptions and behaviours of the workforce are not the problem; they are a rational adaptation to the system's true rewards and consequences. A culture change plan that treats people as defects to be fixed will fail. The plan must target the system that creates the experiences, not the people who rationally respond to them. The artisan who got the pump running by lunchtime did exactly what this organisation has rewarded for twenty years.

One honest boundary. You are diagnosing the culture and designing the plan. You are not securing the mandate and budget for a transformation - that is a charter, and it is its own technique - and you are not running the change programme's mechanics, which is another. A charter without this diagnosis is a wish; a change programme without it is expended effort. This is what makes the first step land on solid ground.

Does it scale? A river's course is not set by the signposts along it. You can move every sign in a day, and the water will go exactly where the ground falls. Culture is the ground. Artefacts are the signs - and an organisation that keeps replacing its signs while the ground is unchanged will keep arriving at last Tuesday.

T4: Your Turn

A regional maintenance organisation has been scored across six dimensions on the five-rung ladder:

DimensionScore
Leadership commitment4
Measurement and data4
Planning versus firefighting3
Investment in competence3
Reporting of problems3
Blame versus learning1

The incident. A technician isolated the wrong valve during a routine job. Nothing was damaged and nobody was hurt. He reported it himself, immediately. He was issued a written warning, and the incident was circulated in a bulletin as a reminder to take care. Since then, near-miss reports across the region have fallen by roughly two thirds, and the leadership team regard this as evidence that the warning worked.

  1. Diagnose. Compute the mean, then state what you would actually report as this organisation's cultural state, and why.
  2. Name the assumption. From the incident, state the basic underlying assumption in one sentence - not the espoused value.
  3. Explain the fall. Say what the two-thirds drop in near-miss reports actually shows, and what the leadership team has misread.
  4. Sequence. Give your first three interventions in order, and justify the order by dependency rather than by appeal.
  5. Indicate. Name one leading indicator that would show the assumption shifting, and say what a good trend would look like.

The test of a real answer: three things, and the third is the one that matters.

First, the mean here is 3.0, which reads as solidly calculative and would let a report describe this as a maturing organisation. It has a dimension at 1. Do not average it away.

Second, in step 3, the fall in reports is not an improvement - it is the system working exactly as designed. The organisation ran an experiment on itself: it took a person who did the difficult right thing and punished him publicly, and the workforce drew the correct conclusion. The reports did not fall because the errors stopped.

Be precise about what is being argued here, because the opposite over-simplification is also dangerous. This is not a claim that errors never carry consequences. A just culture distinguishes a good-faith mistake from a reckless act or a deliberate violation, and it treats them differently - the second still warrants a firm response, and a workforce is entitled to that. What destroys a reporting culture is punishing the first kind, and self-reporting is strong evidence that this was the first kind. The organisation's failure was not that it responded; it was that it could not tell the difference.

Third, notice what this does to every other dimension. Leadership commitment is 4 and measurement is 4 - genuinely good scores - and both are now built on data the culture has stopped producing. A dashboard fed by suppressed reporting will look better every month as the situation gets worse. That is why blame is the blocking dimension and why nothing else can move until it does.

T5: The Guardrails

Culture programmes fail in ways that are visible from the outside and invisible from the inside.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Mistaking artefacts for culture. Posters, launches and dashboards are the most changeable layer and the least consequential. Changing them first is how eleven months pass.

• Taking espoused values as evidence. What an organisation states is a hypothesis. What it does under pressure is the finding.

• Reading the average. The mean is a lie that hides the blocking dimension. Report the lowest score.

• Treating the score as a measurement. It is a structured judgement to force an honest conversation, not data. Precision here is a vanity metric.

• Trying to skip a rung. Reactive to proactive runs through calculative, and calculative needs measurement, which needs reporting.

• Sequencing by appeal. The attractive initiative launched on top of a blocking dimension will stall, and its failure will be blamed on the workforce.

• Leaving blame until later. It blocks reporting, which blocks measurement, which blocks everything above it.

• Measuring activity. Training hours delivered can reach 100 per cent while nothing changes. Measure whether reports are rising.

• Promising a culture in months. The plan becomes an artefact, everyone recognises it as one, and the next attempt starts from further back.

• Depending on one leader. A culture carried by a single person's conviction reverts on the day they are promoted.

• Treating people as the defect. Their assumptions are rational responses to what the organisation has actually rewarded. Fix the system that taught them.

Onalenna had the vision, the charter, the launch and the dashboards, and last Tuesday went exactly as it always had. Diagnose the rung from observed behaviour and report the weakest dimension rather than the average; separate artefacts, espoused values and assumptions, and find the assumption in the gap between what is said and what is done; sequence by dependency, because you cannot skip a rung and blame blocks reporting which blocks measurement; change the experiences and the consequences rather than the posters, and change the artefacts last as evidence; then measure whether the assumption is shifting, over a horizon honestly stated in years. Culture is the ground, not the signposts. And the people were never the defect - they are a rational response to what this organisation has actually rewarded, which is the thing you are here to change.
