Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Mandisa. For three months you have been the proud owner of a small guesthouse, bought from an owner who was retiring. The price was a steal: the building looked clean and freshly painted, the books showed steady bookings, and you dreamed of expanding.

That was three months ago. Today the dream feels like a trap. Rain has started coming through the ceiling in two of the rooms. The builder you called in took one look, sucked air through his teeth, and told you the roof timbers are rotten - patched and painted over just to get the place sold. The repair will cost R250,000. Then there are the "small things": the electricity and water bills are nearly double what the seller's records showed, and the municipality has sent a letter about an old unpaid debt attached to the property.

You sit in the quiet office with the purchase agreement on the desk - a document that once felt like a victory and now feels like a cage. You walked through the place. You looked at the numbers.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

That feeling of being trapped comes from a few natural mistakes we all make when a "bargain" is in front of us. You trusted the seller's paperwork - the maintenance records, the booking and profit sheets - but the seller has one goal, to sell, and their paperwork is a story built to achieve it: it highlights the good, hides the bad, and is not an objective report. You thought a quick walk-round was enough - the place was tidy and freshly painted the day you visited - but a seller can stage a good day, painting over the rot and showing everything at its best for the thirty minutes you are there; you cannot see rotten timbers or a hidden debt with a glance. And you focused on the low price - it felt like you couldn't lose - but the purchase price is only the entry fee, and a price that good can be a warning, hiding a mountain of future cost that makes the "bargain" the most expensive thing you ever bought.

These attempts fail because they ask the wrong question. You were asking "is the price good?". So before you read on: what is the real question - what are you REALLY buying?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough is realising you are not just buying an asset - you are adopting its entire life story: its condition, its hidden problems, its running costs, and all its debts. The price tag is the smallest part of the deal, and your real power is the work you do BEFORE you sign, while you can still say no. Five moves:

  • See what you are really buying - the whole life, not the price: the breakthrough is realising you are not just buying an asset - you are adopting its entire life story: its true condition, its remaining useful life, its real running costs, and every liability attached to it (debts, legal claims, environmental duties, a backlog of deferred maintenance). The purchase price is the smallest part of what you are taking on. So look past the sticker and ask for the WHOLE-LIFE picture - what it actually costs to run and keep, and what hidden obligations come with it. A cheap price on an asset that bleeds money for a decade is the most expensive thing you will ever buy; the only number that matters is the total cost of ownership, not the cost of acquisition.
  • Do the diligence BEFORE the deal: this is the move that changes everything: your ONLY real power is the power to walk away, and you have it for exactly as long as you have not yet signed. Before the deal you can inspect, question, renegotiate or refuse; after the signature, every nasty surprise is yours, at full price, with no leverage left. So do the hard checks BEFORE you commit, not after - the order is the whole point. It feels slow when you are excited and the seller is pushing, but the days you spend understanding the asset before you buy are the cheapest, most powerful days in the entire deal. Never let deal-fever rush you past the one moment when you still hold all the cards.
  • Inspect the asset, not the seller's paperwork: the seller's records and figures are not an objective report - they are a sales pitch, honestly or not, built to show the asset at its best and keep the problems out of sight. And a seller can stage a good day: fresh paint over rot, the quiet setting, everything tidy for your short visit. So do not buy on the folder or the walk-round - bring in your OWN independent expert to inspect the real thing, cold, on a normal day. Trust the metal and the timber, not the testimony. This is the same discipline as bp-0100 and bp-0123: a record or a reassurance you have not verified against the physical reality is just a claim, and at the moment of a big commitment a claim is not good enough.
  • Focus on the deal-breakers - the catastrophic few: you cannot inspect everything, and you do not need to - most faults are survivable and fixable. Concentrate your limited diligence on the FEW things that could be catastrophic or deal-breaking: for a building, the roof, the structure and the legal title; for a machine, its core and its safety; for any deal, the big hidden liabilities (debts, legal, environmental). Find and size those before anything else, because a worn handle is a nuisance but a rotten structure or a hidden debt can sink you. This is the same focusing discipline as bp-0158 - aim your scarce attention where a problem would do the most harm, not spread thin across the trivial.
  • Price what you find into the deal: a problem found is not a disaster - it is information, and information before the deal is power. Each finding is a renegotiation lever ('I will buy it, but for the R250,000 the roof will cost, less') or, if the true cost exceeds the value, a clear reason to walk away with your money intact. The same fault discovered AFTER you sign is simply your bill to pay alone. So write what you find into the price or the terms, and be genuinely willing to walk - the discipline only works if 'no' is a real option. Deal-fever whispers that any deal is better than no deal; the opposite is true - the courage to walk away is what makes every other move count.

See how it would have changed Mandisa's deal - and how she runs the next one. Before signing she would have brought in her own builder (inspect the asset) to check the roof and structure (the deal-breakers), not the seller's folder. He would have found the rotten timbers, and she could have gone back: "I will buy your guesthouse - for the R250,000 the roof will cost, less" (price it in), or walked away with her money intact. The problem becomes a known cost, decided in daylight, instead of a nasty surprise she pays for alone. It is too late for this purchase - but she will never again confuse the price with the cost, and her next acquisition starts with an independent inspection and a number she is prepared to walk away from. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know how to do this - think about buying a used car.

See the same five moves on a used car

• See what you are really buying: not the sticker price, but the whole-life cost - insurance, fuel, tax and likely repairs.
• Do the diligence before you pay: you check it BEFORE the money changes hands, while you can still walk.
• Inspect the asset, not the seller's word: you do not just trust "it runs great" or the shiny paint - you take it to your OWN mechanic.
• Focus on the deal-breakers: the mechanic checks the engine, the gearbox and for signs of a major crash, not the radio.
• Price what you find: if it needs R8,000 of tyres and brakes, you offer R8,000 less - or you walk away and find another car.

You priced the problem into the deal, while you still had the power to. The same five moves, a much bigger purchase.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This is not only about buying a business. It applies to any big commitment - a new project, a major new supplier, a new software system, even a senior hire. Before you sign or say yes, pause.

  1. Name the one thing that could make this a disaster - and find an independent expert to check exactly that, the thing itself, not the brochure.
  2. Ask what the 'seller' has a strong reason NOT to tell you - the supplier, the project sponsor - and work out how to find it out for yourself.
  3. Work out the true whole-life cost of the commitment, not just its upfront price or effort.
  4. Decide your walk-away point in advance - at what finding are you genuinely prepared to say no?

Name the commitment, the one deal-breaker you would get independently checked, and the walk-away point you would set before you start. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Run a pre-deal diligence checklist on one real upcoming commitment together.)

The lesson from that leaking roof is not that you were foolish - the place was painted to deceive, and almost anyone would have been caught. It is a powerful, if expensive, education that you paid for in full; now let it make the next decision your best one. You are not buying an asset, you are adopting all its problems - so understand them while you still hold the one card that matters, the power to say no.
