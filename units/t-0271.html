A graveyard of good ideas

T1: The Ache

You have inherited the improvement register. Sixty-one items.

Eleven are complete. Four are in progress. Forty-six say pending resource, and most have said it for more than two years.

Not one has ever been closed as declined. Nobody could bring themselves to - declining an improvement feels like announcing that the organisation does not care about improving, and so instead everything stays open and nothing moves, which announces the same thing more slowly.

Scrolling down, you find one from a fitter, from three years ago, about labelling on the acid line. Two sentences. Somebody stood in front of a valve, thought this will catch somebody out one day, and went and wrote it down.

Pending resource.

And then you notice the dates, and what they say: almost nothing has been submitted in the last eighteen months.

Forty-six good ideas that nobody will do and nobody will refuse, and people have quietly stopped adding to it. How do I run this so that caring enough to notice something is not a waste of somebody's hope?

Before you read on, decide. Forty-six ideas marked pending resource, most for more than two years, and not one has ever been closed as declined. Among them, from three years ago, two sentences from a fitter about labelling on the acid line — somebody stood in front of a valve, thought this will catch somebody out one day, and went and wrote it down. And the dates say the rest: almost nothing has been submitted in eighteen months. Write down what you would do with this register — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the one that sounds cruellest and is not

• “Prioritise the list and start working through it.” The obvious move, and it leaves forty-six people waiting in a better order. It also takes each item at face value: several of these are the same problem seen from different places, and read one at a time none of them is affordable, while the problem underneath — named once — frequently is.

• “Ask for a budget so we can finally do them.” Occasionally granted, and it does not touch the mechanism. The register did not fill up because money ran out; it filled up because nothing on it was ever refused, and a fund will empty against a list that still cannot say no.

• “Leave them open — closing them would be disheartening.” The kindest-sounding sentence on this page and the one doing the damage. Every one of those forty-six has already received an answer. It is that it does not matter, and it took two years to deliver. Silence is the only genuinely disrespectful response here, and it is the one being given by default.

• “Relaunch it — new form, new push, ask people for ideas.” Asking again is the one thing this register has not earned. Submissions did not stop by announcement; they stopped one person at a time, because a suggestion is an act of hope and hope is limited. A campaign against that history reads as an insult to anybody who is still waiting.

• “Decline most of them, with a reason, this week.” It sounds like the harshest option and it is the most respectful one available. A prompt no with a reason treats somebody as an adult and tells them their idea was read; it costs nothing that was going to be spent anyway. Two cautions: some of these were never improvements at all — a fault to be repaired, a request for equipment, somebody else’s decision — and those are returned to where they belong rather than refused. And a register that only declines is a register nobody submits to; the few that remain have to actually get done, visibly.

Before any of it, read the fitter’s two sentences again and note what they cost him to write. That is the thing being spent here, and it is the thing that has run out.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the pass - reading what the register has actually done, grouping symptoms into problems, sorting out what was never an improvement, declining properly, and running the few that remain.

1. Read the register honestly - and count what has actually happened to the things on it

Before improving anything, read what the register has actually done to the ideas people put on it. Sixty-one items. Eleven complete. Four in progress. Forty-six pending resource, most for more than two years. And not one has ever been closed as declined. Read those numbers as a person, not as a manager. Every one of the forty-six is somebody who noticed something, cared enough to write it down, and has now watched it sit for two years. They have received an answer - it is just not the one anybody meant to give. The answer they received is that it does not matter, and it took two years to deliver. Now notice the second-order effect, which is the expensive one. Submissions have slowed. Nobody announced that they had stopped bothering; they just stopped, one person at a time, because a suggestion is an act of hope and hope is a limited resource. By the time a register looks like this, the organisation has usually already lost the ideas it most needed - the ones from the people closest to the work, who had the least reason to expect a reply. The count is the diagnosis. Write it at the top of whatever you produce.

2. Group the items before judging any of them - several suggestions may be one problem

Read all forty-six before deciding on any of them, because a register is a list of symptoms and symptoms cluster. Of my forty-six, fourteen turned out to describe five underlying problems. Four separate suggestions about label quality, three about finding drawings, two about the same confusing screen - each written by a different person who did not know the others had noticed. Merged into themes, five things become fundable that fourteen small requests never would. And do the merge so that every original stays visible underneath the theme, with its author's name on it. This is not administrative tidiness - it is the whole dignity of the exercise. A person whose suggestion is absorbed into a theme and disappears has been overwritten. A person who can see their line beneath a funded theme has been joined, and will tell you the next thing they notice. The merge also produces the argument you have been missing. One person mentioning bad labels is a preference. Four people independently noticing the same thing is evidence, and it is evidence you already had for two years and could not see because the register was ordered by date.

3. Sort by what each item actually is - some of them are not improvements at all

An improvement register accumulates things that are not improvements, and the most consequential ones are the items that are actually risks. Of my forty-six, five were risks. Somebody had written 'consider installing a second isolation valve on the acid line' as an improvement suggestion, and it had sat at pending resource for two years - because it was on the wrong register, where nothing has an owner and nothing is ever escalated. Had it been on the risk register it would have been rated, owned and reviewed quarterly. Read every item and ask what it actually is. A risk goes to the risk register today, and you say to the person who raised it that it was more serious than the place they put it. A defect - something broken that should simply be fixed - goes to the work management system as a job, not a proposal. A compliance obligation is not optional and does not belong in a queue of nice ideas. What remains after that sort is the actual improvement register, and it is much shorter and much more honest. Do this sort before any prioritisation, because prioritising a risk against a labelling suggestion is a category error that a scoring model will happily perform.

4. Decline the ones you will not do - clearly, with a reason, and finally

This is the part of the job the whole register exists to avoid, and it is the part that makes the register work. Nineteen of my forty-six were declined. Understand what a decline actually is. An idea that is read, understood and respectfully declined with a clear reason has been HONOURED. An idea that sits ignored on a list for two years has been INSULTED. The dignity is in the closure, not in the approval - and everybody who has ever submitted anything already knows this, which is why they stopped submitting. A good no has four parts. It restates the proposal accurately, so the person can see they were understood. It gives a real reason - not resource constraints, which means nothing, but 'this would need the plant down for two days and the saving is about four hours a year'. It is final, because an idea left ambiguously alive is the thing you are trying to stop creating. And it thanks the person specifically for what they noticed, not generically for their engagement. Then tell them yourself, before it appears in a report. Nineteen conversations is perhaps three hours of work, and it is the highest-return three hours available to you - because the alternative is not nineteen ideas kept alive. It is nineteen people who stop noticing things out loud.

5. Run the few that remain properly - with an owner, a date, and a check that it worked

After the sort you have something you can actually run. Mine: nineteen declined, five moved to the risk register, fourteen merged into five themes, and eight scheduled - so thirteen open improvement items instead of forty-six, and every one of them alive rather than pending. Give each an owner, a date and - the part almost always missing - the evidence that will show whether it worked. An improvement closed without verification is indistinguishable from an improvement that was never done, and a register full of those is how an organisation comes to believe it is improving. State the evidence in advance and in measurable terms: not 'labelling improved' but 'the time to locate a valve during the next outage, measured against the ninety minutes it took last time'. Then go back and check, and be willing to record that it did not work - which is uncomfortable and is the only thing that makes the next round's estimates honest. And publish the whole cycle: what was raised, what was declined and why, what was done, and what it actually changed. That publication is what refills the register. People submit to a process they can see working, and the fastest way to get good suggestions is to demonstrate, once, that a suggestion goes somewhere.

Box 4 is the part the register exists to avoid, and it is the part that makes it work. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Running an improvement programme is not collecting ideas. It is being a good steward of other people's hope - which mostly means giving clear answers rather than keeping things open. Five moves:

  • Read what the register has done. The count is the diagnosis. Forty-six pending for two years is an answer that was delivered, slowly.
  • Group before judging. Fourteen items, five problems - and four people independently noticing the same thing is evidence you already had.
  • Sort out what was never an improvement. Five were risks, sitting for two years on a register where nothing is escalated.
  • Give a good no. Nineteen declines. The dignity is in the closure, not the approval.
  • Run the few properly. Owner, date, and the evidence that will show it worked.

The arithmetic of one honest pass. Forty-six pending become: 19 declined, 14 merged into 5 themes, 8 scheduled, 5 reclassified as risks and moved. Open improvement items go from 46 to 13 - and every one of the thirteen is alive rather than pending. Nothing was lost. Nineteen people got an answer, five items went somewhere they can actually be managed, and five themes became fundable that fourteen small requests never would.

The sentence this unit turns on. An idea that is read, understood and respectfully declined with a clear reason has been honoured. An idea that sits ignored on a list for two years has been insulted. Everybody who has ever submitted anything already knows this, which is exactly why submissions stopped. The kindness you think you are showing by keeping something open is not experienced as kindness by the person who submitted it.

What a good no contains. It restates the proposal accurately, so the person can see they were understood. It gives a real reason - not "resource constraints", which means nothing, but "this needs the plant down for two days and saves about four hours a year". It is final. And it thanks them for what they specifically noticed. Nineteen such conversations is about three hours, and it is the highest-return three hours available to you - because the alternative is not nineteen ideas kept alive, it is nineteen people who stop noticing things out loud.

Why the grouping matters more than it looks. One person mentioning bad labels is a preference. Four people independently noticing the same thing, over two years, without knowing about each other, is evidence - and it was evidence you already had and could not see, because the register was ordered by date rather than by cause.

An honest boundary. This runs a register and makes it trustworthy. It does not create the improvements - that is other people's work, funded from somewhere. It does not fix a culture where suggesting things is unsafe; a register can be run perfectly in an organisation where nobody dares submit anything, and that is a different problem. And it cannot recover the eighteen months of suggestions that were never made.

T5: One Way Through

1. Item 1 is not an improvement. An emergency stop that cannot be reached from where a person stands is a safety defect, and it has been sitting in a queue of nice ideas for fourteen months. What you do today: raise it as a job and as a risk-register entry, not as an improvement - and do it before you finish reading the rest of the list. This is the most important thing in the exercise, and it is item 1 for a reason: it is the first thing you read, and it is easy to keep reading.

2. Items 4 and 3 look like the pair and are not. The real pair is 3 and 6 - the tool store key and the 2019 drawings. Both are the same underlying problem: the things people need in order to do the work are held somewhere that suits the organisation rather than somewhere that suits the work. A key in a supervisor's office and a drawing set nobody has reissued are the same failure wearing different clothes, and framed as one theme - work-ready information and access at the point of work - they become a fundable piece of work rather than two small requests.

3. Declining item 2. "You proposed replacing the CMMS because it is slow and widely disliked, and you are right on both counts - I have watched people wait forty seconds for a work-order screen. I am not going to take it forward this year, and I want to be straight about why rather than leave it open. A replacement is a two-year programme costing well over a million, and it needs a business case built on what we would do differently rather than on the system being unpopular. This is closed as it stands. If you want to work with me on the two or three things that would make the current one bearable - the screens people actually wait on - I would take that seriously, because you know where the waiting is and I do not."

Notice what that does. It restates accurately, it agrees where agreement is honest, it gives a real reason with a real number, it closes, and it offers the smaller thing that is actually available. It does not say "resource constraints".

4. Item 3 has been open eight months and costs ten minutes. That fact says almost nothing about the item and everything about the register: there is no route for a trivial thing to be simply done. Every item, regardless of size, is entering the same queue and waiting for the same review that never happens. A register that cannot dispose of a ten-minute job in ten minutes will not dispose of anything. The fix is a triage step at intake - do it, decline it, or take it to the review - and most items should never reach the review at all.

5. Item 5, the vibration analysis on the fans. What you need: how many fans, what their criticality is, what a failure has actually cost historically, what monitoring would cost to set up and to keep running, and whether the existing pump route has capacity. What you tell the technician now: "This is a real proposal and I am treating it as one. I need the criticality and the failure history before I can put it against anything else, and I will have that by the end of next month. I will come back to you either way, and if the answer is no you will get a reason." Then do come back, by the date you gave. A promise to a date is the second most valuable thing after a decline, and the second easiest to break.

6. The fitter, item 1, fourteen months. "I have just read your note about the emergency stop on number 3. You were right, it should not have been sitting on this list for fourteen months, and I am sorry it was. It has gone onto the job list this morning as a safety defect and onto the risk register, and I will tell you when it is done. Thank you for writing it down - and if you see another one, please tell me directly rather than putting it here, until I have this register working properly."

Apologise once, plainly, and do not explain the system that failed them. Then fix it, tell them, and be honest that the register is not yet trustworthy - which is more respectful than pretending it is.

What you can do now that you could not before. Read a register as a record of what happened to people's ideas. Group symptoms into problems while keeping every author visible. Sort out the risks and defects that were never improvements. Give a good no in four parts. And run the few that survive with an owner, a date and evidence.

The fitter who wrote two sentences about the acid line three years ago was doing the most valuable thing anybody at that site did that week. It cost them five minutes and a small amount of hope.

The register is not a backlog. It is a record of what your organisation did with the fact that somebody cared - and forty-six people are entitled to an answer, even when the answer is no.
