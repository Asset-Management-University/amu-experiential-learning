Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

A gentle note before we begin. This unit is about economic and financial abuse in a relationship - a serious and recognised form of domestic abuse. If reading it brings up difficult feelings, please be kind to yourself and pause. If you feel unsafe or controlled, you deserve support: in South Africa the Gender-Based Violence Command Centre is free, confidential and open 24 hours a day.

  • To speak to someone, call 0800 428 428.
  • If you cannot speak safely, you can ask them to call you. Dial *120*7867# (free) or SMS help to 31531.

A warning that matters: these services call you back, so your phone will ring. Be somewhere safe before you ask for a call. Dialling or texting may also leave a trace in your phone's call and message history.

They will listen without judgement, and if you are ever in immediate danger your first priority is to get to safety and contact emergency services. Your safety comes first, always.

You are Ntombi. It is the 25th of the month. Your phone buzzes with a notification from work: "Your salary of R25,000 has been paid." You feel a knot in your stomach, not a sense of relief. The money goes into an account that you cannot access.

Later, you are writing the list for the weekly grocery shop. You need soap, and your daughter needs new school socks. You add them to the list, because tonight you will have to show the list to your partner and ask for the money. You know how it will go. He will look at the list. "Soap? We just bought soap." You will explain that the bar is nearly finished. "School socks? Are you sure she needs them?" You will explain that her old ones have holes. He will sigh, as if you are being difficult, and hand you a few notes, maybe R600. "Bring me the receipt," he will say. "And all the change."

You work hard. You are good at your job. But you feel like a child asking for pocket money. You have no savings of your own; you could not buy a bus ticket to visit your sister without permission. You feel trapped, isolated, and deeply ashamed.

I earn, yet I have nothing of my own - my partner controls every cent, and I feel trapped and ashamed; is this my fault, and what can I even do?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your mind races, looking for a solution, and the natural instincts that rise up are the very ones that keep people stuck. "Maybe if I just budget the allowance better, things will be okay." But this is not a budgeting problem - you could be the best budgeter in the world and it would not change the fact that you have no access to your own money; the problem is not the arithmetic, it is the control, and more money would simply be more for your partner to control. "I should confront them, demand access, stand up for myself." This feels like the brave thing - but it can be the most dangerous: when a controlling person feels their control challenged, they often escalate, with anger, threats, or new ways to punish and trap you; your safety, and your children's, must come first, and this is not about being "braver". "This must be my fault, and I am too ashamed to tell anyone." That shame is a heavy blanket, but it is not yours to carry - it is a core part of the abuse, a tool used (sometimes without a word) to keep you isolated and silent; you are not weak, not foolish, and not alone - this happens to many strong, capable people, and it is not your fault.

So before you read on: the real question is not how to win an argument or stretch an allowance - it is how to get to safety and regain your life, step by careful step.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough is to see the situation for what it is. This is not a relationship problem about money; it is a recognised form of domestic abuse called economic abuse - a deliberate strategy to control and isolate you. Naming it breaks its power, and knowing it is not your fault lifts the shame. The way forward is not confrontation; it is safety, support, and going at your own pace. Five moves:

  • Name it - this is economic abuse, not a money problem: the first and most freeing move is to see the situation clearly and call it by its true name. When a partner takes your wages, denies you access to the money you earn, makes you ask and justify every cent, gives you an 'allowance', demands receipts and change, or runs up debt in your name, that is not strict budgeting, bad luck or normal love - it is ECONOMIC ABUSE, a recognised form of domestic abuse and a deliberate strategy to control and isolate you. This matters enormously, because abuse works by making you doubt yourself and call it something else - 'he is just careful with money', 'I am bad with money' - and naming it correctly breaks that spell. You cannot find the right help for a problem you have been taught to mislabel. Seeing 'this is abuse, and it is about control, not cash' is not an accusation you have to act on today; it is simply the truth coming into focus, and the ground everything else stands on.
  • Know it is not your fault, and you are not alone: hear this plainly: this is not your fault, and you are not weak, foolish or to blame. The shame you feel, and the sense that you must hide it and cope alone, are not signs of your failure - they are part of how the abuse works, a quiet tool used to keep you isolated and silent so the control can continue. You are a capable person; you earn, you manage, you care for others - and you deserve full autonomy over the money you earn and the life you lead. Economic abuse happens to enormous numbers of strong, intelligent, loving people of every kind, in every sort of relationship; you are in a very large and very unalone company, even though the isolation is designed to make you feel singular. Setting down the shame - even a little, even just enough to reach for help - is itself an act of courage, and it is the thing the abuse most wants you not to do.
  • Safety comes first, always: this is the rule that overrides every other: your physical and emotional safety, and that of any children, comes before any rand and before any plan. The dangerous truth about control is that challenging it can be the most dangerous moment of all - so this unit will never tell you to confront your partner, to demand access, to announce that you are leaving, or to secretly move or hide money in a way that could be discovered, because when a controlling person feels their grip threatened they often escalate, and you are the one who would bear that. Being 'braver' is not the answer and not the measure of you; staying safe is. So the pace is yours, secrecy where you need it is wise not cowardly, and if you are ever in immediate physical danger, getting yourself (and your children) to safety and reaching emergency services comes before everything else here. Everything that follows is built to keep you safe, not to push you.
  • Reach confidential specialist help - the key step: this is the most important and most powerful move, and the one thing this lesson most wants you to know exists: you do not have to work any of this out alone. There are people trained for exactly this. A domestic-abuse helpline - and there are services that specialise in economic and financial abuse - will connect you with advisers who listen without judgement, who will not tell you what to do or push you to act, and who help you understand your situation and build a personal SAFETY PLAN tailored to you, at your own pace and in complete confidence. You usually do not have to give your name. They know the dangers you cannot see and the options you do not know you have, and they can help you judge what is safe. Reaching them - a call, a message, a quiet search in a private browser or from a friend's phone or a library - is the single step that opens every door, and it is the bravest and wisest thing you can do. A specialist can also help you think about whether it is safe to involve a trusted friend or family member, and how to do so.
  • Take small, guided steps toward your own life: in time, and only with support and only where it is safe, the path leads back to autonomy over your own money and your own future - but the order matters, and guidance comes first. With a specialist's help you can begin, carefully, to understand what money, accounts and debts exist, to keep copies of key documents or a little money somewhere safe, to learn your rights, and to prepare - but please do NOT treat that as a checklist to action alone, because what is safe for one person can be dangerous for another, and only someone who knows your full situation can help you judge. Let the helpline or adviser walk each step with you, at your pace. The destination is real and it is yours: a life where the money you earn is yours, where you do not have to ask permission to live, and where you are safe. You deserve that life, help to reach it exists, and you do not have to find the way by yourself.

See the one safe first step. One afternoon, while her partner is out, Ntombi sits in her car in a quiet corner of a supermarket car park. Her hands are shaking, but she opens a private browser on her phone and searches for a domestic-abuse helpline. She finds a number. She takes a deep breath and calls; she does not have to give her name. A calm voice answers, listens, and does not tell her what to do - it simply helps her see that she is not alone and that there are options. For the first time in years, she feels a flicker of hope. Nothing about her situation has changed yet, and everything has: she is no longer facing it by herself. When someone controls every cent you earn, that is not a budgeting problem and it is not your fault - it is a form of abuse designed to control you. You deserve your own money and your own life. You are not alone. The bravest, wisest first step is not to confront them, but to reach out to confidential, specialist help who will help you find safety and a way forward at your own pace.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Think of it this way - and be gentle with yourself as you do.

See why this needs the right help, not more willpower

If you fell and broke your arm, you would not try to fix it yourself by gritting your teeth and "being stronger", and you would not blame yourself for being unable to set the bone perfectly on your own. You would go to a doctor - a specialist, someone trained to assess the damage, set the bone safely, and protect it so it could heal.

Some situations do not need more willpower from you; they need the right kind of expert help. Economic abuse is a complex and dangerous situation, and trying to solve it entirely alone can cause more harm. Reaching out to a trained professional is not a sign of weakness - it is the smartest and safest thing you can do, exactly as seeing a doctor for a broken bone is wisdom, not failure.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This is a lot to take in, so be kind to yourself - there is no pressure to do anything until you feel ready and safe. If this situation feels familiar, here are some things to consider, in your own time:

  1. Recognise the pattern. Simply naming what is happening as "economic abuse" - about control, not money - can be a powerful first step.
  2. Know it is not your fault. Let go of the shame; you have done nothing wrong, and you deserve dignity, autonomy and safety.
  3. Put your safety first. Before anything else, always ask: "Is this safe? Could this increase my risk?" If you are ever in immediate physical danger, your priority is to get to safety and contact emergency services if you can.
  4. Consider reaching out - the single most important step. Talk to someone trained to help: search in a private browser (or at a library or a friend's house) for a "domestic-abuse helpline" or "economic-abuse support" in your area. In South Africa, the Gender-Based Violence Command Centre is free, confidential and available 24/7 on 0800 428 428. These services will support you without judgement and without telling you what to do.
