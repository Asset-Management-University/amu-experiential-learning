I have three good ideas now - how do I know which one is worth giving my life to?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sinegugu again, a few months on. You took the advice and you sampled - you helped at the community kitchen, you talked to people who did things you had never thought about, you started really watching the lives around you. And it worked. Your notebook, once empty, now has three ideas in it that feel genuinely possible. The fog of "there are no opportunities" has lifted.

And now you are stuck in a new way. Because three good ideas, it turns out, can paralyse you just as completely as none.

You sit with the notebook on your knee. A phone-help service for older people - you loved that. A little second-hand-uniform exchange for the school - people clearly need it. Helping the spaza owner get his stock onto a simple system - you would be good at that. Each one looks fine from where you sit. And each one would cost you the same precious thing: months of your life, and your hope. Pick wrong and you do not just lose time - you lose heart.

The sampling was like breathing in - months of taking the world in. Now I have to breathe out, and pour myself into ONE thing. I have three good ideas. How do I know which is real - which one I can trust enough to start walking?

"Just follow your passion," the videos say. But you have three passions on this page. Passion is not the question any more. The question is: which of these can actually hold a life?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You do what everyone does: you make a pros-and-cons list for each idea. Within ten minutes it is useless - every idea has pros, every idea has cons, and "which feels best?" changes with your mood. By lunchtime the uniform exchange is the winner; by supper it is the phone-help. Feelings flip by the hour. You need a question steadier than a feeling.

So instead of asking "which idea is best?", you break "good" into the parts that actually decide whether work can last. There turn out to be four, and you can test any idea against all four. Here is the test, run on the one you keep coming back to - helping older people use their phones:

LOVE - do you actually care about this?

Yes - I loved helping my gran make sense of her phone; her relief made my whole week. I would still want to do this on a flat day.

GOOD-AT - can you do it well enough to be trusted?

I am patient and I explain without making anyone feel stupid - which is most of it. I will need to learn the banking apps properly. A small, closeable gap.

SUSTAIN - can it carry its own costs, and a little for you? (the peach tree)

Airtime, data, a bit of transport. A small fee per session, or the family pays - enough to cover the costs and a little for me, so it can keep going.

NEEDED - does someone really need this, enough to choose it?

Yes - I watched it at the clinic and on my street: older people locked out of their own phones, too proud to ask. A real, common ache I have seen with my own eyes.

Look what the four questions did. They turned a vague, mood-driven "I think I like this one" into something you can actually see: a direction that you care about, can do well, can keep alive, and that someone genuinely needs. That is not a feeling. That is a foundation.

Do not ask 'which idea is best?' - that is a feeling, and feelings flip by the hour. Ask something testable instead: what does each idea actually need from me and the world to LAST? Break 'good' into parts I can check one by one.
There are really only four parts. Do I CARE about it? Can I DO it well? Can it SUSTAIN itself and me? Does someone truly NEED it? Run each idea through all four, honestly.
Work whose attention sustains itself needs all four at once: love, skill, a way to last, and a real need. Miss one and a different, predictable kind of trouble follows. So I do not pick the 'best' idea - I find the one with the fewest missing corners, and I get to work on the gaps.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Why those four, and not five, or ten? Because each one, when it is missing, breaks a venture in its own particular, predictable way. The old Japanese idea of ikigai - a reason to get up in the morning - is often drawn as exactly these four circles overlapping. We are not using it as a mystical quest for your One True Purpose. We are using it as a plain diagnostic: a way to see, before you spend a year of your life, where a direction is strong and where it will hurt you. Watch what happens when each corner is missing.

  • The missing corner: LOVE - the work becomes a chore: Mateo bakes the best bread in his town. The town needs it (NEEDED), he is brilliant at it (GOOD-AT), it pays (SUSTAIN) - but he feels nothing for it. The smell of flour has become the smell of a cage; he wakes with dread. Everything works except him, and the success everyone sees is quietly hollowing him out. Without LOVE, attention is forced, not sustained - and forced attention always runs dry.
  • The missing corner: GOOD-AT - you cannot deliver: Meera cares deeply about helping newcomers through the paperwork that traps them (LOVE); they truly need it (NEEDED) and will pay a little (SUSTAIN). But her own admin is shaky - she fills forms in wrong, misses deadlines, gives muddled advice. Her care is real, and it is not enough: she creates more problems than she solves, loses the trust of the people she loves, and it folds. Without GOOD-AT, love cannot keep its promises.
  • The missing corner: SUSTAIN - it runs out of cash: Kwame and two friends turn neglected lots into public gardens. They love it (LOVE), they are skilled (GOOD-AT), the neighbourhood blooms (NEEDED). They pay for it out of their own pockets - until the pockets are empty. No money for tools, soil or transport; they take other jobs to survive, and the gardens go to weed. Work everyone loved, withered because it could not feed itself. A peach tree that makes no sugar for its own roots bears no fruit for anyone.
  • The missing corner: NEEDED - no one comes: Jia spends two years building a beautiful app to catalogue book collections with a clever system she adores (LOVE, GOOD-AT), with a fair price ready (SUSTAIN). She launches. Silence. A few friends download it, then nothing. She built a perfect answer to a question no one was asking. Without NEEDED, even loved, skilful, well-priced work is a monument to wasted effort.

Read those four lives and the lesson lands on its own: work whose attention sustains itself needs all four corners at once - love, skill, a way to last, and a real need. Each missing corner does not cause "failure" in general; it causes its own specific grief - the chore, the broken promise, the empty account, the silent launch. That is why "follow your passion" is only a quarter of the truth: passion is the LOVE corner alone, and love by itself becomes a chore the world never asked for and that cannot pay its way.

The move: do not hunt for the "best" idea. Take each real option and run it through the four corners - honestly. The one worth walking is not the one that feels best today; it is the one with the fewest, smallest missing corners - and a clear sight of the work it would take to fill them.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You run all three notebook ideas through the four corners, and your face falls a little. Not one of them is perfect. The phone-help service is strong on love, skill and need - but you are not yet sure it can pay its way. The uniform exchange is strong on need and lasting - but you are honestly not sure you would still care about it in a year. None of them lights up all four corners cleanly. The old fear creeps back: maybe none of my ideas is good enough.

Wait - does a weak corner mean the idea is no good?

No. This is the most important thing in the whole unit, so hear it plainly: this is a diagnostic, not a pass-or-fail exam. It does not sort your ideas into "good" and "bad". It shows you exactly where the work is. A weak corner is not a verdict - it is a signpost. Is your SUSTAIN corner shaky? That is not a reason to quit; it is the reason the next steps of this journey exist - you will learn to build the very corner you are missing (to deepen the love, to grow the skill, to design how it lasts, to test the need). The formula does not hand you a finished answer. It hands you a blueprint for the work ahead. Almost no good direction starts with all four corners full. You pick the one whose missing corners you are willing to build - and then you go and build them.

So you choose - not the flawless idea, because there isn't one, but the one whose gaps you are genuinely willing to close. The phone-help service. You care about it, you can do it, people need it - and the one shaky corner, whether it can sustain itself, becomes not a reason to stop, but the first real piece of work in front of you.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Three good ideas and no way to choose - that was the trap. The way out was to stop asking which one feels best, and start asking the four steadier questions of each. Now do it for real, with your own ideas.

Take the direction you keep returning to, and run it through the four corners. Be honest - especially where it is weak, because that is the most useful thing the test can show you.
