My supervisor says my proposal is a good report but 'not yet research' - bring the contribution. Every number in it is correct, but anyone could have written it. How do I turn a competent description into genuine new knowledge, and carry it all the way to defending it?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning at the Ironstone Ridge open-pit iron-ore mine, three hours north of the city. You are Atang. You have a desk in the reliability office, a hard hat with your name taped on the front, and a master's research place that your employer is paying for. On your screen is one document: a four-page proposal titled "A study of conveyor-belt failures at Ironstone Ridge".

Your supervisor, Professor Mokwena, has written one line at the top in red: "This is a good report. It is not yet research. We meet Thursday - bring me the contribution."

You read your own four pages again. They are tidy. They describe the belt, the 14 failures last year, the R4.2 million those failures cost in lost production. Every number is correct. And Professor Mokwena is right - anyone could have written this. It says what happened. It does not say anything new.

You feel the squeeze in your chest. You have 18 months and a R180,000 study bursary riding on this. Your manager has told the general manager that "Atang is doing research that will help us". Thursday is two days away. The temptation is loud and simple: add more data, pull another year of failure logs, draw three more graphs, make the document thicker. Surely more pages will look more like research. But you already know, behind the panic, that a thicker report is still a report. You do not need more pages.

I need a reliable way to turn a competent description into a genuine, defensible piece of new knowledge - and to carry it all the way to the day I sit across the table and defend it. I need a method for directing a research programme, not just writing one up.

Before you read on, decide. Atang has three years of mine data, forty-one published sources, and a competent description of how the conveyor belts fail. Nearly every published model predicts belt failure from running hours. At Ironstone Ridge the belts do not fail along their length; they fail at the splice joints, and worse when the ore is wet and abrasive after rain. Her supervisor has read her draft and said it is not yet research. Write down what she would have to do to turn it into research — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the one move that separates a description from a contribution

• “Add another year of data and a few more graphs.” The answer panic offers, and the one she has already reached for. A thicker document is still a description: more evidence for a claim nobody has yet made changes nothing about whether a claim exists.

• “Find a topic nobody has written about.” This sounds exactly like originality and usually is not. An untouched subject is more often one nobody needed, or one whose literature she has not found yet — and neither produces a defensible contribution.

• “Read everything properly first, then decide.” Necessary, and not sufficient. Reading without a sorting question produces a summary of the field, which is the most common thing a competent person hands in when they were asked for research.

• “Decide what she believes and then prove it.” The hunch in academic clothing. It will produce a confident document and it cannot be trusted, for the same reason a twelve-week investigation aimed at a favourite theory cannot.

• “Compare her splice failures against the published running-hours models.” Very close, and it is how the gap becomes visible. But a comparison shows a difference; it does not yet say what she will add that was not there before.

• The move is to sort what has been studied by what each one left out, until the absence has a shape — then state, in one sentence that could turn out to be false, what she will contribute: a splice-failure model using ore-moisture and rainfall as predictors, tested against three years of data, a predictor set the literature does not use. That sentence is the whole difference between a description and a contribution, and everything after it — the questions, the method, the defence — is built from it. Note how modest it is. Original is humbler than it sounds: not a new law of nature, just a result that was not there before.

There is a clear path from a rough idea to defended new knowledge. Here is how to walk it.

T2: The Tool in Action

Research is how you move from being someone who learns what is known to someone who discovers what is not. The goal is an original contribution to knowledge - and "original" is humbler than it sounds: not a new law of physics, just a result that was not there before. The method takes you from a rough idea to a contribution you can defend in front of experts. Watch it run on Atang's conveyor-belt study.

Step 1 - Find the gap, state the contribution

Atang reads for two weeks, gathers 41 published sources, and sorts them by what each studied and left out. Almost every one models belt failure by running hours - but at the Ironstone Ridge mine the belts fail at the splice joints, faster when the ore is wet and abrasive after rain, and nobody has modelled splice failure against rainfall and ore-moisture. Her one-sentence contribution: 'a splice-failure model for hard-rock conveyor belts using ore-moisture and rainfall as predictors, tested against three years of mine data - a predictor set not used in the published literature.' Professor Mokwena reads it and writes one word: 'Now it is research.'

Step 2 - Break it into answerable questions

Atang breaks her contribution into three. RQ1: how does splice-failure rate vary with ore-moisture and rainfall over three years? RQ2: does a moisture-and-rainfall model predict failure more accurately than the standard running-hours model? RQ3: by how much could a moisture-based inspection schedule have cut the 14 failures and the R4.2 million loss? Three years of data already exist in the mine's records, so she need not wait three years - all three are answerable in her 18 months.

Step 3 - Choose the method, and justify it

Atang lays out each decision against its reason: quantitative analysis of existing records (the data already exists; a survey would not measure failure timing); three years of splice logs plus rain-gauge and ore-moisture readings (the exact variables in her contribution sentence); survival analysis comparing the two models (it handles 'how long until failure', which a simple average cannot). She never writes a method choice without the reason beside it.

Step 4 - Get ethics clearance before you touch the data

Atang's failure logs name the technicians who made each splice - personal data. Before she touches them she submits an ethics application, committing to replace each name with a code (Tech-01, Tech-02) so no one is scapegoated for a failure, to store the data on the mine's secure server, and to delete the working copy when the degree is awarded. Only after the committee signs off does she pull the data.

Step 5 - Work with discipline, then defend it

Atang's two models score 61% (running hours) and 84% (moisture and rainfall) - a 23-point jump, her finding - and she states honestly that 38 failures is a modest sample on one mine, refusing to over-claim. She writes the full dissertation so a stranger could check every claim, distils the core into a 6,000-word paper, then prepares for the viva by writing the three hardest questions she would ask herself ('why should anyone believe it generalises?'). On the day an examiner asks almost exactly that, and she answers calmly: a demonstrated method, not a universal law, and here is what a second mine would test next. She passes with minor corrections.

See what the method did. It took a tidy, correct, forgettable report and turned it into one defensible new claim - a moisture-based splice model that predicts failure far better than the standard - examined and defended. And see the mission in it, because this is how a person becomes capable of making knowledge, and it is built on honesty at every turn. The first honesty is the refusal to pad. A frightened researcher reaches for thickness - more pages, more graphs - because volume feels like rigour; the method refuses that and makes her earn a real contribution instead, one sentence that says something new and can be checked. The second honesty is toward the people inside the data. Those failure logs name the technicians who made each splice, and a careless study could hang a name on a failure; so before she touches the data she is required to anonymise every person, protecting them from blame for work done under pressure - research integrity and human dignity in the same act. The third honesty is about limits. Her finding is real - eighty-four per cent against sixty-one - but it rests on thirty-eight failures at one mine, and she says so plainly, hunting her own weak points before the examiners can, because a contribution you have tested to its edges is worth more than one you have oversold. That is what a viva rewards: not a researcher who claims everything, but one who knows exactly what she has shown and what she has not. The aim is a person grown from a competent reporter into someone who can stand as the expert on a question and add, honestly, to what we all know.

T3: The Blueprint

You direct the research in five steps.

  • 1. Find the gap, state the contribution. Spot what the literature has not said; write the one new sentence.
  • 2. Break it into answerable questions. A few specific questions you can answer in your time and budget.
  • 3. Choose the method, and justify it. Each choice with its reason beside it, before the examiner asks.
  • 4. Get ethics clearance first. No clearance, no research; protect the people in your data.
  • 5. Work with discipline, then defend it. A repeatable logbook; the dissertation and paper; the viva.

The ideas underneath. The whole effort aims at an original contribution to knowledge - a result that was not there before, however small and clear. You begin by reading the published literature to find the gap (what has not been asked), then turn that gap into a few answerable research questions. Your methodology is your recipe - the data, the tests, the analysis - and every choice carries its justification, the reason you can give an examiner. Before any data is touched you need ethics clearance: the formal check that your work is safe, that you anonymise the people in your data and handle it lawfully (no clearance, no research). You execute against a disciplined logbook so another could repeat you, then write two things - the full dissertation for the examiners and a short publishable paper for the world - and finally defend at the viva, an oral examination where two or three experts test that your contribution is real. Use this when your goal is new, defensible knowledge for a degree or publication; for a standard report at work, a simpler process is enough.

T4: Your Turn

Take a topic of your own that you might research for a degree or a paper. Two disciplines separate research from a thick report. First, earn one defensible new claim, and do not pad - the test of research is not how many pages or graphs it has but whether it adds a single result that was not there before and can survive a sceptic; if you cannot write your contribution as one honest sentence, you do not yet have one, and more data will not give you one. Second, protect the people, and own your limits - clear the ethics and anonymise before you collect, because a name attached to a failure can harm a real person and an examiner can reject a study built without clearance; and state plainly what your finding does not show, because a result tested to its edges and honestly bounded is stronger than a grand claim that a single hard question can topple.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your research honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A thicker report is still a report. Volume is not rigour. If you cannot state your contribution as one new, checkable sentence, more pages will not make it research.

• Write the reason beside every method choice. An examiner will ask "why this way?". Answer before they ask, or the gap in your reasoning becomes the gap in your defence.

• No clearance, no research. Get ethics approval and anonymise the people in your data before you collect a single record. A study built without it can be thrown out.

• Protect the people in your data. A name attached to a failure can harm a real person. Replace names with codes; store securely; use the data only for this study.

• Own your limits before the examiner does. Hunt your own weak points first. A finding bounded honestly beats a grand claim one hard question can topple.

You came to the desk with a tidy report, a red line that said "not yet research", and a loud urge to make it thicker. You can leave with a direction: one gap, one defensible contribution, a method whose reasons are written down, a clearance that protects the people in your data, and a finding you can defend to its honest edges - the moisture model at 84% against 61%, real, and bounded by the 38 failures and one mine it rests on. Research is not a thicker report - it is one honest, defensible new claim. Find the gap, protect the people in your data, and own your limits before the examiner does. That is how a competent professional becomes someone who adds to what we all know: not by writing more, but by discovering one true thing and standing behind it across the table.
