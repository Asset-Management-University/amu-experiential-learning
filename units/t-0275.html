A differential seized on the N1 at two in the morning with no warning at all - how do I read what is happening inside a sealed housing, months before it strands the next driver?

T1: The Ache

The workshop at Vosloo Transport smells of diesel and hot brake dust. Twelve long-haul trucks run the Gauteng-Cape corridor; eleven of them are out on the road right now.

The twelfth came back on a low-bed. Truck 7's differential seized at ten past two in the morning, ninety kilometres north of Colesberg, with the driver, Sipho, alone on the shoulder of the N1 until first light. He is unhurt. He is also not sleeping much, and he has asked twice this week whether it could happen again.

Riaan Steenkamp, the workshop foreman, has the housing open on the bench. The crown wheel is blue and the teeth are gone. R380,000 with the downtime, and a driver who will hear that noise for a while yet.

"There was nothing," Riaan says, and he is not making an excuse - he is genuinely puzzled. "No noise. No heat you could feel. Nothing on the dash. It was fine at the Kroonstad stop and it was scrap by Colesberg." He is right. A sealed housing gives you nothing from the outside. There is no panel to open, no surface to touch, nothing to hear until the moment it is already over.

Behind him, at the service bay, the apprentice is draining a differential into a bucket. It goes into the waste drum. It has gone into the waste drum after every service, on every truck, for eleven years.

The only thing that has been inside that housing is the oil - and we throw it away. How do I read what is happening inside a sealed machine, months before it strands the next driver?

Before you read on, decide what you would do about the other eleven. They are on the road tonight and you have no idea which one is next. Write down what you would actually do, and roughly what it would cost. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from - it matters that you commit, because the rest of this unit is a conversation with it.

Now compare - the four answers a workshop reaches for, and what each one can and cannot do

• "Shorten the service interval - change the diff oil more often." Real, and worth doing, and it does not answer the question. It replaces the oil that would have told you what is happening, on a machine that may be perfectly healthy and on the one that is failing, at the same cost and on the same day.

• "Strip and inspect every diff." This would find it. It also takes each truck off the road for two days, costs more than the failure did across a fleet of twelve, and every strip-down is itself a chance to introduce dirt. It is the answer of someone with no way to tell the healthy from the failing.

• "Listen and feel at every service." Skilled, cheap, and genuinely useful on plenty of machines - but truck 7 was silent and cool at Kroonstad. On a sealed housing full of oil, the outside has almost nothing to report.

• "Wait, and carry a spare." Rarely written down, and it is what a fleet does by default whenever none of the answers above is affordable. It is also the answer Sipho is asking you about.

• And the thing sitting in plain sight at the back of the workshop: the one witness that has been inside the housing the whole time is going into the waste drum. Every particle that came off those gears is suspended in it. The evidence has been collected, on schedule, for eleven years, and then poured away unread.

T2: The Tool in Action

Take the sample the same way every time so the readings can be compared. Then ask which metal is climbing, because inside a sealed housing each metal comes from a particular part. Ask what one thing would move those particular numbers together - and let how fast the line is moving, not how big the number is, decide what you do. Watch it run on the Vosloo fleet.

Step 1 - Take the sample so it tells the truth about the machine, not about your bucket

Riaan Steenkamp, the workshop foreman, fits a sampling valve to the differential housing of every truck in the fleet - one drilling job, once. From then on the rule is written on the bay wall: warm machine, within ten minutes of coming off the road, same valve, flush 200 ml through first and throw it away, then fill the bottle. He labels it with the truck number, the odometer and the hours on that oil. It takes four minutes per truck, and it is the difference between twelve trend lines and twelve unrelated numbers.

Step 2 - Ask WHICH metal is climbing, because each one names a different part

Truck 7's last four differential samples, laid side by side: iron 30, 45, 90, 210 parts per million. Copper flat at 6, 7, 6, 7. Silicon 12, 15, 28, 48. Riaan reads it aloud. Iron climbing steeply means steel is wearing - the crown wheel and pinion. Copper flat means the bronze thrust washers are fine, so this is not a lubrication starvation problem. Silicon climbing means dirt is getting in. Nobody had to open anything to know that.

Step 3 - Ask what would make those particular numbers move together

Silicon and iron rising together, copper flat. What single thing does that? Dirt getting in, and dirt grinding steel. Riaan goes and looks at truck 7's breather and finds it caked solid - so every time the diff cooled it drew air, and dust, past a perished seal. That is the cause. The iron is only the damage. Fix the iron and you clean up after the problem; fix the breather and the problem stops.

Step 4 - Grade what you do by how fast the line is moving, not by how big the number is

Truck 7 is beyond grading - it is on a low-bed coming back from Colesberg. But the same four lines are now read across the other eleven trucks, and truck 4 shows iron 28, 35, 62 with silicon 14, 19, 31. Same shape, two stages earlier. Riaan pulls truck 4 in on Thursday for a R900 breather and seal, samples it again in two weeks instead of three months, and it is back on the road on Friday morning. Nobody will ever be able to prove what that Thursday prevented, and that is the ordinary, invisible shape of this work.

Step 5 - Close the loop: confirm the cause was really the cause

Truck 4's next sample, two weeks later: silicon back to 15, iron 64 - flat, not climbing. The dirt path is closed and the steel has stopped losing itself. That flat line is the proof, and it is the only proof this work ever gets. Riaan writes both numbers on the bay wall next to the sampling rule, because a fleet that cannot see what the method bought will stop paying for it.

See what the five steps did with material that was already being collected and thrown away. Truck 7's report had been legible for months - iron tripling while copper sat flat, and silicon climbing alongside it - which is a sentence, not a number: dirt is getting in, and it is grinding the steel. And the same shape, two stages earlier, was running down the N1 that night in truck 4. A R900 breather and seal on a Thursday. Nobody will ever be able to prove what that Thursday prevented; there is no photograph of the failure that did not happen, and Riaan will not get thanked for it. That is the ordinary shape of this work - and it is why the flat line on truck 4's next sample gets written on the bay wall, because a fleet that cannot see what the method bought will quietly stop paying for it.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the first one is the one people skip.

  • 1. Sample identically every time - same point, warm machine, straight after running, flush and discard first. A sample taken differently is a different machine as far as the trend is concerned.
  • 2. Read which metal is climbing, beside the last three or four samples. Direction, not value.
  • 3. Ask what one thing would move those particular numbers together. That names the cause; the metal is only the damage.
  • 4. Grade by rate of change, not by size - and shorten the interval on anything that is moving.
  • 5. Sample again sooner and check the line has turned. That is your only proof you found the cause.

This is oil analysis, and now that you have read a report its vocabulary is just names for what you did. The metals you tracked are wear metals, each one mapping to a component - iron for steel gears and shafts, copper for bronze bushes and thrust washers, so which one climbs tells you what is wearing. Silicon stands for dirt, and dirt arriving with rising iron is contamination-driven wear: a path has opened from outside. Even the three numbers for how dirty the oil is, like 19/17/14, have a standard behind them - the ISO 4406 cleanliness code, where each step up is roughly a doubling of particles. And the line you have been reading across four samples has a name too - trending - which is the whole method, because a single report is almost uninterpretable and four of them are a diagnosis.

It fits any sealed, oil-wetted machine you cannot see or hear into - gearboxes, differentials, hydraulic systems, compressors, turbines. It is the wrong instrument for a failure that arrives without wearing anything first, and it can only ever tell you about the parts the oil actually touches.

T4: Your Turn

Find a sealed, oil-wetted machine you depend on - a gearbox, a compressor, a hydraulic pack, a vehicle. Write the sampling rule for it on one line and put it where the work happens. Then get three or four samples in a row and read them side by side: which metal is moving, what one thing would move those together, and how fast the line is going.

Two disciplines make the difference between a folder of reports and a machine you can actually see into. First, the sampling rule is the method - not the laboratory, not the report. Samples taken from different points, at different temperatures, from settled sumps, cannot be compared with each other, and a trend built from them will invent alarms and hide real ones. Write the rule down, tape it up, and make it identical for everyone who ever holds the bottle. Second, go and find the cause before you act on the damage. Fresh oil after a high iron reading feels like action and changes nothing; the breather, the seal, the path the dirt came in by - that is the repair. If the next sample has not flattened, you fixed something real and not the thing that mattered, and knowing that early is itself worth the price of the sample.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your reading true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• One report tells you almost nothing. There are no universal safe numbers; a healthy gearbox on one duty runs at levels that would be alarming on another. Machines are compared with their own history, never with a table.

• A big steady number is calmer than a small doubling one. Rate of change is the signal. Something that has sat at 90 for two years is where that machine lives; something that went 28, 35, 62 is on its way somewhere.

• Read which metal, not how much metal. The total is the least useful number on the page. Iron and copper moving apart is a diagnosis; their sum is noise.

• Never sample from the drain stream or a settled sump. The heavy particles you most want to see are exactly the ones that have fallen out, and a clean-looking sample from a dirty machine is worse than no sample at all.

• Changing the oil resets your evidence. After an oil change the trend starts again from zero - so note the hours on the oil with every sample, or you will read a fresh fill as a machine that healed itself.

• Show what it bought, or it will be cancelled. This work is invisible when it succeeds. Write up the flat line and the failure that did not happen, or the sampling budget will go in the first hard month.

You started with a seized differential on a bench, a driver who is not sleeping, eleven trucks on the road tonight and no way to tell which one is next. What you can put in place instead is four minutes per truck at every service: a sampling valve, one rule written on the bay wall, and four numbers in a row read for direction. This is how a sealed machine that told you nothing becomes one that has been telling you for months - and how the evidence stops going into the waste drum. For Sipho, none of that is a method. It is the difference between the N1 at two in the morning and getting home. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring three consecutive reports from one machine and read them together.)
