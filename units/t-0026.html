T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday and you are on the steel walkway above the pump house at a water reclamation works. The plant pushes treated water uphill to the town reservoir, and Pump 3B is doing most of that work this week because its twin, 3A, is stripped down for a seal change.

In your hand is a printed trend sheet. The night operator, Vhutshilo, has circled a number in red pen and pushed it across the desk: the overall vibration on the 3B motor - the single "how rough is it" number the wall sensor spits out - has climbed from 2.8 mm/s three weeks ago to 7.1 mm/s this morning. The amber alarm is lit; the red trip level is 11.2 mm/s. You can feel it before you read it: a palm flat on the motor bracket picks up a steady, deep shudder, and over the normal hum a new low droning beat - waaah... waaah... waaah - about once a second, like the machine is breathing wrong.

Vhutshilo looks at you. "Do I keep it running or shut it down? If I shut 3B and 3A is still in bits, the reservoir drops and half the town has no water by lunchtime. If I run it to failure, we lose the pump and the town." Your name is on the duty roster; the call is yours. And here is the trap: the easy move is to read that one big number - 7.1 and rising - and bet yes or no on it. But a single overall number cannot tell you what is wrong, and "what is wrong" is exactly what decides whether you have three weeks or three hours. An imbalance creeping up is a very different clock from a bearing tearing itself apart.

The overall vibration is 7.1 mm/s and rising - do I shut down half the town's water supply, or risk wrecking a R380,000 motor by running it to failure? One number cannot tell me which.

Before you read on, decide. Pump 3B’s overall vibration reads 7.1 mm/s and is rising. The motor turns at 1485 rpm. Shutting down means half a town without water; running on risks a R380,000 motor. Somebody can feel a slow beat in the housing, about once a second. Write down what you would do to decide — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what one number can never tell you

• “7.1 is over the limit. Shut it down.” Defensible, and it treats every fault as the same fault. An imbalance creeping up and a bearing tearing itself apart give the same reading and are not the same clock — one gives you weeks, the other hours.

• “Trend it. Take readings daily and watch the number climb.” Useful, and slow: it tells you the number is rising without ever telling you what is rising. You will learn the answer when it fails.

• “Change the bearings — it is usually the bearings.” A guess with a spanner. If it is imbalance you will have opened a healthy machine, disturbed something that was working, and still have 7.1 mm/s.

• “Send it for a specialist survey.” Correct, and the town’s water does not wait for a booking. It also leaves you unable to read the report you paid for.

• “Feel it, listen to it — that beat means something.” It does, and a hand can tell you something is being modulated without telling you by what.

• The 7.1 is not one shake. It is many different shakes added together into a single number, and adding them is what destroyed the information. Split it back out — each rhythm at its own frequency, each with its own height — and it stops being a level and becomes a fingerprint, because every fault shakes a machine at its own steady rhythm. Then the reading is done relative to one anchor: the shaft’s own turning speed, here 1485/60 = 24.75 Hz. A tall peak at that speed means imbalance; a tall one at twice it means misalignment; a peak at a rhythm that is not a whole multiple of it can only be something rolling inside a bearing. And the facts you need to read it are written down before you take a single reading — the running speed, and the bearing’s own defect frequency from its data sheet — because a peak you look up afterwards is a peak you can talk yourself into.

You need a way to look inside that one number - to take the rough overall reading apart and see which fault is actually growing, so you can put a real clock on it instead of betting. Here is that tool, worked on Pump 3B.

T2: The Tool in Action

The overall 7.1 mm/s is many different shakes added into one. A vibration spectrum (an FFT) splits that one shake into a row of peaks - each peak sitting at one rhythm (one frequency), its height saying how strong that rhythm is. Because each fault shakes the machine at its own steady rhythm, the peaks are a fingerprint. Watch the four boxes turn one rough number into a named fault with a clock.

Box 1 - Establish the facts (before any reading)

Motor nameplate 1485 rpm -> running speed 1x = 24.75 Hz (1485 / 60). The drive-end bearing is a 6314, whose data sheet gives a ball-pass-frequency-outer-race (BPFO) of 3.62 x 1x = 89.6 Hz - a table value, not a sum you do. Write these BEFORE you take a reading.

Box 2 - Capture the signal honestly

Wipe the bearing housing to bare metal, stick the magnet flat. Span 0-500 Hz (wide enough for 1x, 2x and the 89.6 Hz bearing line), 1600 lines (~0.3 Hz each), unit velocity mm/s. Read horizontal, vertical and axial - the once-per-second beat felt by hand hints at modulation, but the spectrum will say what it is.

Box 3 - Read the fingerprint (anchor at 1x, every peak relative to it)

1x = 24.75 Hz at 2.1 (modest); 2x = 49.5 Hz at 1.3 (small); but a strong peak at 89.6 Hz at 5.4 - the BPFO line - carrying sidebands at +/-24.75 Hz (1.8, 1.6) on a 200-400 Hz haystack. Not imbalance (1x modest), not misalignment (2x small), not looseness (no harmonic row): a non-integer BPFO peak + 1x sidebands + haystack is a rolling-element bearing outer-race defect.

Box 4 - Name the fault, set the clock, make the call

The fault is a bearing, not imbalance - and the 1x sidebands say it is no longer early. A spalling outer race has a SHORT clock: days to a couple of weeks, ending in a seizure that can wreck the shaft. So: run 3B at reduced duty to hold the reservoir, fast-track its twin 3A back into service today, swap the 3B bearing within 72 hours (not three weeks), and repeat the reading in 12 hours - if the BPFO peak or its sidebands jump, bring the swap forward. The town keeps its water; a R380,000 motor is not scrapped.

See what splitting the number did. The overall 7.1 was real - but it was bearing energy, not imbalance, and that changes everything. A single number tells you how rough the machine is; only the spectrum tells you what is wrong - and what is wrong sets the clock. An imbalance at this level might have given weeks; a spalling bearing already carrying 1x sidebands gives days and ends in a seizure that wrecks the shaft. The same 7.1 could have meant "leave it" or "stop now" - and the only way to know which was to read the fingerprint, not the number. The impossible yes/no choice became a clear, timed plan.

T3: The Blueprint

You diagnose a rotating machine in four steps. The anchor is running speed, called 1x - the speed the shaft turns, in hertz (rpm divided by 60). Every other peak is read relative to 1x.

  • 1. Establish the facts. Before any reading, write 1x (rpm / 60), and the fingerprint numbers for the bearings and any gears or blades - each as a multiple of 1x, from a data-sheet table (the bearing's BPFO, the gear-mesh, the blade-pass). Without 1x you cannot read a single peak.
  • 2. Capture the signal honestly. Mount the accelerometer on clean, bare metal, flat and firm - a wobbly mount lies at high frequency. Read horizontal, vertical and axial, because direction is evidence. Set a wide span, fine resolution, velocity in mm/s.
  • 3. Read the fingerprint. Mark 1x, then match the five signatures: 1x alone (radial) = imbalance; strong 2x, often axial = misalignment; a tidy row of harmonics (1x, 2x, 3x...) = looseness; a non-whole-number peak + 1x sidebands + a high-frequency haystack = a bearing defect; a peak at teeth or blades x 1x + sidebands = gear-mesh or blade-pass.
  • 4. Name the fault, set the clock, make the call. The named fault sets the urgency, and the decision follows from the clock, not from the one number.

A little background makes the peaks readable. "Frequency" just means how many times per second, measured in hertz (Hz); rotating machines are also spoken of in CPM (cycles per minute) because we think in rpm - they are the same idea (25 Hz is 1500 CPM). The raw time waveform (the wiggle your palm felt) is honest but mixed-up; the spectrum is that same shaking sorted into peaks you can name. This technique fits rotating machinery - motors, pumps, fans, gearboxes, compressors - and is the core diagnostic of a condition-based programme. It does not suit slow or reciprocating machines without specialist methods, and it only sees mechanical rhythms: for an overheating winding or a blocked filter, lean on temperature, oil analysis and pressure instead.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your diagnosis honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Get 1x before anything else. The running-speed anchor is non-negotiable - every peak is read as a multiple of it. Guess the speed and you will name the wrong fault; a peak at "3.6x" is a bearing line only if your 1x is right.

• Direction is evidence - always read axial. Horizontal and vertical alone will let a misalignment masquerade as an imbalance. The axial reading is the cheap, fast tie-breaker, and it is the one most often skipped.

• A wobbly sensor lies at high frequency. Mount on clean, bare metal, flat and firm. The bearing and gear faults live in the high-frequency range where a poor mount invents or hides peaks - so the mount is part of the measurement, not a detail.

• Sidebands mean it is late, not early. A bearing line on its own may be months away; the same line growing 1x sidebands and a haystack is being hammered once per turn and has a short clock. Read the sidebands, and shorten your timeline when they appear.

• The spectrum sees rhythms, not everything. It tells you what is shaking and how - not an overheating winding or a starved bearing for oil. Pair it with temperature, oil analysis and pressure, and never let a clean spectrum reassure you about a fault it cannot feel.

The impossible choice Vhutshilo handed you - the town's water or a R380,000 motor - was only impossible while the evidence was a single number. Split that number into its rhythms and the machine tells you, in its own fingerprint, exactly what is wrong and how long you have. A single number tells you that it is shaking; the spectrum tells you why. Do not bet on the number - decide on the fingerprint. Do that, and the night-shift gamble becomes a timed, defensible plan that protects both the people downstream and the asset. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
