I know the engineering is perfect - so why could the fitter not just do what my procedure said?

T1: The Ache

It is 02:10. You are on the cold mezzanine floor above the No. 3 roughing mill at a hot-strip rolling plant - the mill that takes steel slabs, heats them, and rolls them into coils, three shifts a day. The night air smells of hot oil and scale. Below you the mill is silent: it tripped twenty minutes ago, and silence here costs R180,000 every hour.

In your gloved hand is a single laminated sheet - the lubrication change-out procedure you wrote last month. Khulani, the night-shift fitter, is crouched at the gearbox with the sheet on the floor beside him, and his face is not friendly. "This thing says isolate the drive," he says. "Isolate it how? There are three isolators. Which one? And do I drain the oil first or after? It doesn't say. I've been standing here guessing for fifteen minutes." Fifteen minutes. At R180,000 an hour, his guessing has already cost the plant R45,000 - and he is one wrong guess from either hurting himself on a live drive or making the stoppage longer while he phones you to ask.

You feel the pull to just do it yourself - grab the sheet, throw the right isolator, drain the oil. You know the steps cold. But you cannot be here every night shift. Khulani has to do this alone, in the dark, and get it right the first time - every time, whether you are asleep or on leave. Your procedure failed him. Not because you do not know the work - you know it cold - but because what you wrote could not be executed by the person holding it.

I know the engineering is perfect - so why could the fitter not just do what my procedure said?

Before you read on, decide. Your procedure said: “3. Isolate the drive and drain the oil, checking the level.” The person holding it is the night-shift fitter, alone at 02:10, cold, half-asleep, with the mill silent and losing about R180,000 an hour, and nobody to phone. The engineering in it is correct. He could not do it. Write down what you would change, and why — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the only test that matters

• “Add more detail — explain the gearbox and why the oil matters.” More words for a man who is cold and alone at two in the morning. Background is what he must read past to reach the thing he has to do.

• “Train the fitters better.” Perhaps, and it is also the answer that makes the procedure’s failure into his failure — and the next unfamiliar job will go the same way.

• “Add photographs and a diagram.” Genuinely helpful, and it does not fix a step that contains three separate actions and no warning. A picture of an ambiguous instruction is an ambiguous instruction.

• “Have an engineer available on call.” That is a plan for the procedure to keep failing, staffed.

• “Rewrite it more clearly.” The right intention with no test attached, so “clear” means clear to the person who already knows the answer — which is exactly how the current version was written.

• There is only one test: did the reader do the right thing because of what you wrote? Judged that way the failures are specific and fixable. That step asks for three actions at once, so it can be half-done. It names nothing — which isolator, where, what colour — so it must be interpreted by somebody with no one to ask. And the hazard arrives, if at all, after the instruction that would expose him to it, when the rule is that the danger comes before the contact. Rewritten, it stops being prose: one exact action per step, each thing named where it physically is, the warning ahead of the reach, and a way for him to confirm he has done it — pressing test-start to prove the drive will not move. Then read it cold, as somebody who does not know the job, because you cannot check your own writing for the knowledge you already have.

Because technical writing is not judged by how it reads - it is judged by whether the reader does the right thing. Here is the tool that turns "what I know" into "what they can safely do, alone, first time" - worked on this very procedure.

T2: The Tool in Action

Technical communication is just this: writing that another person has to act on, judged by one test only - did the reader do the right thing because of what you wrote? Pretty sentences do not matter; action matters. Here is that test turned into a four-part tool, worked on the failed lubrication procedure.

Box 1 - The actor and the action

Reader: the night-shift fitter, alone at 02:10, cold and half-asleep, the mill silent and bleeding R180,000 an hour. The action: isolate the gearbox drive safely and drain the oil - get it right the first time, with no one to phone.

Box 2 - The answer, first

Bad: a paragraph of gearbox history with 'isolate the drive' buried near the end. Good: the first thing on the page is the DANGER line and the first action - 'Turn isolator IS-3 (red, south wall) to OFF.' For a business case the first line would be the ask: 'Spend R200,000 to prevent a R2.4m gearbox failure.'

Box 3 - Unambiguous instructions (the four rules)

The killing version: '3. Isolate the drive and drain the oil, checking the level' - three actions, no name, no warning. The safe version: 'DANGER - the drive is live until IS-3 is locked off; contact will kill you.' then one action each: turn IS-3 (red, south wall) OFF; lock it with your own padlock; press test-start and confirm it does not move; open drain valve DV-1 (yellow handle) into the 20-litre tray; check the sight glass sits between MIN and MAX.

Box 4 - The cold-read test

Read cold at 02:10: every isolator is named, every step is one action in order, and the killing hazard is read before any contact. The fitter can do it alone, right, first time - so it passes. The old version failed the test: it left him guessing for fifteen minutes (R45,000), one wrong move from a live drive.

Read the four together and the failure becomes obvious. The old step - "Isolate the drive and drain the oil, checking the level" - failed at every box: it never named the actor's real state (a tired fitter at 02:10), it buried the action, it bundled three actions with no exact names and no warning, and it could not survive a cold read. The rewrite passes all four: the killing hazard is read before any contact, every isolator and valve is named, one action per step, in order. Same engineer, same knowledge - the only thing that changed is whether the person at the sharp end could act on it safely, alone. That is the whole of the craft.

T3: The Blueprint

You build any act-on-it document - a procedure, a report, a business case, a briefing - by walking the same four boxes, in order.

  • 1. Name the actor and the action. Picture the real reader in their real state - a fitter alone at 02:00, a manager with ten minutes, a non-engineer who needs reassuring - and name the single thing they must do or decide. Write for that person, not for yourself.
  • 2. Put the answer first. Lead with the action, the ask, or the headline - never make the reader wait for the point. Keep the background in your back pocket for whoever asks.
  • 3. Make every instruction unambiguous - the four rules. One action per step. Name the exact thing (not "the isolator" but "IS-3, red, south wall"). Steps in the order they happen. And put every DANGER / WARNING / CAUTION above the step it protects, because a reader acts the moment they read - a warning below is read too late.
  • 4. Run the cold-read test. Read it back as the reader would: cold, alone, with no one to phone. If they would still have to guess, it is not done.

The same four boxes shape the other documents an asset engineer writes - the discipline never changes, only the reader does. An RCFA report (root cause failure analysis) traces the chain from symptom to root cause and ends in corrective actions that each name who does what by when ("add an oil-level check to the weekly schedule by month-end", not "improve maintenance"). A one-page business case leads with the ask and the rand figure, then problem-in-money, return, options (including "do nothing"), and a plain recommendation - the key idea is payback (a R200,000 fix that prevents a R2.4 million failure). A mixed-audience briefing pitches to the least-technical person, headline first, depth on request. A bow-tie brief lets a non-engineer see, at a glance, that every threat has a preventive barrier and every consequence a defence. Different documents, one test: did the reader act right?

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your technical writing honest and safe.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The warning goes ABOVE the step. This one is life-and-death. A reader acts on a step the instant they read it, so a DANGER placed below it is read too late to save anyone. Always put the hazard before the action it guards.

• Never bundle actions, never use a vague noun. "Isolate the drive" with three isolators is a guess waiting to hurt someone. One action per step, and name the exact thing - the colour, the tag, the location.

• Write for the reader, not for yourself. You are not the audience - the tired fitter, the ten-minute manager, the non-engineer are. Pitch to the least-technical person who must act, and lead with what it means for them.

• "Actionable" means who, what, by when. A recommendation or corrective action with no owner and no date is a wish, not a plan. If nobody is named and no date is set, nothing will change.

• Match the document to the job. A long procedure for a one-off task you will personally supervise is wasted effort; a casual note for a task done by many people on every shift is a hazard. Use the right tool for who must act, and how often.

Your engineering can be flawless and still fail at the last metre - in the gap between what you know and what the person at the sharp end can safely do. Close that gap with four moves: name the actor and the action, put the answer first, make every instruction unambiguous, and pass the cold-read test. The test is never whether your document is understood, but whether it is acted on - correctly, safely, and without hesitation. Get that right and your knowledge reaches the dark mezzanine at 02:10, even when you are fast asleep. (Working with an AMU Facilitator? Bring a real procedure or report - reading it cold, as the reader would, is a powerful thing to do together.)
