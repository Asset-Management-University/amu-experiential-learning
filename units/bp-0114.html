I keep fixing this properly - so why does it keep breaking?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and it's Tuesday morning when Sipho rings: "Boss, the bakkie won't start again - that same clicking. Same as last time." Your stomach drops. Last time was only two weeks ago. You spent a whole afternoon on it: bought a brand-new, expensive battery, cleaned the contacts until they shone, fitted it yourself, tightened the clamps until they felt rock-solid. It started perfectly. For two weeks.

Now it's dead again - another R500 call-out, a day's deliveries lost, an angry customer. You are paying again and again for the very same problem, and it makes no sense: you are not ignoring it, you are fixing it, you are buying new parts. So why does the same failure keep coming back to steal your time and money?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first thought is to blame the part: "they sold me a faulty battery!" - and you picture a fight at the shop. Or you blame bad luck: "this bakkie is just cursed." Or you decide that next time you'll get it done as fast and cheap as possible to stop the bleeding - which, quietly, makes the workmanship worse. You try a different brand of part; a few weeks later, the same failure returns.

So stop, and ask a different question. A new part failed in the exact same way as the old one. A cheap mechanic's fix failed the same way as your own. What is the one thing that has stayed constant through every single "repair"?

The bakkie keeps dying - same clicking, same fault - two weeks after I fixed it properly with a new battery I cleaned and tightened myself. Another R500 call-out, another lost day. I AM fixing it. So why does it keep breaking?
A new part failed the same way as the old one; the cheap mechanic's fix failed like mine. What's the one thing constant across every repair? The WAY it was done. What if my repair, done by feel, is building the next failure?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The constant across every repair is the repair itself - your method. The breakthrough is to realise the cure might be the cause: done by feel, the fix is quietly building the next failure. Five moves:

  • Make the repair the suspect: when a fault comes back soon after you fixed it, your first suspect must be the repair itself - not the part, not bad luck, not a 'cursed' machine. A new part that fails the same way as the old one is telling you something plainly: the part was never the problem. Turn and look at your own workmanship.
  • Find the unmeasured step: go back through the job and ask: which steps did I do 'by feel'? Tightened the terminal until it 'felt tight'; lined it up until it 'looked right'; judged it 'about there'. You never measured it or checked it against a standard - you used your judgement. That unmeasured step is exactly where the failure hides.
  • 'By feel' is not 'to a standard': the vital insight: doing a job CAREFULLY by feel is NOT the same as doing it to a MEASURED standard. Your hand can tell you something is tight - it cannot tell you it is tight ENOUGH to survive weeks of engine vibration. The small gap between 'feels right' and 'is right by a standard' is the exact space where the repeat failure grows.
  • Define a small measurable 'done': for that one key step, set a simple, checkable test that proves the job is truly complete - not just 'looks complete'. Old way: tighten the terminal till it feels tight, done. New standard: tighten it, then try to twist the connector hard with your bare hand - if it moves at all, it is NOT done; tighten and re-test until it cannot move. A measurable 'done', no special tool needed.
  • Pay the small up-front cost: the new standard costs you an extra minute and a little care, once. That is the whole price. The reward is ending the cycle - no R500 call-out, no lost day's deliveries, no angry customer in a fortnight. A tiny up-front cost buys you out of an expensive repeat, for good.

Put the cost side by side and it's almost funny. The repeat cycle costs you an R500 call-out plus about R750 in lost deliveries - over R1,200 every fortnight, on and on. The fix that ends it - the twist-test on the terminal - costs you about sixty seconds. That's the whole trade. And here's the heart of it: doing a job carefully by feel is not the same as doing it to a measured standard, and the narrow gap between "feels right" and "is right by a standard" is precisely where the failure has been living. Your careful work should build reliability into the bakkie - never the next breakdown.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This trap - a fix that fails because a step was done by feel - lives far beyond engines. Watch it at a kitchen tap.

See the same gap at a leaking tap

Your kitchen tap drips, so you turn off the water, take it apart, fit a new washer, and screw the main fitting back on by hand until it feels nice and tight. You turn the water on - no leak. Sorted. The next morning: drip... drip... drip.

You did the job "by feel". A plumber does the exact same job - new washer, same parts - but at the end he takes a wrench and gives the fitting another half-turn, past where your hand could ever reach. It feels alarmingly tight to you; he knows that is the standard (it properly compresses the seal). The drip is gone, for good.

Your "hand-tight" was the problem - not the washer. His "wrench-tight, to a standard" was the cure. Same job, same parts; the only difference was a measured "done".

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of something you keep "fixing" that keeps failing the same way - a door you keep adjusting by eye that still sticks, a connection you keep tightening that keeps working loose, a routine you keep redoing that keeps breaking. Don't blame the door, the part, or your luck - make your own method the prime suspect:

  1. Name the recurring problem, and suspect the repair. If your own fix keeps failing the same way, the fix - not the part - is the first thing to examine.
  2. Find your "by feel" step. Which part of your fix do you do by judgement, by eye, by feel - never measured or checked against a standard? That's where the failure hides.
  3. Define a small, measurable "done". Add one simple check that proves it's truly complete, not just looks-complete (a level on the hinge; "still tight after an hour"; following the maker's instructions to the letter) - and pay that small one-time cost to end the cycle.

Effort was never your problem - you've been working hard and carefully all along. The missing piece is smaller and quieter than effort: a number, a check, a standard that tells you the difference between a job that looks finished and one that is. Add that one check to the step that matters, and the same hands that kept rebuilding the fault will start building something far more valuable in its place - reliability you can trust, and time and money that stop leaking away.
