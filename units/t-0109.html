Why the change did not stick

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. You have built something genuinely good - the clear RACI ownership, the honest competency matrices. The plan is elegant, logical, better in every way. So you do the obvious thing: you call a big meeting, explain the new system, hand out crisp laminated charts. Everyone nods. It makes complete sense.

Three months later, you walk the works - and your heart sinks.

The laminated charts are coasters under tea mugs, or tucked behind a pipe, or simply gone. The team has quietly drifted back to the old, familiar, broken way of doing things, as if the meeting never happened. Your perfect plan has utterly failed - not because it was wrong, but because the people never truly came with it. You won the argument in that room and somehow lost the people outside it. And worst of all: you have no idea which part of them you lost, so you do not know what to fix. Shout the announcement louder? You suspect that will change nothing.

I designed a better way, explained it clearly, and trained everyone - and months later they have all slipped back to the old way. A change is not done when you announce it. So how do I find out exactly why it did not stick, and fix that?

Before you read on, decide. The new way is genuinely better and you can prove it. You made the case in a meeting and the room agreed — they accept the old way causes the failures. You ran the training; everyone attended and understood it; the new method is well within their reach and they have the time and tools. Months on, everybody has drifted back. Nobody refused, nobody argued. Somebody said in passing, not unkindly, that the old way works fine for them. Write down what you would do — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the step almost everybody skips

• “Explain it again — clearly they did not really understand why.” They did. They agreed in the meeting and they still agree. This is the most natural response and it repeats the part that already worked, which is why it changes nothing.

• “Run the training again.” They know how. Nobody drifted back because they had forgotten the method.

• “Make it mandatory and check compliance.” That buys behaviour while somebody is watching, and teaches people to perform the new way for you and the old way otherwise — which is worse than the current position, because now you cannot see it.

• “They are resistant to change — that is just people.” A description dressed as a diagnosis. It explains everything and therefore identifies nothing, and it has the additional cost of quietly blaming people who did attend, did understand, and did not argue.

• “Get the manager to insist.” Authority applied to an unknown problem. If the reason it failed is not one that authority addresses, you will have spent your credit and still not know.

• Notice what you actually know: they understood why, they knew how, they were able to. Three separate conditions, each genuinely met — which means adopting a change is not one event but a short sequence of distinct human conditions, any of which can fail while the others hold. And look again at the sentence somebody said in passing. The old way works fine for them. Understanding why a change is right and personally wanting it are different questions with different answers, and a case that persuades a room answers only the first. That step is the one almost everybody skips, precisely because the meeting felt like it had done the job. So: find which condition actually broke, be honest about the ones that held — and fix the earliest break first, because repairing a later one while an earlier one is still unmet changes nothing at all.

There is a simple map of the human journey of change that shows you precisely where you lost them. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is Onalenna's diagnosis of why the change failed - five honest questions about the people, with the one place it broke circled in red.

1. Awareness - do they understand WHY it must change?

Awareness - do they understand the real, data-backed reason the old way is a problem? RATED: GOT IT. The big meeting did land the 'why' - they agree the old way causes failures.

2. Desire - do they actually WANT it? (what is in it for them?)

Desire - do they personally WANT this; what is in it for them? RATED: MISSING. <-- THE BARRIER POINT. They understood the why, but 'the old way works fine for me' - she never answered why THEY should want it.

3. Knowledge - do they know HOW to do the new way?

Knowledge - do they know HOW to do the new way? RATED: GOT IT. The training was done and the laminated grids handed out.

4. Ability - can they actually DO it (skills, tools, time)?

Ability - can they actually DO it, with the skills, tools and time? RATED: GOT IT. The new way is well within reach.

5. Reinforcement - is anything keeping the new habit alive?

Reinforcement - is anything locking in the new habit (follow-up, recognition, removing the old way)? RATED: MISSING. So even where it started, it quietly decayed back. (Fix the FIRST barrier - Desire - first; then come back for this one.)

Look at what it reveals. She did almost everything right - they understood the why, they knew how, they could do it. But she skipped the step that mattered most: she never made anyone actually want it. That is the barrier - and no amount of re-explaining the "how" will ever fix a problem of "why should I?". The diagnosis points her at the one true fix. Now let us walk through the map.

T3: The Blueprint

Onalenna did not need to shout louder - she needed a map of the journey every person takes through a change. This is that map: the ADKAR model. A person only truly adopts a change when they pass through five steps, in order - each one answering a question in their head:

  • A - Awareness: Why is this change happening? Do they understand the real reason?
  • D - Desire: What is in it for me? Do they actually want to take part?
  • K - Knowledge: How do I do it? Have they been shown the new way?
  • A - Ability: Can I really do it? Not just know it - do it, with the skills, tools and time?
  • R - Reinforcement: Why should I keep doing it? Is anything locking in the new habit?

Now the one insight that makes this a tool and not a list. Picture five dominoes, A-D-K-A-R, that must topple in order. A change breaks at the first one that will not fall - the barrier point. And here is the trap that catches everyone: if your barrier is Desire, you can push the Knowledge domino - more training, more memos - as hard as you like, and nothing happens. You must go back and fix the first weak block, with a fix that matches it. You cannot train an unwilling person into wanting something; Desire needs a Desire-shaped answer ("here is what is in it for you"), not another lesson.

And carry this with dignity: when people resist, that is not stupidity or laziness - resistance is data. It is a signal pointing straight at the block you have not yet met. "The old way works fine for me" is not an obstacle to bulldoze; it is an honest unmet need to answer.

Does it scale? Diagnosing why Corathyl's operators will not adopt a new checklist uses the exact same discipline a hospital uses to learn why its surgeons are not following a new safety protocol - find the first missing block, fix that.

T4: Your Turn

Now diagnose a change of your own that did not stick. A study habit that lasted a week. Getting flatmates onto a new chore or recycling system. Getting a project team to use a new tool. Something that made sense - and faded anyway.

Walk the five blocks for the people you were trying to change (or for yourself):

  1. Rate each block honestly - got it / weak / missing.
  2. Find your barrier point - the first block that was weak or missing. That, not the whole thing, is why it failed.
  3. Name the fix that matches that block - and notice if your instinct (usually "explain it again") was aimed at the wrong domino.

T5: The Guardrails

Leading change is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you roll anything out.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The memo fallacy. Mistaking announcing a change for making one. The meeting is the start of the work, not the end of it.

• Skipping Desire. Assuming a good idea sells itself, so you never answer "what is in it for me?". This is the most common barrier of all - and the most ignored, because engineers love the logic and forget the wanting.

• The training cure-all. Pushing more Knowledge (training, instructions) when the real barrier is Desire. You cannot train an unwilling person into willingness. Match the fix to the block.

• The fade-out. Forgetting Reinforcement, so the new habit quietly withers and the old one creeps back. A change with no follow-up has a short half-life.

• The contempt trap. Treating resistance as ignorance or malice. People resist for reasons that are real to them. Resistance is a message about an unmet block - listen to it, do not bulldoze it.

Onalenna's plan did not fail because it was a bad plan - it failed because she treated a change as a memo instead of a journey, and never noticed which step her people could not take. Now you hold the map: a change is not done when announced, only when it sticks, and it sticks when you find the one human reason it is blocked and meet exactly that - with respect for the resistance, not contempt for it. Lead change like that, and good ideas will stop drifting back. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, bring a change of your own that did not stick to your next session.
