Step 1 of 5

I have 300 vessels and a four-week shutdown, and I can open maybe twelve of them - how do I choose which twelve, by risk and with numbers, instead of by whoever shouts loudest?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a Tuesday and you are standing on a platform of the Diesel Hydrotreater at the Saltpan Refinery, one hand on the cold steel handrail. In your other hand is a printed inspection report for V-201, a tall steel vessel that has run hot and under pressure for 18 years. The new ultrasonic reading says the wall at the bottom course has thinned to 9.8 mm. When the vessel was built, that wall was 12.0 mm. It has lost 2.2 mm of steel - a bit more than 1 mm every ten years.

Your maintenance planner, Zama, is at the bottom of the stairs, clipboard against her hip. "I have a four-week shutdown window in eleven months," she says. "I need to know, today, which vessels and lines I am opening up and which I am leaving shut. Every vessel I open costs us money and risk. Every vessel I skip and get wrong could split open at 40 bar." She is not unkind. She just needs an answer she can stand behind.

You feel the pull to do the easy thing: open everything, be safe by being thorough. But the plant has over 300 vessels and kilometres of pipe - you cannot open them all in four weeks, and opening a sound vessel wastes the budget and risks damaging it on reassembly. You also feel the opposite pull: trust that the steel "looks fine" and inspect on the old fixed calendar - every vessel, the same five-year clock, whether it is rusting fast or barely ageing. Neither is right. The fixed calendar treats a quiet, dry nitrogen drum the same as a hot, sour, fast-corroding reactor; and "open everything" is not a plan, it is a panic.

And under that first worry sits a second: that 9.8 mm wall on V-201 - is it even safe to keep running right now, until the shutdown? You have a measured, known thinning. You need to say, with numbers behind you, either "yes, run it safely" or "no, act sooner." A shrug is not an answer Zama can sign.

Three hundred vessels and room to open about twelve. How do I choose which twelve, in an order I can show and defend, instead of by whoever shouts loudest?

Before you read on, pick your twelve. Three hundred vessels, four weeks, one register in front of you, and every area manager convinced their equipment is the one. Write down how you would choose - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five ways this gets decided, and what each order is actually built on

• "Open the oldest ones." Simple, defensible, and age is a poor guide: a forty-year-old nitrogen drum in an open field may be barely touched, while a six-year-old reactor on sour service is being eaten. Age is a proxy for damage, and proxies fail exactly where the duty is unusual.

• "Open the ones due by the calendar." What most plants actually do, and it distributes effort evenly across equipment whose risks are not remotely even. It also cannot answer the question the register cannot answer: which of the ones not due are quietly the dangerous ones.

• "Open the ones that worry the engineers most." Their judgement is real and it is where good hypotheses come from. It is also unwritten, unrankable, and it changes with whoever is in the room - so it cannot be shown to an auditor or handed to a successor.

• "Open whatever failed or leaked recently." Recency, which feels like evidence. It reliably points at what has already announced itself, and the twelve slots are precisely for the ones that have not.

• "Open the biggest and most expensive." Value is easy to rank and is not the same as risk. A large tank of water and a small drum of hydrogen sort in the wrong order every time.

• What all five have in common: each ranks on one thing - age, date, worry, recency, value. What decides how much a vessel actually matters is two things multiplied together: how likely it is to fail, and how bad it would be if it did. Neither alone produces the right twelve, and neither is in the register.

T2: The Tool in Action

Score each item on how likely it is to fail, from its own measured damage rather than its age. Score it separately on how bad a failure would be, from what it holds and who is near it. Then multiply the two, rank the whole register, and let that order decide the twelve. Watch it run on three items from the Saltpan Refinery register - deliberately three that a calendar would sort in the wrong order.

Step 1 - Assess the Probability of Failure (PoF)

Three items from the unit: V-201 (wall 9.8/12.0 mm, ~0.12 mm/yr, min 7.5) -> remaining life (9.8 - 7.5)/0.12 = 19 years -> PoF Medium. D-110 nitrogen drum (11.7/12.0, ~0.02 mm/yr, min 4.0) -> (11.7 - 4.0)/0.02 = 385 years, effectively never -> PoF Low. Line 12-P-104 sour water (5.1/8.0, ~0.16 mm/yr, min 3.0) -> (5.1 - 3.0)/0.16 = 13 years, and thin already -> PoF Medium-High.

Step 2 - Assess the Consequence of Failure (CoF)

V-201 holds flammable hydrogen and hot diesel at 40 bar on a manned platform -> CoF High. D-110 holds inert nitrogen at low pressure in an open field, no one nearby -> CoF Low. Line 12-P-104 carries sour water (a toxic H2S risk) past a walkway -> CoF Medium-High.

Step 3 - Rank on the risk matrix, set the inspection plan

V-201 sits at Medium likelihood, High consequence - HIGH risk. 12-P-104 at Medium-High x Medium-High - HIGH risk. D-110 at Low x Low - LOW risk. So the plan: V-201 opened at this shutdown (full internal ultrasonic wall-mapping); 12-P-104 inspected in 2 years (ultrasonic on all elbows and low points); D-110 left shut for 10 years (external visual only). Every line traces to a number, so Zama can sign it.

See what multiplying the two scores did to the order. The nitrogen drum has effectively no remaining-life problem and nothing near it, so it drops out despite being one of the oldest items on the plant - while the sour-water line, which no calendar had flagged, comes up alongside the reactor because it runs past a walkway. Neither of those two moves is available to any single-factor ranking, and both are the difference between opening twelve vessels and opening the right twelve. There is a mission in that arithmetic and it is not efficiency: a fixed calendar and an open-everything panic both look responsible and both betray the people on the platform - the first by treating a sour reactor like a nitrogen drum, the second by spreading scarce inspection so thin that nothing is looked at properly. Every line of this traces to a measured number, which is what lets an engineer sign the order and a regulator audit it.

T3: The Blueprint

Three moves, and the third is where the two scores meet.

  • 1. Assess the Probability of Failure (PoF). How likely - from corrosion rate and remaining life.
  • 2. Assess the Consequence of Failure (CoF). How bad - from contents, pressure and who is near.
  • 3. Rank on the risk matrix, set the plan. Risk = PoF x CoF; high risk inspected first and most often.

The ideas underneath. Risk-Based Inspection means ranked by risk, not by a fixed calendar; risk is two things multiplied - the Probability of Failure (PoF), how likely (a fast-corroding old vessel is high, a slow clean one low), times the Consequence of Failure (CoF), how bad (flammable gas by a manned platform is high, a small inert drum is low). Plot likelihood up the side and consequence along the bottom on a risk matrix, and the high-risk items appear in the top-right corner. A clean screening estimate of PoF is the remaining life - (current wall minus the minimum required wall) divided by the corrosion rate. Its honest limits: it fits equipment where damage builds slowly - vessels, tanks, columns, exchangers, piping - and it is the wrong frame for sudden brittle failure or rotating machinery. And it stops precisely where it should: it tells you what to open, and the moment inspection finds actual damage, judging whether that item may keep running is a separate assessment with its own arithmetic - and a different one again depending on whether the damage is loss of material or a crack. Ranking is not judging, and a plan that quietly turns into a verdict is how sound method produces unsound decisions.

T4: Your Turn

Take a handful of your own pressure equipment - vessels, drums, lines - and run the three moves. Score each one's PoF from its corrosion rate and remaining life, and its CoF from what it holds and who is near; rank them on the matrix and set each one's inspection interval - then look hard at the items your current calendar would have ranked differently, because those disagreements are the entire output.

One discipline keeps the ranking honest, and it is the whole difference between a defensible order and a decorated opinion: source every number, and write the source next to it. A likelihood score resting on a corrosion rate somebody remembered, or a consequence score somebody felt, produces a matrix that looks exactly as authoritative as one built on measurements - and the twelve vessels it chooses will be the wrong twelve. Cite the design data sheet, the corrosion study, the historic thickness readings and the process safety information, so that anybody who disagrees with your order has to disagree with a specific figure rather than with your judgement. That is also what lets the ranking survive you leaving.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your inspection plan and your verdict true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Risk, not the calendar. A fixed five-year clock treats a sour reactor like a nitrogen drum. Rank by likelihood times consequence, and let the high-risk corner earn the effort.

• Both halves of risk, or you are guessing. A fast-corroding vessel that harms no one may matter less than a slow one full of flammable gas by a walkway. PoF without CoF, or the reverse, misranks the danger.

• A ranking runs on measurements, not on memory. The likelihood score comes from a measured rate of damage with its source cited. A rank built on a remembered number is a guess wearing a matrix.

• Ranking is not judging. This decides what to open. Whether a damaged item may keep running is a different question with its own method, and answering it from the risk matrix is the most tempting misuse of this work.

You came onto the platform with three hundred vessels, a four-week window, room to open about twelve, and a planner who needed an order she could sign. You can leave with one: V-201 and the thin sour-water line opened at this shutdown, the quiet nitrogen drum left shut for a decade, and every position on that list traceable to a measured rate and a named consequence. Inspect by risk, not by the calendar - and be able to show, line by line, why each item sits where it does. That is how an ageing plant stays safe without being either reckless or wasteful: not by opening everything in a panic, and not by trusting that the steel looks fine, but by sending scarce effort exactly where a failure would hurt people most.
