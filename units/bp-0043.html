We trust each other completely - so why are we suddenly fighting all the time?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and you built Tank You with your brother Ibraaheem on trust and a handshake - you the dreamer who wants to push and grow, he the careful one who watches every rand. For a long time you simply agreed, without ever once discussing how. It worked.

But now the business is growing, and the cracks are showing. You tell Sipho to take on the new housing development; an hour later Ibraaheem tells him to hold back until the cash is stronger - and Sipho, caught between two bosses, doesn't know who to listen to. A big decision - expand now, or wait - has the two of you stuck, re-arguing the same fight every week, getting nowhere. And the worst part isn't the business: it's that the constant clashing is starting to hurt the thing you treasure most - being brothers.

We trust each other completely - so why are we suddenly fighting all the time, and how do I fix it without it feeling like I don't trust my own brother?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

You try the obvious things, and each makes it worse. You try to win each argument as it comes - exhausting, and someone always loses, so resentment quietly builds. You try to avoid the conflict and let things drift - so decisions stall, Sipho stays confused, and the tension festers. Or one of you quietly starts overruling the other - which feels, to the other, like betrayal. Every approach re-fights the same ground from scratch, every single time.

So before you read on: these fights flare up over "expand or wait" - but they never resolve. What's the real thing that's missing underneath them?

Ibraaheem and I built Tank You on trust and a handshake - we just agreed, without ever discussing it. Now it's growing and we clash constantly: I tell Sipho one thing, Ibraaheem another, and a big decision is stuck. We trust each other completely - so why the fighting?
The fights aren't really about expand-or-wait. We have no agreed answer to fall back on - we never said who decides what, who owns what, who does what, how we share. What if we made those explicit, in writing?
Agree the four things in writing, plain language, before (or at the first sign of) trouble: ownership, decision rights (+ a tie-breaker for deadlock), roles, reward. Writing it down isn't distrust - it's how you PROTECT the relationship: one agreed place to point to when memories and moods differ.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The fights have no agreed answer to fall back on because four things were never made explicit - you've been agreeing by instinct, and instinct only holds until growth or stress arrives. The cure is to make the implicit explicit, on purpose, in writing and plain language. Five moves:

  • Name the four hidden gaps: your fights feel personal, but underneath they're structural: you never explicitly agreed FOUR things - who OWNS the venture, who gets to DECIDE what, who's RESPONSIBLE for what, and how the REWARD (and the work) is shared. While you agreed by instinct, the gaps were invisible. The moment instinct breaks (growth, stress, change), there's no agreed answer to fall back on - so every disagreement is re-fought from scratch.
  • Ownership - write down who owns what: agree, in writing, who owns what share of the thing (50/50, or whatever is fair and true). It feels obvious now - which is exactly why it's never said - but memories drift and stakes rise. Written ownership is never in doubt, and it quietly settles a hundred future arguments before they start.
  • Decision rights + a tie-breaker: agree who has the FINAL say on what - say Ibraaheem on money and spending, Muhammad on customers and growth - so day-to-day calls don't stall. And for the big shared decisions where you deadlock, agree a TIE-BREAKER in advance (a rule, a trusted third person, a cooling-off-then-revisit) so no decision can be frozen forever by a tie.
  • Roles + reward: agree who is RESPONSIBLE for what, day-to-day - so no one (like Sipho) ever gets two contradictory orders. And agree how the REWARD and the work are shared - and how that changes if effort changes - so fairness is settled by agreement, not silently assumed and quietly resented.
  • Writing it down is how you protect the bond: the resistance is real: 'we're brothers, we trust each other - why write it down?'. Here's the reframe: you write it down BECAUSE you value the relationship and want to protect it from needless fights. It gives you ONE agreed place to point to when memories and moods diverge. Do it before trouble, or at the first sign. And it can't fix a deep difference in what you each truly want - but it clears the needless conflicts and surfaces any real one early, gently, to handle with care.

Here is the heart of it, and the hardest part to feel: writing this down is not an act of distrust - it is an act of care. You are not protecting yourself from your brother; you are protecting the brotherhood, together, from the needless fights that quietly wear a bond away. A written agreement is one calm, agreed place to point to when memories drift and moods flare, so the two of you never again have to re-fight a settled question or leave Sipho guessing. Draw the map together, in good faith, before the storm - or at its very first gust. And be honest about what it can and can't do: it clears away the dozens of needless fights, and if a real, deep difference remains in what you each truly want, it brings that into the light early and gently - so you face it as brothers who chose to, not as brothers ambushed by it.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only for brothers - it's for anyone who shares control of something they care about.

See the same fix save two friends' venture

Two friends start a venture together, splitting everything equally - equal say, equal pay - on pure goodwill. A year in, the cracks open: one is putting in far more hours than the other, but the pay is still equal, and resentment simmers. Their first hire quits, exhausted from taking orders from "two bosses" who say different things. They love each other, so they keep avoiding the awkward conversation - and the business drifts.

The fix is the same four things, made explicit:

• Ownership - they write down the real split (and revisit it honestly given the effort gap).

• Decision rights + a tie-breaker - each gets the final say in their area, with an agreed way to break a deadlock.

• Roles - who's responsible for what, so no employee ever gets two contradictory orders again.

• Reward - pay tied honestly to ownership and effort, agreed openly, not silently resented.

The conversation is uncomfortable for an hour - and saves the friendship and the business for years.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of anything you share control of with someone - a business, a project, a pooled fund, even a household. Propose this conversation not as suspicion, but as an act of care for the partnership. Together, make the four things explicit and written:

  1. Ownership. Who owns what share of the thing - written down, fair, and clear.
  2. Decision rights + a tie-breaker. Who has the final say on what, and an agreed way to break a deadlock so nothing stalls forever.
  3. Roles. Who is responsible for what day-to-day, so no one in the middle ever gets two contradictory orders.
  4. Reward + work. How the rewards and the work are shared - and how that adjusts fairly if effort changes.
R

Name the one gap behind your needless fights, and propose the conversation this week - as care, not suspicion. (A savings club, a co-op, a family arrangement - the same four pillars hold; an AMU facilitator can help you draft them.)

Most partnerships that break don't break over money or strategy - they break over the things no one ever said out loud. The handshake that starts a venture is built on trust; the written agreement that sustains it is built on the very same trust, made durable. You're not protecting yourself from the other person - you're protecting the two of you, together, from the needless fights that would otherwise wear your bond away. Say the unsaid things, kindly and early, and you get to keep both the business and the brother.
