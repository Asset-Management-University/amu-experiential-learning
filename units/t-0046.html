The data is all there - millions of rows of sensor and work-order history - yet the pumps keep failing by surprise. How do I turn all that data into an early warning I can actually trust and act on?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning at Tarka Ferrochrome, a smelter in an inland mining region. You are the reliability engineer for the cooling-water plant that keeps the furnace alive - six big pumps running day and night. In your hand is a printed log. Last night, at 02:40, Pump P-204 seized. The night shift caught it just in time and swung the standby pump in.

Yusra, the maintenance superintendent, is standing at your desk with her arms folded. "That is the third unplanned pump failure this quarter," she says. Her voice is flat, tired. "Each one is a near-miss. One day we will not catch it in time."

You feel the heat from the furnace floor through the office wall. Every pump has a sensor pack - vibration, bearing temperature, motor current, flow - sampled every minute, and a CMMS (the maintenance database of every work order) going back four years. So the data is there. Millions of rows. The tempting move is to grab last night's vibration chart, eyeball the spike, and write "bearing wear - replace bearing" on the work order. That is what you have always done. But you have done that three times this quarter, and the pumps keep failing anyway. Eyeballing one chart at a time is not finding the problem early enough - and Yusra trusts you to fix this.

The data is all there - millions of rows of sensors and work orders - yet the pumps keep failing by surprise, and one day the night shift will not catch it in time. How do I turn all that data into an early warning I can actually trust and act on?

Before you read on, decide. Six cooling-water pumps, a reading a minute, four years of history — about 3.15 million rows of sensor data, plus the work-order history. Somewhere in there are 41,000 rows reading exactly 0.00 mm/s, because a probe was unplugged for 28 days, and three readings of 620 °C that never happened. And in four years there have been only nine actual bearing failures. The pumps keep failing by surprise. Write down how you would turn this into a warning the night shift can act on — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two numbers that decide everything

• “Plot it all and look for patterns.” This is what is being done now, one chart at a time, and it is why the failures are still surprises. Three million rows do not fit in anybody’s eye.

• “Feed everything into a model and see what it finds.” It will find the unplugged probe, confidently, because 41,000 identical zeros are the strongest pattern in the dataset and they mean nothing at all.

• “Clean the data first, then decide what to do with it.” Half right, and it invites cleaning without a purpose — and whether those zeros should be dropped, marked missing, or filled depends entirely on the question being asked.

• “Train on the nine failures.” Nine events across four years is thin, and it is not nothing — but it decides the shape of everything that follows, so it must be faced at the start rather than discovered at the end.

• “Get a better sensor fitted and start collecting properly.” Sound, and it discards four years you already have to begin waiting again.

• The two numbers that decide this are not in the sensor data at all: how much warning the team actually needs, and how many real failures you have to learn from. So the first move is to sharpen the question until it is answerable and testable — not “analyse the pumps” but warn the team at least a certain number of hours ahead, chosen because that is the difference between swapping a bearing on a normal day shift and doing it at 02:40. Everything downstream follows from that: it tells you what counts as a success, which readings are relevant, and what to build the label from. And look before you model, because the 41,000 zeros and the 620 °C readings are not data — one is an unplugged probe, the other never happened — and no amount of modelling recovers from feeding them in as though they were real.

You do not need a better guess. You need a reliable, repeatable way to turn all that sensor and work-order data into an early warning a human can act on - the same steps every time, so you and others can trust the result. Here is that workflow.

T2: The Tool in Action

What you need is a data-science workflow: a fixed set of steps that takes raw asset data and turns it into a trustworthy answer to a question. It is repeatable on purpose - follow the same steps every time, so you can trust the result and others can check it. Watch it run on the six cooling-water pumps.

Step 1 - Frame the question (sharp, and early enough to act)

Not 'analyse the pumps' but: 'Using the sensor and CMMS data, can I warn the team at least 48 hours before a cooling-water pump bearing fails?' - early enough to plan a swap on a normal day shift, not at 02:40.

Step 2 - Explore the data (EDA: look before you model)

The raw table is 3.15 million rows (6 pumps x 1 reading a minute x 365 days). You find 41,000 impossible 0.00 mm/s vibration rows - a probe was unplugged for 28 days - so you mark them missing, not zero; and 3 glitch readings of 620 C, which you drop. And only 9 real failure events in 4 years of CMMS history - thin. Hold that thought.

Step 3 - Shape the clues (feature engineering + the label)

Per pump per hour: rolling 24-hour average vibration; how fast that average rises over 7 days; bearing-temperature rise rate; current-to-flow ratio (a climbing ratio means the pump works harder for the same water - a classic wear sign); days-since-bearing-change from the CMMS. The label: fails_within_7d = 1 if a bearing failure lands in the next 7 days, else 0.

Step 4 - Find the signal (supervised + unsupervised + time-series)

Supervised (split by time, 3 years train / last year test): catches 2 of 2 real failures with a 63-hour median warning, plus 3 false alarms a year. Anomaly detection (no labels - the net for only 9 failures): flags P-204 71 hours before it seized. Time-series: vibration trend climbs 2.1 -> 4.8 mm/s; an FFT peak at 24.5 Hz (1470 rpm / 60 = once-per-revolution) points to imbalance or a worn bearing - telling the millwright what to look for.

Step 5 - Trust it and explain it (no leakage; SHAP)

A random split let future hours of the same failure run-up leak into training and falsely claimed 98% accuracy; splitting by time (train on the past, test on the future) gave the true, lower, real picture. Then SHAP shows why the P-204 alarm fired: bearing-temperature slope (largest), vibration slope, then current-to-flow. Now Yusra gets a one-page alert she can act on - 'plan a bearing swap on day shift Thursday' - not 'the computer said so'.

See what the five steps did. They turned a flood of three million rows into a single honest alert: P-204, sixty-three hours out, bearing temperature and vibration rising, once-per-rev fault - plan the swap for Thursday day shift. And notice what makes it trustworthy. The point was never a clever model; it was an honest one that lets a human act in time. Two things guard that honesty: refusing leakage (the random split that flattered the model to 98% was a lie - the honest time split told the truth), and refusing the black box (SHAP says why, so Yusra acts on a reason, not on "the computer said so"). The data does not fix the pump. It buys a person time and a reason - which is the whole gift.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the answer in five moves (the third move, finding the signal, folds in three classic tools).

  • 1. Frame. Write the question sharply, and early enough to act on - "warn me 48 hours before a bearing fails", not "analyse the pumps".
  • 2. Explore (EDA). Look at the data before modelling: count rows, find gaps and impossible values, draw a quick chart - catch the broken sensor before it fools you.
  • 3. Shape. Feature engineering: turn raw readings into shaped clues (rolling averages, slopes, ratios, days-since-X) and build the label you want to predict.
  • 4. Find the signal. Supervised learning predicts a labelled outcome; unsupervised anomaly detection flags "weird" with no labels (the net when failures are rare); time-series (decomposition + FFT) shows trend and the repeating frequency that names the fault.
  • 5. Trust and explain. Validate without leakage (split by time, not randomly), and explain each alarm (SHAP) so a human can act on the reason.

The key ideas: a feature is one shaped column the model learns from (a 7-day slope beats a single noisy reading); leakage is future information sneaking into training, the single most common way a model looks brilliant in testing and proves useless in life; anomaly detection learns what normal looks like and flags what strays, so it needs no labels; and interpretability (SHAP) turns a verdict into a reason. This workflow fits when you have enough good history and a real decision to support; it does not fit when you have a handful of rows, when the data is mostly broken, or when a simple rule ("replace the bearing every 12 months") already does the job - data science earns its keep on hard, data-rich problems, not on every problem.

Two disciplines keep the answer honest. First, split by time, never at random - if hours from the same failure run-up land in both training and testing, the model has secretly seen the answer; it will dazzle you and then fail you, and judging it on plain accuracy when failures are rare hides the lie (judge it on real failures caught early and false alarms raised). T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your analysis honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Leakage is the cardinal sin. A model that has glimpsed the future scores beautifully and helps no one. Split by time; let every feature use only what you would truly know at the moment of prediction.

• Do not trust the headline accuracy. With 9 failures in millions of rows, a model that always says "fine" is 99.99% accurate and worthless. Judge on real failures caught early and false alarms raised, not on accuracy.

• Clean before you model. A 0.00 from an unplugged probe or a 620 C glitch will quietly poison everything downstream. EDA is not a formality; it is where you catch the lie in the data.

• Explain, or it will not be trusted. "The computer said so" is not actionable and will not survive a hard question. If you cannot say why the alarm fired, you do not yet have a tool a team can use.

• Know when not to. Too few rows, mostly-broken data, or a problem a simple rule already solves - then this is the wrong tool. "Not yet" is a professional answer, not a defeat.

You came in with a flood of data and a superintendent who only wanted the failures to stop catching her by surprise. You can give her something far better than another eyeballed chart: a single, honest alert that says what is failing, why it is failing, and when she must act - early enough to fix it on a calm day shift instead of at 02:40. That is the whole craft here: move from a flood of raw data to one trustworthy warning a person can act on - built honestly, explained plainly, and humble enough to say "not yet" when the data is not ready.
