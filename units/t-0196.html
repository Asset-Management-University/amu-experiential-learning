Step 1 of 5

Six true answers and none of them the answer

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna. A pump station failed overnight and the review asks a simple question: who was accountable for it?

Six managers give six different answers. Operations says the maintenance team; maintenance says they work to the plan they are given; planning says the capital project still holds it; the project manager says it was handed over in March; finance says it has been on the operations budget since April; and the engineering manager says he has never had it on any list of his.

Every one of those answers is true. Not one of them is the answer.

So you go looking for the document that settles it, and you find the organisation chart. It tells you precisely who reports to whom - six neat columns, every name in a box, nothing missing. And it tells you absolutely nothing about who was answerable for that pump station.

We have a complete map of our people and no map at all of who answers for our assets. What does the second map look like, and what will it show me that I do not already know?

Before you read on, decide. The pump station failed and you asked who was answerable for it. The operations supervisor runs it daily. The maintenance planner holds its plan. The area manager holds its budget. The projects engineer specified it and handed it over three years ago. The technical manager was consulted when it was last modified. And the asset manager says he has never had it on any list of his. Every one of those answers is true. Not one of them is the answer. So you go looking for the document that settles it, and you find the organisation chart: six neat columns, every name in a box, nothing missing — and nothing whatever about who was answerable for that pump station. Write down what the missing map looks like — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what a delegation actually moves

• “The area manager holds the budget, so it is his.” Budget is one relationship among six, and choosing it is arbitrary — you could equally argue for whoever operates it. Picking a rule after the event settles today's argument and none of the next ones.

• “Redraw the organisation chart more clearly.” The chart is already complete and correct. It maps who reports to whom, which is a different map of a different shape — improving it cannot make it answer a question it does not address.

• “Make the asset manager accountable for all assets.” Tidy, and it produces one person nominally answerable for everything and therefore genuinely answerable for nothing — with no relationship to where decisions are actually made.

• “It was delegated to the contractor who maintains it.” Here is the point most worth having: responsibility can be delegated, accountability cannot be transferred. Every delegation moves the doing. None of them moves the answering.

• “Write it into everybody's job description.” Six job descriptions each mentioning the pump station reproduces exactly the six true answers you already have, in a more durable format.

• So draw a second map, with a different unit of analysis: the asset systems down the page rather than the people, listed at the level where real decisions are actually made about them — the abstraction works, the raw-water main, the filtration stream, the chlorination plant, the reservoir and its telemetry. Against each, exactly one name answering. And note the arithmetic nobody has looked at: you have six managers and thirty-four asset systems. There is no reason those numbers should map neatly onto one another, and the silent assumption that they do is where assets fall through. Then test the map in two directions, because both findings are the output rather than a by-product. Gaps — rows where no name can be written, which are things that have been running unanswered-for, exactly like your pump station. And doubles — rows where two people each believe they answer, which is what produces contradictory decisions and the mutual assumption that the other one had it in hand.

Here is the map that answers it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the asset accountability map - the systems, the roles defined locally, one name with the answering marked, the two tests, and the findings.

1. Start from the asset systems, not from the people

EVERYTHING IN THIS TECHNIQUE FOLLOWS FROM ONE PRINCIPLE, SO SAY IT FIRST AND SAY IT PLAINLY: RESPONSIBILITY CAN BE DELEGATED. ACCOUNTABILITY CANNOT BE TRANSFERRED. While responsibilities can be delegated, ACCOUNTABILITY ALWAYS REMAINS WITH THE OWNER AND CANNOT BE HANDED ON - and ISO 55001 says the same thing about the top of the organisation: an individual may be appointed to OVERSEE asset management, but OWNERSHIP AND ACCOUNTABILITY REMAIN AT TOP MANAGEMENT LEVEL. Every delegation moves the DOING. None of them moves the ANSWERING. NOW THE MOVE THAT MAKES THAT PRINCIPLE VISIBLE. THE ORGANISATION CHART MAPS WHO REPORTS TO WHOM. IT DOES NOT MAP WHO IS ANSWERABLE FOR AN ASSET, AND THE TWO MAPS ARE DIFFERENT SHAPES. Corathyl has SIX MANAGERS AND THIRTY-FOUR ASSET SYSTEMS. There is no reason those two numbers should map neatly onto one another, and the silent assumption that they do is where assets fall through. SO DRAW THE SECOND MAP, IN THE SAME FAMILIAR FORMAT, WITH A DIFFERENT UNIT OF ANALYSIS: ASSET SYSTEMS DOWN THE PAGE, NOT PEOPLE. List them at the level real decisions are actually made about them - the abstraction works, the raw-water main, the filtration stream, the chlorination plant, the reservoir and its telemetry, the reticulation network, the standby generation, the laboratory - NOT individual pumps, which is too fine to be governed, and NOT 'the water system', which is too coarse to be answerable for. IF A ROW IS SOMETHING NOBODY WOULD EVER BE ASKED TO EXPLAIN AT A BOARD MEETING, IT IS THE WRONG SIZE. And do this before you look at a single name, because the moment the org chart is open beside you, the systems will quietly reshape themselves to fit it.

2. Define the roles in your own words before you use the words

THE ESTABLISHED STRUCTURE IS AN ASSET OWNERSHIP MATRIX MAPPING EVERY ASSET TO ITS OWNER, CUSTODIAN AND USER GROUP. THE ASSET OWNER IS TYPICALLY A SENIOR MANAGER OR BUSINESS UNIT LEADER WHO SETS CLASSIFICATION, APPROVES RIGHTS AND REMAINS ULTIMATELY ACCOUNTABLE EVEN WHEN TASKS ARE DELEGATED. THE ASSET CUSTODIAN IS THE TECHNICAL OR OPERATIONAL STAFF WHO MAINTAIN AND SUPPORT IT - MAINTAINERS ARE THE CUSTODIANS OF ASSETS, MAINTAINING THEM ON BEHALF OF ALL STAKEHOLDERS. THAT IS THE SHAPE. NOW THE HAZARD, AND IT IS A REAL ONE. THE SAME TWO TITLES CARRY THREE DIFFERENT MEANINGS IN THREE ADJACENT PROFESSIONAL WORLDS. In finance, an ASSET OWNER holds capital on behalf of beneficiaries and an ASSET MANAGER invests it under a mandate. In information-security management, an ASSET OWNER is the person accountable for the classification and protection of an information asset. In physical asset management they mean something different again. YOUR FINANCE DIRECTOR, YOUR INFORMATION-SECURITY LEAD AND YOUR ENGINEERING MANAGER WILL EACH READ THEIR OWN PROFESSION'S MEANING INTO YOUR CHART, BELIEVE THEY UNDERSTAND IT, AND BELIEVE THEY AGREE WITH EACH OTHER. A CHART THAT USES THESE TITLES WITHOUT DEFINING THEM LOCALLY IS WORSE THAN NO CHART, because no chart at least leaves people asking. SO WRITE THE DEFINITIONS FIRST, IN YOUR ORGANISATION'S OWN WORDS, AND WRITE THEM AS DECISIONS RATHER THAN DESCRIPTIONS: the OWNER decides what level of service and risk this system will carry and answers for its performance, cost and risk over its whole life; the MANAGER decides how that is delivered, plans the work and holds the budget; the CUSTODIAN does and maintains the work and holds the condition knowledge; the USER operates it within stated limits. Four rows, four sentences, each beginning DECIDES or ANSWERS FOR. If you cannot write the sentence, the role does not yet exist in your organisation and no chart will create it.

3. One accountable name per system - and mark where the answering stops

NOW PUT ONE NAME AGAINST EACH SYSTEM - A PERSON, NOT A DEPARTMENT AND NOT A COMMITTEE - FOR ITS PERFORMANCE, ITS COST AND ITS RISK. Three things, one name, because splitting them is how an organisation ends up with a system that performs well, costs too much, and is nobody's problem. Then do the part almost every chart omits. FOLLOW THE DELEGATION DOWNWARD AND MARK WHERE THE ANSWERING STOPS. The owner delegates delivery to the manager; the manager delegates the work to the custodian; the custodian instructs a contractor. FOUR STEPS, AND THE DOING HAS MOVED FOUR TIMES WHILE THE ANSWERING HAS NOT MOVED AT ALL. A map that shows the four steps and does not mark the stop is not a map of accountability - IT IS A MAP OF WHO WAS ASKED TO DO THINGS, and it is precisely the document an organisation produces shortly before discovering that nobody was answerable. SO DRAW THE STOP EXPLICITLY: a line under the accountable name, with the delegations below it shown as delegations. AND HERE IS HOW TO TEST WHETHER YOU HAVE THE RIGHT NAME, IN ONE QUESTION: IF THIS SYSTEM FAILED TONIGHT AND THE FAILURE WERE PUBLIC TOMORROW, WHO WOULD BE ASKED TO EXPLAIN IT, AND WOULD 'I DELEGATED THAT' BE AN ACCEPTABLE ANSWER FROM THEM? Where the honest answer is that the explanation would be acceptable, you have found a delegation, not an owner, and you must keep going up. NOW SORT WHAT YOU HAVE INTO THREE PILES: systems with ONE name, systems with NO name, and systems with TWO OR MORE people who each believe they hold it. At Corathyl that is NINETEEN, NINE AND SIX. And notice that the six contested ones are not a bookkeeping error - if each of those people has been acting as though they held it, this suggests the work has been done twice or in two directions.

4. Test every name against authority and against the lifecycle

A NAME IN A BOX IS NOT ACCOUNTABILITY. TWO TESTS TURN IT INTO ONE. FIRST, THE AUTHORITY TEST: CAN THIS PERSON AUTHORISE WHAT THIS SYSTEM TYPICALLY NEEDS? Put the person's delegated approval limit next to the cost of the intervention that system usually requires, and look at the two numbers together. AT CORATHYL, NINETEEN SYSTEMS HAVE EXACTLY ONE NAME, WHICH LOOKS LIKE SUCCESS UNTIL THIS COLUMN IS FILLED IN - BECAUSE ELEVEN OF THE NINETEEN NAME SOMEBODY WHOSE APPROVAL LIMIT IS BELOW THE COST OF THE WORK THAT SYSTEM NEEDS. THAT LEAVES EIGHT OF THIRTY-FOUR, 23.5 PER CENT, WITH ONE NAMED PERSON WHO CAN ACTUALLY ACT. ACCOUNTABILITY WITHOUT AUTHORITY IS NOT A DEMANDING JOB - IT IS AN IMPOSSIBLE ONE, and it is created by the organisation, not by the person holding it. Where the two numbers do not match, you have two honest options and only two: raise the authority, or move the accountability. Writing the name down again more firmly is not one of them. SECOND, THE LIFECYCLE TEST: DOES THIS ACCOUNTABILITY CHANGE HANDS PART-WAY THROUGH THE ASSET'S LIFE? Almost always it does, and almost never is the handover written down. The commonest one by a distance is PROJECT TO OPERATIONS - a capital team is accountable while the asset is being built and an operations team afterwards, and the transfer happens on a date nobody has agreed, in a meeting nobody minuted, sometimes months after the asset started working. A SYSTEM IN THAT GAP IS ACCOUNTABLE TO NOBODY WHILE BEING VISIBLY LOOKED AFTER BY EVERYBODY. So for every system, write the lifecycle stages across the top and show WHERE THE NAME CHANGES AND WHAT EVENT TRIGGERS THE CHANGE - not a date, an event: practical completion, first production, handover certificate, end of defects liability. Then check that the event is one somebody actually declares.

5. Read the map for its gaps - then take it to whoever can assign

THE MAP IS NOT THE PRODUCT. THE FINDINGS ARE. READ IT FOR FOUR THINGS AND WRITE EACH ONE AS A SENTENCE SOMEBODY CAN ACT ON: THE UNOWNED, THE CONTESTED, THE UNDER-AUTHORISED, AND THE SYSTEMS SITTING IN AN UNCOMPLETED HANDOVER. THEN LOOK FOR THE PATTERN, BECAUSE THE GAPS ARE USUALLY NOT SCATTERED. THE MAP OFTEN REVEALS THAT ACCOUNTABILITY GAPS ARE MORE COMMON FOR ASSETS THAT CROSS ORGANISATIONAL BOUNDARIES - between a capital project and operations, between the works and the network, between the physical asset and the system that controls it. AT CORATHYL, SEVEN OF THE NINE UNOWNED SYSTEMS CROSS A BOUNDARY. THERE ARE ELEVEN BOUNDARY-CROSSING SYSTEMS IN ALL, SO SEVEN OF ELEVEN - 64 PER CENT - ARE UNOWNED, AGAINST TWO OF THE TWENTY-THREE THAT SIT INSIDE ONE MANAGER'S WORLD, 8.7 PER CENT. THAT IS A RATE OVER SEVEN TIMES HIGHER AT THE BOUNDARIES, AND IT IS A TYPICAL FINDING RATHER THAN A LAW - thirty-four systems is enough to see a pattern clearly and not enough to prove one, so report it as what it is. The reason is not mysterious: a boundary-crossing system is the one no single manager's chart contains, so nobody's map was ever missing it. AND NOW THE MOST IMPORTANT SENTENCE IN THE WHOLE TECHNIQUE. WHEN ACCOUNTABILITY IS UNCLEAR, IT IS A SIGN OF A SYSTEM PROBLEM, NOT A PEOPLE PROBLEM. THE PURPOSE OF THE MAP IS TO REVEAL THE SYSTEMIC GAPS SO THEY CAN BE FIXED. IT IS A TOOL FOR CLARITY, NOT FOR BLAME. Nobody chose the gaps; they are what an organisation grows when it maps its people and forgets to map its assets. FINALLY, HAND THE MAP ON. You have exposed the gaps. You do not fill them, because assigning accountability is a decision for the governing body, and a map that quietly assigns names on its author's authority will be politely received and permanently ignored. Take it to whoever can actually assign, with the four findings written as questions they can answer in the room.

Box 4 is where a tidy map usually falls apart. Box 5 is the only part anybody else will read. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Responsibility for doing the work can be delegated. Accountability for the outcome cannot be transferred. Everything here follows from that one sentence. Five questions:

  • What are the rows of this map? Asset systems, not people. The organisation chart maps who reports to whom. This one shows who is answerable for what.
  • What do these words mean here? Owner, manager, custodian, user - defined in your organisation's own words, as decisions. The titles mean different things in adjacent professions and everyone reads their own.
  • Who answers, and where does the answering stop? One name per system for performance, cost and risk - and the stop marked explicitly, with the delegations below it shown as delegations.
  • Can they actually act, and for how long? Authority against the cost of the work, and the point in the life where the name changes.
  • What does the map show? The unowned, the contested, the under-authorised and the unhanded-over - and the pattern in where they sit.

The arithmetic of a map that has never been drawn. Corathyl has six managers and thirty-four asset systems, and there was never a reason those two numbers should fit together. Map the systems and they sort into three piles: nineteen with exactly one name, nine with none, and six with two or more people who each believe they hold it. Nineteen out of thirty-four looks like a decent result - until the authority column goes in, and eleven of the nineteen name somebody whose approval limit is below the cost of the work that system typically needs. That leaves eight of thirty-four, 23.5 per cent, with one named person who can actually act. And the six contested systems are not a bookkeeping error: if each of those people has been acting as though they held it, this suggests the work has been done twice, or in two directions.

The pattern in the gaps, stated as carefully as the evidence allows. The gaps are usually not scattered. At Corathyl, seven of the nine unowned systems cross an organisational boundary - between a capital project and operations, between the works and the network, between the physical asset and the system that controls it. There are eleven boundary-crossing systems in all, so seven of eleven - 64 per cent - are unowned, against two of the twenty-three that sit inside one manager's world, 8.7 per cent. That is a rate over seven times higher at the boundaries. Thirty-four systems is enough to see a pattern clearly and not enough to prove one, so treat it as a typical finding rather than a law - and note that the reason is not mysterious. A boundary-crossing system is the one no single manager's chart contains, so nobody's map was ever missing it.

And the sentence the whole unit rests on. When accountability is unclear, it is a sign of a system problem, not a people problem. The purpose of the map is to reveal the systemic gaps so they can be fixed. It is a tool for clarity, not for blame. The six managers in T1 were not evading anything - each was answering truthfully from inside their own part of the organisation, and the organisation had never asked the question in a form any one of them could answer. Nobody chose these gaps. They are what an organisation grows when it maps its people and forgets to map its assets.

One honest boundary. You map the accountabilities and expose the gaps. You do not assign them - that is the governing body's work. A map that quietly assigns names on its author's authority will be politely received and permanently ignored.

Does it scale? You have been looking at the organisation chart trying to find where the assets fit. That is the wrong way around. Start with the assets and ask which people are answerable for them, and the same boxes and lines tell you something they could never tell you before.

  2. Name the root error. The map is complete. So why did two reviews still fail to find anybody accountable?
  3. The two undefined words. Why does that matter more here than it would in most documents?
  4. Four names, four limits. What has the organisation actually done to those four people, and what are the only two honest fixes?
  5. Rebuild it. What is the first thing you would do differently, and what would you expect the new map to look like that this one does not?

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the scoring should feel uncomfortable, because the map passes the checks a reviewer usually applies. One name per system, no blanks, no duplicates, signed off. Every fault in it is invisible from the page.

Second, the root error is in box 1, and everything else follows from it: the map was built by walking the organisation chart. You can see it in the layout - the systems are grouped under the departments, which means the departments came first and the systems were sorted into them. Once that happens, a system that does not fit inside one department has to be forced into one, so the six boundary-crossing systems each got assigned to whichever side had the stronger claim. The map did not miss the hard cases. It resolved them, silently, in favour of tidiness - and the two failures since have both been on exactly those systems. The telemetry system going to the information technology manager and the raw-water main going to the treatment manager are the same error twice: the boundary was settled by drawing the line, not by deciding who answers.

Third, the undefined words matter here more than usual because the readers of this map do not share a profession. "Asset Owner" and "Asset Manager" mean one thing in finance, another in information security, and another again in physical asset management. This chart goes to a finance director, an information technology manager and an engineering manager, and each will read their own meaning, understand it perfectly, and believe they agree with the others. A chart with undefined titles is worse than no chart, because no chart at least leaves people asking.

Fourth, those four people have been given accountability without authority, which is not a demanding job but an impossible one - and it was created by the organisation, not by them. The only two honest fixes are to raise the authority or move the accountability. Writing the name down again, more firmly, in a document with more signatures on it, is not a third option, and it is what usually happens.

Fifth, the first thing to do differently is to list the systems before opening the organisation chart, at the level decisions are actually made about them, and only then to ask who answers. Expect the rebuilt map to be untidy: blanks where nobody answers, contested rows where two people both do, systems that span two departments and refuse to sit in either, and three construction projects whose handover event has not happened. That untidiness is the finding. The first map had none of it, and that was never because the organisation was well governed.

T5: The Guardrails

The dangerous version of this map is the tidy one.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Building it from the organisation chart. Open the chart first and the systems will quietly reshape themselves to fit the departments. List the systems before you look at a single name.

• Using "owner" and "manager" without local definitions. Three professions, three meanings. Everyone understands it, and they understand different things.

• A delegation chain with no marked stop. Four steps of delegation move the doing four times and the answering not at all. Unmarked, that is a map of who was asked to do things.

• Accountability without authority. A name whose approval limit is below what the system needs is an impossible position the organisation created. Raise the authority or move the accountability.

• Rows at the wrong size. Individual pumps cannot be governed; "the water system" cannot be answered for. If nobody would ever be asked to explain that row, it is the wrong size.

• Splitting performance, cost and risk between people. That is how you get a system that runs well, costs too much, and is nobody's problem.

• Naming a department or a committee. Neither can be asked to explain anything the following morning.

• Forgetting the mid-life handover. Project to operations is the commonest, and it usually happens on a date nobody agreed, in a meeting nobody minuted.

• A handover triggered by a date rather than an event. Dates slip silently. Practical completion, first production and handover certificates are declared by somebody.

• Treating a tidy map as a correct one. No blanks and no contested rows usually means the hard cases were resolved in favour of the layout.

• Filling the gaps yourself. Assigning accountability is the governing body's decision. A map that assigns names on its author's authority is received politely and ignored permanently.

• Reading the gaps as a list of people who failed. Nobody chose them. They are what an organisation grows when it maps its people and forgets to map its assets.

Onalenna had six true answers and none of them the answer. Start from the asset systems rather than the people, at the level decisions are actually made about them, and do it before the organisation chart is open beside you; define owner, manager, custodian and user in your organisation's own words as decisions, because those titles mean different things in three adjacent professions and everyone will read their own; put one name against each system for performance, cost and risk, and mark explicitly where the answering stops, since responsibility can be delegated and accountability cannot be transferred; then test every name twice - against the authority to act on what that system needs, and against the point in the asset's life where the name is supposed to change hands - and finally read the map for what it shows you, which is the unowned, the contested, the under-authorised and the systems sitting in a handover nobody completed. Expect the gaps to cluster where the assets cross a boundary, because those are the systems no single manager's chart ever contained. When accountability is unclear, it is a sign of a system problem, not a people problem. The map is a tool for clarity, not for blame. Then take it to whoever can actually assign, because exposing the gaps is your work and filling them is theirs. Take one asset system your organisation depends on and ask who would be required to explain its failure in public tomorrow. If more than one name comes back, or none does, you have found your first row.
