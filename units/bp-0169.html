Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Kgaugelo, six weeks into being the new Head of Asset Performance at an open-pit manganese mine and processing plant. It is a Tuesday morning and you are standing at the back of the main boardroom; the blinds are half open, the morning light is hot on the long table, and the air-conditioner rattles in the corner - it has been on the maintenance list for a year. In your hands is a single sheet of paper. Your finance director, Nomathemba, printed it and slid it across the table five minutes ago without a word. The heading reads: "Digital spend - last 36 months." You count the rows. There are 31 of them.

Row by row, it is a list of pilots. A vibration-sensor trial on the primary crusher. A drone survey app for the pit walls. A "smart" lubrication tracker for the conveyor gearboxes. A predictive-maintenance dashboard for the haul trucks. A separate predictive-maintenance dashboard for the screening plant, bought eighteen months later by a different manager who did not know the first one existed. A tablet app for shift handover. A second tablet app for shift handover. A machine-learning trial that a consultant ran on twelve weeks of pump data and then left. The total at the bottom of the page is R47 million.

Nomathemba is watching you. She is not angry. She is tired, and she is precise. "I can show you the invoice for every one of these," she says quietly. "What I cannot show the board next month is what any of them did for us. They want one number - what did R47 million of 'digital' do for the value of our assets? And I don't have it." You look down the list again. You happen to know that the vibration trial on the crusher actually worked - it caught a failing bearing in March and saved an estimated R3.8 million in avoided downtime. But it ran on the trial vendor's laptop. When the trial ended, nobody renewed it. The sensors are still bolted to the crusher, dead, because the data had nowhere to go. The haul-truck dashboard is beautiful and nobody opens it, because the fitters do their planning in a spreadsheet that does not talk to it. Two pilots measured the same gearbox temperatures in two different units and disagreed.

Your CEO, Refentse, puts his head round the door, holding his phone. "The board's strategy session is in four weeks," he says. "They've read about a competitor cutting maintenance cost by twenty percent with 'the digital mine'. They want to know our plan. Not thirty-one plans. OUR plan." He taps the door frame twice and is gone. And here is the temptation, and you feel it in your chest: there is a vendor demo this afternoon for a single, shiny, all-in-one platform that promises to "unify everything". The salesman is already in reception. It would be so easy to walk in, be impressed, and tell the board THAT is the plan - buy the big box, and the mess on this page becomes someone else's product. You could have an answer by lunch. But you keep looking at the page. Thirty-one rows. R47 million. One bearing saved. Nothing connected. The crusher sensors dead on a working machine.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The CEO wants a plan and the salesman is in reception. "Buy the shiny all-in-one platform and tell the board that's the plan." But a platform that unifies thirty-one things still pointed at nothing is just pilot number thirty-two with a bigger invoice; the box is not a strategy. "Pick the single best tool and standardise on it." But 'best tool' is still the wrong question - some of these pilots work and still deliver nothing, because they connect to no goal and no owner. "Kill all 31 and start clean." But one of them caught a bearing and saved R3.8m, and one is the only thing watching the pit-wall safety - a blind cull throws away the wins with the waste. So before you read on: name why even a WORKING pilot can be worthless here, and the one thing you would put on the page before you judge a single tool.

I am Kgaugelo, six weeks into being the new Head of Asset Performance at a manganese mine. My finance director, Nomathemba, has just slid a single sheet across the boardroom table: 'Digital spend - last 36 months.' Thirty-one rows. A vibration trial on the crusher; a drone survey for the pit walls; a smart lubrication tracker; a predictive-maintenance dashboard for the haul trucks; a SECOND predictive-maintenance dashboard bought 18 months later by a different manager who did not know the first existed; two separate shift-handover apps; a machine-learning trial a consultant ran for twelve weeks then left. The total is R47 million. Nomathemba can show the invoice for every one, but she cannot show the board what any of them did for us. And I know the crusher vibration trial actually WORKED - it caught a failing bearing in March and saved an estimated R3.8 million - but it ran on the vendor's laptop, the trial ended, nobody renewed it, and the sensors are still bolted to the crusher, dead, because the data had nowhere to go. The CEO, Refentse, wants OUR plan in four weeks - not thirty-one plans. And there is a salesman in reception this afternoon with a single shiny platform that promises to 'unify everything' - I could buy the box and have an answer by lunch. But thirty-one rows, R47 million, one bearing saved, nothing connected. Where do I start?
The temptation is to treat this as a technology problem - pick the best tool, or buy the big platform that swallows them all. But the 31 rows are not 31 bad tools; several of them WORKED. The problem is that nothing on the page is tied to anything the business is actually trying to achieve - each pilot was bought to 'be more digital', which is not a goal. So stop asking 'which tool?' and start asking 'in service of WHAT?' Go and find the few real goals the organisation has already committed to - they are in the business plan, just never connected to the digital spend (here: lift availability from 82% to 88%, cut unplanned maintenance cost by 15%, reduce the safety risk near unstable pit walls - three objectives, each with a number, each already owned by the board). Then build the cheapest instrument that makes the invisible visible: one page, a column for each objective, a row for every pilot, and map each pilot to the goal it serves - writing down in advance what a healthy page would look like. The mapping does the work: you will see four pilots crowded on one goal (overlap), one good pilot serving a goal with no owner (an orphan), and a pile that serves no goal at all (waste). The real question is not 'which platform?' but 'which of these efforts has a line of sight to a goal we already own - and what shared foundation would let the good ones connect and scale?'
Five moves turn 31 pilots into one plan. (1) REFUSE THE TECHNOLOGY FRAME: the tools are not the problem (several work); nothing is tied to a business goal; ask 'in service of what?' not 'which tool?' - and cancel the vendor demo. (2) FIND THE GOALS FIRST + SET A SUCCESS LINE: the three objectives the board already owns (82%->88% availability, -15% unplanned maintenance cost, reduce pit-wall safety risk); build a one-page instrument (three objective columns x 31 pilot rows) and write the YES/NO sign before filling a cell. (3) MAP EVERY PILOT ONTO THE OBJECTIVES (the step that does the work, a morning with a pen): four maintenance pilots crowd one goal (overlap), the drone pit-wall survey is the only thing serving safety (an orphan, on a free trial expiring in 60 days), two vague handover apps, and TWENTY-FOUR map to no goal at all (waste - someone had been to a conference). (4) LET THE PAGE RE-RANK YOU: you walked in certain the headline was the maintenance overlap, but the page says the overlap is a slow fixable inefficiency while the genuine EMERGENCY is the orphaned safety drone 60 days from death - renew it THIS WEEK, before the tidy clean-up. (5) BUILD THE STRATEGY + THE FUNDING RULE: not a bigger platform but a shared foundation (one place for the data, agreed units of measurement, a single owner per objective) so the good pilots connect and scale; one plan - three objectives, the value each must deliver in rands and percent (a 15% cut on a R310m maintenance bill is ~R46m a YEAR, more than the whole three-year pilot spend), the capabilities every investment must plug into, and the rule: no pilot funded unless it names which objective it serves and how it connects; then sort every pilot scale / fix-then-scale / stop. The board's first question becomes 'who owns the maintenance-cost number?' - which is exactly right.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is what you and the team actually did. You did not go to the vendor demo. You cancelled it. Five moves:

  • Refuse the technology frame - ask 'in service of what?': you did not go to the vendor demo. You cancelled it. Because the 31 rows were not 31 bad tools - several of the tools were good; the vibration trial actually caught a failing bearing and saved R3.8 million. The problem was that nothing on the page was tied to anything the business was trying to achieve. Each pilot had been bought to 'be more digital', which is not a goal - it is a mood. So the first move was to refuse the frame everyone else was using. The salesman in reception, the competitor's 'digital mine', the CEO's pressure all pulled toward the question 'which tool, or which platform?' You put that question down and picked up a better one: 'in service of WHAT?' A shiny platform that unifies thirty-one things still pointed at nothing is just pilot number thirty-two with a bigger invoice. The disease is not the tools; it is the missing line of sight from each tool back to something the business actually needs.
  • Find the goals first, then build the instrument: so you went and found the one thing every asset-management decision is supposed to serve: asset value, expressed as the goals the organisation has already set. The mine already had them - they were in the business plan, just never connected to the digital spend: (a) lift plant availability from 82% to 88%, (b) cut unplanned maintenance cost by 15%, and (c) reduce the safety risk of people working near unstable pit walls. Three real objectives, each with a number, each already owned by the board. Then you built the smallest instrument that could make the invisible visible - nothing more than a single page: three columns, one for each objective, and a row for every one of the 31 pilots. And before you filled a single cell, you set your success line out loud: 'If these pilots are a real strategy, most of them will land cleanly under one objective, and the few that do not will be obvious mistakes. If instead the page is a mess of overlaps and orphans, the spend was never a strategy at all - it was scatter.' You wrote the YES-sign and the NO-sign down before you knew the answer, so the page, not your hope, would return the verdict.
  • Map every pilot onto the objectives: then you mapped every one of the 31 pilots onto those three objectives, and this is the step that did the work - a morning with a printed list and a pen, no consultants, no platform. The crusher vibration trial, the haul-truck dashboard, the lubrication tracker and the pump machine-learning trial all pointed at the SAME objective: cut unplanned maintenance cost by catching failures early. Four pilots, one goal, no connection between them - and two of them measured the same gearbox temperatures in different units and disagreed. The drone pit-wall survey pointed straight at the safety objective; it was the only one in the entire R47 million that did, and it was running on a free trial that expired in two months. The two shift-handover apps pointed at availability, vaguely, neither with a number attached. And twenty-four of the 31 pilots could not be honestly mapped to any of the three objectives at all - they existed because someone had been to a conference. The page was no longer a technology zoo; it was four overlapping bets on one goal, one orphaned safety win about to die, and twenty-four things to stop.
  • Let the page re-rank your priorities: the page returned a verdict, and then a surprise that changed your plan. Your NO-sign came true - it was overlaps and orphans, not a strategy - and you had expected that. But the page told you something you had NOT expected. Walking into the room, you had been certain the headline for the board would be the maintenance overlap: four pilots fighting over one goal, the obvious waste, the obvious thing to consolidate. The single page said otherwise. The overlap was a slow, fixable inefficiency. The genuine EMERGENCY on the page was the one pilot you had almost overlooked - the drone survey, the only thing in the whole R47 million serving the safety objective, was sixty days from dying, and its death would leave a hazard to human life with no digital eyes on it at all. The instrument had quietly re-ranked your priorities. So you changed the plan: the very first action could not be the tidy maintenance clean-up you had walked in expecting; it had to be renewing the dying safety trial THIS WEEK, before anything else. That is what an honest instrument does - it overrules the headline you walked in carrying, and the discipline is to let it.
  • Build the foundation and the funding rule: then you asked what the four maintenance pilots needed to become one capability instead of four trials - and the answer was not a bigger platform. It was something humbler and harder: a shared place for the data to live, agreed units of measurement (so two pilots stop disagreeing about gearbox temperature), and a single owner accountable for the maintenance-cost objective who could decide which approaches to scale and which to retire. The dead crusher sensors did not need replacing; they needed somewhere to send their data and a person whose job it was to act on it. Then you wrote the one plan the CEO asked for - not a list of tools but a short document: here are our three asset objectives; here is the value each must deliver in rands and percent (a 15% cut on a R310 million annual maintenance bill is roughly R46 million a year, more than the entire three-year pilot spend, every year); here is the small set of digital capabilities - shared data, common standards, clear ownership - that every digital investment must plug into; and here is the rule going forward: no pilot is funded unless it names which objective it serves and how it will connect to the others. Each existing pilot was then sorted into scale (3), fix-then-scale (3), or stop (24) - plus the immediate renewal of the dying drone. Four weeks later the CEO presented one plan, not thirty-one, and the board's first question was not 'which platform?' but 'who owns the maintenance-cost number?' - which is exactly the right question, and you had an answer. (Proving to stakeholders what a spend already DELIVERED is a kindred reporting problem; each team optimising its own line until the whole loses is its own problem - here the cure is one prospective plan, not a better report.)

See where it lands. One page, three objectives, the value at stake; scale three pilots, fix-then-scale three, stop twenty-four, and renew the dying safety drone this week - and one new rule for funding anything digital. A scatter of disconnected pilots - each separately reasonable, none tied to a shared goal - is not a digital strategy; it is a spend of money, or time, or attention that nobody can defend by the value it created. The fix is not a bigger platform. It is a line of sight from every investment back to the few asset-value goals the organisation already owns, plus the shared foundation - data, standards, ownership - that lets the good ones connect and scale. Name the goals first, map every effort onto them, let the page (not your hunch) dictate the plan, sort each into scale, fix-then-scale or stop, and write the one rule that stops the pile growing again. The strategy is never the tools; it is the line of sight from each tool back to the value it is meant to create.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You have seen it solved once, on a big stage - a mine, a board, R47 million. Now take one step with a guide beside you. The trick is to recognise the same problem when it is wearing a completely different costume. There is no board this time. No finance director. No rands.

See the friend who has "tried everything" and is no further along

Your friend Rofhiwa is not running a mine. She is twenty-nine, works full time, and feels stuck. Over coffee she tells you, half-laughing, that she has "tried everything". You ask what she means, and she counts on her fingers: a paid language app she opened twice; an online coding course she is 20% through; a gym contract she is still paying for; a budgeting app; a second budgeting app; a morning-run habit that lasted three weeks in January; a podcast subscription "for self-improvement"; and a Saturday pottery class. Eight things. "I'm always busy bettering myself," she says, "but a year goes by and I honestly can't tell you I'm any further along." It does not look like the mine at all. So let us look properly.

At the mine, your very first move was to refuse the obvious frame - the problem was not "we need a better platform", it was "nothing is tied to a goal". The obvious frame for Rofhiwa is the personal version of "buy a better tool": "you just need more discipline" or "pick one and stick to it" - which treats the pile as a willpower problem. Run the case move instead. At the mine you did not start with the 31 tools; you started with the GOALS, the real already-owned outcomes, each with a number. So the question for Rofhiwa is not "which app should you keep?" but "what are the two or three things you are actually trying to become or achieve this year?" When you ask her, it goes quiet - she has never said it out loud - and then: "Honestly? Get out of debt. And change career into something technical." Two goals. Suddenly the eight efforts are not eight equal choices. Now do the step that did the work: map the eight onto the two. The coding course and (arguably) the language app point at CHANGE CAREER. The two budgeting apps both point at GET OUT OF DEBT - that is the duplication, two efforts doing one job, neither used. The gym, the run, the podcast and the pottery point at no stated goal at all - not bad, but not serving the two things she said matter most. And here is the one to catch: the January running habit ACTUALLY WORKED - she felt great, slept better - and it died, because nothing held it up. That is Rofhiwa's dead crusher sensor: a proven win that quietly stopped because nothing connected to it and no system owned it. It is the exact same page - four overlapping bets, an orphaned win, a handful of things to stop - only the costume changed. The spend was not money; it was a year of her attention, and that is what made it so much harder to spot, because nobody sends Rofhiwa an invoice, so nobody ever printed the page. The deep structure is the same at R47 million and at one person over coffee: a pile of separately-sensible efforts, each defended on its own, none tied to a shared stated goal - so the spend cannot be defended by the value it created. It hides best where nobody is counting. The fix is identical: name the few real goals first, map every effort onto them, then scale, fix or stop - and write the one rule that stops the pile growing again.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The shape of this problem is not about mines or sensors. It is this: a pile of disconnected initiatives, each separately reasonable, none tied to a shared goal, adding up to a spend nobody can defend in terms of the value it creates. That pattern lives almost everywhere. Find it where you are:

  1. Find your version of the 31 rows. Where is there a scatter of separate efforts - tools, projects, subscriptions, courses, habits, small initiatives - that were each a good idea on their own but that nobody has ever added up or connected? Name the pile, and roughly what it has cost in money, time or attention.
  2. Name the value they are supposed to serve. What are the two or three real goals you (or your organisation) have already committed to, written with a number where you can? Now the hard question: can you honestly map each item in your pile to one of those goals - or are some just things you do because a conference, a colleague, or a feeling said so?
  3. Look for your dead crusher sensor. Is there something in the pile that actually worked but quietly died or never spread, because it had nowhere to connect to or nobody to own it? What would it have needed to live and grow?
  4. Write your one rule, and sort the pile honestly. What single test must every new initiative pass before you fund it or start it? Then put each existing item into scale it, fix-it-then-scale, or stop it - and notice how it feels to write "stop" next to something you started.

Your world is not a manganese mine, and the numbers and tools will be completely different. But the trap is identical: doing many separate, reasonable things in the absence of one plan that links them to value - and then being unable to say what any of it was for. The discipline that fixes it is the same at every scale, from a R47 million mine to a single person with eight half-finished side projects. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
