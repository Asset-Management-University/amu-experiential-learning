Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

Before you begin - a note of care. This unit deals with an online scam and the loss of money that was hard to save. If this has happened to you, go gently - it happens to careful, capable people every day, and it is not a failure of intelligence. If money has just gone, the fastest first step is to phone your bank's fraud line, the number on the back of your card, and then to open a case at a police station.

You are Mbalenhle. It is a Tuesday evening, and you are tired after a long day. Your phone buzzes with a text message. You glance at it, and your heart gives a little jump: it is from your bank. The logo is right, the colours are right, the tone is formal and official.

The message says unusual activity has been detected on your account, and that for your protection the account will be frozen in the next ten minutes unless you verify your identity. It gives a link to log in to your "secure portal". Panic sets in - you need that money for rent tomorrow, you cannot be locked out. You do not even think. You just act. You tap the link, a page opens that looks exactly like your bank's real website, and you quickly type in your username, your password, and the one-time code the page asks for.

A minute later your phone buzzes again - this time a real notification from your actual banking app: "Payment of R15,000 successful." The blood drains from your face. The money is gone. Your savings. You stare at the screen, at the fake message and the real notification, and a cold, sick feeling washes over you.

A message that looked exactly like my bank panicked me into acting, and my savings were gone in minutes - how could that happen, and how do I make sure it never does again?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to blame yourself. "It looked completely real, so surely it was safe to trust." But that is the first mistake: the entire job of a scammer is to look real. Their skill is not hacking computers but copying logos and faking pages so perfectly that even a careful person is fooled - looking real is the trap, not a sign of safety. Your next feeling is shame: "I cannot tell anyone, not even the bank - they will think I am an idiot." This is the scammer's second victory: shame keeps you silent, and silence gives them time, when the faster you report, the better the chance the bank can stop the payment or recover the funds. Finally you promise yourself, "I will just be smarter next time, I will pay more attention." But "being smarter" is not a plan: scammers are professionals at creating panic, using urgency to switch off the slow, careful part of your brain, and in the next moment of engineered fear a vague resolve to "be more careful" will fail. You need a simple, automatic habit that works even when you are frightened.

So before you read on: if being careful is not enough, what actually beats this?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough is realising that scammers do not break into your account - they trick you into opening the door for them. The weak point they attack is not your password; it is your human trust and your fear. They win with two tools: impersonation (pretending to be someone you trust) and urgency (making you feel you must act NOW). Because the attack is a simple trick, the defence is a simple set of habits. Five moves:

  • Understand the real attack - it is a trick, not a hack: the breakthrough is realising scammers do not break into your account - they trick YOU into opening the door, because the weak point they attack is not your password but your trust and your fear. The attack is SOCIAL, not technical: they win with two tools - IMPERSONATION (pretending to be something you trust, like your bank) and URGENCY (manufacturing a frightening deadline so you act before you think). That is why looking 'completely real' is not reassurance - it is the whole craft of the scam; copying a logo, colours and a login page perfectly is exactly what these criminals do for a living. And it is why there is no shame in being caught: they are professional manipulators who fool careful, intelligent people every single day. Once you see the attack is aimed at your feelings, not your computer, the defence becomes simple - and it is the same for everyone, regardless of technical skill.
  • Never act on an inbound message: this is the first unbreakable habit: treat EVERY unexpected message that arrives at you - text, email, call - claiming to be your bank or any authority, and asking you to act, as a possible scam until you have proven otherwise. So never click the link inside it, never call the number it gives you, never reply with anything, and never log in through it. The message itself is the scammer's territory - every link and number in it leads back to them, no matter how genuine it looks. This single rule defeats most scams on its own, because the whole attack depends on you acting WITHIN the channel they chose and controlled. The moment you refuse to act on the inbound message, their script falls apart.
  • Stop, and verify on a channel YOU start: having refused to act on the message, replace it with a channel you control. STOP. Put the phone down and take a breath - that pause alone defeats the urgency the scam runs on. Then contact the organisation YOURSELF, using a method you already know is genuine: the phone number printed on the back of your own bank card, or the official app you installed yourself, never the contact details the message provided. Ask them plainly: 'is there really a problem with my account?' If the message was real, you have lost nothing but a minute; if it was a scam, you have just stepped completely out of the trap. Verifying on your own channel is the heart of the whole defence - you are not trying to judge whether the message 'looks real enough', you are simply checking the story through a door only you can open.
  • Know the bright lines: a few hard rules never bend, and knowing them lets you spot a thief instantly: a real bank will NEVER ask you for your full PIN, your full password, or a one-time code (OTP) - by phone, by message, or on a page you reached from a link. The OTP especially is the key to your money: it is the last thing standing between a thief and your account, so the very act of asking for it marks the asker as a criminal, however official they sound. And remember the tell that runs through every scam: URGENCY is their number-one tool - 'act now or your account is frozen', 'verify in ten minutes'. So invert it: the more urgent and frightening a message makes you feel, the MORE certain you should be that it is a scam, and the more important it is to slow right down. Panic is the thing they are trying to create; calm is your shield.
  • If it happens, act fast and without shame: if you are ever caught - and remember millions of careful people are - the two things that matter are SPEED and the refusal to let shame silence you. Contact your real bank immediately on their genuine number (the one on your card) and tell them exactly what happened; the faster you report, the better the chance they can stop or claw back the payment and lock the account. Then report it to the police or the appropriate fraud or consumer authority in your country - which can help protect you and others. Change the passwords the scammer may now have. Do NOT wait, and do NOT hide it in embarrassment: shame is the scammer's last weapon, the thing that delays the one fast action that limits the harm. You were targeted by a professional, not foolish - and the brave, sensible thing is to raise the alarm at once.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - picture a stranger at your door.

See the verify-on-your-own-channel habit

A person in a convincing power-company uniform knocks and says there is a dangerous gas leak on your street and they must come in immediately to check your pipes. It feels urgent, and they look official. Do you just open the door?

No. You keep the door closed and tell them to wait. Then you walk away, find your last utility bill or look up the company's official number yourself, and phone the company to ask: "did you send someone to my address?" You do not argue with the person at the door, and you do not try to judge whether their uniform looks "real enough" - you simply switch to a channel YOU control, one you know is genuine, and verify the story there. It is the exact same habit that keeps your bank account safe: never decide on their channel; always check on yours.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You have the power to make yourself safe from these scams. It does not take technical genius - just a few unbreakable habits, for the fake messages and calls that pretend to be your bank, a delivery company, the tax office, anyone you trust.

  1. Your new rule. Any inbound message - text, email, call - that creates urgency and asks you to act is a possible scam until you prove it is not.
  2. Your new habit. STOP. Close the message; do not click, do not reply. Open your own banking app, or call the number on the back of your card, to check whether there is a real problem.
  3. Your bright lines. Never share your full PIN, your password, or a one-time code (OTP) with anyone, for any reason - and the more panicked you feel, the slower you should go, because urgency is the scammer's number-one tool.
  4. Your emergency plan. If you are ever caught, act fast and without shame: call your bank's real fraud line immediately, then report it to the police or fraud authority in your country.
