Where do I find a 'replace it now' number that's actually true for me?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and your biggest fleet headache is batteries. The supplier, the internet, the old mechanic - everyone repeats the same rule: "replace your battery every two years." So you do. Every 24 months you pull out a battery that still cranks the engine first try, and fit a new one. It feels like setting fire to a stack of R500 notes.

But the other thing happens too. A bakkie sits dead on the roadside, a customer waiting - and the logbook says that battery was only eight months old. You followed the rule for the strong one you threw away, and this one didn't get close. You're whipsawed both ways: wasting money scrapping good batteries, and losing money when young ones die without warning. That "two-year" number feels like a thumb-suck made by someone who doesn't own your bakkies or drive your roads.

This borrowed number is always wrong for me - so where do I find a "replace it now" number that's actually TRUE for my bakkies?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to hunt a better guess. Trust the supplier's number more strictly? Split the difference and replace every 18 months? Or, to be safe, just replace them all every year? But pause - each of those is still picking one borrowed number to rule them all. None explains why one battery dies young and another lasts for years; each ignores the evidence your gut already feels: the failures are all over the place.

You're treating this as if the answer must be a better number from outside. But the real answer has been sitting in your own filing cabinet the whole time. So: where is it hiding, and how do you read it?

The 'replace every 2 years' rule is always wrong for me - I scrap strong batteries AND get stranded by 8-month deaths. It's a number someone else made up. But my own logbook holds every battery's real age-at-death.
What if I stopped hunting a better borrowed number and read my OWN history - line up the ages each battery actually died, and look at the pattern?
Order your own ages-at-death (keep the survivors too); the middle one is the age by which half had failed; then read the SHAPE - die old (wear-out, plan it), die randomly (a calendar won't help, run-to-fail), or die young (keep the proven old ones).

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Stop hunting a better borrowed number. The true story of how your batteries fail is already written in your own logbook - you're sitting on the answer. Don't see those failure dates as random bad luck; read them as a population telling you its own secrets. Here's how to listen:

  • Stop hunting a better BORROWED number: a vendor's interval, or 'we've always done every X', is a fair STARTING guess when you have no history - but it's a number someone else made up, for someone else's assets and roads. The contradiction you keep feeling (some die young, some last for years) IS that borrowed number failing. The real answer isn't a better guess from outside - it's your own evidence.
  • Gather your own ages-at-death - and keep the survivors: go through your logbook and write down, for every one you've replaced, how long it actually lasted - its age-at-death. CRUCIALLY, also note the ones STILL GOING and their current ages: they are your proof of long life. Don't throw out the good news - survivors are evidence too.
  • Find your REAL typical life: take the ones that failed, write each age on a sticky note, and line them up youngest to oldest. Count to the middle note: that age is when HALF of them had failed - your true typical life, a fact from your own fleet, not a guess. (For Muhammad's batteries it was about 24 months.)
  • Read the SHAPE on the wall: step back and look at how the notes are spread - the shape tells you HOW they fail. DIE OLD: bunched together late (wear-out). DIE RANDOMLY: scattered all over, no pattern (an old one is no likelier to fail than a young one). DIE YOUNG: a few clustered at the very start, the rest lasting for ages (early duds, then proven). A single average hides this; the shape reveals it.
  • Let the shape decide the action: DIE OLD -> a planned replacement near your REAL typical life is smart (re-time it to your number, not the borrowed one). DIE RANDOMLY -> a calendar interval is pure waste; run-to-fail with a spare on the shelf, or check condition instead - never scrap a good young one. DIE YOUNG -> never replace a proven old survivor; handle the early duds on warranty. Set your interval, spares, and budget from YOUR shape.

Watch it work. You pull the ages-at-death of your last ten failed batteries and line the sticky notes up: 8, 11, 15, 18, 23, 25, 28, 30, 38, 45 months. Count to the middle - about 24 months: so half had failed by two years. At first that looks like it proves the two-year rule! But now step back and look at the shape: the notes are spread right across, 8 months to 45, with no bunching anywhere. That's the "die randomly" shape - a battery's age barely predicts its death. And there's the trap exposed: the average matched the rule, yet the average was a lie - they don't die at two years, they die at any age. So a calendar replacement is the wrong tool entirely: stop scrapping good batteries on a date, run them to failure with a charged spare on the shelf, and do a quick load-test to catch the weak ones. You just went from guessing with a borrowed number to deciding from your own evidence.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

The very same reading can point the opposite way - watch it re-time a schedule instead of scrapping it.

See it in Lerato's warehouse (a different shape, a different answer)

Lerato manages a warehouse with 100 big, costly ceiling lights. The rule she inherited: "block-replace all 100 every 12 months." It's expensive and disruptive, and it nagged at her.

She read her own history - the ages at which lights had actually failed - and lined up the notes: 18, 20, 21, 22, 22, 23, 23, 24, 25, 26 months. This time the shape was completely different: tightly bunched around 22 months. That's the "die old" shape - real wear-out, with a clear danger zone.

So her answer was the opposite of Muhammad's. A planned block-replacement is exactly right for wear-out - she just had the timing wrong. The borrowed "12 months" was scrapping lights with nearly a year of life left. She re-timed the block replacement to about 17 months (just before the failures start), still pre-empting outages but getting far more life from each light - a big saving in lamps and labour, with no extra disruption.

Same method, opposite shapes, opposite actions: Muhammad dropped the calendar; Lerato re-timed it. The shape decides.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

A borrowed number is a fine place to start, but it should never be the final word - your own experience is the higher truth. Think of a group of identical things you manage: filters, work boots, printer cartridges, tyres, light bulbs. Then read their story:

  1. Find your history. When did each one actually fail or get replaced? Don't forget to count the ones still in service - your survivors, proof of long life.
  2. Find your real "half-way" point. Line up the failure ages youngest to oldest; the middle one is the age by which half had failed. Is it what you expected?
  3. Read the shape, then decide. Do they die old (plan it, re-timed to your real life), die randomly (drop the calendar - run-to-fail with a spare, or check condition), or die young (keep the proven old ones)? Is your current schedule smart, or is it costing you money and risk?

Pull the records for one such group this week, line up the ages, and read the shape. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Lay out the sticky notes together.)

You were never going to find the right number by borrowing a better one - it was in your own logbook all along. The average can match the rule and still be a lie; the shape is the truth. Read the story your own assets are telling, and you stop guessing and start knowing - scrapping less, breaking down less, and spending exactly where it counts.
