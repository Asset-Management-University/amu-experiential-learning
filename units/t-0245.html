The shape of the work

T1: The Ache

The work keeps failing at the seams. Jobs fall through the gap between the central team and the sites. Standards drift. When something goes wrong, no one is clearly answerable. So someone redraws the org chart - new boxes, new titles, a reshuffle - and everyone braces for the improvement.

And six months later the same problems are back, wearing new titles. The jobs still fall through the cracks. And the people have been unsettled, their teams broken up and their relationships scattered, for nothing. You have been through a reorganisation that changed the chart and fixed none of the work.

We keep redrawing the org chart and the same problems keep coming back under new titles - how do I evaluate an organisational design honestly, and know when a new structure is the answer and when it is not?

Before you read on, decide. The restructure is done. New chart, new titles, everybody moved. And the two things that prompted it are happening exactly as before: nobody owns the handover between the day and night shifts, and the standby generator's servicing still falls between the electrical team and the facilities contract. Meanwhile the planning pair who had worked together for eleven years are now in different areas and have stopped talking, and a fitter who had no say in any of it has been moved to a site an hour further from his home. Write down how you would evaluate an organisational design honestly — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two tests that come before anybody moves

• “The design was wrong — try a different structure.” A third chart against a problem no chart addresses. You would unsettle everybody again and rediscover the same two gaps under a third set of titles.

• “Give it time to bed in.” Reasonable for genuine transition friction, and it will not create an owner for the shift handover. Waiting does not assign accountability that nobody assigned.

• “Copy the structure that works at the other works.” Adopts a design fitted to their work, their scale and their people. Their strengths may be irrelevant to you and their weaknesses unaffordable.

• “The problem is people not taking responsibility.” Nobody was ever given the handover to own. Blaming individuals for a gap the design never closed guarantees a fourth restructure.

• “It is done now — make it work.” Accepts both the unfixed work and the cost already paid, and quietly teaches everybody that restructures are things that happen to them and change nothing.

• Un-learn the idea that there is a correct or modern structure everything should move towards. There is not. Every structure is a set of trade-offs: grouping by expertise buys deep skill, consistent standards and a professional home, and costs silos and slow coordination; grouping by site or output buys local ownership and fast decisions, and costs duplicated specialists and divergent standards. Neither is better — they are differently shaped. So judge which structure's strengths match what this organisation most needs and whose weaknesses you can most afford, which means evaluating by fit to the strategy and the work, never by tidiness on a chart. Then two tests, before anybody moves. The human cost: a restructure severs the relationships people actually use to get work done and unsettles lives — your eleven-year planning pair and the fitter with the longer journey are that cost, and it is paid by them rather than by the organisation in the abstract. And whether this is structural theatre: whether the problem is structural at all. Yours were an unowned handover and a boundary between two contracts — process and accountability gaps that no chart can touch, which is exactly why they came back.

An organisational design is a tool you evaluate by its fit to the work and its effect on the people - not by how tidy it looks on a chart. Let us learn to evaluate it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you assess and evaluate an organisational design - seeing what each structure optimises and pays for, diagnosing which fits your strategy and work, and - before you move anyone - weighing the human cost and checking you are not staging reorganisation theatre.

1. No single best design - evaluate by fit, not tidiness

The first thing to un-learn is that there is a CORRECT or MODERN structure that every organisation should move towards. THERE IS NOT. Every structure is a set of TRADE-OFFS: it makes some things easy and other things hard, and the craft is not to find the perfect one but to evaluate which one's strengths match what your organisation most needs and whose weaknesses you can most afford. YOU EVALUATE A DESIGN BY FIT TO THE STRATEGY AND THE WORK, NEVER BY TIDINESS. An organisational design is a tool you judge by its fit to the work and its effect on the people, not by how tidy it looks on a chart.

2. The archetypes - what each buys and costs

A FUNCTIONAL structure groups people by expertise - all maintenance together, all operations together. It buys deep specialist skill, consistent standards, economies of scale, and a clear professional home; it costs SILOS, slow coordination across functions, and weak local ownership. A DIVISIONAL or AREA-BASED structure groups people by site, area or output - a self-contained team per plant. It buys strong local focus and ownership and fast decentralised decisions; it costs DUPLICATED SPECIALISTS and the risk of standards drifting apart. A MATRIX runs both at once - an area team plus functional specialist lines cutting across - so it buys dynamic sharing of scarce specialist skill and cross-cutting coordination; it costs DUAL REPORTING, contested priorities, and BLURRED ACCOUNTABILITY if run badly. Cutting across all three is the MECHANISTIC (centralised, formalised, high-accountability, consistent - suits safety-critical, policy-driven work) to ORGANIC (decentralised, flexible, responsive - suits changing conditions) dial. These are dials to set, not tribes to belong to.

3. Diagnose the fit - what must this organisation be good at?

You evaluate a design by reading what the organisation most needs to do well. The STRATEGY AND WHAT IT MUST OPTIMISE: consistency and safety across many sites pulls towards functional and central; local responsiveness across varied plants pulls towards divisional and area; scarce specialists who must be shared pull towards a matrix. The SCALE AND GEOGRAPHY: large and dispersed strains a purely central function that cannot be everywhere. The STABILITY of the environment: stable favours mechanistic, turbulent favours organic. And the decisive one: IS ACCOUNTABILITY CLEAR? Whatever the shape, can you name who is answerable for each asset and each outcome, or does the structure blur it? A STRUCTURE THAT LEAVES ACCOUNTABILITY CONTESTED HAS FAILED ITS MOST IMPORTANT TEST, however elegant it looks. Once the shape is chosen you map that accountability and assign the responsibilities - the applied work that comes after this evaluation, not instead of it.

4. Weigh the human cost - a reorganisation is never free

A structure is not a diagram; it is the CONTEXT OF REAL PEOPLE'S WORKING LIVES. So before you move a single person, you weigh what the change will cost them. A REORGANISATION IS NEVER FREE. IT COSTS PEOPLE THEIR STABILITY, THEIR WORKING RELATIONSHIPS, AND THE INFORMAL KNOWLEDGE THAT MAKES A TEAM WORK. THAT COST IS REAL, IT IS PAID BY THE PEOPLE AFFECTED, AND IT MUST BE WEIGHED HONESTLY AGAINST ANY EXPECTED BENEFIT. A CHANGE THAT BUYS LESS THAN IT COSTS IS A FAILURE OF LEADERSHIP. The human cost does not forbid reorganising - sometimes the fit is genuinely wrong and the change is worth it - but it must be named, weighed, and planned for honestly, never waved away as if a chart could be redrawn without anyone bleeding.

5. The reorg-theatre test - and the verdict

This is the trap that catches even well-meaning managers. REORGANISATION THEATRE IS CHANGING THE CHART TO AVOID FIXING THE REAL PROBLEM - a broken process, a lack of resources, a failure of leadership. IT IS A PERFORMANCE OF DECISIVENESS THAT TREATS PEOPLE'S WORKING LIVES AS PROPS. BEFORE YOU MOVE A SINGLE PERSON, YOU MUST BE ABLE TO PROVE THAT THE STRUCTURE ITSELF IS THE PROBLEM A NEW STRUCTURE CAN SOLVE. The tell is a reshuffle that leaves the actual fault untouched: if jobs fall through the cracks because the planning process misroutes them, no new box will catch them. And running underneath both tests is the final measure of any design: A GOOD STRUCTURE GIVES A PERSON A WHOLE ROLE AND CLEAR ACCOUNTABILITY. A BAD ONE LEAVES THEM CAUGHT BETWEEN MASTERS OR ANSWERABLE FOR THINGS THEY CANNOT CONTROL. THE FINAL TEST OF ANY DESIGN IS THIS: DOES IT GIVE PEOPLE A CLEAR LINE OF SIGHT BETWEEN THEIR WORK AND A MEANINGFUL OUTCOME, OR DOES IT CREATE CONFUSION?

T3: The Blueprint

A good structure fits the work and makes accountability clear - and you do not reorganise people to avoid fixing what a new chart cannot fix. An organisational design is a tool you evaluate by its fit to the work and its effect on the people, not by how tidy it looks on a chart. Five questions:

  • Is there a best structure? No - each optimises for something and pays elsewhere. Evaluate a design by its fit to the strategy and the work, not by whether it looks modern or tidy.
  • What are my choices? Functional, divisional, matrix - on a mechanistic-to-organic dial. Group by expertise, by area, or both at once; centralised and consistent, or decentralised and responsive.
  • How do I evaluate fit? Diagnose what the organisation must be good at. The strategy, the scale and geography, the stability of the environment - and whether accountability comes out clear.
  • What is the first test before I move anyone? The human cost. A reorganisation breaks teams, relationships and knowledge; it is never free and must buy more than it costs.
  • What is the second? The reorg-theatre test. Is the structure genuinely the problem, or am I redrawing the chart to avoid fixing a broken process, a shortfall, or a leadership failure a new chart will not touch?

The second test - reorganisation theatre. This is the trap that catches even well-meaning managers. It is a performance of decisiveness that treats people's working lives as props. The tell is a reshuffle that leaves the actual fault untouched: if jobs fall through the cracks because the planning process misroutes them, no new box will catch them; if the team is failing because it is short of people or short of leadership, a new chart only hides that for a quarter. Does it scale? This is how you evaluate the shape of any group that must get work done together - a department, a committee, a family sharing a home. Ask not "what is the neat structure?" but "what must we be good at, which shape's strengths fit that, and does it leave everyone with a clear, whole, accountable role?" - and before you rearrange people, weigh honestly what it costs them and prove the shape is really the problem. The structure is never the point; the work and the people are the point, and the structure is only good insofar as it serves them.

T4: Your Turn

Onalenna's utility has grown from one plant to four dispersed sites. The complaint is constant: jobs fall between the central maintenance team and the sites, standards vary, and no one is clearly answerable when something is missed. A reorganisation is on the table. Your job is to evaluate it honestly.

Two candidate designs are proposed:

  • Divisional / area: a self-contained maintenance team at each of the four sites.
  • Matrix: an area team at each site for daily work, plus a thin central line of shared specialists holding common standards.

Then this comes to light: the jobs that "fall between teams" are being lost by a single broken work-planning-and-control process - work requests are logged in one place, routed by hand, and regularly misdirected or dropped before anyone is assigned. It fails the same way no matter which team should have picked the job up.

  1. Evaluate the two designs against the fit criteria - what does each optimise, and what does each cost, for a growing multi-site utility?
  2. Check accountability under your preferred design - would each asset and outcome have one clearly answerable owner?
  3. Apply the two tests - the human cost, and reorganisation theatre - in light of the new information.
  4. Give your final recommendation: (a) reorganise to divisional; (b) reorganise to matrix; or (c) do not reorganise now - fix the process first - and justify why.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the evaluation: divisional would optimise local responsiveness and site ownership but duplicate the utility's scarce specialists across four sites and let standards drift - a real risk for safety-critical water work. Matrix would fit a growing multi-site utility better in principle: area teams give local responsiveness while a thin central specialist line keeps standards consistent and shares the rare skills - provided accountability is kept clear.

Second, accountability: a matrix only works if each asset still has one answerable owner; if the dual reporting is left vague, it blurs exactly the accountability the utility is already missing, and makes the problem worse. Any recommendation must state how the single answerable owner is preserved.

Third, the tests in light of the new information: this is the crux. The stated pain - jobs falling through the cracks - is caused by the broken planning-and-control process, not by the structure. No new chart catches a job that a broken routing process drops before anyone is assigned. Reorganising to "fix" that is reorganisation theatre: it would churn people through the real human cost of a reshuffle while the actual fault survives untouched, and in six months the same jobs would fall through the same gap under new titles.

Fourth, the recommendation: (c) do not reorganise now - fix the process first. Repair the work-planning-and-control process so jobs stop being dropped; that removes the stated pain at its source, at a fraction of the human cost. Then, and only then, evaluate whether a genuine structure-fit problem remains - the growth-driven strain on a single central team is a real question, and a modest matrix may still be the right long-term shape - but that is a decision to make once, deliberately, on its own merits, not a reflex to make the pain look addressed. Reorganise if and when the structure itself is proven to be the problem, never to avoid fixing what a new chart cannot fix.

T5: The Guardrails

A good structure fits the work and makes accountability clear - and you do not reorganise people to avoid fixing what a new chart cannot fix.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Believing there is one best structure. Every design trades strengths for weaknesses. Evaluate by fit to your strategy and work, not by what looks modern or tidy.

• Choosing by tidiness, not fit. A clean chart that does not fit the work is worse than a messy one that does. Ask what the organisation must be good at, then judge the shape against that.

• A matrix that blurs accountability. Dual reporting is powerful but dangerous. If each asset does not still have one clearly answerable owner, the matrix makes confusion worse, not better.

• Ignoring the human cost. A reorganisation breaks teams, relationships and knowledge, and is paid for by real people. It is never free; weigh it honestly, and a change that buys less than it costs is a failure of leadership.

• Reorganisation theatre. Redrawing the chart to look decisive while the real fault - a broken process, too few people, a leadership problem - survives untouched. Prove the structure is the problem before you move anyone.

• Duplicating scarce specialists. A divisional split can scatter your few experts across sites and drift the standards apart. Where specialists are scarce and standards matter, a shared central line usually earns its keep.

• A central function too slow for a dispersed operation. As sites multiply and spread, a purely central team cannot be everywhere. Growth in scale and geography is a genuine reason to revisit the shape.
