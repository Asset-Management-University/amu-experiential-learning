I'm being pressured to sign what I shouldn't - the tender, the inspection, the certificate

Movement 1 - The Simulation

Before you begin - a note of care. This unit deals with being pressured to sign off something you know is wrong, and the intimidation that can follow refusing. If this is part of your working life right now, it may feel heavy - go gently, and take a break whenever you need to. You do not have to face it alone, and you should not: raise it through a formal route rather than confronting anyone yourself - your professional body or registration council, an ethics or whistle-blower line, or a trusted senior outside the chain of pressure - and keep your own dated records, with copies held somewhere else. If you are ever in immediate danger, phone the police emergency number, 10111.

It is a Tuesday in late winter at the Karelbos Bulk Water depot, on the dry edge of a Karoo town. You are the technical signatory for the refurbishment of Reservoir 3 - a 12-megalitre concrete tank that supplies 40,000 people. Your signature is the thing that says, in writing, this is true, this is safe, this is fair. Three of them are wanted from you this week.

The room smells of instant coffee and warm dust off the heater. On the table in front of you are three documents.

The first is a tender evaluation sheet. Five contractors bid to reline the reservoir floor. Your scoring puts Madiba Linings top on price and past quality - R4.8 million, two similar jobs done clean. Second is Coral Civils at R5.6 million. But the scoresheet in front of you has been re-weighted overnight. Someone moved "local economic development" from 10 points to 30, and now Coral Civils sits top by 4 points. Your operations manager, Pieter, slides it across and says, quiet and friendly: "Just initial the new weighting, hey. It's policy now. Coral are good people. Madiba can bid next time." Coral's director, you happen to know, braais with the municipal manager.

The second is an inspection record. The relining trial patch - 12 square metres - failed its adhesion pull-test at 0.9 MPa against a spec minimum of 1.5 MPa. It should be torn out and redone. The site inspector, Lwazikazi, phoned you an hour ago, her voice tight: "They're telling me to write it up as a pass. They said the number was a 'calibration error'. I haven't signed anything. I don't know what to do." You can hear a generator droning behind her, and someone laughing.

The third is a completion certificate. The province's grant for this project expires Friday. If no certificate is signed by Friday, the municipality loses R6.2 million and the reservoir stays empty through summer. The certificate says the floor is complete and watertight. Right now, 40% of the floor is the failed patch material. The finance officer has already emailed you the certificate, pre-filled, with one line: "Sign so we don't lose the grant. We'll fix the rest after."

You are 34. You have a bond, two children, and a contract that the municipal manager renews each year. Last person who sat in your chair was "restructured" out after he flagged a payment. Part of you thinks: initial the one sheet, it's only a weighting; let the inspector make her own call; sign the certificate and chase the fix afterward - everyone does it, and nothing bad will happen today. Your hand is resting on the pen.

Three documents. One pen. Each one, on its own, is "just this once".

Where do you start? What is actually being asked of you here - and what do you need to know before any pen touches any page?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Before you read how it was resolved, work it yourself. Sit in that chair for a few minutes.

Make your best attempt first. Then reveal the worked solution.

To focus your thinking - and so you can judge your own answers from the facts already on the table, not wait for ours:

  1. Read each pen-stroke as a present-tense truth-claim. Write each one out as a sentence that says, right now: the tender initial = "this bid and this figure are true and fair"; the inspection record = "this item was inspected and is sound"; the certificate = "this work meets the standard and is complete." Now run one test across all three: In each case, am I being asked to attest to something I have NOT personally verified to be true? Check your own answer: if the answer is YES for all three, they collapse into one problem - you are being asked to vouch for an untruth in three costumes. You don't need our solution to confirm it: look for the common thread (signing for what isn't verified or true) and see whether it shows up in all three. If it does, you have proven "it's one problem, not three" to yourself.
  2. What is the difference between a decision that is yours to make and one that is not? Read the three again and sort them: which of these are you even allowed to decide alone - and who else's job is being quietly handed to you? (The facts you need are all in the scenario: who set the weighting, whose finding the test result is, what a completion certificate certifies.)
  3. Run a precedent test you can score yourself. Pick one of the three signatures. Write, word for word, the sentence the person who asked will use next time to ask again: "You signed it last time - what's different now?" Now write your honest reply to that sentence, as the version of you who already signed the first one. Check your own answer: is your reply weaker than the reply you could give today? (Today you can still say "I never sign unverified work." After signing once, you can't - because you just did.) If your future refusal comes out weaker, you have demonstrated the cost to yourself: the first signature destroys the clean line you could otherwise hold. The cost isn't abstract - it is the loss of your ability to refuse later, and you just tested it by trying to write next time's refusal.
  4. You feel alone in that room. Who is not in the room - and for each request, whose decision or whose job is being moved off them and onto your single pen? (List them from the scenario: a bid committee, the inspector, the funder, the municipal manager. Notice which ones are conveniently absent.)

Now set your own yardstick - before you read on. Thinking about your own answer (or a real pressure you face), write two short lines and keep them in front of you:

  • "I would change my mind if ___ ."

The worked solution below is not the verdict on your answer - it is one worked example to set beside the line you just drew for yourself.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is how the technical signatory worked through it - not as a hero, but as someone who refused to be moved one signature at a time.

Step 1 - Name what a signature actually is

You start by being honest about the pen. Each signature is not a favour, a formality or a feeling. It is a personal attestation: you are putting your name to a statement of fact and saying I checked this, and it is true and fair. The tender initial says this evaluation was fair. The inspection record says the work meets spec. The certificate says the work is complete.

Right now, none of those three statements is true. That is the whole problem, stripped of the pressure around it. You are not being asked to make a judgement call. You are being asked to write down something false and sign it.

That single reframe changes everything. Pieter framed it as policy. The finance officer framed it as cash flow. But the thing on the table is the same in all three: a request to certify a falsehood because the truth is inconvenient and expensive - and to carry the personal risk of doing so, alone.

Step 2 - Separate the decisions that are yours from the ones that are not

Next you sort the three, because they are not the same kind of problem.

The tender (the re-weighting). Changing the evaluation criteria after bids are opened, to lift a favoured bidder, is not a policy tweak - it is bid-steering. Whether the weighting "should" be 10 or 30 is a decision that must be made before tenders close, in the open, by the bid committee - not by you, overnight, with a pen. This is not your decision to ratify. Initialling it makes you the person who signed off the irregularity.

The inspection (the "calibration error"). The 0.9 MPa result is Lwazikazi's finding to record, not yours to overwrite. A real calibration concern is settled by re-testing with a verified gauge, witnessed, and documented - not by retyping a number you prefer. Pressuring her to falsify it pulls her into the same trap.

The certificate (the grant deadline). This is partly yours - but you can only certify what is true. 40% of the floor failed. A completion certificate over failed work is a false certificate, deadline or no deadline.

So: one of these you cannot decide alone (the tender), one is not yours to overwrite (the inspection), and one you can only sign honestly (the certificate). In none of the three does "just sign it" survive contact with what the document actually means.

Step 3 - See the ratchet, and set a line before you act

Before you do anything, you draw a line in advance, so you'll know whether you held it: I will have done right by this only if, a week from now, my name sits beside nothing that wasn't true when I signed it - and the three decisions are sitting where they belong, not on my pen. I will know I've failed if I find myself the only person carrying any of these.

Then you look at the word "after". "Fix the rest after." "Bid next time." Each request comes with a promise that this is the only one.

It never is. Once you have initialled a steered tender, you cannot credibly object to the bad work that contract produces - you certified the contractor. Once you have signed a false inspection pass, the false completion certificate becomes easy - it just agrees with the record you already faked. Once your name is on the certificate, the leverage flips onto you: now you have something to hide, and the next "favour" is no longer a request, it is a threat dressed as a reminder.

This is how good people are ground down - not by one big crime, but by a ratchet of small, deniable, "just this once" signatures, each one making the next harder to refuse. The first signature is the expensive one. After that, you are theirs.

Step 4 - Make the real cost visible to yourself

You don't argue the pressure in your head, where it always wins. You make the cost a thing you can see, the way you'd add up a month of spending you'd rather not look at. You take a sheet and write, in plain numbers, what each signature actually buys and what it actually costs - the reckoning Pieter and the finance officer are hoping you'll skip:

  • The certificate. It "saves" R6.2 million on Friday. But 40% of a 12-megalitre floor is failed material. The reckoning isn't the grant - it's the 40,000 people on the other end of a floor that won't hold water, and the fact that when it leaks next summer, the investigation reads one name: yours. You write that down. The grant is a number this week; the failure is a number that compounds for years, and it is charged to you.
  • The tender. Initialling it "costs nothing today". But you write out the next line of the story: a steered contract, work you can no longer credibly challenge, and your initials on the page that started it.
  • The inspection. Overwriting 0.9 MPa "helps everyone move on". You write the real cost: a structural lie in the record, and a colleague, Lwazikazi, pulled under with you.

Seeing it on paper does to the pressure what daylight does - it stops being a vague dread and becomes a plain sum, and the plain sum is not close. The signature you give under pressure is the one you defend, alone, for years.

Step 5 - Rehearse what you'll say, then reach the people who belong in the room

You feel alone in that room. You are not supposed to be. The point of governance is that no single person should carry this on their own salary and their own conscience. There are people not in the room who belong in it - and the honest next step is not to fight this single-handed, but to say the true thing calmly and route each decision to where it belongs.

So, before you open your mouth or your inbox, you rehearse the lines - out loud if you can - so that when Pieter is standing over you, you are not improvising under pressure:

  • To Pieter, on the tender: "I can't initial a weighting that was changed after bids opened - that's a committee decision, not mine. I'm tabling it for them to rule on." (Calm. No accusation. The decision goes up and out.)
  • Backing Lwazikazi, in writing: "The trial patch recorded 0.9 MPa against a 1.5 MPa minimum. If a calibration error is suspected, we re-test with a verified gauge under witness. Until then the result stands as recorded." You put your name beside the truth, not against it, so she isn't alone either.
  • To the finance officer, on the certificate: "I can't certify a floor that's 40% failed. I'll certify the 60% that passed, log the rest as outstanding, and we ask the province today, in writing, for a short extension or a partial drawdown." The deadline is real; the answer is to tell the funder the truth early, not to lie to them.

Then you reach the trained people whose job this actually is. You decline to initial the scoresheet and email the bid committee chair and the supply-chain office that the criteria appear to have changed after bid opening and must be tabled before any award. Where the pressure shades toward threat - your contract, your safety - you do not investigate it alone: you take it to a professional body, an ethics or whistle-blower line, or your engineering registration council, the people trained to handle exactly this. And you write it all down - a dated file note of every request, every refusal, every email - because a documented trail is the protection of the person who said no.

Step 6 - Hold the line, and name the cost honestly

Now you check your work against the line you drew in Step 3. Did you find yourself carrying any of the three alone? No - each one is now sitting where it belongs: the tender with the committee, the test with the verified re-gauge, the certificate honest at 60% with the funder told the truth, and the danger with the people trained to hold it. The line holds. You don't change course; you confirm it.

What you have just practised is professional and procurement integrity, protected by governance controls and escalation routes - and grounded in personal ethical resolve. Three layers, working together:

  • Controls that separate duties so no one person both does the work and signs it off (the inspector records, you verify, the committee awards - by design, not one pen).
  • Escalation routes so that when you are pressured, the decision goes up and out to a body, not down and onto you.
  • Personal ethical grounding - knowing, before the pressure arrives, that your signature is your name, and your name is not for sale.

Be honest about the trade-off, because this is not a story where virtue is free. There may be a real cost. The grant might still be reduced. Pieter may freeze you out. Your contract is up for renewal, and the last person who flagged something is gone. AMU will not pretend otherwise.

But weigh the other cost - the one you put on paper in Step 4. The failed floor is structural - a false certificate does not make the reservoir hold water; it just moves the failure downstream onto 40,000 people and onto you, because your name is the one on it. When the floor leaks next summer, the investigation will not ask Pieter why he asked. It will ask you why you signed. The integrity move costs something today. The signature costs more, later, and forever. You decline all three, in writing, and you send the decisions to where they belong. You put the pen down.

A note on personal pressure. If refusing a signature ever exposes you to threats, intimidation or risk to your safety or livelihood, you are not expected to face that alone. Document what is happening, keep copies off-site, and use a formal route - a professional body, an ethics or whistle-blower line, your engineering registration council, or a trusted senior outside the chain of pressure. Do not investigate corruption single-handedly or confront anyone where you might be at risk. Raising it through a proper channel is both safer and stronger than carrying it by yourself.

Do this yourself. When someone leans on you to sign, approve, tick or confirm something, do not argue the pressure - test the request. Put each thing you are asked to sign on its own row and fill the grid in honestly before any pen moves. The headers are the questions to ask yourself:

What I'm asked to signWhat it actually claims is trueIs that true right now? (Y/N)Whose decision is this really? (mine alone / a committee / a standard / a second checker)Who is being moved off the hook onto me?The "after" promise attached to itWhere this goes if I decline (the route - name it)

The bare recipe: (1) Read the signature as a sentence - write out the claim it makes. (2) Check if the claim is true today - not "will be", is. (3) Sort the decision - is it even yours to make, or is someone handing you a call that belongs elsewhere? (4) Spot the "after" - every "just this once" is the first click of a ratchet. (5) Name the route - decline in writing and send the decision up and out, to a body, not back onto yourself. If any row reads "claims something untrue" + "not really my call" + "no route I can name", that row is the warning, and the answer is: do not sign - escalate.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You've watched it worked in the clean, dramatic case - three documents, big numbers, 40,000 people. Now let's take one guided step together on a version that doesn't look dramatic at all, because that is where this pattern usually hides. I'll walk beside you, ask a few questions, and pause for you to think before each reveal.

Here is the new situation. You run a small two-person plumbing and maintenance outfit. You finished a job for a good, friendly client - a guesthouse owner you've worked with for years. The work is mostly done, but you still have to come back next week to fit two taps you're waiting on from the supplier. This morning she sends you a cheerful text:

"Hi! To get your payment through our system before month-end I just need the job-completion slip dated today. Can you pop your name on it and send it back? You'll finish the taps next week, no stress - you know I'm good for it. Saves us both the admin!"

It's a text, not a stamped certificate. It's your own R18,000, not millions. She's lovely. And honestly, you will finish next week. Easy, right?

First, a question - don't skip it. Look at the slip she wants dated today. Read it as a sentence, the way you learned in Step 1. What does a "job-completion slip" actually claim is true? And is that claim true today?

(Pause. Say it out loud before you read on.)

Show me ▸

It claims "this job is complete" - and it isn't. Two taps are missing. So the warm, small, friendly text is asking for the exact same thing Pieter and the finance officer asked for: your name on a statement that isn't true yet. The surface looks completely different - a text instead of a document, your cash instead of a public grant, a friend instead of a manager - but the deep structure is identical: a pressured false attestation.

Next question. Notice how easy this one feels compared with Reservoir 3. Why is that - and does "easy" make it safer?

(Pause.)

Show me ▸

It feels easy because the cost of saying yes is hidden and the cost of saying no is right in front of you (an awkward reply to someone you like, a delayed R18,000). That's the same trap as the certificate, just quieter. The harm is still deferred - onto the day the guesthouse's own auditor, or insurer, or next buyer asks "was this job actually finished on this date?" and the paper says yes when it wasn't. Your name is on that paper.

Now the ratchet - where have we seen this before? She says "you'll finish the taps next week." That's the word "after" again, in a friendly voice. Ask yourself: once you've signed "complete" for work that isn't, what happens if the taps slip, or the supplier lets you down, or she asks for one more early-dated slip next month?

(Pause.)

Show me ▸

You've already shown you'll date a slip ahead of the work. The next ask is easier for her to make and harder for you to refuse - you did it last time. And if it ever goes wrong, you are the one who certified a false completion, not her. The leverage has quietly moved onto you. That's the ratchet, scaled down to a guesthouse and a text - and it bites just the same.

Last one - the route. Reservoir 3 had a bid committee and an inspector to escalate to. This little job seems to have no committee, no whistle-blower line, nothing. So is there any honest move here at all?

(Pause.)

Show me ▸

There is - and it's the same move, scaled down: don't sign what's false; offer what's true. You reply, just as warmly: "Happy to get your payment moving! I'll send a slip today for the 90% that's done and finished, marked 'taps outstanding, fitting [date]', and a final completion slip the day they're in. That keeps your records clean for your accountant too." You decline the false claim, you put the true one in writing, and you route it through the honest paper trail - even when the only "body" is her own bookkeeping. No committee needed; the principle scales all the way down.

The deep-structure lesson: the pattern doesn't need a boardroom, a big number, or a villain to be the same pattern. Any time someone you'd rather not disappoint asks you to put your name to something that isn't true yet - however small, however friendly, however good for cash flow - it is a pressured false attestation, it carries a ratchet, and the answer is the same: sign only what is true, in writing, and let the date catch up to the work, never the other way round.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The reservoir is not the point. The ratchet is the point - the pressure to put your name to something untrue, one small "just this once" at a time, while you carry the risk alone. That pattern lives in almost every working life. Find it in yours.

Work these with your AI facilitator. It will build the same problem-type inside your world, then coach you through it.

  1. Find your pen. What do you sign, approve, log, confirm or "tick off" - a timesheet, a sign-in sheet, a test result, an invoice, a safety check, a grade, a code review, an expense claim? Name the one where your approval says "I checked this and it's true." That is your signature.
  2. Find the "just this once". Recall a time you were asked to approve something not quite right "to help out", "because of the deadline", or "because everyone does it". What was the first small one - the cheap-looking one that would have made the next harder to refuse?
  3. Sort the decision. Take a real pressure you face. Is it actually yours to decide - or is someone quietly handing you a decision that belongs to a committee, a manager, a standard, or a second checker? Who is not in the room who should be?
  4. Map your route. In your organisation, where does it go when you can't approve something? Name the actual person, body, or line you would escalate to. If you don't know - that gap is itself the finding, and worth raising before you ever need it.
  5. Price both sides honestly. For one real example: what does saying no cost you (today, in this relationship, this deadline)? What does saying yes cost you (later, when it's your name on the failure)? Write both down. Decide which cost you can live with.
  6. (If it touches your safety.) If any pressure you face shades into intimidation or threat, do not work it alone. Identify one trusted person or formal channel outside the chain of pressure you could raise it with - and stop there. The goal of this exercise is clarity and a safe next step, never confrontation.

The transfer is honest: this is the same problem-type - being pressured to certify what isn't true, one signature at a time - moved out of a water utility and into your own world. The documents change. The ratchet does not.
