Our kit sits idle most of the year, so hiring it out sounds like free money - how do I find out whether it actually is?

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have had what feels like a genuinely clever idea.

The works owns three mobile water-quality testing rigs. They sit in the yard most of the year. The small utilities down the valley are required to test their water regularly and cannot justify buying rigs of their own for the handful of days they would use them. So: hire them ours.

It sounds like free money. The rigs are bought and paid for. They are doing nothing. Every day one of them is out earning is a day of pure gain - that is the shape of the thought, and it is a very comfortable shape.

But something about it will not settle, and you cannot say what. You have run a plant for years. You know that a rig standing in a yard is not actually free - somebody services it, somebody insures it, and it is worth less every year whether it moves or not. You just have no idea whether those things add up to a little or a lot, or how to weigh them against hire fees you have not yet earned.

And the board meets in a fortnight, where you will either propose this or not. "It feels like free money" is not a sentence you can say in that room, and "I have a bad feeling about it" is not one either.

Our kit is standing idle and hiring it out sounds like free money. How do I find out whether it actually is, before I propose it?

Before you read on, decide. Three rigs standing in a yard, neighbours who need testing done, and a board meeting in two weeks. Write down how you would work out whether this is a good idea - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does with an idle asset

• "The rigs are already paid for, so anything they earn is profit." The thought the whole idea rests on, and it is the one that has to be tested rather than assumed. Something bought and paid for still costs money every year to keep - and that cost arrives with no invoice, which is exactly why it gets left out.

• "Work out a fair day-rate by seeing what others charge." Useful and it answers half a question. A rate that matches the market is still a loss if the rig is only hired a few days a year, and the market rate cannot tell you how often yours will go out.

• "Ask the neighbours whether they would be interested." Genuinely the right instinct and it needs a harder question than interest. "Would you use this?" gets a yes from everyone; "how many days last year, and what did you pay?" gets a number.

• "Try it for a year and see." Sounds pragmatic and it is the most expensive way to find out, because a year of hiring out kit that loses money looks exactly like a year of hiring out kit that makes money until somebody does the sum at the end of it.

• "Build the full business case - customers, model, financials." Thorough and it is a fortnight of work to answer a question that turns on one number, and it can easily produce a beautiful document with that number missing.

• What the first five have in common is that none of them prices the yard. An idle asset does not sit there neutrally - it loses value, it gets serviced, it is insured, and all of that runs whether or not anybody hires it. Once you know what a year of simply owning the thing costs, one division tells you how many days a year it must be hired before it earns anything at all - and then you can put that number next to what demand honestly is, and the answer stops being a feeling.

T2: The Tool in Action

Five moves on the rig-hire idea, before a single rig is ever hired out.

1. What does this asset cost you every year, whether or not it earns a cent?

Onalenna prices one testing rig honestly. It cost R1.08 million and will last about six years, so it loses R180,000 of value a year. Servicing, calibration and repairs come to R60,000 a year whether it works or not. That is R240,000 a year to OWN this rig - before anybody has hired it, and whether or not anybody ever does. The rigs have been sitting in the yard 'costing nothing' for three years, and that has never been true.

2. Divide by the day-rate: how many days a year must it be hired just to break even?

At a day-rate of R4,000: R240,000 divided by R4,000 = 60 hire-days a year. Every rig must be out earning for sixty days - a day in six of the working year - before the venture makes anything at all. Onalenna writes 60 at the top of a clean page, because until now the idea had no number attached to it and could not be argued with.

3. Now the honest demand - and get it from somebody other than yourself

Onalenna rings the three neighbouring utilities rather than estimating for them. Each is required to test roughly monthly and would want a rig for three or four days at a time - but two of them already share a contractor, so realistically it is about 40 days a year across all three. She writes 40 next to her 60, and does not adjust either one to be closer to the other.

4. Name who carries the asset risk - because it is probably you

The rigs stay Corathyl's. If a customer's operator damages a probe, Corathyl repairs it. If nobody hires for two months, Corathyl carries R40,000 of ownership cost in that time regardless. If the resale market softens, that is Corathyl's loss. The customer carries none of it - they simply stop calling. Onalenna writes it plainly: the utilities get the use, we keep every one of the risks, and our R4,000 day-rate was priced as if that were free.

5. The verdict, and the number that decides it

Sixty days needed, forty days real. At R4,000 a day that is R160,000 of revenue against R240,000 of cost: an R80,000 LOSS per rig per year, and Corathyl owns three of them. VERDICT: not viable as conceived - and the number says exactly where it breaks, so this is a redesign rather than a no-go. Three routes: charge more (R6,000 a day makes forty days break even), find more customers (twenty more hire-days), or share the ownership so the risk and the cost are not all Corathyl's. What Onalenna will not do is adjust the forty. The whole value of this page is that it happened before a single rig was ever hired out.

Watch what the sum did to the idea. Each rig costs R240,000 a year simply to exist - R180,000 of value lost, R60,000 of servicing - so at R4,000 a day it must be hired 60 days a year to break even, and honest demand is 40. That is R160,000 of revenue against R240,000 of cost: an R80,000 loss per rig, per year, on three rigs. The "free money" idea takes money out of Corathyl every year it runs. And notice the two things Onalenna does that make the answer trustworthy. She gets the 40 from the neighbours rather than estimating it herself, because the one number capable of rescuing the idea is exactly the number she is tempted to lift. And when the answer comes out wrong she does not adjust the 40 - she names the three things that could change instead, so the board gets a redesign with a price on each route rather than an idea that quietly died. The rigs were never free. They have been costing R720,000 a year across the three of them for as long as they have stood in that yard, and nobody had ever written it down.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the first one is the whole insight.

  • 1. Price a year of simply owning it - value lost, servicing, insurance, storage, licensing, the person who looks after it. This runs whether it earns or not.
  • 2. Divide by the day-rate to get the hire-days it must find each year to break even. Days, not a percentage.
  • 3. Get the honest demand from somebody other than you - counted, or asked of the actual customers.
  • 4. Name who carries the wear, the breakdowns, the idle days and the falling resale value. It is usually the owner.
  • 5. Put the two day-counts side by side and say which of three things would have to change.

The number in move 2 is called utilisation - how much of the time an asset is actually earning - and the reason it decides asset ventures is a fact about ownership rather than about business: the cost of owning something is fixed and its earnings are not. The yearly cost arrives whether the rig moves or stands; the revenue arrives only on days somebody hires it. So the whole venture rests on a count of days, and the count is knowable in advance. That is why this is worth doing on paper: nearly every "we should rent that out" idea can be settled in an afternoon, and the ones that cannot are the ones worth building a full case for.

Two things are worth naming honestly. The value lost each year is the biggest number and the easiest to forget, because the money went out years ago and nothing arrives monthly to remind you - which is precisely why an idle asset feels free. And the risks stay with the owner: when somebody else uses your machine, the wear, the breakdown, the empty month and the softening second-hand market are all still yours. Their usage is your maintenance. If the day-rate was not priced with that in it, it was priced for a different business.

Three limits. This tests whether the venture pays, not whether anyone wants it - whether there is a real customer with a real problem, and whether your offer fits it, is the prior question, and if the answer is no then no day-rate saves you. It is a first-cut, not a financial model: it ignores tax, financing and the timing of the money, all of which matter once the idea survives this page. And a break-even that clears comfortably is not permission - it is permission to do the proper work.

Two neighbouring questions have their own units. Laying out a whole venture on one page - who it is for, what problem it solves, how it reaches them, how it gets paid and what it costs - is bp-lean-canvas, in nine plain-word boxes, and it is where the customer half of this belongs. And whether the asset is worth keeping at all, hired out or not, is the question t-0277 and the wider asset-lifecycle units take up.

T4: Your Turn

Do each task before opening its reveal, with a calculator.

Task 1 - The crane. The works owns a specialist mobile crane used only for its own occasional big lifts. Someone proposes hiring it out between jobs. It cost R2.4 million and will last about 8 years; servicing and repairs run R90,000 a year; insurance and storage another R30,000. The proposed day-rate is R9,500. How many days a year must it be hired to break even?

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

Value lost each year: R2,400,000 ÷ 8 = R300,000. Add R90,000 servicing and R30,000 insurance and storage: R420,000 a year to own. Break-even: R420,000 ÷ R9,500 = about 45 days a year. Good answers include the R300,000 of value lost - leaving it out gives 13 days and a completely different decision, and it is the single most common way this sum goes wrong.

Task 2 - The honest demand. Local contractors tell you they would hire the crane about 30 days a year between them. What is the verdict, in rands - and what are the three things that could change?

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

30 days x R9,500 = R285,000 of revenue against R420,000 of cost: a R135,000 loss a year. The three routes: raise the rate (R420,000 ÷ 30 days = R14,000 a day - ask whether contractors would pay that, which they may not); find more days (45 needed against 30 found, so half as many again); or change who owns it (share it with the contractors, or hire one in for your own big lifts instead of owning one). Good answers give the verdict as a number and name routes rather than concluding "no".

Task 3 - The one that works, and the trap inside it. A tipper truck cost R900,000 and will last 5 years; maintenance is R70,000 a year. At R3,200 a day it would be hired an honest 110 days. Is it viable - and what is the first thing you check before saying yes?

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

Cost to own: R180,000 + R70,000 = R250,000. Break-even: R250,000 ÷ R3,200 = about 78 days. Honest demand is 110, so it clears break-even by 32 days and earns about R102,000 a year. Viable - and the first thing to check is who carries the risk on those 110 days: a tipper on somebody else's site wears faster than one on yours, and the R70,000 maintenance figure was measured on your own gentle usage. Good answers notice that a comfortable margin makes the risk question more important, not less, because it is the assumption most likely to be quietly wrong.

Now take a real "we should hire that out" idea of your own - a vehicle, a machine, a hall, a workshop, a piece of land - and run the five moves.

1. What does a year of owning it cost with nobody hiring it? Price divided by years of life, plus servicing, insurance, storage, licence, and whoever looks after it.

2. Divide by your day-rate. Write the number of days at the top of the page.

3. Go and get the honest demand from the people who would hire it - how many days last year, and what did they pay? If the only source is you, mark the figure as yours and treat the answer with suspicion.

4. List the bad days - it breaks mid-hire, nobody calls for two months, the resale market softens - and write who carries each one.

5. Put the two day-counts side by side. Then say which of the three would have to change: the rate, the demand, or who owns it.

T5: The Guardrails

Asset-hire ideas go wrong in a few very consistent ways, and most are caught by this one page if you fill it honestly.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• "It is already paid for, so anything it earns is profit." The idea this whole method exists to test. What is already paid for still loses value, still gets serviced, still gets insured - and none of that sends you an invoice, which is why it feels like nothing.

• The value lost each year left out of the sum. It is usually the biggest line. Leave it out of the crane and break-even reads 13 days instead of 45, and you will confidently propose a loss.

• The demand figure supplied by the person who wants the answer. The one number that can rescue any of these ideas is the days, which is exactly why it must come from the customers or from something counted. If it is yours, say so on the page.

• The demand adjusted after the answer comes out wrong. The temptation arrives the moment the loss appears, and it feels like optimism rather than dishonesty. Change the rate, the customer base or the ownership - never the estimate.

• Utilisation quoted as a percentage. "Sixty per cent utilisation" is something you can talk yourself into; "sixty days out of the yard, every year" is something you have to go and find.

• The asset risk assumed to travel with the asset. It does not. Their usage is your maintenance, their quiet month is your empty yard, their rough handling is your resale value. If the rate was not priced with that in it, it was priced for a different business.

• A break-even that clears, taken as a green light. Clearing it means the idea is worth proper work - the customer question, the contract, the tax and the financing. It does not mean go.

• The sum done only for the good version. Run it once at your honest demand and once at two-thirds of it. If the venture only works at the number you hope for, you have priced a hope.

Onalenna's rig-hire idea sounded like free money and was quietly an R80,000 loss per rig per year, and the only reason she knows is that she priced the yard before she proposed the venture. You can do the same in an afternoon: what a year of owning it costs with nobody hiring it, divided by your day-rate, gives the days it must earn - then put that beside what demand honestly is, and name who carries the bad days. An idle asset is not free. It has been costing you all along, quietly, and the only question is whether hiring it out costs less than leaving it standing. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, bring one "we should rent that out" idea; the day-count is usually where the truth is.
