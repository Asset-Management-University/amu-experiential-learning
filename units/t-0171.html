Thirty-four good ideas and six slots

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and the improvement list has thirty-four items on it.

Every single one is a good idea. Several were proposed by people you respect. Two came from your director. Not one of them is stupid, and you could defend any of them in a meeting.

You have two improvement leads.

Last year the department started eleven projects and finished three - and the three that finished were the ones whose sponsors pushed hardest, not the ones that mattered most. You can feel precisely what happens if you do the reasonable-looking thing and try to sequence all thirty-four: everything will be in progress, nothing will be delivered, and the people doing the work will spend a year being busy, interrupted and quietly demoralised.

So you have to say no, about twenty-eight times.

And you cannot. Not because you lack the nerve - because every refusal you could make right now sounds like a preference. Your opinion against theirs. You have no way to say no that anybody, including you, would find legitimate.

I am drowning in good ideas with capacity for six, and every "no" I can give sounds like my personal opinion. How do I decide what we actually do this year - in a way that is visible, defensible, and not about who pushed hardest?

Before you read on, decide. Thirty-four proposals. Capacity for about six. Every one of the thirty-four is genuinely good, put up by somebody competent who believes in it, and you could defend any of them on its merits. Right now the organisation has accepted most of them: everything is half-done, everybody is interrupted, and people are quietly demoralised. So you must say no about twenty-eight times — and you cannot. Not for want of nerve: every refusal available to you sounds like a preference, your opinion against theirs, and you would not find it legitimate either. Write down how you would decide what actually gets done — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what makes a refusal legitimate

• “Score them all and take the top six.” Closer than most, and a score you invent is your preference with arithmetic on it. The first person to lose will ask where the weightings came from, and they will be right to.

• “Do them all, just more slowly.” This is the current state described as a plan. Thirty-four things running at a fraction of the attention each needs is how everything came to be half-done.

• “Let the executive decide — take it up the line.” Moves the same illegitimate choice to somebody with more authority and less context, and you will get back a list shaped by who has the executive's ear.

• “Fund the ones with the best business cases.” Rewards the proposers who write well and have time to write, which is not the same population as the proposals that matter most. It is also still one criterion you chose.

• “Rotate — do six this year, six next.” Feels fair and defers rather than decides. It also guarantees that nothing coherent is achieved in any year, because the six are unrelated to each other.

• What you need is a system rather than a preference, and its core move is uncomfortable: commit to a very small number of multi-year intentions — three to five — knowing that choosing three means declining eleven others that are also genuinely good. That is not modest ambition, it is arithmetic about attention: an organisation told that twelve things are critical has been told nothing, and will revert to doing whatever is loudest, which is exactly where you are. Then everything nearer in time is derived rather than proposed — each year's objectives must trace up to one of those few, and the trace is made visible rather than asserted, so anything orphaned is exposed as belonging to nobody's stated intent. That is what converts your refusal from an opinion into an observation: the answer becomes this does not serve what we said we were doing — arguable on the record, not personally. One more thing, or you will get compliance instead of commitment: negotiate the few up and down the organisation before fixing them, so the people who must deliver them have genuinely shaped them.

You need a system rather than a preference. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the policy deployment matrix - what we intend, what that means this year, and which six things we will actually do.

1. Choose three to five breakthrough objectives (the programme is won here)

The SOUTH quadrant of the X-Matrix holds the BREAKTHROUGH OBJECTIVES: the changes you intend over the next THREE TO FIVE YEARS. And the discipline is the whole method - LIMIT THEM TO THREE TO FIVE. Too many is not ambition, it is the classic failure mode: IF YOU HAVE TWELVE BREAKTHROUGH PRIORITIES, YOU HAVE ZERO, because a department told everything is critical will simply revert to doing whatever is loudest. This box is where the programme is won or lost, and it is uncomfortable precisely because choosing three means declining to choose eleven others that are also good. At Corathyl: maintenance planned from evidence rather than habit; unplanned downtime materially reduced; and an asset information base that can be trusted for decisions. Three. Not because there is nothing else worth doing, but because three is what an organisation can actually change at once.

2. Derive this year's objectives from them (nothing orphaned)

The WEST quadrant holds THIS YEAR'S OBJECTIVES - the annual goals that make the long-term breakthroughs attainable. The rule that makes them useful: EVERY ANNUAL OBJECTIVE MUST TRACE TO A BREAKTHROUGH OBJECTIVE, and the correlation is marked on the matrix so the trace is visible rather than asserted. If an annual objective traces to nothing, it is not a strategic objective at all - it is work somebody wants done, which may be perfectly reasonable and does not belong in the strategy. This is the year the breakthrough gets a fraction closer, expressed as something achievable in twelve months: this year, every critical asset has a maintenance plan derived from its failure modes; this year, the asset register passes a data-quality threshold. The EAST quadrant then carries the TARGETS TO IMPROVE - the measures that will show it happened - and the responsibilities, so the matrix records who as well as what.

3. Test every candidate for genuine correlation (and be honest about the answer)

The NORTH quadrant holds the CANDIDATE INITIATIVES, and here the department's 34 ideas meet the test: does this genuinely MOVE an annual objective - STRONGLY, WEAKLY, or NOT AT ALL? Mark the correlation honestly in the corner dots, and resist the strong pull to correlate everything with everything, because a matrix where every dot is filled carries no information and has quietly restored the situation it was built to fix. At Corathyl the honest answer is 8 STRONG, 11 WEAK, 15 NONE - and 8 + 11 + 15 = 34. Read that result properly: FIFTEEN GOOD IDEAS CORRELATE WITH NOTHING. They are not bad ideas and they are not being judged; they simply do not serve anything this organisation has committed to this year, which is a statement about the strategy as much as about them.

4. Allocate against REAL capacity, and name an owner for each

Now the arithmetic that most programmes refuse to do. CAPACITY: 2 improvement leads x 3 DMAIC projects each a year = 6 SLOTS. Demand: 34 ideas. That is 5.7 ideas for every slot. Of the 8 strongly correlated candidates, 6 ARE SELECTED and 2 ARE DEFERRED WITH A STATED REASON AND A DATE - a deferral without both is not a decision, it is a queue that quietly becomes a graveyard. Each selected initiative becomes a DMAIC PROJECT with a NAMED OWNER and a TARGET TO IMPROVE on the east axis, so the matrix carries RESPONSIBILITY and not merely intention. And note what allocating beyond capacity actually costs: last year this department started ELEVEN projects and finished THREE - and the three that finished were the ones whose sponsors pushed hardest rather than the ones that mattered most, which is what happens to every organisation that treats capacity as negotiable.

5. Catchball - the step that makes it real, not a top-down list

CATCHBALL is structured TWO-WAY communication - open dialogue and consensus-building between managers and the people who do the work - and it is part of the method, NOT a courtesy. Managers propose; the people who will deliver respond on feasibility and on what the matrix has missed; the objectives are then ADJUSTED. A matrix issued downward is an instruction list, and the method's power comes precisely from the people closest to the work having genuinely shaped it - which also means a catchball that changes nothing is worse than none, because it teaches everybody that being asked is theatre. Then handle the unselected ideas with the respect they are owed, since behind each one is a person who proposed it. The response is not 'rejected' but CATEGORISED: this is a valid improvement idea; based on the objectives committed to this year it does not align with the programme's focus, so it is not a candidate for a formal DMAIC project RIGHT NOW; and one of three paths follows - pursue it as a local 'just do it' improvement, add it to the continuous improvement register for future consideration, or hold it and re-evaluate it against next year's annual objectives.

Watch what box 3 does. Fifteen good ideas correlate with nothing at all - and that is not a verdict on the ideas, it is information about the strategy. Refusing them is now a traceable consequence of a commitment rather than an opinion about their merit. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

An improvement programme fails not from a lack of good ideas but from an inability to refuse them. The X-Matrix is a tool for strategic refusal. Five questions:

  • What are we changing over three to five years? The breakthrough objectives (south), limited to three to five. If you have twelve breakthrough priorities, you have zero.
  • What must be true this year? The annual objectives (west), each traceable to a breakthrough objective - with the targets to improve and the responsibilities on the east axis.
  • Which candidates genuinely serve them? The initiatives (north), each tested for correlation - strong, weak or none - and marked in the corner dots. A matrix where every dot is filled carries no information.
  • How many can we actually do? Allocate against real capacity, defer the rest with a reason and a date, and give every selected initiative a named owner and a measure so the matrix carries responsibility, not intention.
  • Who shaped it? Catchball - structured two-way dialogue, before the matrix is fixed.

On catchball, which is not a consultation ritual. Managers propose; the people who will do the work respond on feasibility and on what the matrix has missed; the objectives are then adjusted. That last clause is the whole thing. A matrix issued downward is an instruction list, and a catchball that changes nothing is worse than no catchball at all, because it teaches an organisation that being asked is theatre. The method's power comes from the people closest to the work having genuinely shaped what they are being asked to deliver.

And on the twenty-eight ideas you do not select - behind each one is a person who proposed it. The response is not "rejected"; it is categorised. This is a valid improvement idea. Based on the strategic objectives we have committed to for this year, it does not align with the programme's focus, so it is not a candidate for a formal DMAIC project right now. One of three paths follows: pursue it as a local "just do it" improvement; add it to the continuous improvement register for future consideration; or hold it and re-evaluate it against next year's annual objectives. That is dignity, clarity and a way forward - and it is also the only version of "no" that people keep bringing you ideas after.

One honest boundary. You are building the matrix and allocating this year's improvement capacity. You are not running the DMAIC projects it selects - that is its own discipline - and you are not writing the transformation charter. The charter is the constitutional document: it answers why we must transform and grants the authority for a multi-year programme. This matrix answers a narrower and more immediate question: of everything we could do, what must we do this year to serve that ambition?

Does it scale? A hospital with one theatre and forty people needing surgery does not solve the problem by starting all forty operations. It triages against stated criteria, publishes them, and does the six it can complete properly - and the reason that is bearable to the thirty-four who wait is that the criteria are visible and were not invented for them.

T4: Your Turn

A rail maintenance depot has agreed three breakthrough objectives for the next four years:

  1. B1: Fleet availability sustained above 92 per cent.
  2. B2: Maintenance cost per kilometre reduced by a quarter.
  3. B3: Zero safety incidents arising from maintenance work.

This year's objectives have been drafted as: A1 cut unplanned removals of traction motors by half; A2 raise first-time-fix rate on brake defects; A3 eliminate working-at-height incidents in the depot.

Eight candidate projects are on the table. The depot has one improvement lead, who can run three DMAIC projects a year.

CandidateProposed by
P1 - Root cause analysis of traction motor bearing failuresReliability engineer
P2 - Redesign the brake overhaul work instructionDepot supervisor
P3 - Install fixed access platforms over the bogie pitsSafety representative
P4 - Replace the depot's fuel management systemDepot manager
P5 - Rationalise the brake component spares catalogueStoreman
P6 - Introduce a staff wellness programmeRegional director
P7 - Improve traction motor cleaning before reinstallationArtisan
P8 - Refresh the depot's visual identity and signageRegional director
  1. Trace the annuals. Confirm each annual objective traces to a breakthrough objective, and name which.
  2. Correlate. For each of P1 to P8, mark the correlation with the annual objectives as strong, weak or none, and say why.
  3. Allocate. Select three against capacity. Defer or categorise the rest, each with a reason and a path.
  4. Write the refusal. Draft the two sentences you would say to the person who proposed P8.
  5. Catchball. Describe the catchball step you would run before fixing this matrix, and name one thing it could legitimately change.

The test of a real answer: three traps, and the second is the one this unit exists for.

First, P4 and P6 and P8 are the interesting ones. None of them correlates with A1, A2 or A3 - and two were proposed by the regional director. If your allocation quietly finds room for them, you have drawn a matrix around exactly the failure described in T1 and given it the appearance of rigour, which is worse than having no matrix at all.

Second, notice that P6 - a staff wellness programme - may be a genuinely excellent thing for that depot to do. "Does not correlate with this year's objectives" is not "bad idea", and your step-4 sentences must make that distinction audible. If your refusal would make somebody stop bringing you ideas, rewrite it.

Third, look hard at P7, proposed by an artisan. It is small, unglamorous, and may correlate strongly with A1. Ask yourself honestly whether it would have survived a selection process based on who was in the room - and what that tells you about why catchball exists.

T5: The Guardrails

Policy deployment fails in ways that leave the matrix looking perfect.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• More than three to five breakthrough objectives. The defining failure. Twelve priorities is zero priorities, and the department reverts to whatever is loudest.

• Annual objectives that trace to nothing. If it serves no breakthrough objective it is not strategy, it is work somebody wants done.

• Correlating everything with everything. A matrix with every dot filled has quietly restored the situation it was built to fix, while looking rigorous.

• Selecting by sponsor seniority. The T1 failure, now with a diagram around it.

• Allocating beyond capacity. Eleven started, three finished. Capacity is not a negotiating position.

• Treating uncorrelated ideas as rejected. They are unaligned, not bad. Categorise them and give each a path.

• Deferring with no reason and no date. That is not a decision, it is a queue - and queues become graveyards.

• A matrix issued downward. Without catchball it is an instruction list, and the people who could have told you it was undeliverable were never asked.

• Catchball that changes nothing. Worse than none. It teaches an organisation that being asked is theatre, and next year nobody will bother answering.

• Building it once and never revisiting. The year changes underneath the matrix; an objective overtaken by events should be retired openly rather than pursued out of embarrassment.

Onalenna had thirty-four good ideas, six slots, and no legitimate way to say no. Choose three to five breakthrough objectives and hold the limit, because twelve priorities is none; derive this year's objectives so that nothing is being pursued that serves nothing; test every candidate for genuine correlation and mark it honestly, including the fifteen that correlate with nothing; allocate against the capacity you actually have and defer the rest with a reason and a date; and run catchball before fixing anything, letting it genuinely change the plan. The matrix is a device for refusal as much as for alignment, and its value lies in what it does not select. Behind every idea you decline is a person - so categorise, never reject, and give each one a path. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, count the improvement projects your organisation started last year and how many it finished, then compare that number to the people available to run them.
