I service it religiously by the calendar - so why am I BOTH wasting money on good parts AND still getting blindsided by failures?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sizwe, and your minibus taxi is your business. You're a responsible owner: you service it by the book, exactly when the calendar and the kilometres say - every 30,000km, like clockwork - and you're proud of that discipline. But you're being stung from both sides. Last month the mechanic replaced the brake pads, just as the schedule said; the old ones looked almost new, and he shrugged - "still loads of life in these, maybe another 20,000km." That felt like throwing a handful of rands in the bin.

Worse, the month before, a wheel bearing failed - suddenly, on a busy morning run - leaving you stranded with angry passengers, a tow, an emergency repair and a whole day's income gone. Your next service was only weeks away. The calendar didn't save you.

I follow the service plan perfectly - so why am I BOTH wasting money on good parts AND still getting blindsided by surprise breakdowns?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first thought is to service even more often - every 20,000km instead of 30,000. But that just throws away brake pads with even more good life in them, and risks a mechanic disturbing a perfectly good part and causing the next problem. So maybe stretch the services out to save money? - but the thought of another breakdown on the roadside makes you feel sick; you can't risk it. So you sit stuck, blaming bad luck or poor-quality parts.

But notice the one thing all your problems share: the service calendar itself. So before you read on: what does a date on a calendar actually know about how worn the parts on your taxi really are?

The calendar knows nothing about your machine - let the machine tell you. A fixed date is blind to real condition, so you BOTH replace healthy parts too early AND miss the ones failing in the gaps. But things warn you before they fail (a hum, a squeal, a wear bar, oil that looks different, rising cost). Watch those signs as a trend, act when condition crosses a sensible 'act now' level - on the costly/critical parts. Keep a simple calendar for the cheap, low-risk bits.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The calendar knows nothing about your machine - so let the machine tell you when it needs you. A fixed date has no connection to the real, live condition of your taxi, and that one mismatch costs you twice at once. Five moves:

  • See the two losses of the calendar: a fixed date has no connection to the real, live condition of your machine - and that one mismatch costs you twice at once: you replace still-healthy parts too early (wasting money, parts and labour - and sometimes disturbing a good part and causing the next fault), AND things genuinely wearing out fail in the GAPS between services, because nothing was watching their true state.
  • The thing warns you before it fails: most parts don't just suddenly break - they give clues first. The wheel bearing probably hummed faintly for weeks; brakes go soft or squeal; tyres show wear bars; the oil on the dipstick looks and feels different; running cost creeps up; a gauge drifts. Learn the handful of signs that matter for your machine - that's where the real information lives, not on the calendar.
  • Swap the trigger - let condition decide when: stop waiting for the date; watch for the signal. Track the few signs that matter as a TREND, set a sensible 'act now' level, and act when the condition crosses it - fixing the bearing while it's still a hum, not when it strands you; running the brake pads until they're actually worn, not when a date arrives. The machine, not the clock, tells you it needs you.
  • Choose which items to switch: this isn't for everything. Put condition-watching on the costly and critical parts - engine, brakes, tyres - where early waste or a surprise failure really hurts. For cheap, simple, low-risk bits (wiper blades, a cabin filter) a fixed interval is perfectly fine and cheaper. The skill is deciding which parts earn watching and which can stay on a simple schedule.
  • The honest boundaries: watching costs a little time and skill, and it catches slow, creeping faults far better than sudden ones - so watch the critical things closely, act early on their signs, and keep a sensible calendar where the watching wouldn't pay. You're not stopping maintenance or waiting for things to break; you're making the timing smart.

See it turn for you. The brake pads were good for ~50,000km but the calendar binned them at 30,000 - ~20,000km of life, near enough 40%, thrown away. The wheel bearing, meanwhile, almost certainly hummed faintly for weeks before it failed - a sign no calendar could ever carry. So you swap the trigger on the parts that matter: you learn the few signs (the hum, the soft brake, the wear bar, the changed oil, the creeping fuel use), watch them as a trend, and act when condition crosses a sensible "act now" level - fixing the bearing while it's still a hum, and running the pads until they're genuinely worn. Wiper blades and the cabin filter? Keep them on the simple calendar - it's cheaper and fine. A fixed schedule wastes good life AND misses real failures; the thing warns you before it fails, so watch the signs and act on the trend - and you stop being stung from both sides. You're not maintaining less; you're maintaining smart - serving reliability and wasting nothing.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

The calendar-versus-condition idea reaches well beyond machines. Watch it with your own health.

See the same move with a health check-up

You might have a yearly check-up with the doctor - that's your calendar, and it's a sensible backstop. But if you develop a bad cough that won't shift, you don't wait six months for the appointment: the cough is a signal. You listen to what your body is telling you and go now - acting on your condition, not the date.

That's exactly the move: watch the signs and act on them, with the calendar as a back-up, not a blind rule. The yearly check still catches the slow, silent things you'd never notice; the cough catches what's changing now. Use both - the calendar for what creeps unseen, your senses for what's started to warn you.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of something you maintain or replace purely by the calendar - your car, a filter in your water jug, the gutters once a year:

  1. Name the two losses. What good life might you be throwing away by replacing too early - and what surprise failure could strike in the gaps between your scheduled checks?
  2. List the warning signs. What few, simple signals would tell you its condition is worsening before it fails - a change in sound, look, smell, speed, or cost?
  3. Make a smarter plan. For the costly or critical parts, switch the trigger: stop listening to the calendar and start listening to the thing - watch the signs and act when they cross your "act now" level. For the cheap, simple, low-risk bits, keep a sensible calendar.

Your discipline was never the problem - it's a real strength, the mark of someone responsible. The only flaw was trusting a date over the thing itself, and a date is blind: it cannot see the pad that still has half its life, nor hear the bearing that has begun to hum. Learn to read what your machine is plainly trying to tell you, and you waste nothing and get blindsided by nothing - you keep faith with the people who climb aboard trusting it'll get them safely there. Let the calendar guard against what creeps unseen; let your eyes and ears catch what's already calling for help.
