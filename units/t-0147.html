One shared password and a hope

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have been handed an instruction that makes your stomach drop: "make the CMMS secure."

The CMMS holds everything that matters - the asset register, the entire maintenance history, the safety-critical records for the plant. And right now it is protected by a password the whole maintenance team shares, and a general hope that nothing bad happens. You know that is not secure. But when you try to think about what "secure" actually means, you freeze. Your only instinct is "ask IT to put antivirus on it", and something tells you that is nowhere near enough - you just cannot say what is missing.

What is missing is this. There is no backup, so a single ransomware attack would erase the plant's whole maintenance memory in an afternoon. The shared password means anyone - or anyone who guesses it - can silently alter a safety-critical record, and nobody would ever know who. You have a system full of irreplaceable, safety-relevant data, protected by almost nothing, and no framework at all for what protecting it should even involve.

I have been told to secure a system that holds our safety records and our whole maintenance memory, and my only idea is "antivirus" - which I know is not enough. What does securing an asset-information system actually require, and which controls does this one need?

Before you read on, decide. The maintenance system holds the asset register, every work order ever raised, and the safety-critical records. It is the plant's entire memory, and none of it exists anywhere else. Everybody logs in with the same shared password — it is written on a card by the office printer — the backups have never been tested, and protection consists of whatever the supplier installed. Anyone could alter a safety record and nobody could ever say who. You have been told to secure it, and your only idea is antivirus, which you know is not enough. Write down what securing this actually requires — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and which property matters most here

• “Buy a proper security product.” A purchase standing in for a decision. Without knowing what you are protecting and from what, you cannot tell whether the product addresses your actual exposure — and the biggest one here is not technical at all.

• “Lock it down — restrict access to as few people as possible.” Secrecy as the goal, which is the instinct worth examining. Restricting access to a system people need in order to do maintenance can cost you more than a leak would, and it still leaves the shared credential doing its damage.

• “Follow a recognised standard and implement it fully.” Closer, and taken literally it is unaffordable — the published catalogues offer far more than any one system needs, and a programme that cannot be funded is a programme that does not happen.

• “It is only maintenance data — nobody wants to steal it.” Probably true and the wrong axis entirely, as the next paragraph shows. Nobody has to want it for you to lose it.

• “Make sure there are backups.” Essential, and note what you actually have: backups nobody has ever restored, which is a belief rather than a backup. An untested backup and no backup differ only in how you find out.

• The frame starts by asking what actually matters for this system, and here the answer inverts the instinct. Security is usually imagined as keeping things secret — but for operational records, that they are correct and that they are there when needed both outrank secrecy. A leaked asset list is embarrassing; a silently altered safety record can hurt somebody, and a destroyed history erases everything this plant knows about its own equipment. Get that ordering right and it tells you what is worth paying for. Then select controls against the real threats rather than adopting a catalogue wholesale — proportionality is what makes security affordable and therefore real. And start where the damage actually is: a credential shared among many people destroys the ability to attribute any action to anybody, so a record can be changed and nobody can ever say by whom. No technical control repairs that. Named accounts are unglamorous, cost almost nothing, and are the single most important thing on your list.

There is a standard for exactly this, and a way to build a proportionate defence on one page. Here it is, for the CMMS.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the Cybersecurity Resilience Annex for the CMMS - a proportionate, justified set of controls, not a wall of jargon.

1. What are we protecting, and from what? (assets, threats, and the CIA priority)

The CMMS holds the asset register, the maintenance history, and safety-critical records - the plant's whole maintenance memory. The threats: RANSOMWARE (encrypts it), UNAUTHORISED CHANGE (someone alters a record), DATA LOSS (no backup), INSIDER error or misuse. Now the priority, using the CIA triad: for asset information, INTEGRITY (the data is correct and un-tampered) and AVAILABILITY (it is there when needed) matter more than CONFIDENTIALITY. A leaked asset list is embarrassing; a silently altered safety record can hurt someone, and a ransomwared register can erase the plant's memory. Protect integrity and availability first.

2. Which proportionate controls, across all four themes? (select on risk, not all 93)

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 offers 93 controls in four themes; you SELECT the proportionate ones for these threats, not all 93. ORGANISATIONAL: an access policy, and security terms in the CMMS vendor's contract. PEOPLE: unique NAMED accounts (retire the shared password), and basic awareness training. PHYSICAL: controlled access to the server room. TECHNOLOGICAL: multi-factor authentication, TESTED backups, timely patching, a logging/audit trail, and encryption of sensitive fields. Note that the single most important fix - killing the shared password - is a PEOPLE control, not antivirus. Security is a mix across all four themes, chosen for the risk.

3. Which part of CIA does each threat attack - and which control answers it? (the mapping)

The mapping that proves the selection. RANSOMWARE attacks AVAILABILITY -> answered by TESTED BACKUPS (a copy it cannot reach, proven by restoring it). UNAUTHORISED CHANGE attacks INTEGRITY -> answered by NAMED ACCOUNTS + an AUDIT LOG (now every change has a who and a when, and the shared password that made tampering anonymous is gone). A LEAKED EXPORT attacks CONFIDENTIALITY -> answered by ACCESS CONTROL + ENCRYPTION. Each control earns its place by answering a specific threat to a specific part of CIA - and if a control answers no threat, it does not belong in this annex.

4. What is on the record? (the resilience annex / Statement of Applicability)

The Cybersecurity Resilience Annex, in the form of a Statement of Applicability: the threats identified; each selected control with its justification (WHY it is here - which threat, which CIA element); and, importantly, what is EXCLUDED and why (a control for a threat this system does not face is honestly left out, not silently ignored). This puts the security decisions on the record so they can be reviewed and challenged - 'we chose these, deliberately, for these reasons' is a defensible position; a pile of controls with no rationale is not.

5. How does it recover when prevention fails? (resilience, not just prevention)

Prevention fails eventually, so the annex is judged on RESILIENCE, not just resistance. The load-bearing measure is RECOVERY: a backup that has actually been restored in a rehearsed test - because an untested backup fails exactly when you finally need it. If ransomware encrypts the CMMS tomorrow, a tested restore turns a catastrophe into a bad afternoon. And when a control is breached, the incident-response plan takes over - detect, contain, recover. Prevention buys you fewer incidents; recovery is what lets you survive the ones that get through.

Notice the single most important fix in box 2: it is not antivirus. It is retiring the shared password for named accounts - a people control. And notice box 1 sets the priority before any control is chosen: for this system, keeping the data correct and available matters more than keeping it secret. Security here is a proportionate mix, aimed at the threats that actually matter. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Securing a CMMS is not "install antivirus" - it is a risk-based selection of proportionate controls. Five questions build it:

  • What are we protecting, and from what? Name the assets (register, history, safety records), the threats (ransomware, unauthorised change, data loss, insider), and then use the CIA triad - Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability - to set the priority. For asset information the crucial insight is that integrity (the data is correct and un-tampered) and availability (it is there when you need it) usually matter more than confidentiality. A leaked asset list is embarrassing; an altered safety record can hurt someone, and a ransomwared register can erase the plant's memory.
  • Which proportionate controls, across all four themes? ISO/IEC 27001:2022 offers 93 controls in four themes - organisational, people, physical, technological - and you select the ones the risk needs, not all 93. Crucially, security is not just the technological theme: the biggest single fix here, replacing the shared password with named accounts, is a people control. A defence built only of technology, with people and process ignored, has a hole straight through the middle.
  • Which part of CIA does each threat attack, and which control answers it? Map them: ransomware attacks availability, answered by tested backups; unauthorised change attacks integrity, answered by named accounts and an audit log; a leaked export attacks confidentiality, answered by access control and encryption. Every control earns its place by answering a specific threat to a specific part of CIA - and a control that answers no threat does not belong.
  • What is on the record? Assemble the Statement of Applicability: the threats, the controls selected with their justification, and what is deliberately excluded and why. Security decisions belong on the record - "we chose these, for these reasons, and left those out on purpose" is defensible; an undocumented pile of controls is not.
  • How does it recover when prevention fails? No control set stops everything, so the annex is judged on resilience, not just resistance. The load-bearing measure is recovery: a backup that has actually been restored in a test, because an untested backup fails exactly when you need it. If ransomware hits tomorrow, a tested restore turns a catastrophe into a bad afternoon.

One honest boundary. Your job is to select and specify the controls - risk-based, proportionate, across all four themes. You are not implementing them (that is the IT security team's craft), nor are you running the incident response when a control is breached (that is a separate plan for detect-contain-recover). Your role is to decide, on the record, what this system needs and why.

Does it scale? Securing a building is the same discipline: you do not fit a bank vault to every door - you assess what is valuable and what the threats are, choose a proportionate mix of locks, alarms and staff procedures, and make sure you can recover if someone gets in anyway.

The main worry: an insider could quietly alter records to hide a developing fault, and a ransomware attack could lock the team out during a critical period.

T5: The Guardrails

CMMS security fails in a few very consistent ways, almost all of them a failure to think past technology and past prevention.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Security equals antivirus. A single technological control while people, process and recovery are ignored. The shared password and the missing backup are the real exposures, and neither is an antivirus problem.

• Applying all 93 controls as a checklist. The standard is a menu to select from on risk, not a list to tick end to end. Over-engineering wastes effort and buries the controls that matter.

• Protecting confidentiality while ignoring integrity and availability. For a CMMS, a tampered safety record and a locked-out register are worse than a leaked asset list. Set the CIA priority for the system you actually have.

• Tolerating the shared password. It is the people control that quietly undoes every technological one - it makes every change anonymous and every account guessable. Named accounts are often the highest-value single fix.

• Prevention with no recovery. Without a tested backup, the first ransomware attack is fatal. Resilience, not resistance, is the goal - build in the way back.

• A backup nobody has ever restored. An untested backup is a hope, not a control. It fails precisely on the day you stake everything on it. Restore it in a rehearsal, on a schedule.

• No Statement of Applicability. Controls chosen with no recorded rationale cannot be reviewed, defended or improved. Put the decisions - and the deliberate exclusions - on the record.
