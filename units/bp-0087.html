Movement 1 - The Simulation

It is a grey Tuesday morning at the Riebeek pump station, just outside the small town of Modderkloof. You are the maintenance engineer for the Modderkloof Water Board, and the station's two raw-water pumps push every drop of drinking water up the hill to the town's reservoir. Pump No. 2 is the problem child. You are standing on the concrete plinth next to it, one hand flat on its casing, and you can feel it: a rough, knocking shudder that travels up your arm. It should hum. It grinds.

In your other hand is a quote, two pages, slightly creased from your back pocket. The repair shop wants R 180,000 to strip and rebuild the old pump - new bearings, a reground shaft, a fresh impeller. Below that line, almost as an afterthought, the rep has pencilled in a second option: a brand-new pump for R 520,000, "the modern one, far more efficient, you'll see."

Across the desk in the cramped site office sits Dumisani, the Board's finance manager, who drove out from town specially. He has a way of tilting his head when he is not convinced, and his head is tilted now. "So," he says, tapping the quote, "you want me to take to the board a number that's nearly three times bigger. On what basis? Because the rep says it's better?" He is not unkind. He is just doing his job, and his job is to ask the question you cannot yet answer.

You feel the heat climb your neck. You have been here eleven years. You know this pump. The old one drinks electricity - it is tired, its efficiency has slid, and it trips out on hot afternoons when demand is highest. The new pump would sip power and run cool. You are sure, in your gut, that the new pump is the right call. But "I'm sure" is not a sentence Dumisani can carry into a board meeting. Last quarter the board rejected a colleague's machine request for exactly this reason - "price too high, justification too thin" - and that colleague is now nursing a far more expensive breakdown. You do not want to be him.

Somewhere under the floor you can hear the steady drip of the gland seal, a slow tick-tick into the sump, counting time. The repaired pump would cost a quarter of the new one today - that is the number that shouts loudest, and part of you just wants to grab it, sign it off, and make the knocking stop before the afternoon peak. Cheaper now. Done by Friday.

But you also know the old casing already carries fifteen years on it. The repair buys you maybe four or five more years before the next failure, and every one of those years it will keep drinking R 14,000 a month in electricity. The new pump should last fifteen years and draw closer to R 9,000 a month. Those are real rand, every month, for years - but they are future rand, scattered across time, and right now they are just numbers in your head, not on Dumisani's page.

He slides a blank sheet across the desk to you. "Don't tell me which one is cheaper today," he says. "I can read. Tell me which one costs the Board less over its life - and show me the working, so I can defend it when they push back."

The pump knocks again under your hand.

Where do you start? What would you need to know - and to show - to turn your gut feeling into a number the board cannot argue with?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Before you read how the Modderkloof team solved it, have a real go yourself. This is the part that makes it stick.

Take Dumisani's blank sheet and think it through. You do not need a finance degree - you need to organise what you already feel into something you can show.

Work through these:

  1. Pick the common period both options must cover - the longer pump's lifespan (15 years). Now write the rule for a fair comparison in one line: "a fair comparison must total EVERY rand each option costs over that same number of years." Self-test: look at your method - if it leaves out the cheaper pump's later replacement (the repair only lasts about 5 years, so it must be redone to cover 15) or leaves out its higher monthly electricity, it is not fair yet. You can see the omission yourself: if a cost that will really happen is not in your total, the total is wrong.
  2. You have a purchase price for each option, and a monthly electricity cost for each, and a rough lifespan for each. If you laid every one of those future costs out on a timeline - this year, next year, the year after - what would the full picture look like for each pump?
  3. Do the cheap real test: if a bank offered you R 100 today OR R 100 in ten years, which would you take, and why? Then apply what that tells you - shrink each future year's cost by a small fixed percentage before you add it in. Built-in check: a rand you spend today must end up heavier in your total than the same rand spent in year ten. If your numbers do not show that, you have not handled it yet - go back and re-weigh the later years.
  4. Add up, for each pump, its purchase price PLUS every monthly electricity cost across the shared 15-year window PLUS any mid-life replacement - into ONE total-cost-of-ownership figure per pump. Self-test before you read any solution: the cheaper-to-buy pump should NOT automatically come out with the smaller total. If it does, redo it - you have probably left out its thirstier running cost or its repeated replacements. You can check your own winner by re-summing the columns yourself; you do not need us to tell you whether the bottom numbers add up.

Now set your own yardstick. Before you read on, write two short lines about your own repair-or-replace situation - the one you will tackle in Movement 4:

  • "I would change my mind if ___ ."

Keep these two lines beside you. When the worked solution arrives, you are comparing it to your own pre-stated line - not waiting for us to hand you a verdict.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is what you and Dumisani actually worked out, sitting in that cramped office with two coffees going cold.

You started by agreeing the real question. It was never "which pump is cheaper to buy?" - the repair obviously wins that. The real question was "which choice costs the Modderkloof Water Board less across its whole working life, today's rand and tomorrow's rand together?" Once you said it out loud, you both knew the purchase price alone could never answer it.

You set the deciding line in advance - before you ran a single number. Dumisani made you say it plainly so neither of you could move the goalposts later: "The option with the lower whole-life cost in today's money wins - even if that turns out to be the one I personally don't fancy. If the two totals land within about 5% of each other, we'll call it too close to split on cost alone and decide on reliability instead." You wrote that at the top of the sheet. The number would choose, not your gut and not the rep's patter.

So you laid out every cost each option would create, across all the years it would live. This is the heart of it: you stopped looking only at the price tag and started looking at the whole life of the asset - buy it, run it, maintain it, retire it.

Option A - Repair the old pump (R 180,000 today)

  • Lifespan after repair: 5 years
  • Electricity: R 14,000 per month = R 168,000 per year
  • Add a likely mid-life top-up repair in year 3 of each repair cycle: R 40,000

Option B - Buy the new pump (R 520,000 today)

  • Lifespan: 15 years
  • Electricity: R 9,000 per month = R 108,000 per year
  • A minor service in year 8: R 25,000

Straight away a fairness problem jumped out, the one you half-felt in the office: the repair only lasts 5 years and the new pump lasts 15. You cannot fairly compare 5 years of one against 15 years of the other. So you put them on the same footing - a 15-year window - which means the repair would have to be done, then repeated roughly twice more to keep water flowing for the same period. Comparing whole lives, not single purchases, is the first honest move.

Then Dumisani taught you the second move, the one your gut had been missing. He said: "A rand we spend in ten years is not as painful as a rand we spend today. We could put today's rand in the bank, or pay down debt, and it would grow. So a future cost should weigh less in our decision." Money has a time value. To compare fairly, you pull every future cost back to what it is worth in today's money before you add anything up. Bringing a future amount back to its value today is called discounting, and you used the Board's cost of money - about 8% a year - as the rate.

The mechanics are simpler than they sound. A cost of R 100 next year, at 8%, is worth about R 93 today (because R 93 invested at 8% would become R 100). A cost two years out is worth about R 86 today. The further away the cost, the lighter it weighs. You did this for every line - every year of electricity, every repair, the purchase prices - and added up the today's-value of all of them for each option.

When the dust settled, here is what the sheet showed, every figure expressed in today's rand across the 15-year window:

  • Option A - repeated repairs: roughly R 1.89 million in today's money (three rounds of repair plus fifteen years of that thirsty R 14,000-a-month bill, all discounted back).
  • Option B - the new pump: roughly R 1.46 million in today's money (one big purchase, then fifteen years of the leaner R 9,000-a-month bill, discounted back).

The cheaper-today option was the dearer option over its life - by about R 430,000. The electricity saving alone, year after year, quietly out-weighed the scary purchase price. Your gut had been right. But now it was not a gut feeling; it was a column of figures with a single, defensible number at the bottom.

Then you closed the loop - you tested the decision against reality before betting R 520,000 on it. A column of figures is only as good as the numbers feeding it, and the whole answer leaned on one number you had simply assumed: that the new pump really draws R 9,000 a month against the old one's R 14,000. So you found out, by the cheapest honest means. You did not buy the pump to see. You phoned the manufacturer for the new pump's rated power draw, then - the part that cost you nothing but an afternoon - you went out to Pump No. 2 with a clamp meter and the electricity bill and measured what the old pump was actually pulling over a normal running day.

And reality returned a surprise. The old pump was not drinking R 14,000 a month. On the hot afternoons when it tripped and a standby kicked in, its real average over the month came to nearer R 16,500 - worse than you had assumed, because the inefficiency bit hardest exactly when demand peaked. That changed things, but not the way you might fear: the gap between the two pumps was wider than your sheet had shown, not narrower. The new pump's case got stronger, not weaker - and now it rested on a measured number, not a remembered one. (Had the meter shown the opposite - the old pump sipping only R 10,000 - your own success line would have forced you to redo the sums and quite possibly switch to the repair. That is the point of setting the line first: it binds you to the evidence.)

You re-ran the bottom line with the measured R 16,500, and Option A climbed to roughly R 2.15 million in today's money - now about R 690,000 dearer than the new pump over its life, comfortably outside your 5%-too-close band. Hold, not change. The decision stood, and it stood on something you had checked rather than guessed.

The technique you used has a name. It is the discounted lifecycle cost of an asset - sometimes called whole-life costing or net present value of the options. You add up every cost across the asset's whole life, pull each future cost back to today's money by discounting, and compare the totals. The option with the lower discounted lifecycle cost is the one that costs the organisation least, honestly measured.

Be honest about the trade-offs. This is a model, not a crystal ball. Three things can move the answer, and a good analyst says so out loud:

  • The discount rate matters. At a very high rate, future savings shrink and the cheap-now repair starts to look better. You showed the board your 8% and what happened at 12%, so no one could accuse you of picking a flattering number.
  • The lifespan and the running costs are estimates. If the new pump only lasts 12 years, or electricity rises faster than expected, the gap changes. You said which numbers you were least sure of - which is exactly why you went and measured the running cost rather than trusting the figure in your head.
  • Cash today is real. A smaller organisation might genuinely not have R 520,000 to spare now, even if it is cheaper over time. Lifecycle cost tells you the best value; it does not tell you what you can afford this month. Those are two different questions, and you named both.

Dumisani took the sheet, looked at it for a long moment, and nodded. "This I can defend," he said. Not "I'm sure." A number, with the working shown - and a meter reading behind the number.

Method & Finding

  • The Method (the lesson - reusable): set the deciding line in advance ("lower whole-life cost in today's money wins"), then before you commit, measure the one assumed number the whole decision leans on - by the cheapest honest means - and re-run the totals against the line.
  • The Finding (in this story only): the old pump's measured running cost was worse than assumed (R 16,500, not R 14,000), which widened the gap and confirmed the replacement - a surprise that made the case stronger, not weaker.
  • Your Check (how you will know in your world): take one real reading of your own - a clamp-meter measurement of an old motor over a day, or twelve months of fuel/electricity bills for the asset you are replacing - and put that measured rand figure into your sheet, not the number you carry in your head.

Do this yourself

You do not need the pump. You need the recipe and a blank sheet. Here it is in five plain moves:

  1. Write the real question at the top: "Which option costs less over its whole life, in today's money?" - not "which is cheaper to buy?"
  2. List every cost each option creates across its whole life - buy/repair, run (power, fuel, licences), maintain, dispose. The ones you keep forgetting are the running costs.
  3. Put both options on the same time window. Stretch the shorter-lived one out by repeating it until the lengths match.
  4. Discount each future cost back to today's money - multiply each year's cost by that year's discount factor, then add the column up.
  5. Compare the two bottom numbers - and separately ask whether you can afford the better-value one this month.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Now take one step with a guide beside you, before you walk into your own world alone.

Here is a new, smaller case. Read it, then we will work it together.

Your home geyser has started leaking. The plumber says he can replace the element and the thermostat for R 3,500 and "it'll be fine for a year or two." Or, he says, you could put in a new high-efficiency geyser for R 9,000 that "barely sips power and should give you ten years easy." You glance at the R 3,500 quote and the R 9,000 quote side by side. The cheap one is obviously the one to take - it is barely a quarter of the price, and money is tight this month. You reach for your phone to tell him to go ahead with the repair.

It feels like a completely different problem from the pump, doesn't it? Smaller. Simpler. Not even worth a spreadsheet. Let's slow down.

Hint 1. Look only at the shape of the two numbers, not their size. One option is cheap to buy and the plumber promises only "a year or two". The other is dear to buy and promises "ten years easy".

Pause. Where have you seen that shape before - cheap-now-and-short-life against dear-now-and-long-life?

Show me ▸

Reveal. It is the exact same shape as the pump. R 3,500 / R 9,000 instead of R 180,000 / R 520,000 - but cheap-and-short versus dear-and-long is the same trap wearing smaller clothes. The small numbers are the disguise. They tempt you to skip the thinking precisely because "it's too cheap to bother analysing." That is the misleading surface feature.

Hint 2. The plumber gave you two prices and two lifespans. What did he not put on the table? Think about why the new geyser "barely sips power."

Pause. What is missing from this quote that was front-and-centre in the pump case?

Show me ▸

Reveal. The running cost - the monthly electricity to heat the water. An old leaking geyser with a tired element heats slowly and wastes power; the efficient one uses far less. That cost never appears on either quote, because it is not something the plumber bills you for - the electricity company does, quietly, every month, for years. This is a partial instance: the trap is there, but the most important number hasn't even been gathered yet. Your first real move is to go and find it (check your bill, or ask what each geyser draws).

Hint 3. Now do the two honest moves you learned from Dumisani. The repair lasts maybe 2 years; the new one lasts 10.

Pause. Before you can compare them, what has to happen to the 2-year option? And once both run for 10 years, does a rand of electricity you'll spend in year 8 weigh the same as a rand today?

Show me ▸

Reveal. Put them on the same 10-year window - which means the R 3,500 repair has to be repeated several times to keep hot water running that long, so its real cost is far more than R 3,500. Then discount each future cost back to today's money, just like the pump. Once you add up buy + repeated repairs + ten years of higher electricity for the repair, against one new geyser + ten years of lower electricity for the replacement, the "obvious" cheap choice may well flip - the same way it flipped at the pump station.

The deep-structure lesson: the size of the numbers does not change the problem. A R 9,000 geyser and a R 520,000 pump are the same problem-type - cheap-to-buy-but-costly-to-own against dear-to-buy-but-cheap-to-own - and they need the same honest answer: total every cost across a matched whole life, pull future rand back to today, then compare. When something looks "too small to analyse," that is exactly the moment the price tag is most likely to fool you.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The pump was never really the point. The point is the trap: choosing big, long-lived things on price-today and gut feel, then having no defence when someone asks "why?" That same trap is waiting wherever an upfront cost hides a stream of future costs.

Find this exact problem-type in your own world. Work through these:

  1. Name a "repair-or-replace" decision you face right now. It might be a delivery vehicle, a roof, a software system, a generator, a laptop fleet - anything where one option is cheap-now-but-costly-to-run and the other is dear-now-but-cheaper-to-run. Write down both options.
  2. List every cost each option creates across its whole life - not just the price. Purchase or repair, running costs (fuel, power, licences, consumables), maintenance, and what it costs to retire or dispose of it. Which future costs have you been ignoring because they are not on the price tag?
  3. Put the options on the same time window. If one lasts three years and the other lasts nine, how would you compare them fairly? What has to be repeated to make the lengths match?
  4. Decide how you will weigh future rand against today's rand. What rate would be honest for your situation - what your money could earn, or what your debt costs you? Then ask: does a cost five years out really deserve the same weight as a cost today?
  5. Build the one number at the bottom of each column - the whole-life cost in today's money - and notice whether the cheaper-today option is still the cheaper-overall option. Was your gut right, or did the timeline surprise you?
  6. Separate "best value" from "can we afford it now." If the better-value option needs cash you do not have today, say so plainly - that is a real and separate constraint, not a flaw in your analysis.

The honest transfer: this is the same problem-type you just felt at the Riebeek pump station - comparing long-lived choices by their whole discounted lifecycle cost, not their price tag - moved into the assets and decisions of your own organisation. The numbers will be yours. The trap, and the way out of it, are the same.

Close your own loop this week. Do not stop at the spreadsheet. Take the one number your whole comparison leans on - the running cost of the asset you are weighing - and measure it the cheapest honest way you can: pull twelve months of fuel or electricity bills, or take a single meter reading over a normal working day. Put that measured figure into your sheet in place of the one in your head, and see whether your bottom line holds. Then tell us how it went, the Ubuntu way: "I predicted ___, reality (the measured number) did ___, so I now think ___ ." That is the loop closing inside you - where reality, not AMU, is the teacher.
