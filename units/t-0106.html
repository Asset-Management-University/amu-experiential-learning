Step 1 of 5

How sure are you, really?

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. A computational model has just told you how much life is left in a critical water main: 8.0 years. A clean, confident number. You report it; management is delighted, and defers the pipe's expensive replacement for seven years. The budget is balanced on that 8.0.

Four years later, the main bursts. It floods a pump-house and halts the town's supply for days.

The model did not lie to you. 8.0 was its honest best guess - an average. But that single, tidy number hid everything that mattered: that pipes from that batch naturally vary by years; that nobody had ever measured the corrosion rate in this soil; that you used the nominal wall thickness, never the real, thinned thickness of this pipe. All of that doubt was real, and all of it was invisible - crushed into one decimal place. You did not build your plan on a fact. You built it on a guess wearing the costume of a fact.

My model gave me one confident number, so we treated it as truth - and reality landed nowhere near it. How do I turn a prediction into an honest one: how sure am I, and what should I do about the parts I am unsure of?

Before you read on, decide. The model said the pipe had 8.0 years of life left. That went to management, the replacement was deferred, the budget was set. The pipe failed at four. Looking back at the inputs: a corrosion rate taken from a generic table rather than measured on this soil, the nominal wall thickness rather than this pipe's actual thinned thickness, and a genuine spread between pipes from the same batch that nobody disputes. The model itself is sound and was used correctly. Write down what should have gone to management instead — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two very different kinds of doubt

• “Use a more conservative model, or apply a safety factor to the answer.” That produces a different single number, equally confident, whose doubt is equally invisible — and it does not tell you whether the margin you added is anywhere near the size of the doubt you were covering.

• “Say it is approximate — make clear it is only an estimate.” Everybody nods, and nothing changes, because “approximate” is not a quantity. A plan cannot be built differently on the strength of a hedge.

• “Get a better model.” The model was sound. It answered the question it was asked, on the inputs it was given, and two of those inputs were not measurements of this pipe at all.

• “Report a range instead of a number.” Right, and it is the first half. A range of four to twelve years is honest, and on its own it leaves management with a wider version of the same problem and no idea what to do about it.

• “Management wanted one number — that is what they will always want.” True, and giving it to them is how a guess acquires the costume of a fact. The number was not wrong; the deletion of everything around it was.

• The range is necessary and not sufficient, because the doubt in it is two different substances that need opposite responses. Some of it is genuine scatter — pipes from a batch vary, ground varies — which no amount of study removes, and which is therefore something to build margin around and live with. The rest is not a property of the world at all but a description of what nobody has gone and measured: a generic corrosion rate where this soil's rate was never taken, a nominal thickness where this pipe's was never checked. That part is not uncertainty; it is a to-do list, and it shrinks the moment somebody does it. Split the range that way and the decision makes itself: if most of the doubt is scatter, add margin and proceed — and if most of it is ignorance, as it is here, the honest answer is not a wider margin but go and measure, then decide.

There is a discipline for telling the whole truth about a prediction. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the honest version of that very prediction - the sheet Onalenna wishes she had taken to management instead of the bare "8.0 years".

1. The naked number (the model's single best guess)

The model's single best guess: 8.0 years of life left in the pipe. (A confident-looking number - and a quiet lie of omission, because all its doubt is hidden inside it.)

2. The honest range (the truth the number hid)

The honest answer is a RANGE: somewhere between 4 and 12 years - and the pipe failing at 4 was always the bottom edge of it. That wide spread - not the tidy 8.0 - is the truth the single number was hiding.

3. The aleatoric part - irreducible scatter (DESIGN for it)

Aleatoric (irreducible): natural scatter - pipes from this batch vary, the ground varies - worth roughly +/- 1.5 years, and no amount of study removes it. ACTION: build this into the safety margin and live with it.

4. The epistemic part - ignorance you can fix (MEASURE it away)

Epistemic (reducible): pure ignorance - we used a generic corrosion rate and never measured THIS pipe's actual wall thickness - worth roughly +/- 2.8 years. ACTION: this is a to-do list. Inspect it, and the range shrinks.

5. The decision (add margin, or go and measure?)

Decision: the doubt is far too wide to safely defer replacement for 7 years. Most of it is epistemic - so go shrink it NOW: ultrasonic thickness testing and a soil-corrosion test on this pipe, then decide on a tight, honest range.

Look at what the honest sheet does that the single number could not: it shows the real range, and then it splits the doubt into two kinds that demand two different responses - the scatter you simply design a margin for, and the ignorance you go and measure away. The 8.0 said "relax for seven years". The honest sheet says "go inspect this pipe now". One of those would have saved the pump-house. Now let us walk through the thinking.

T3: The Blueprint

That shock - the pipe bursting at four years when the model said eight - is the price of trusting a single number. The discipline of telling the whole truth instead is Uncertainty Quantification, or UQ - and for any model serious enough to bet a career on, it is a recognised engineering discipline with its own standards. Its heart is one move: never give a prediction without its doubt, and split that doubt into its two very different kinds. Each box answers a question:

  • What is the single best guess? The naked number (8.0 years). Useful - but on its own, a lie of omission.
  • What is the honest range? The full spread of what could really happen (4 to 12 years - and notice the pipe failed at 4, the very bottom edge the "8.0" hid). This is the truth.
  • How much of the doubt is irreducible randomness? The aleatoric part - natural scatter you cannot remove no matter how much you study (one casting differs from the next). The answer here tells you to design a margin for it and live with it.
  • How much of the doubt is just ignorance? The epistemic part - a gap in your knowledge that more data would close (you never measured this pipe). The answer here is a to-do list: go and shrink it.
  • So what do we DO? The whole point: knowing which kind dominates, you either add margin (aleatoric) or go and measure (epistemic). UQ is not a description - it is a decision.

And keep one deeper doubt in view: sometimes the biggest gap is not in the numbers you fed the model, but in the model itself - the corrosion equation may simply be wrong for this case. That, too, is ignorance you can investigate. A good engineer is sceptical of the model, not just its inputs.

Does it scale? Just as Onalenna must state the pipe's life not as "8 years" but as a range with its sources of doubt named, an aerospace engineer states a wing's fatigue life with its uncertainty split - the scatter in the metal (aleatoric) apart from the doubt in the load models (epistemic) - because lives ride on telling those two apart.

T4: Your Turn

Now make one of your own predictions honest. Pick one: how long your commute takes, how long your phone battery will last today, your mark on the next test.

  1. The naked number - your single guess ("my commute takes 25 minutes").
  2. The honest range - what it could really be (15 to 50 minutes).
  3. The aleatoric part - the irreducible randomness (the luck of the traffic lights, other drivers). What margin do you build for it? ("I always leave 10 minutes early.")
  4. The epistemic part - the ignorance you could fix (roadworks or an accident you do not yet know about). What is your to-do list? ("Check the traffic before I leave.")
  5. The decision - for tomorrow: do you add buffer, or go and check something? Often, both.

T5: The Guardrails

Uncertainty quantification is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust a prediction - yours or a model's.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• False precision. Reporting "8.015 years" as if the extra decimals were real. Spurious precision is a lie dressed as rigour - it implies a certainty you do not have. State the range, not a fake-exact point.

• Confusing the two actions. Trying to measure away irreducible scatter (you cannot - it is aleatoric), or piling on a huge, costly safety margin to cover a gap a single cheap measurement would have closed (lazy and expensive - that was epistemic). Match the action to the kind of doubt.

• Forgetting the model itself can be wrong. The biggest ignorance is often not in the inputs but in the model's own equations being a poor fit for this case. Be sceptical of the model, not just the numbers you feed it.

• Trusting a confident-looking output. A polished graph from a powerful programme feels like truth. It is still only as good as its assumptions and its data. Pretty output is not proven output.

• Never stating the range at all. Handing over the bare best guess and letting everyone treat it as certain. If you do not show the doubt, the doubt does not disappear - it just ambushes you later, as it ambushed Onalenna.

The pipe did not betray Onalenna - a single confident number did, by hiding every doubt that mattered. Now you hold the discipline that tells the whole truth: give the range, not just the guess; split the doubt into the scatter you design for and the ignorance you go and erase; and let that tell you what to do next. Predict like that, and reality will far more often land where you said it would - and when it surprises you, you will already know why.
