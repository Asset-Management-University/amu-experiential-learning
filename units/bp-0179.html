We trust each other - so why write anything down?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and Tank You has the strongest foundation you can imagine: you and your brother Ibraaheem, a handshake, and total trust. You've never written a word down about who owns what or who decides what - why would you? You're brothers. You agree on everything. The trust IS the agreement.

Then you watch it happen to someone else. Two friends you know - Themba and Lwazi - started a delivery business the same way you did: inseparable, a handshake, "we'll sort it out as we go." Then they landed a big contract, and real money walked in the door. Suddenly the questions they'd never asked all arrived at once: Themba felt he'd earned more because he worked every weekend; Lwazi felt he'd earned more because the contract came through his cousin. There was nothing written to settle it. They fought, the client got nervous and pulled out, and now the business is dead - and so is the friendship. They don't speak.

You drive home with a stone in your stomach. Because you and Ibraaheem have nothing written either. Today you agree on everything. But what about the day Tank You lands its big contract? The day one of you is sick for months, or wants to move away, or you simply can't agree? You realise the handshake isn't proof of your trust - it's a hidden crack running right through the thing you love most.

We trust each other completely - so writing down "who owns what" feels like an insult. But I just watched trust alone destroy two friends. How do we protect our partnership BEFORE something tests it?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your instinct is to do nothing - to trust the bond, the way Themba and Lwazi trusted theirs. "We're brothers, we'll be fine" feels like loyalty. But that's the exact sentence the ruined friends would have said a year ago. The other instinct - wait until a disagreement actually erupts, then sort it out - is worse: by then you're on opposite sides, and the very thing you never defined has become the weapon you fight with.

Before you read on: if doing nothing is a trap and fighting-it-out-later is a trap, what's left? When is the RIGHT time to settle the rules of a partnership - and what exactly would you need to settle?

A handshake feels like proof of our trust - but my friends had a handshake too, and it didn't survive their first big payday. The silence costs nothing now; it costs everything the day money or a hard choice arrives.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The fight that killed Themba and Lwazi was never really about the weekends or the cousin - it was the un-agreed rules finally coming due, all at once, on the worst possible day. The way to never have that fight is to settle the rules on the BEST possible day: now, while you're calm and you agree on everything.

  • A handshake hides a crack: you build on trust and 'we'll sort it out as we go', and while things are easy the silence costs nothing. But the unanswered questions don't disappear - they wait. When real money or a hard choice arrives, the AMBIGUITY ITSELF becomes the fight.
  • It's not distrust - it's relationship insurance: you don't insure your home because you expect a fire; you insure it to protect what you love from the unexpected. A clear agreement protects the friendship, not just the business. Writing it down is the deepest respect, not suspicion.
  • Settle the four questions on a calm day: (1) OWNERSHIP - what share each holds, and how it's earned. (2) CONTROL - who has the final say on which decisions. (3) CONTRIBUTION - what each must do. (4) CHANGES - what happens if someone leaves, slacks, or you deadlock.
  • Earn your share over time (vesting): don't hand over half the future forever on day one. Let each person EARN their share over a few years by staying and contributing - so someone who leaves early takes only what they've earned, and the one who stays to do the work is protected.
  • Name a tie-breaker before you're tied: two equal partners can deadlock with no way forward - so agree NOW how a stuck decision gets unstuck (a trusted third person, a defined casting vote). Settle it calm, so it never becomes the fight.

Hold onto the second idea, because it's what makes the whole thing possible: this is not distrust - it's relationship insurance. You don't take out insurance on your home because you expect it to burn; you do it to protect what you love from a future nobody can predict. Writing down your partnership's rules is the same - and it's the deepest respect you can show your brother, not a sign you doubt him. The strongest trust isn't the kind that refuses to plan; it's the kind that's reinforced with clarity, so no future storm can ever crack it.

(One note: a one-page plain agreement you both understand and sign is the start. For a venture that grows large or holds big money, have someone qualified check it too - but the thinking here is yours to do, today.)

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad doesn't bury the worry. He brings it to Ibraaheem - gently, and as an act of love.

See the conversation, and the one page that protects a brotherhood

How he opens it (this is the hard part - so he plans the words): "I saw what happened to Themba and Lwazi, and it made me realise our partnership is the most valuable thing we have. I want to protect it. Let's write down our rules now, while we're calm and agree on everything, so a future problem can never come between us."

What killed the friends - and how the brothers answer each piece:

• Ownership: the friends never agreed shares. The brothers write 50/50 - but vested over 4 years: each earns his half by staying and contributing, so if one walked away after three months he wouldn't take half of everything the other built.

• Control: the friends both thought they had the final word. The brothers split it: Muhammad has the final say on operations, Ibraaheem on the money. Clear lanes, no collisions.

• Contribution: they write down what each is expected to do, so "you're not pulling your weight" can never become a vague, festering grudge.

• Changes: if one wants out, the other can buy his share at a pre-agreed fair basis. And if they ever deadlock, a trusted uncle they both respect casts the deciding vote.

The result: one page, signed on a good day, locked in a drawer they may never open again. They didn't weaken their bond by writing it - they made it unbreakable. The questions that destroyed their friends can never ambush THEM, because they were answered while love, not money, was in the room.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The brothers' trust didn't need protecting from each other - it needed protecting from an unknowable future. And the page that did it cost them nothing but one calm, loving conversation. For anything you build with someone else - a business, a side-hustle with a friend, even a family money arrangement:

  1. Settle it on a calm day, not a stormy one. Have the conversation while you still agree on everything - never wait for the fight, because by then the silence has become the weapon.
  2. Answer the four questions, in writing. Who owns what share (earned over time, not gifted forever on day one); who has the final say on which decisions; what each must contribute; and what happens if someone leaves, slacks, or you deadlock.
  3. Frame it as protection, and use the words. "I value this too much not to protect it - let's agree our rules while we're calm." Open it as love, because that's what it is.
