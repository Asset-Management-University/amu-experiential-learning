Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Noluthando, head of maintenance at a factory that makes school uniforms - proud to be trusted with keeping all the machines running. You have a workshop, a budget for parts, and a system for people to report problems. But you feel like you're drowning, and it isn't the workload - it's being set up to fail.

The operators run the sewing machines too fast to hit their targets, ignoring the warning lights. The buying team gets a bonus for saving money, so they order the cheapest needles and thread, which snap and jam the machines. Nobody reports a strange rattle - they wait until the motor seizes, smoke pouring out. And when a machine breaks and stops a whole line, everyone looks at you: it's your failure, you're the Head of Maintenance. You spend 80% of your time cleaning up messes you didn't make and 20% trying to prevent the next, and every improvement you propose - even a simple pre-start check for operators - dies the moment someone says, "that's Noluthando's job, not ours."

I was given this job and the budget for it - so why does it keep failing, and why does giving me more authority only make people work around me harder?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to ask for more power - if it's your job, give you the authority to do it properly, the final say on new parts. But the buying team just reorders the cheap stuff under a different code, calling it 'consumables'; you can't watch every operator; you become the enemy who gets in the way of their targets. So you try to work harder - stay late, service every machine yourself before it can break - but you're one person holding back a tide, and the day you take off, the system floods and the blame lands back on your desk. Finally you try reporting people - the box of broken cheap needles, the logbook showing the same machine failing after the same shift - and it only makes things worse: you're the complainer, trust collapses, and now faults are hidden from you even more carefully, so you find out later still.

The job keeps failing no matter how much power or effort you pour in. So before you read on: what does that tell you about whether this is a job that can be owned by one person at all?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

It keeps failing because it was designed to fail: it was never a task one person could own. Looking after the tools you all depend on is a shared way of working, wrongly squeezed into one job description - and the decisions that truly help or harm the machines are made everywhere else, by people taught it isn't their concern. The fix is never a stronger lone owner; it's making it everyone's, led from the top. Five moves:

  • See the trap - a shared job squeezed into one owner: an important, ongoing job that everyone depends on got 'handled' by handing it to one person with a budget and a problem-log - and everyone thought it sorted. But its fate is decided by everyone else's daily choices, who believe it's not their concern; that is exactly why it fails no matter how hard the one owner works. This is the opposite of a thing that has simply fallen in a gap and needs one clear owner (bp-0108) - this is a responsibility that must be shared, not singled out.
  • More power for the owner is not the fix: the tempting answer - if it's your job, give you the authority to enforce it - is the trap. You cannot police every decision everyone makes; you become the enemy who blocks their targets, and people route around you (reordering under a different code, hiding faults harder). You cannot bolt a shared way of working onto unwilling people through one person, however empowered. The shape of the job, not the strength of the owner, is what's wrong.
  • Make it a leadership priority, said aloud: it becomes everyone's only when the most senior people say, plainly and often, that it matters and is part of how we all work - 'we can't deliver to the people who count on us with broken machines; looking after them is part of everyone's job'. Not a poster slogan, but the message that opens meetings and guides decisions. Leadership naming it is what gives every other move its weight and gives everyone permission to care.
  • Build a small piece into every role: don't centralise it - distribute it: put one specific, doable part of the responsibility into each person whose choices have an impact. The operator does a five-minute pre-start check and is praised for reporting a noise early; the buying team's goal shifts from 'cheapest price' to 'best lifetime value'; the team leader checks the checks are done. The way of working then lives in the work itself, in everyone's hands - not locked in one office.
  • Remove the fear, and show the cost of neglect: people hide problems when reporting them brings blame - so make it safe to surface a fault early: thank the report, treat the repair as a routine success, not a failure. And make the real cost of neglect visible to all - a simple wall chart of hours lost to breakdowns, the day a hidden fault caused a four-hour stoppage costing R5,000 and delayed an order - so everyone sees the old way hurt the mission and the shared way helps it. (Honest trade-off: slower, asks busy people to change, never 'finished' - a culture is kept alive, not ticked off.)

See it lift the weight off Noluthando. Leadership names equipment-care as part of everyone's job; operators get a five-minute pre-start check and praise for early reports; the buying team's goal shifts to best lifetime value; a wall chart shows hours lost to breakdowns, including the day a hidden fault cost R5,000 and delayed a school's order. She stops being a lone firefighter and becomes the guide of a way of working the whole factory shares - and the machines, at last, stop failing. Some jobs can't be delegated to a department and forgotten - they're a shared way of working the whole place must own; the decisions that help or harm it are made everywhere else, so don't give the lone owner more power (it overwhelms them and invites everyone to route around them) - make it a leadership priority said aloud, build a small piece into every role, remove the fear that hides problems, and make the cost of neglect visible. A culture is kept alive, not ticked off.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a factory - any shared job dumped on one person breaks the same way. Watch a family where one person is "in charge of the budget".

See the same pattern around a family's money

One person tracks all the spending, worries about the bills, and nags everyone else to spend less - while the others keep spending as they please, assuming it's "being handled". The budget-owner feels stressed and resentful, and the shared goal (a holiday in August) never gets closer. The fix is not for them to get stricter or hide the bank cards - it's for the family to:

• Agree a shared goal, said aloud - "we all want that holiday in August".
• Build a piece into every role - each person has a clear, agreed amount for their own weekly spending.
• Remove the fear - money becomes a calm weekly check-in, not a monthly fight.
• Show the cost (and reward) - a simple chart on the fridge of how much is saved, so everyone sees their shared effort pay off.

The same shape: not a lone owner with more power, but a way of working everyone holds a piece of.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think about your own work or life. Is there an important, shared job - keeping data safe, looking after people's wellbeing, maintaining quality, managing money - that's been pushed into being one person's or one department's chore? Do you see them drowning, cleaning up messes made by others, their improvements met with "that's not my job"?

  1. Stop reaching for the trap. Don't fix it by giving that lone owner more power or telling them to work harder - both fail, because the shape of the job is wrong, not the owner.
  2. Name it as everyone's, from the top, and put a piece in every role. Say out loud that it's a shared priority; build one small, specific, doable part of it into each relevant person's normal work.
  3. Make it safe to report, and show the cost of neglect. Thank early warnings instead of punishing them; find a simple way to show everyone what neglect really costs - and what shared care saves.

Name the shared job, who's been left holding it alone, and the first piece you'd build into someone else's role. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Map the pieces across the roles together.)

If you are that lone owner, hear this: you were not failing through weakness - you were handed an impossible shape of job and told it was yours alone, and no amount of effort can carry what was built to be shared. And the people who shrugged "not my job" were not villains; they were simply never asked to own a piece, and were taught it wasn't their concern. The fix indicts the design, not the people - which is why it's both fairer and kinder: it lifts an unbearable weight off one person and shares it as it always should have been. Keep it alive as the way you all agree to work, and you stop setting one person up to fail - and you protect, properly and together, the thing everyone was depending on all along.
