Step 1 of 5

Three suspects, one line I can only stop twice, and testing them one at a time would take nine weeks and still miss the answer - how do I test all three at once?

T1: The Ache

Ditsobotla Coatings powder-coats steel shelving for a retail chain. Fourteen per cent of it comes off the line rejected - blistering, patchy adhesion, colour flaws - and every rejected panel is stripped and run again, so the reject rate is also the reason the line is behind.

You are the process engineer. Three things are suspected, by three different people, all of them experienced:

The line supervisor says it is the surface preparation - the current wipe-down is not enough and the panels need blasting. The oven technician says it is the cure temperature - 165 degrees is marginal and always has been. The quality inspector says it is film thickness - the guns are laying it on too thin to flow properly.

None of them is guessing. Each has watched this line for years and each has a reason.

And the constraint that shapes everything: you can stop the line for a controlled trial twice before the retail order ships. Not nine times. Twice.

So the plan everyone expects is the obvious one - test the suspects one at a time, properly, changing nothing else. Blast a batch and see. Then turn the oven up and see. Then thicken the film and see. It is careful, it is clean, it is what good practice sounds like. It is also three trials you do not have, and it has a worse problem than that which nobody in the room has mentioned, because nobody has thought of it.

Three suspects and two chances to stop the line. How do I test all three at once - and how would I ever see it if two of them only matter together?

Before you read on, plan the trial. Three suspects, three experienced people, 14 per cent rejects, and the line can stop twice. Write down how you would actually run it - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four plans, and what each one can and cannot find

• "One at a time, changing nothing else." Textbook, careful, and the plan almost everybody writes - it is what "controlled" means to most of us. It needs three trials where two exist, and it has a deeper fault: it can only ever see each suspect on its own, against whatever the others happen to be set at. If blasting only helps when the oven is hot, a blast trial run at the current cold setting will report almost nothing and the supervisor will be told he was wrong.

• "Change all three at once and see if rejects drop." Fast, fits the two stops, and it will very likely work - and afterwards you will not know which of the three did it, or whether one of them is costing money for nothing. A fix you cannot attribute is a fix you cannot defend when somebody proposes removing part of it.

• "Go with the most experienced voice." Faster still, and it decides by seniority. All three are experienced; that is the actual problem. And the person overruled will be right about their own suspect roughly a third of the time, and will remember.

• "Analyse the historical production data instead of running a trial." Genuinely worth doing and it cannot settle this: nothing on this line was ever varied deliberately, so preparation, temperature and thickness have all drifted together with the season and the shift, and no amount of arithmetic separates what was never separated.

• The gap under all four: each treats the three suspects as three separate questions. But the answer might not be a suspect at all - it might be a pair. "Blasting helps, but only once the oven is hot enough to cure what it exposed" is a sentence no single-suspect trial can produce, in either direction, no matter how carefully it is run. If that is what is happening, one-at-a-time testing will report that two of the three suspects do almost nothing.

T2: The Tool in Action

Give each suspect two settings you can actually hold. Build the grid of every combination and run them in a scrambled order. Then read each suspect's effect using all the runs at once - and go looking for the pair whose members only matter together. Watch it run on the coating line.

Step 1 - Name each suspect and the two settings you will actually run it at

Nomsa Wessels writes the three suspects with the settings the line can actually hold: surface preparation, either the current wipe-down or a full abrasive blast; cure oven temperature, 165 or 185 degrees; film thickness, 60 or 90 microns. She resists two temptations out loud. She does not add a fourth suspect, because every one doubles the work. And she does not test four temperatures, because she is not yet asking what the best temperature is - she is asking whether temperature matters at all, and two settings answer that.

Step 2 - Build the grid of every combination, and run them in a scrambled order

Eight runs: wipe/165/60, wipe/165/90, wipe/185/60, wipe/185/90, blast/165/60, blast/165/90, blast/185/60, blast/185/90. Nomsa numbers them and draws the order out of a mug: 5, 2, 7, 1, 8, 3, 6, 4. The line supervisor asks why they are not doing all the blast runs together, which would be far easier to set up - and that is exactly why: doing them together would mean every blast run happened in the afternoon, and any afternoon effect would arrive in the results wearing the blast's name.

Step 3 - For each suspect, compare the average of its high runs against its low runs

Reject rates across the eight runs: 18, 15, 11, 9, 14, 12, 3, 2 per cent. Preparation: the four blast runs average 7.75 against the four wipe runs at 13.25 - blasting takes about 5.5 points off. Temperature: the four hot runs average 6.25 against 14.75 cold - about 8.5 points. Thickness: 9.5 against 11.5 - about 2 points, the weakest of the three. Every one of those numbers uses all eight runs, which is the part worth stopping over: eight runs have just measured three suspects, where testing one at a time would have needed the line stopped three times to learn less.

Step 4 - Now look for the thing one-at-a-time testing cannot see: does one suspect depend on another?

Nomsa looks at preparation twice. On the cold runs, blasting takes rejects from 16.5 to 13.0 - about 3.5 points, useful. On the hot runs, blasting takes them from 10.0 to 2.5 - 7.5 points, more than twice as much. So blasting and heat are not two separate improvements to be added up: the blast only really pays once the oven is hot enough to cure what it exposed. That is the finding, and it is the one nobody could have reached from three separate trials - a colleague who tested blasting alone on the current cold setting would have reported a modest 3.5 points and moved on.

Step 5 - Set the combination the grid points to, and confirm it before you believe it

The grid says blast, hot, thin: run 7, at 3 per cent. Nomsa does not report 3 per cent. She runs that combination six more times over two days and gets a mean of 4.1 per cent against the line's baseline of 14, and that is the number in the report - lower than the baseline by nearly ten points, and honestly higher than the single lucky cell. She also writes what the grid does not cover: it says nothing about 200 degrees or about a thickness of 40, because those were never run, and anybody proposing them is proposing a new test rather than reading this one.

See what eight runs did that three trials could not. Each suspect's effect was measured using all eight runs rather than a third of them - and then the finding that mattered turned out not to be a suspect at all. Blasting takes 3.5 points off a cold line and 7.5 off a hot one, so the blast only really pays once the oven is hot enough to cure what it exposed. That sentence is the answer, and every plan in the room would have missed it. Worse than missed it: a blast trial run properly at the current cold setting would have reported a modest 3.5 points, and the supervisor - who was right - would have been told his suspect was minor. Then the confirmation: the best cell of the grid read 3 per cent and the confirmed figure is 4.1, and 4.1 is what goes in the report, because one run out of eight will be the luckiest of eight about as often as it is the truth.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the fourth is the one that pays for the method.

  • 1. Two settings per suspect - specific values you can hold, far enough apart to show a real effect. Not a range, and not five settings of one thing.
  • 2. Every combination, in a scrambled order - so that anything drifting through the day cannot attach itself to one suspect.
  • 3. Read each suspect by averaging its high runs against its low runs; the others cancel out because each appears in both halves.
  • 4. Look for the pair - is a suspect's effect the same under both settings of another? If not, you have the answer.
  • 5. Confirm the chosen combination with repeat runs, and report the confirmed figure rather than the best cell.

This is a designed experiment, and the shape you built - every combination of three things at two settings - is a full factorial, written 23 because that is how the eight runs arise. Each suspect is a factor and each setting a level. The comparison in move 3 is a factor's main effect, and it works because the design is balanced - every level of every factor meets every level of the others equally often, so their influences cancel instead of contaminating each other. Scrambling the run order is randomisation, and it exists to stop time itself becoming an uncontrolled factor. What move 4 finds is an interaction, and it is the entire reason to prefer this to testing one thing at a time: an interaction is invisible to one-factor-at-a-time testing by construction, and when one exists, that testing will report the interacting factors as weak.

It fits any situation with a handful of suspects you can genuinely set, a measurable outcome, and fewer chances to test than suspects to test - which is most real investigations. Its limits are worth stating. It tells you about the settings you ran and nothing about settings you did not, so extrapolating past your levels is inventing. It needs the outcome to be measurable on the runs you have, so a defect appearing once a month is not amenable to an eight-run grid. And it grows fast: a fourth factor doubles the runs to sixteen, which is the pressure that leads people to drop the confirmation step - and the confirmation step is the one that keeps you honest.

T4: Your Turn

Take a problem you have with more than one suspect - a reject rate, a delay, a failure, a complaint, a yield. Write each suspect down with two settings you could genuinely hold for a run. Build the grid of every combination. Then, before you run anything, write down which pair you would be least surprised to find only matters together - and see whether the grid agrees with you.

Two disciplines decide whether the grid tells the truth. First, scramble the run order, and hold the line when somebody objects - and they will, because running all the settings of one factor together is far easier to set up and everybody can see it. That convenience is precisely the danger: it makes one factor's runs share a time of day, a batch of material, an operator, and whatever those contribute arrives in your results carrying that factor's name. The awkwardness of the scrambled order is the whole protection. Second, always run the confirmation, and report its figure rather than the best cell. The best of eight runs is chosen partly for being lucky, and a number selected for being the lowest will not be reproduced - so a report built on it will fail publicly a fortnight later, on the shop floor, in front of the people who told you what they suspected.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the trial honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Testing one suspect at a time cannot see a pair. This is not an efficiency point, it is a blind spot: where two factors only matter together, single-factor trials will report both as weak and the people who suspected them as wrong.

• Run order chosen for convenience. Grouping a factor's runs together makes them share a time, a batch and an operator. Scramble it, and expect to be argued with.

• Too many settings of one factor. Two levels answers "does this matter". Finding the best value is a later, smaller question - and five levels of one thing spends the runs you needed for the other suspects.

• An extra factor added late. Each one doubles the grid. If a fourth suspect is genuinely credible, that is a decision about the size of the trial, made before it starts and not during it.

• The best cell reported as the result. One run out of eight is as likely to be lucky as true. Confirm the chosen combination and publish the confirmed figure.

• Extrapolating past the settings you ran. The grid describes 165 and 185 degrees. It says nothing whatever about 200, and proposing 200 on its strength is proposing a new trial while calling it a conclusion.

• The people who named the suspects left out of the result. They watched the line for years and their suspicions shaped the grid. Tell them what their factor did, including when it did little - and especially when an interaction shows they were right in a way a single trial would have denied.

You had three experienced people, three credible suspects, fourteen per cent rejects, and two chances to stop the line. You can put one page up instead: eight runs in a scrambled order, all three suspects measured from the same eight, and the finding none of the obvious plans could reach - that blasting pays more than twice as much on a hot oven as a cold one, so the two are one decision and not two. Confirmed at 4.1 per cent against a baseline of fourteen. The answer to "which of these three is it?" is sometimes "two of them, together" - and the only way to hear that answer is to ask all three at once. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
