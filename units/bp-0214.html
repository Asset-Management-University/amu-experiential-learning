Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Nolwazi, and you built your small catering business from nothing, one order at a time. You have no advertising budget and no fancy website. What you have is a reputation, and it rests on a single thing everyone says about you: every order is exactly as promised - the same quality, on time, every time. That reputation is why the phone keeps ringing. It is, quite literally, your business.

This morning a huge opportunity lands: a corporate client wants catering for a big event next month - far bigger than anything you have ever handled, and worth more than three ordinary months of work. Winning it would change everything. But saying yes honestly would mean stretching your small team and your suppliers past what you know they can reliably do. To win it, you would have to promise a scale and a standard you are not sure you can deliver - and if you got stretched on the day, the quiet fix would be to substitute cheaper ingredients on a few dishes, or let the finish slip, and hope no one notices.

The pressure is real. The money would ease things you have worried about for months. The shortcut is right there, and a part of you is already reaching for it. And yet something in your gut has gone still, because you can feel that the very thing that would help you win this contract is the thing that could quietly poison everything you have built.

A shortcut that shades the truth or breaks my word would help me win this contract right now - but something says it would cost me more than it gains. What do I actually do?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

This is a real fork, and the pressure to grab the win is powerful and completely human - you need this. Three moves tempt almost every owner here, and each one quietly costs more than it saves.

1. The optimist's gamble: "Just say yes and figure it out later. Everyone over-promises a little." But an over-promise you cannot keep is simply a broken promise with a delay on it. This client's very first experience of you would be a let-down - late, or thinner than you swore - and a first impression like that does not stay private; it travels exactly as far as your good reputation did.

2. The secret shortcut: "Take the job, and if we get stretched, quietly use cheaper ingredients on a few items. No one will notice." Someone always notices, eventually - a guest, a staff member, the client's own eye. And the day they do, the damage is not to one dish; it is that every order you have ever delivered suddenly becomes suspect. You would trade a small saving for the one thing that cannot be bought back.

3. The rigid refusal: "Be bluntly honest - say 'no, we can't', and lose the opportunity entirely." This feels safe and clean, but it confuses honesty with saying no to everything. Honesty does not mean turning away every chance that stretches you; it means being truthful about what you can and cannot do - and there is almost always a truthful path that still finds a way forward.

There is a fourth path, and it is the one that treats honesty and consistency not as a sacrifice but as the smartest business move you have.

Think about the very first experience this new client will have of your business. If it is a let-down - late, or thinner than you promised - that becomes the story they tell about you. A first impression travels exactly as far as your good reputation did.
Your reputation took years of kept promises to build. How long does it take to lose? The day a hidden corner is discovered, the damage is not to one dish - it is that every order you have ever delivered suddenly becomes suspect. A small saving is a ruinous trade.
Is 'no' really the only honest answer? Honesty is not the same as refusing everything that stretches you. There is almost always a truthful 'yes, if...' or 'yes, but only this part, done perfectly' - a path that keeps your word AND finds a way forward.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You are not choosing between doing right and doing well. For a business built on reputation, they are the same choice - and seeing that is the whole unlock. Five moves:

  • 1. See trust as your core asset: Your repeat clients, your referrals, the phone that keeps ringing - all of it runs on trust. It is the one asset a competitor cannot buy and marketing cannot manufacture; it is earned. Before you weigh the contract, recognise what the contract is really made of.
  • 2. Practise costly honesty: Say what is true even when it costs you the full contract today. Do not promise a scale or a standard you only hope to reach - tell the client your real capacity, and offer what you can deliver perfectly rather than what you might deliver poorly.
  • 3. Make consistency your brand: Do what you said you would do, every single time. Reliability is trust made visible - the same word kept and the same quality delivered, order after order. One brilliant order and one broken one do not average out; the broken one is what gets remembered.
  • 4. Pay the trust tax upfront: The short-term cost of honesty - a smaller first order, the more expensive real ingredient, the contract you did not over-reach for - is not a loss. It is the price of an uncopyable reputation, paid upfront, and it compounds. The cheap shortcut is the most expensive thing you can do.
  • 5. When you slip, own it fast and fix it: You will make honest mistakes. Trust survives a mistake that is owned quickly and put right - that can even deepen it. Trust does not survive a mistake that was hidden and later discovered. Speed and honesty in the repair matter as much as the promise itself.

The whole thing lives in one line: honesty and consistency are not a constraint on your business - they ARE your business. The trust they build is your most valuable asset precisely because it is the one thing a competitor cannot copy and marketing cannot buy - it is earned, slowly, by telling the truth and keeping your word, order after order. A shortcut does not just risk one contract; it spends the asset that wins you every contract. The honest, reliable path is not the nice path instead of the smart path. On a reputation business, it is the smart path.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own business, watch the same opportunity play out two ways - and see that the difference is not luck, but which asset each owner chose to protect.

The same big contract, two caterers, two futures

The first caterer takes the shortcut. She says yes to the whole thing, over-promising on a scale she cannot reliably reach. On the day, stretched to breaking, her team quietly cuts corners - cheaper ingredients here, a rushed finish there. The food goes out late and uneven. She gets paid, once. But the client never calls again, sends no referrals, and mentions the disappointment to two others in the same industry. Her team, made to deliver work they were not proud of, is demoralised. She won the contract and lost the asset - and the asset was worth far more than the contract.

The second caterer protects the trust. She is honest about her real capacity: "I cannot deliver the whole event to my standard yet - but I can deliver the main course for two hundred guests flawlessly, and I would rather do one thing perfectly than everything poorly." The client, who has been let down by big over-promisers before, is quietly stunned by the honesty. She delivers that smaller order exactly as promised - on time, to the letter. The client is so impressed by the truthfulness and the reliability that they hand her the catering for every event that follows. She did not win the big contract that day. She won the client for years - by refusing to spend the one thing that cannot be replaced.

Same opportunity. Same pressure. Opposite futures - decided entirely by whether each one protected the trust or spent it.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Trust is not built in the big moment - it is built in a thousand small ones, long before the big contract ever calls. This is a capability you lay down now, while it is calm:

  1. Name your promise. Write down, in one plain sentence, exactly what your customers trust you for - the quality, the timing, the price, the thing they can count on every single time. You cannot protect a promise you have never made explicit.
  2. Never promise what you cannot consistently deliver. Before you say yes to anything, ask not "can I do this once?" but "can I do this every time, to my standard?" Promise a little less than you can do, and deliver a little more - the opposite of the over-promiser, and the whole secret of a reputation that grows.
  3. Practise the honest hard word small. The next time a small truth is awkward to tell - a slight delay, a genuine mistake, a limit you have reached - tell it, plainly and early, rather than hiding it and hoping. Build the muscle on the small things, so it is strong and steady on the day something big is on the line.

Trust is the one asset no competitor can copy, no advertising can buy, and no quick win is worth spending. Protect it, order after order, truthful word after kept promise, and you are not just being good - you are building the most durable business there is. That is you, being the change.
