Worth for what

T1: The Ache

The board asks a simple question: what is our asset base worth? And nobody in the room can give a straight answer - or rather, everyone gives a different one. Finance quotes the book value from the ledger. The engineers say it would cost far more than that to rebuild. The insurer's schedule shows a third figure again. Four numbers for the same plant, and no one can say which is right.

Then the real problem surfaces. The plant is insured for its book value - the number in the accounts. And the book value is a fraction of what it would cost to actually rebuild the plant if it burned. If there is ever a serious fire, the payout will not come close to replacing what was lost, and a hole will open in the finances that no fire caused - the wrong number was simply used for the question that mattered.

The mistake was believing the asset base had a value - one true number - when in truth its worth depends entirely on the question you are asking of it.

Four people give four different values for the same plant, and the one we insured on is dangerously wrong. How do I put a value on an asset - and know it is the right value for what I actually need it for?

Before you read on, decide. Four people, four values for the same plant. The accountant says R14 million — cost less depreciation. The insurer's schedule carries that same figure. An engineer says it would cost R40 million to build it again today. A broker says the second-hand market for plant this specialised is essentially nil. The argument in the room is about which one is right, and everybody is being sincere. Then it lands: you are insured on the book value. If there is a serious fire, the payout will not come close to replacing what was lost, and a hole will open in the finances that no fire caused. Write down how you would value this properly — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the argument cannot be settled

• “Get a professional valuation and use that number.” They will ask you what it is for. If you cannot say, you will receive a number produced for somebody else's question and use it for yours.

• “Take the highest — it is the most cautious.” Right for the insurance and wrong everywhere else: put full replacement cost on the books and you overstate the business, distort every return calculated on it and pay for cover you may not need on assets you would not replace.

• “Average them.” Produces a number that is correct for no question at all, and does so with a reassuring appearance of balance.

• “The accountant's figure is the official one.” It is official for the accounts, and it is the figure currently insuring you, which is exactly how the hole gets made. Official for one purpose is not correct for another.

• “Fix the insurance and move on.” Fixes the instance you happened to notice and leaves the practice intact — the next number to travel without its purpose will do the same thing somewhere else.

• The argument cannot be settled because the question is malformed. An asset does not have one true value waiting in the ledger to be read; it has a value for a purpose, and the number genuinely changes with the question. The same plant is worth one thing to insure, another on the books, another to sell, another to keep — and none is the real value with the others as fakes. All four of your people are right, and the dispute is unanswerable until somebody names the purpose. So the first move in any valuation is never “what is it worth” but “worth for what” — because the purpose, with the nature of the asset, selects the method, and the wrong method for the purpose produces a confident, precise, wrong number that carries no warning label. The approaches ask three different questions: what it would cost to have it, what somebody would pay for it, and what it will earn. Your broker has just told you the second is unavailable here — which is why, for specialised plant, replacement cost adjusted for age and condition is the workable basis. And whatever you produce, make it carry its basis with it, or it will eventually be used for a question it was never fit to answer. As it just was.

Here is how you value an asset properly.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you put an honest value on an asset - asking worth-for-what first, choosing among the three approaches, using depreciated replacement cost where it fits, matching method to purpose, and stating the basis.

1. There is no single value - only value for a purpose

THIS IS THE WHOLE UNIT IN ONE IDEA, AND IT IS THE THING TO UN-LEARN: AN ASSET DOES NOT HAVE ONE TRUE VALUE SITTING IN THE LEDGER WAITING TO BE READ. IT HAS A VALUE FOR A PURPOSE, and the number genuinely changes with the question you are asking of it. The same pump is worth one thing TO INSURE (what would it cost to replace if it were destroyed?), another ON THE BOOKS (what did it cost, less depreciation?), another TO SELL (what would a buyer actually pay?), and another TO KEEP (what future value does it earn?). Here is the crucial point that trips people: NONE OF THESE IS THE 'REAL' VALUE WITH THE OTHERS AS FAKES - they are ALL real, each correct for its own question, and arguing about which single number is right is unanswerable until someone names the purpose. SO THE FIRST MOVE IN ANY VALUATION IS NEVER 'WHAT IS IT WORTH?' IT IS 'WORTH FOR WHAT?' - because the purpose, together with the nature of the asset, SELECTS THE METHOD you will use, and USING THE WRONG METHOD FOR THE PURPOSE PRODUCES A CONFIDENT, PRECISE, WRONG NUMBER. Insure a plant at its book value and you are catastrophically under-insured the day it burns; put a full replacement cost on a sale and no buyer ever comes; value a revenue asset at scrap and you give it away. The discipline is not a formula - it is the habit of pinning down the purpose FIRST, because once the purpose is clear the right method almost chooses itself, and the number that follows can be trusted. Ask worth-for-what, and the rest is craft.

2. The three approaches - three different questions

THERE ARE THREE FAMILIES OF VALUATION METHOD, AND EACH ANSWERS A FUNDAMENTALLY DIFFERENT QUESTION ABOUT THE ASSET - LEARN THEM AS THREE QUESTIONS, NOT THREE FORMULAS. THE COST APPROACH ASKS: WHAT WOULD IT COST TO REPLACE THIS ASSET'S SERVICE? It comes in two forms - REPLACEMENT COST (the cost of a substitute of comparable utility at today prices, usually built with today better technology) and REPRODUCTION COST (the cost of an exact duplicate of the original, same design and materials). The cost approach is where you turn when you must answer 'what would it take to put this capability back?'. THE MARKET APPROACH ASKS: WHAT HAVE SIMILAR ASSETS ACTUALLY SOLD FOR? It reads real transaction prices for comparable assets from auctions, dealers and equipment databases, and it is the most intuitive and transparent approach when it can be used - BUT IT NEEDS A GENUINE, ACTIVE MARKET of comparable transactions to read, which exists for vehicles, forklifts, standard tools, and does not exist for a bespoke process vessel. THE INCOME APPROACH ASKS: WHAT FUTURE CASH WILL THIS ASSET GENERATE? It forecasts the asset future earnings and discounts them to a value today, and it only makes sense for assets that INDIVIDUALLY EARN REVENUE - a toll road, a leased unit, a rental fleet. Three questions, three answers, and often three different numbers for the same asset. THE ART IS KNOWING WHICH QUESTION YOUR PURPOSE IS REALLY ASKING, and therefore which approach has anything true to say - because an approach applied where it does not fit (a market approach with no market, an income approach on a non-earning asset) will still produce a number, and the number will be meaningless.

3. The cost approach and DRC - the asset manager's workhorse

HERE IS THE PUNCHLINE FOR PHYSICAL ASSETS, AND THE REASON THE COST APPROACH MATTERS MOST IN A PLANT. MOST SPECIALISED INDUSTRIAL ASSETS - a process pump, a bespoke pressure vessel, a section of buried pipeline - ARE RARELY TRADED (so there is no market of comparable sales to read) AND DO NOT INDIVIDUALLY EARN CASH (so there is no income stream to discount). The market and income approaches simply have nothing to bite on. For these, THE ONLY HONEST APPROACH IS THE COST ONE: value the asset by what it would cost to replace its SERVICE. You start with the REPLACEMENT COST - the current cost of a modern equivalent that does the same job - and then, because the asset in front of you is not new, you take off for what it has lost, which gives DEPRECIATED REPLACEMENT COST (DRC). NOW THE SUBTLE, IMPORTANT PART: WHAT YOU TAKE OFF IS OBSOLESCENCE, AND OBSOLESCENCE IS BROADER THAN ACCOUNTING DEPRECIATION. It is three things at once: PHYSICAL deterioration (the wear and age of use); FUNCTIONAL obsolescence (a better technology now exists, so the old design is worth less even while it still works - a slower, less efficient machine); and ECONOMIC or EXTERNAL obsolescence (something outside the asset has reduced its worth - the demand for its output has fallen, a regulation has changed, the site is closing). Accounting depreciation is a simple formula applied to historical cost for the books; DRC OBSOLESCENCE IS A JUDGEMENT ABOUT THE ASSET REAL REMAINING WORTH, and a machine can be almost new physically and still heavily obsolete functionally or economically. DRC IS THE WORKHORSE OF PHYSICAL-ASSET VALUATION precisely because it gives an honest answer exactly where the market and income approaches fall silent - which, in a specialised plant, is nearly everywhere.

4. Choose by purpose and asset

NOW PUT THE TWO HALVES TOGETHER, BECAUSE CHOOSING IS THE ACTUAL SKILL - THE PURPOSE AND THE ASSET, TOGETHER, PICK THE METHOD. Walk the common purposes. TO INSURE AN ASSET: use REPLACEMENT COST - the undepreciated, full cost of a new equivalent - because if it is destroyed you must buy a whole new one at today price, and you must never depreciate what you will have to replace at full cost (this is the single most expensive valuation mistake there is). FOR THE ASSET REGISTER OR A FINANCIAL FAIR VALUE of specialised plant: use DEPRECIATED REPLACEMENT COST, because it reflects the asset current worth-in-use, not its full-new cost. FOR AN ASSET WITH A REAL SECOND-HAND MARKET - a fleet vehicle, a forklift, a standard compressor: use the MARKET APPROACH, what it would actually fetch. FOR A REVENUE-EARNING ASSET - a toll road, a leased unit: use the INCOME APPROACH. AND FOR SPECIALISED PLANT WITH NO MARKET AND NO DIRECT INCOME - which is MOST of a process plant: the COST APPROACH and DRC. Notice the logic runs the same way every time: name the purpose, look at the nature of the asset (is it traded? does it earn?), and the method that has something true to say is the one you use. CHOOSING WRONGLY IS NOT A ROUNDING ERROR - it is the difference between a plant properly insured and a plant that cannot rebuild itself after a loss, between a fair balance sheet and a fictional one. The value of this whole technique is that it stops you reaching for the number that is nearest (usually the ledger) and makes you reach for the number that is RIGHT for the question.

5. State the basis - and keep it honest

FINALLY, THE HONESTY THAT MUST TRAVEL WITH EVERY VALUATION, BECAUSE A VALUATION CAN BE MISUNDERSTOOD OR MISUSED. FIRST, ITS NATURE: A VALUATION IS AN ESTIMATE BUILT ON STATED ASSUMPTIONS, NOT A FACT. The remaining life, the degree of obsolescence, the condition - these are all JUDGEMENTS, and two honest, competent professionals can look at the same asset and reach different numbers, because they weighed the assumptions differently. That is not a weakness to hide or a failure of rigour; it is the genuine nature of valuation, and pretending a valuation is a hard fact is itself a kind of dishonesty. SO THE PROFESSIONAL STANDARD IS THIS: A PROFESSIONAL STATES THE BASIS OF THEIR VALUATION - THE METHOD, THE ASSUMPTIONS, THE CONDITION - SO THE NUMBER CAN BE UNDERSTOOD AND REVIEWED. A value with no stated basis is a number nobody can check, trust, or challenge; a value WITH its basis ('DRC, replacement cost 2 million, 25-year life, 15 years elapsed, fair condition') is a claim a colleague can examine and either accept or improve. SECOND, THE INTEGRITY LINE, WHICH IS NON-NEGOTIABLE: YOU VALUE AN ASSET TO TELL THE TRUTH ABOUT WHAT IT IS WORTH FOR THE PURPOSE, NOT TO PRODUCE THE NUMBER AN AUDIENCE WANTS. Over-valuing an asset to secure a bigger loan, under-valuing it to reduce a tax or a rate, nudging the assumed life to make a balance sheet look healthier - none of these is clever valuation, and all of them are misstatements that mislead whoever relies on the number, and that eventually surface. The professional answer to 'make it come out to this figure' is to show the basis and let the number be what it honestly is. State the basis, value for the purpose, and tell the truth - that is what makes a valuation worth having.

T3: The Blueprint

An asset does not have a value; it has a value for a purpose. Do not ask "what is this asset worth?" - ask "what is it worth, for this purpose?" Five questions:

  • Worth for what? The purpose comes before the number. The same asset is worth one thing to insure, another on the books, another to sell. The wrong method for the purpose is a confident wrong number.
  • Which family of method? Cost, market, or income - each answers a different question: what to replace it, what it would sell for, what it will earn.
  • How do I value specialised plant? Depreciated replacement cost. Replacement cost less obsolescence - the workhorse when there is no market and no direct income.
  • How do I choose? The purpose and the asset decide. Insure at replacement cost; register at DRC; a traded asset by market; a revenue asset by income.
  • Is the number honest? State the basis. A valuation is an estimate on stated assumptions - name the method, the life, the condition - and value to tell the truth, not to hit a number.

The three approaches - three different questions. There are three families of valuation method, and each answers a fundamentally different question about the asset. The cost approach asks: what would it cost to replace this asset's service? - and it comes in two forms, replacement cost (a substitute of comparable utility at today's prices, usually with today's better technology) and reproduction cost (an exact duplicate). The market approach asks: what have similar assets actually sold for? - it reads real transaction prices for comparable assets, and it is intuitive and transparent, but it needs a genuine, active market of comparables to work (vehicles, common equipment, standard tools). The income approach asks: what future cash will this asset generate? - and discounts those future earnings to a value today, which only makes sense for assets that individually earn revenue. Three questions, three answers, and the art is knowing which question your purpose is really asking.

The cost approach and depreciated replacement cost - the asset manager's workhorse. Here is the punchline for physical assets, and the reason the cost approach matters most in a plant. Most specialised industrial assets - a process pump, a bespoke vessel, a section of buried pipeline - are rarely traded (so there is no market of comparable sales to read) and do not individually earn cash (so there is no income to discount). For these, the only honest approach is the cost one: value them by what it would cost to replace their service. Start with the replacement cost - the current cost of a modern equivalent - and then, because the asset in front of you is not new, take off for what it has lost, giving depreciated replacement cost (DRC). What you take off is obsolescence, and it is important to see that obsolescence is broader than accounting depreciation: it is the physical deterioration (wear and age), plus the functional obsolescence (a better technology now exists, so the old design is worth less even if it works), plus the economic or external obsolescence (a change outside the asset - demand fallen, regulation shifted - has reduced its worth). Accounting depreciation is a simple formula on historical cost; DRC obsolescence is a judgement about the asset's real remaining worth. DRC is the workhorse of asset valuation precisely because it works when the market and income approaches have nothing to bite on.

Choose by purpose and asset. Now put the two halves together, because choosing is the skill. Let the purpose and the asset pick the method. To insure: replacement cost (you must buy a whole new one if it is lost - do not depreciate what you will pay full price to replace). For the register or financial fair value: depreciated replacement cost for specialised plant, because it reflects the asset's current worth-in-use. For an asset with a real second-hand market (a fleet vehicle, a forklift): the market approach - what it would actually fetch. For a revenue-earning asset (a toll road, a leased unit): the income approach. For specialised plant with no market and no direct income - which is most of a process plant: the cost approach and DRC. Choosing wrongly is not a rounding error: it is the difference between a plant properly insured and a plant that cannot rebuild itself after a loss.

One honest boundary - it is an integrity line. A valuation is an estimate built on stated assumptions, not a fact. The remaining life, the obsolescence, the condition - these are judgements, and two honest professionals can reach different numbers. That is not a weakness to hide; it is the nature of the work, and it is why a professional states the basis of their valuation - the method, the assumptions, the condition - so the number can be understood and reviewed. And the line that must never be crossed: you value an asset to tell the truth about what it is worth for the purpose, not to produce the number an audience wants. Over-valuing an asset to secure a bigger loan, or under-valuing it to cut a tax or a rate, is not clever valuation - it is a misstatement that misleads whoever relies on the number, and it will eventually be found out.

Does it scale? A person selling a five-year-old car looks up what similar cars actually sell for (the market approach); the same person insuring that car needs its replacement value, not its resale value; and the same car on a business's books carries yet another figure. One car, three numbers, three purposes - and anyone who insures their home for what they paid for it, rather than what it would cost to rebuild, is making the exact mistake the plant made. Value for a purpose is how the world actually works, once you look.

  1. Insurance value. What value would you insure it for, and which method is that - and why not the market or income approach here?
  2. Register value (DRC). Compute the depreciated replacement cost for the asset register. Show the calculation.
  3. The hole. If the unit were destroyed but had been insured at its historical-cost book value, how big is the shortfall against a full replacement?
  4. The record. Write the one-line valuation statement you would enter in the register - stating the basis (method, assumptions), the value, and the date.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, insurance: R2.0 million, the full replacement cost (the cost approach), because if it is destroyed you must buy a whole new one at today's price - you do not depreciate what you will pay full price to replace. The market approach is out because there is no active market to read; the income approach is out because the unit earns no revenue on its own - so the cost approach is the only one with anything to bite on.

Second, the DRC: it is 15 years into a 25-year life, so 10 years remain. DRC = R2.0m x (10 remaining / 25 total) = R2.0m x 0.4 = R800,000. (A straight-line physical depreciation; a fuller answer would also weigh functional and economic obsolescence, and say so.)

Third, the hole: the historical-cost book value is R1.2m x (10/25) = R480,000. Insured at that, a total loss pays R480,000 while a replacement costs R2.0m - a shortfall of R1.52 million, opened not by the loss but by insuring on the wrong value. That gap is the whole reason this unit exists.

Fourth, the record, stating the basis, for example: "Processing unit, fair value R800,000 as at [date], on a depreciated-replacement-cost basis: replacement cost R2.0m, straight-line over a 25-year life, 15 years elapsed; no material functional or economic obsolescence identified; condition assessed as fair. Basis recorded for review." A real answer names the method, the assumptions and the date - so the next person can understand and challenge it.

T5: The Guardrails

An asset does not have a value; it has a value for a purpose. Your sharpest tool is not a formula - it is knowing which question you are answering before you begin.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Treating one number as "the" value. The ledger figure is one value for one purpose, not the truth of the asset. Ask worth-for-what before you quote any number.

• Insuring at book value. Book value is depreciated historical cost; a total loss must be replaced at today's full price. Insure at replacement cost, or carry a hole you cannot see until the fire.

• Putting replacement cost on a sale. A buyer pays what the asset is worth to them, not what it would cost you to replace. Use the market approach where a market exists.

• A market approach with no market. Comparable prices need comparable, actual transactions. For specialised plant that never trades, the market approach has nothing to read - use the cost approach.

• An income approach on a non-earning asset. Discounting future cash only works for assets that earn cash. A shared process pump does not - do not invent a revenue stream to value it.

• Confusing accounting depreciation with obsolescence. Depreciation is a formula on historical cost; obsolescence (physical, functional, economic) is a judgement about real lost worth. DRC uses the second.

• Hiding the assumptions. A valuation with no stated basis cannot be understood or checked. Always record the method, the life and the condition behind the number.

• Gaming the number. Valuing high for a loan or low for a tax is misstatement, not skill. Value to tell the truth for the purpose; the number that is wanted and the number that is right are not the same thing.

Four people gave four values for the same plant, and the plant was insured on the one that would leave it unable to rebuild - because a single number was treated as the value when value depends entirely on the question. So never ask what an asset is worth; ask what it is worth for the purpose, and let the purpose and the asset choose the method: the cost approach, and depreciated replacement cost, for the specialised plant that has no market and no income; the market approach where real comparable sales exist; the income approach for what genuinely earns; replacement cost, undepreciated, for what you must insure. Take obsolescence broader than accounting depreciation - physical, functional and economic - and remember that a valuation is an estimate built on stated assumptions, so state them. You value an asset to tell the truth about what it is worth for the purpose, not to produce the number an audience wants - and the basis you state is what turns a number into one that can be trusted. The next time someone asks what an asset is worth, do not reach for the ledger; ask them what they need the value for, and only then begin.
