The insurer's auditor arrives in two weeks, and I have a pressure vessel, a pipe and a storage tank with no inspection plan - each following a different rulebook. How do I turn the codes into a defensible plan that says when each is due and what is checked?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday at a road-tanker fuels terminal on the edge of an industrial town. You are the new integrity engineer; the yard smells faintly of diesel and wet steel. In your hand is a printed email from the terminal manager, Lindokuhle, with three lines highlighted: the insurer's auditor arrives in fourteen days, and for every pressure-holding asset on site he wants a written plan saying when each was last inspected, when it is next due, and what will be checked.

You walk the yard with the asset list. There are three things that hold pressure or product, and not one has a plan you could hand an auditor:

  • A horizontal LPG pressure vessel, V-101, built in 2008 - last internal inspection: you cannot find a date.
  • A run of carbon-steel product piping, line P-2, carrying petrol from the pumps to the loading arms.
  • A large welded storage tank, T-300, holding 4 million litres of diesel, last entered eight years ago.

Lindokuhle catches you by the control room, calm but arms folded. "If we fail this audit, the insurer can suspend our cover," she says. "And without cover, the terminal stops loading. That is forty jobs, including yours." Your first instinct is to climb on each asset, look it over, and write down whatever looks worn. But fourteen days is not long - and a vessel, a pipe and a tank are three different beasts with different failure modes and different rules. Guess the intervals and you over-inspect one and dangerously under-inspect another, and the auditor will know the difference.

The auditor arrives in two weeks. How do I write a defensible inspection plan for our vessel, piping and tank, when each one follows a different rulebook?

Before you read on, decide. A vessel last entered eight years ago, a piping circuit and a tank, an auditor in fourteen days, and an insurer who can suspend cover — which stops the terminal and forty jobs, including yours. Write down how you would produce the plan. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what an auditor does with each

• “Climb on each asset, look it over, write down what looks worn.” The honest engineer’s instinct, and it produces a condition report rather than a plan. An auditor is not asking what you saw last week — he is asking what you inspect, how often, why that often, and who is entitled to sign it.

• “Inspect everything now, before he arrives.” Fourteen days, three assets, three different kinds of work — and even done, it answers for one moment in time. A plan is a rule about the future, and a burst of activity is the opposite of evidence that one exists.

• “Put everything on the same interval — simple and consistent.” Tidy, and it is the trap the situation is built around. A vessel, a pipe and a tank are three different beasts with different failure modes, so one interval necessarily over-inspects one and dangerously under-inspects another — and the auditor will know which.

• “Leave the overdue vessel out until we’ve caught up.” Nobody says this out loud, and the pull is real with cover at stake. A plan that hides its worst item is worth less than no plan: it destroys the one thing you were being asked for, which is a document he can trust.

• “Ask the insurer what they want to see.” Worth doing and it is not the source. The intervals, the scope and the sign-off authority are already settled by the recognised codes — one per kind of asset — which is why this is a plan you can defend rather than a preference you negotiated.

Keep the constraint in view: fourteen days is not enough to inspect your way out of this, and it is more than enough to write a plan that says what happens next and when.

You do not need to invent how to do this. The industry already agrees on how often each kind of asset must be inspected, what must be checked, and who may sign it off. You need a reliable, repeatable way to turn those rules into a defensible plan - the same way every time. Here it is, worked on the three assets.

T2: The Tool in Action

You build an in-service inspection plan: a written schedule that says, for each pressure asset, what to inspect, how, how often, and when it is next due - backed by a recognised rulebook so an auditor accepts it. The rules come from the API (American Petroleum Institute) in-service codes, one per asset type: API 510 for vessels, API 570 for piping, API 653 for tanks. Watch the plan built one asset at a time.

Step 1 - Pick the right code for the asset

V-101 holds LPG under pressure, so it is a pressure vessel - API 510 applies. (A petrol product pipe would be API 570; a flat-bottomed welded storage tank, API 653.) Picking the code is the first decision, because each code sets its own intervals and its own checks.

Step 2 - Gather the history and a thickness baseline

Dig out the file: there is no recorded internal inspection - so V-101 is due NOW (no history is a finding in itself). A UT survey gives the thickness picture: original wall 12.0 mm, thinnest reading today 9.6 mm, minimum safe thickness 7.0 mm. In service since 2008, so 16 years.

Step 3 - Work out the corrosion rate and remaining life

Corrosion rate = (12.0 - 9.6) / 16 = 2.4 / 16 = 0.15 mm/year. Remaining life = (9.6 - 7.0) / 0.15 = 2.6 / 0.15 = 17.3 years of metal left above the safe limit.

Step 4 - Set the intervals and name the sign-off

Internal interval = the lesser of the API 510 cap (10 years) and half the remaining life (17.3 / 2 = 8.65) - so 8.65, rounded down to 8 years to stay safe. External is capped at 5 years. V-101's plan: internal (emptied, cleaned, entered) due NOW then every 8 years; external every 5 years; UT thickness with each internal; signed off by an API 510 authorised inspector.

Run all three and you have a one-page table the auditor can accept - asset, code, inspection type, interval, last done, next due, who signs:

The plan does not hide that V-101 is overdue - it flags it, which is exactly what a good plan does. The auditor is not looking for a perfect history; he is looking for a defensible, code-based plan that knows where the risks are - so the right asset is inspected at the right interval, neither over-inspected nor dangerously left too long.

T3: The Blueprint

You build a plan for each asset in four moves.

  • 1. Pick the right code. 510 = vessels, 570 = pipes, 653 = tanks. Each sets its own intervals and checks, so the code is the first decision.
  • 2. Gather the history and a thickness baseline. Find the last inspection dates - no history means due now. Take a UT thickness reading; note the original wall and the minimum safe thickness.
  • 3. Work out the corrosion rate and remaining life. Rate = metal lost ÷ years; remaining life = (current - minimum safe) ÷ rate.
  • 4. Set the intervals and name the sign-off. Each interval is the lesser of the code maximum and half the remaining life; name the authorised inspector who certifies it.

Three inspection types run through every code: external (the outside, asset live - corrosion, supports, leaks); internal (inside - emptied, cleaned, entered; the most thorough and disruptive); and on-stream / thickness (how much metal is left, usually by UT, ultrasonic testing - a probe that reads the steel's thickness without cutting it open). Each code sets a maximum interval - you may inspect sooner, never later: API 510 external at most 5 years, internal at most 10 years or half the remaining life; API 570 (ordinary Class-2 piping) external 5 years, thickness 10 years; API 653 (tanks) external 5 years, internal based on the floor corrosion rate and capped at 20 years. The half-the-remaining-life rule is the heart of it: never let an asset run all the way to the edge between inspections - always check back when it is only halfway there. It fits any in-service vessel, pipe or tank you must keep safe and compliant; it does not cover brand-new fabrication (a build code does that), and for very high-hazard plant a fuller risk-based inspection (RBI) method sets the intervals instead.

T4: Your Turn

Take your own pressure vessels, pipes and tanks and run each down the four moves - one row per asset. Pick the code, gather the history and a thickness reading, work out the rate and remaining life, and set each interval at the lesser of the code cap and half the remaining life.

Two disciplines keep the plan defensible. First, pick the code before anything else - a vessel, a pipe and a tank are different beasts, and the interval that is safe for one is reckless for another; the code is what makes the plan the same every time, for every asset. Second, the interval is always the lesser of the code cap and half the remaining life, and you flag honestly what you do not know - an asset with no history is due now, a single old reading is a weak basis for a rate, an overdue asset is named as overdue. A plan that hides a gap fails the audit; a plan that flags it passes, because it shows you know where the risk is.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your inspection plan honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Pick the code first. Each asset type has its own rulebook with its own intervals. Apply a vessel's rule to a tank and the plan is wrong from the first line - 510 = vessels, 570 = pipes, 653 = tanks.

• Sooner is allowed; later is not. The code interval is a maximum. You may always inspect earlier if the condition warrants it; you may never wait longer than the cap or half the remaining life, whichever is shorter.

• No history means due now. An asset with no recorded inspection is not "probably fine" - it is unknown, and unknown is treated as due. Say so plainly; the absence is itself a finding.

• Half the remaining life, never the whole. Always plan the next look for when only half the remaining metal is gone, so a faster-than-expected corrosion rate cannot run the wall to the edge before you check again.

• Name who signs it. The plan must be certified by an authorised inspector qualified to that code. You may draft it; you may not self-certify the engineering - name the person who will.

You walked into the yard with three assets and no plan, and the temptation to climb on each one and trust your eye. You can walk into the audit with a one-page table that names the code for each asset, the interval the code demands, the date each is next due, and the inspector who will sign it - and that flags the overdue vessel rather than hiding it. An inspection plan is not an opinion; it is a calculation anchored to a recognised code. Pick the right rulebook, read the wall, halve the remaining life, and name the sign-off - and a yard of unknowns becomes a defensible plan that keeps the equipment safe, the cover in place, and forty people in work.
