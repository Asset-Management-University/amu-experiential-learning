Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Farai, and you run a small joinery workshop making beautiful wooden furniture. At the end of each week the yard holds the leftovers: a skip half-full of wood offcuts, several big bags of sawdust, and this week an old belt sander that finally gave up. You do the responsible thing and pay a company R150 to haul it all away - a cost of doing business, written in your books as "waste disposal".

Then you visit a competitor's workshop across town, and it's an eye-opener. There's no big skip. Large offcuts are stacked neatly on a rack; smaller clean pieces are bundled with string and priced for local hobbyists; bags of sawdust are labelled "Pet & Animal Bedding"; and someone is carefully lifting the motor out of a broken machine onto a shelf marked "Spares for Sale". It stings - they are earning money from the exact same things you pay to throw away. That R150 cost in your books could be income; your "waste" is their raw material for another sale.

I've been paying to throw away what my competitor sells - how much value have I been dumping, and how can I tell what's truly rubbish from what's worth recovering?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to react - but how? Carry on as before: easiest, but now you can't un-see the cash going into the skip every week - the habit of calling it "rubbish" was the very thing that stopped you looking. So hoard everything: "I'll keep it all, it might be worth something!" - and soon the workshop drowns in scrap and broken tools, a fire hazard you can't move through, and you admit some of it truly is rubbish. So sell the whole skip for a pittance: a dealer takes the lot for R20 - better than paying R150, but you've mixed valuable re-machinable oak offcuts with dusty floor sweepings, and he pays you for the lowest-value thing in the heap, not the highest.

You treated it all as one undifferentiated pile called "waste" - and the real problem was never the stuff, it was the label. So before you read on: what is each individual thing actually worth, on paths other than the bin - and how would you tell them apart?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

"Waste" is a decision, not a fact - it's a label you apply, and the moment you apply it you stop asking what the thing is worth on any path but the bin. The cure is to stop treating your "waste" as a single heap and start sorting it by value. Five moves:

  • See the blind spot - 'waste' is a label, not a fact: the moment you call something 'waste' or 'rubbish', you stop thinking about what it could still be worth - you've decided its fate by habit, not by a clear-eyed look at its value. The label itself is the trap: it closes the question before you ever ask it. The first move is simply to challenge the word.
  • Pause the default disposal: make a simple sorting space between your workbench and the skip - and let nothing go in the skip until it has paused there and been looked at. That one deliberate pause breaks the automatic habit of 'leftover = bin', and gives each thing the chance to be seen for what it's really worth.
  • Build your ladder of recovery (highest value to lowest): for anything you might discard there's a ladder of paths, most valuable first: USE IT AGAIN as it is (a big clean offcut back on the rack); REFURBISH/REPAIR AND RESELL it (an old tool needing a new belt); REPURPOSE it (small offcuts as craft wood, sawdust as animal bedding); STRIP IT for the valuable parts (the motor and cast base of a dead sander); RECYCLE it down to material; and only LAST, pay to dispose. The same item is worth far more high on the ladder than low.
  • Sort honestly against the ladder, not as one heap: now you don't have a pile of 'waste' - you have a collection of small assets, each sorted to its highest-value path. The big offcuts get reused, the small ones bundled, the sawdust bagged, the dead machine stripped for parts - and only the true, valueless rubbish (fine dust, oily rags) reaches the bin. Mixing it all into one heap is exactly what pays you the lowest price for your best stuff.
  • Decide by value, not habit - and stay honest: the goal is to capture value, not make more work than it's worth: for each item ask 'what is this worth, MINUS the time and cost to recover it?' If saving a scrap worth R5 takes an hour, let it go. Be honest that some things genuinely are finished and that recovery isn't free - this is smart, honest sorting, not hoarding everything.

See it transform Farai's week. He sets a sorting space before the skip and runs each thing down the ladder: the big clean offcuts go back on the rack, the smaller ones are bundled as craft wood, the sawdust is bagged for pet shops, the dead sander is stripped for its motor and cast base, and only the fine dust and oily rags are paid to be removed. The skip he used to pay R150 to empty now yields roughly R70 of craft wood, R40 of sawdust, and R80 of salvaged parts - a R150 cost turned into about R190 of income. "Waste" is a decision, not a fact - so before you throw a thing away, ask what it's worth on every path, build a ladder of recovery from highest value to lowest, sort each item honestly to its best path, and decide by value minus the cost to recover it. Not hoarding, not penny-pinching - honest sorting.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about wood - the same ladder works wherever "waste" hides value. Watch an office.

See the same ladder with 100 retired laptops

An office upgrades its 100 laptops every three years. The habit: pile the old ones in a store room and pay a firm to collect them for "secure disposal" - a pure cost, labelled "e-waste". Run the ladder instead:

• Refurbish & resell: 60 work perfectly - professionally wiped, they become affordable computers for students or families who can't afford new.
• Strip for parts: 30 have broken screens but good memory and drives, sold to repair shops.
• Recycle for materials: the final 10 are truly broken, sent to a specialist who safely recovers the gold, copper and other metals.

A disposal cost becomes income - and, more importantly, 60 good-as-new laptops get a second life helping people, instead of being shredded.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look around your business, project, or home: what do you routinely throw away, scrap, write off, or pay to remove? Challenge the "waste" label - is that pile of offcuts, those old computers, those returned goods, that food surplus truly worthless, or have you just stopped asking what they're worth?

  1. Pause and build the ladder. Before it goes in the bin, the skip, or the write-off column, stop - and list the paths for each item, from used-again-as-is right down to paying for disposal.
  2. Sort honestly. Where does each part of your "waste" stream sit on that ladder? Send each to its highest-value path, not all to one heap.
  3. Decide by value. Choose each item's fate by its true worth minus the effort to save it - and be honest about what is genuinely at the end of its life.

This was never stupidity, and it isn't penny-pinching either - the word "waste" is one of the most universal blind spots there is, and paying to dispose of things responsibly was the conscientious thing to do. It simply hid a quieter truth: that even the things we are about to discard can still hold value, and that recovering it is one more way to serve - a cheaper good for someone who needs it, material kept out of the ground, an income that helps your work continue. So challenge the label, look once more before you let go, and stop throwing away value - start seeing what it's worth.
