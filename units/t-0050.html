T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning on the concrete pad next to the Tank You cylinder filling line - the line that fills your 9 kg LPG cylinders one after another so the drivers can load them and go. The plan was simple: an 8-hour shift at 40 cylinders an hour, so 320 cylinders by knock-off. Ibraaheem has already promised 300 of them to customers across the valley, and the drivers are leaning on the bakkie, mugs of tea going cold, waiting for the first full pallet.

In your hand is the plant logbook, its cover gone soft and grey where everyone grips it. The morning's lines are scrawled in blue pen and your stomach tightens as you read: line started 40 min late, filling head stuck. Stopped 25 min, seal applicator jammed. Running slow, about 30/hr not 40. Rejected 18 cylinders, underweight. Muhammad walks over, wiping his hands on a rag that smells of gas and machine oil. "How many are we actually going to make today?" he asks - not angry, just tired of guessing. Last week you promised cylinders you could not fill, and two customers went to the supplier down the road and did not come back.

You count the full pallet: 198 cylinders, not 320. Short by more than a third - and you genuinely do not know where the time and the cylinders went. The late start? The slow running? The jams? The rejects? The logbook tells a hundred small stories but it does not add up to one number you can trust. You feel the pull to just say it - "the machine is old, we need a new line" - and ask Ibraaheem for the money. It would feel decisive. But you have no idea whether a new line is the answer, or whether you are about to spend money fixing the wrong thing.

Before you read on, decide. The pallet holds 198 cylinders where 320 should be. A late start, a jammed applicator, the line running at about thirty an hour instead of forty, and eighteen rejected underweight. Muhammad is not angry, just tired of guessing — and last week’s unfillable promise cost two customers who did not come back. Write down how you would find where the missing cylinders went. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one would have you buy

• “The machine is old — we need a new line.” Decisive, expensive, and a guess. A new line mostly buys back the time the old one spent stopped — and until you know whether stopping is where the output actually went, you are about to spend a fortune on your third problem.

• “Add up the downtime and fix the breakdowns.” The obvious place to look, because a stopped machine is loud and visible. A line that never stops and crawls all day loses output just as surely, and it does it quietly, with everybody standing around it looking busy.

• “Work overtime to make up the shortfall.” It fills today’s pallet and buys tomorrow’s identical shortfall at a higher cost. You would be paying extra for the same losses, and after a week of it nobody can tell you anything new about why.

• “Push the line harder — run it faster.” Eighteen cylinders came off underweight this morning. Speed you buy at the cost of rejects is not output, it is work you do twice, and the customer counts only what is good.

• “Read the logbook properly and see what happened.” Necessary, and you have already done it. The logbook tells a hundred small stories honestly and they do not add into one number you can trust, which is exactly why you cannot answer the only question Muhammad asked.

Notice what all five have in common: each one picks a loss and acts on it, and none of them measures the three kinds against each other. The loud loss is rarely the big one.

The answer is one honest number, built from three plain questions - and it points straight at the loss to fix first. Here is what it looks like, worked from this very morning's logbook.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is this morning's messy logbook turned into a single, honest score - OEE, Overall Equipment Effectiveness: of all the good cylinders this line could have made, how many did it actually make? It is built from three separate things, multiplied together, so a line that looks busy can still score badly - all three must be good at once.

Step 1 - Availability: was it running when it should have been?

Planned shift: 480 minutes (8 hours). Stops: 40 (late start, filling head stuck) + 25 (seal-applicator jam) = 65 minutes. Run Time = 480 - 65 = 415. AVAILABILITY = 415 / 480 = 86.5%.

Step 2 - Performance: when it was running, did it run at full speed?

Ideal speed: one 9 kg cylinder every 1.5 minutes, so in 415 minutes it should make 277. It actually made 216. PERFORMANCE = 216 / 277 = 78.0%.

Step 3 - Quality: of what it made, how much was good enough to sell?

Of the 216 cylinders filled, 18 were underweight rejects, so 198 were good. QUALITY = 198 / 216 = 91.7%.

Step 4 - OEE and your worst loss: the one number, and what to fix FIRST

OEE = 0.865 x 0.780 x 0.917 = 62%. The lowest is PERFORMANCE (78%) - slow running from low head pressure - NOT availability. So the instinct 'buy a new line' (which only fixes breakdowns, the third problem) would chase the wrong thing. Fix the head pressure first: no new machine.

Read the three together and the fog lifts. OEE = 0.865 x 0.780 x 0.917 = about 62%. Notice what the multiplying does: three scores in the high 80s and 90s do not average to "nearly fine" - they compound to 62%, because small losses stack. And the one number you would have missed: the lowest score is Performance, 78% - the line crawling at low head pressure - not Availability. So the morning's instinct, "buy a new line", would mostly fix breakdowns (availability), which is only your third problem. The real recoverable output is hiding in why the running line is slow. OEE finds your weakest link automatically - and it is rarely the loud one.

T3: The Blueprint

You build OEE from your own logbook - real records, never guesses - in four steps. Each is a plain question with a simple sum.

  • 1. Availability - "Was it running when it should have been?" Take your Planned Production Time (the time you intended to run, minus scheduled breaks), subtract every stop (late starts, breakdowns, jams) to get Run Time, and divide: Availability = Run Time / Planned Time.
  • 2. Performance - "When it was running, did it run at full speed?" Work out what it should have made in the run time (run time / ideal time per unit), and divide the actual output by that: Performance = Actual Output / Ideal Output for the run time. This catches slow running and tiny stops.
  • 3. Quality - "Of what it made, how much was good?" Divide the good units by everything that came off the line: Quality = Good Units / Total Units.
  • 4. OEE and your worst loss - multiply the three for the single score, then read off the lowest of the three - that is the loss eating the most output, and the one to attack first.

One layer deeper: every loss of output falls into one of the six big losses - two behind each OEE part. Availability loses to breakdowns and setup/changeovers; Performance to small stops (idling) and reduced speed; Quality to start-up rejects and production rejects. Naming the loss turns "the machine is just slow" into something you can actually fix - here, the "reduced speed" loss points straight at the low head pressure in the log.

And the fix is not always a cheque. The most powerful first move is usually the cheapest: put basic daily care in the hands of the operator who hears the first odd noise - clean, check, tighten, lubricate, and report small problems early. bp-0137 shows why that works, through a driver who never knew that the way he drove was quietly killing an R8,000 part. That is where almost every small operation should start.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your OEE honest and useful.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Real records, never guesses. OEE is only as honest as the logbook. A guessed stop time or a rounded reject count is a lie you tell yourself - count from the real log, the real scale, the real clock.

• OEE is a measure, not a target to chase. A line running flat out making cylinders nobody ordered scores a high OEE and is still wasteful. Use OEE to find losses, not to push a machine past real demand - the goal is the right output, not the biggest number.

• Trust the multiply - the weakest link wins. Three scores of 90% give 73%, not 90%. Do not average the parts in your head; multiply them, and let the lowest one set your priority. The loud loss (breakdowns) is often not the costly one (slow running).

• It needs a designed speed. OEE fits a machine or line with a repeating cycle and an ideal rate. For one-off, custom or purely manual jobs there is no "ideal speed" to measure performance against, so OEE is weaker there - use it where the work repeats.

• Start the fix with the operator, not a budget. Autonomous maintenance - daily clean, check, tighten, report-early - costs almost nothing and attacks the losses at their source. Reach for a new machine only after the numbers prove the old one truly cannot be recovered.

The fog was never your fault - you simply had a logbook full of small stories and no way to read them as one truth. Now you do: three plain questions, one honest number, and a worst-loss that tells you exactly where to start. OEE turns a messy logbook into a single score that forces you to fix the weakest link in the chain before you spend a cent on a new one - and that is how "the machine is just old" stops being a guess and becomes a decision you can prove.
