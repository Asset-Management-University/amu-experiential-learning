Do I really need to buy more - or am I using what I have all wrong?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and Tank You has grown well - three bakkies now, with Sipho and Thabo driving alongside you. But one headache won't go away: the first bakkie you ever bought, the old reliable, is in the workshop. Again - it feels like another R2,000 in repairs every month. That bakkie and Sipho are the backbone; they do the most deliveries, every single day. And as you stare at the empty bay, your newest bakkie sits there, clean and barely used - a few runs this morning, then parked since noon.

The phone rings: a customer, grumbling, a gas delivery late, because the old bakkie is out of action again. The answer screams at you - we need more capacity, we need a fourth bakkie. A quick look: a decent used one is R40,000. Another huge expense you're not sure you can carry. You feel trapped: forever short of bakkie-hours, forever breaking the one you lean on most.

Do I really need to spend R40,000 on another bakkie - or am I just using the ones I already have all wrong?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The obvious move is to buy the fourth bakkie. You scrape the money together - and months later the pattern is eerily the same: the old reliable is still doing the most runs (you give Sipho the biggest, most urgent jobs by habit) and still breaking down, while now two newer bakkies idle for parts of the day and your leasing and insurance bill is bigger. You added cost, not a cure. So you try the other thing: push harder - tell Sipho to be faster, squeeze more in before the bakkie gives up. The result is predictable: it breaks down sooner, and Sipho is exhausted and close to quitting.

You know which bakkie is the busiest - you can see the mileage. But before you read on: busy compared to what? Is that level of "busy" good - or is it the very thing breaking your business?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The trap is measuring only raw activity - mileage, runs - with no yardstick to judge it against. Put a yardstick beside it and the truth appears: your assets are almost certainly misallocated, not insufficient. Five moves:

  • See you're measuring only 'how much': you know Bakkie A does the most work - but that's a number with no context. 'Busiest' looks like success; it tells you nothing about whether that workload is healthy or wrecking the asset. Raw activity alone can't tell the difference between your hardest worker and the thing quietly breaking your business.
  • Put activity beside a yardstick: for each asset, set a rough, honest healthy limit - not a fake-precise spec, just a sensible level it can sustain without early wear - AND the real job need. For a bakkie, allowing for routes, loading and traffic, you might judge a sustainable day at about 17 deliveries. Now you can compare actual use to something, instead of admiring a bare number.
  • Spot BOTH imbalances: with Ibraaheem you check the logs. Bakkie A is doing 25 a day - 47% past its healthy limit; the R2,000/month in repairs isn't bad luck, it's the predictable cost of overuse. Bakkie C is doing 8 a day - idle, yet still costing R2,000/month to run. Both are losses: one breaking early, one paying for output it never makes.
  • Rebalance with what you own: the picture is suddenly clear: you don't have a capacity problem, you have an allocation problem. You need ~48 runs a day and own capacity for over 50. Move about 9 runs off the hammered bakkie onto the idle one - now A, B and C each do ~16, every one a notch under its limit. The exact same deliveries get done; A's repair bills fall; C earns its keep - and you just saved R40,000. Misallocated, not insufficient.
  • Decide against the number, not blindly: the goal is never to run every asset flat-out at its limit - that leaves no room for a breakdown or a busy day. Keeping each a notch under (16 of a ~17 limit) is built-in slack; on a quiet day one can be the designated standby. That isn't waste - it's deliberate insurance. The yardstick is your guide, not your master; limits are estimates, so aim for a sensible balance.

Look at what just happened: the same ~48 deliveries a day still get done, but no bakkie is run into the ground and none sits idle on your money - and you kept the R40,000 in your pocket. That's the whole lesson, and it reaches far past bakkies: before you spend to buy more, check whether you're misallocated rather than insufficient - put each asset's real use beside an honest yardstick, rebalance the load across what you already own, and keep a deliberate notch of slack as your insurance. This is a deliberate decision against a number, not blind maximisation: you don't manage assets by how busy they look; you manage them by a number you chose on purpose, with slack built in as your insurance.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about machines - the same misallocation quietly burns out people. Watch it on a small team.

See the same trap in a manager and her team

Sarah leads three report-writers: David, Emma and Ben. David is a star, so he gets every urgent, high-stakes report - he works late, he's brilliant, and he's burning out. Emma and Ben handle smaller routine tasks and have spare time. Sarah feels at capacity; her only idea is to "hire another David".

Same move, calmly:

• The yardstick: a healthy week might be one major report plus two minor updates. David is carrying three majors - far past his limit; Emma and Ben sit under theirs.

• Rebalance with what she has: her team's skill and time are misallocated, not insufficient. Instead of hiring, she trusts Emma with a bigger report (with support), and gives Ben part of a major project.

• The result: David's load eases, Emma and Ben grow, the team gets more resilient - and she never needed a bigger team, just a wiser use of the one she had.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You may be running one asset into the ground while another stands idle - a machine, a vehicle, a budget, a person. It might even be you: overworking one part of your life while another (your health, your family) sits neglected. When the "I need more..." feeling hits, pause and check the allocation first:

  1. Find the yardstick. Name the raw activity you're watching (hours, runs, tasks) - then set an honest healthy limit for it (the sustainable level, not the maximum possible).
  2. See both imbalances. Put the real activity beside the limit: which asset is run past it, and which sits under-used yet still costing you? Both are losses.
  3. Rebalance before you buy - with deliberate slack. Move load from the over-stressed to the under-used; solve it with what you own before you spend or hire; and keep each a notch under its limit on purpose, as insurance.

The most expensive bakkie - or person - is often the one you already own and are quietly destroying, while another sits idle beside it. "We need more" is the loudest answer and usually the wrong one; the quieter, richer answer is to use what you have wisely. Choose your numbers on purpose, share the load with care, and the very same assets that felt too few will carry you further than a fleet you bled yourself dry to buy.
