We bought the smart sensors and we pay for a dashboard, but it warns us one sensor at a time, after the main has already burst. How do I learn the slow story across days and the story across a connected network - with only 31 labelled failures - and keep it honest after I switch it on?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on a cold Tuesday in the control room at Meander Water, the utility that pumps drinking water to about 180,000 people across a hilly town. On the big wall screen the live map glows: 312 km of pipe, 4 reservoirs, 9 pump stations, and 640 pressure and flow sensors blinking green.

In your hand is a weekend incident report. Zone 7 burst at 02:10 on Sunday - a 300 mm main let go under a road. By the time the night crew found it, 1.9 million litres had run into the storm drains and the road was closed for nine hours. The repair bill came to R420,000, and the angry calls are still coming in.

Across the desk, Onthatile, the operations manager, taps the report with one finger. She is not shouting; that is almost worse. "This is the third burst this quarter," she says. "We already bought the smart sensors. We already pay for a dashboard. So why did nothing warn us?"

You know the painful answer. The dashboard looks at one sensor at a time and only fires when a single reading crosses a fixed line. But a burst does not announce itself on one sensor. It builds up slowly over days as pressure drifts, and it shows up across many connected pipes at once - upstream, downstream, the whole neighbourhood of the network reacting together. Your tools cannot see a slow story told across time, nor a story told across a network of connected things. And you have a cupboard of 18 years of maintenance reports but only 31 confirmed, labelled bursts in the whole dataset. Thirty-one.

I need a dependable way to learn patterns across long stretches of time and across a connected network of assets, even with very few labelled failures - and a way to keep that method honest after I switch it on.

Before you read on, decide. You have 640 sensors reading once a minute for three years, and 312 km of pipe that forms a network — 418 junctions joined by 497 pipes, each with its own age and material. Bursts build slowly, across neighbourhoods, not at single gauges. And in eighteen years of maintenance reports there are only 31 confirmed, labelled bursts. Thirty-one. Write down how you would build an early warning — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two shapes the data actually has

• “Set thresholds on each sensor and alarm when one crosses.” This is what you have. It sees one gauge at one moment, so a slow story told across a week and across a neighbourhood arrives as a single late alarm — which is the situation you are trying to leave.

• “Train a model on the 31 labelled bursts.” Thirty-one examples will teach a model the thirty-one, confidently and uselessly. Scarce labels are a real constraint, and pretending otherwise produces something that looks trained.

• “Collect more data and wait.” You already have about a billion readings. The shortage is not data; it is labelled failures, and waiting for more means waiting for bursts.

• “Buy the platform the vendor is offering.” It will fit whichever shape they built for, and your data has two shapes at once.

• “Use the biggest, newest model available.” Size is not fit. A model with no way to represent a network cannot learn that a pipe’s neighbours matter, however large it is.

• The choice is not about power, it is about shape. This data has two: a long time shape, three years of history per sensor, and a network shape, where what matters about a pipe includes what stands next to it. Different architectures exist because different shapes exist — one built to read long sequences, another built to spread information across a graph — and the honest move is to name the shapes first and pick the tools that match, rather than picking a tool and hoping the data suits it. The thirty-one labels are a third shape, scarcity, with its own answer: learn the ordinary behaviour from the billion unlabelled readings, and use the few labels only for what nothing else can supply. And none of it stays true on its own — a model is a claim about a world that keeps moving, so keeping it honest after you switch it on is part of building it, not a later chore.

You do not need new mathematics. You need the right architecture for the shape of your data. Here is how to choose.

T2: The Tool in Action

Modern AI is not one model - it is a small toolbox, and the skill is matching the tool to the shape of your problem: a Transformer for long time, a graph neural network for a connected network, self-supervised and few-shot learning for scarce labels, causal inference for honest cause, a foundation model for piles of documents, and MLOps to keep it all true over time. Watch them build an early-burst warning for Meander Water.

Step 1 - Match the architecture to the shape of your data

At Meander Water the data has two shapes: a TIME shape (640 sensors, one reading a minute, kept three years) and a NETWORK shape (312 km of pipe as a graph of 418 nodes and 497 edges). So you choose a combined model - a Transformer to read each sensor's long history, feeding a Graph Neural Network that spreads those readings across the connected network - to output, for each node, a daily 'burst risk in the next 7 days' from 0 to 1.

Step 2 - Learn across long time, and across the network

The Transformer reads each sensor's three-year history; the GNN spreads it across the 418-node graph, each edge carrying the pipe's diameter, material and age - so the model knows the Zone 7 main was 47-year-old cast iron. A burst that builds slowly over days, across a whole neighbourhood of pipes, now shows as one rising risk score instead of one late alarm on one gauge.

Step 3 - Learn from scarce labels without guessing

With about 1.0 billion mostly-unlabelled readings (3 years x 640 sensors x 1,440 a day), self-supervised pre-training learns normal pressure to within +/- 1.8 kPa. The 31 confirmed bursts are split 24 to teach and 7 to test honestly; few-shot fine-tuning uses the 24 to shape the danger pattern. A foundation model fine-tuned on 2,140 scanned reports reads 1,910 of them correctly (89%) and flags the 230 it is unsure of for a human - it does not guess - feeding pipe facts back into the graph.

Step 4 - Prove the repair caused the gain

Before claiming victory, you pick 8 high-risk zones: 4 get proactive valve-and-pipe work guided by the model, and 4 similar zones - the control group - do not. Over six months the 4 treated zones have 0 bursts; the 4 control zones have 3. Because the zones were similar and the period the same, the weather cannot explain the gap - the work did real work, and you can defend the spend to the board.

Step 5 - Test honestly, then keep it honest

On the 7 held-out bursts, the model warned early before 5, with a median lead time just over 3.5 days - enough to send a crew - and honestly missed 2 (both sudden third-party digger strikes with no slow drift to detect), at about 1 false alarm per zone every 6 weeks. In operation it scores every node nightly with drift detection: when a month-4 drought shifts demand and drift crosses the threshold, the model retrains overnight and recovers - no human had to notice first.

See what the toolbox did: it caught slow bursts days before the pipe gave way, traced them across the network, and proved its worth honestly. And see the mission in it, because this is frugal, honest engineering in the service of people, not a chase after the cleverest model. The frugality is in the first move: you read the shape of your data and pick only the tools that shape demands - time, network, scarce labels, cause, documents - and a shape you do not have is a tool you leave in the cupboard, because every model you add costs real money and real risk for no gain. The honesty is everywhere after that, and it is the same honesty this whole craft is built on, now written into machine learning. The system respects how little it truly knows - thirty-one failures - so it holds seven of them back, never letting the model see them in training, and then reports plainly that it caught five and missed two, rather than flattering itself. When it reads the old reports it flags the two hundred and thirty it is unsure of for a human instead of guessing. It refuses to claim a win the weather might have caused, and proves the repair did real work against a control group before anyone tells the board. And it watches itself for drift, because a model that quietly rots while the world changes is worse than no model - it is a stale warning light a tired control room still trusts. The aim is the 180,000 people whose water keeps flowing because a warning arrived in time and could be believed.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the system in five steps.

  • 1. Match the architecture to the shape of your data. Time, network, scarce labels, cause, text - one tool each.
  • 2. Learn across long time, and across the network. Transformer for time; graph neural network for the network.
  • 3. Learn from scarce labels without guessing. Self-supervised and few-shot; a foundation model for documents.
  • 4. Prove the repair caused the gain. Causal inference with a control group, not just correlation.
  • 5. Test honestly, then keep it honest. Held-out failures; MLOps and drift detection.

The ideas underneath. A Transformer is a pattern-learning network whose trick, attention, lets each moment learn which other moments to weigh - so it reads a long signal and finds the far-back clue. A graph neural network (GNN) learns on a graph of nodes (junctions, sensors) joined by edges (pipes); by message passing each node soaks up its neighbourhood, so a network-wide pattern emerges. Self-supervised learning learns "normal" from unlabelled data by predicting hidden parts of the signal (a failure is then a surprise); few-shot learning fine-tunes that to a new task from a few examples. Causal inference separates correlation from cause, simplest with a control group. A foundation model is a large pre-trained AI you fine-tune on your own text to read documents. And MLOps runs all this over time, watching for drift - the slow divergence of the world from the training data - and retraining before the model rots. Use this when your data has these shapes; for a short simple signal with clean labels, a plain model is cheaper and just as good.

Two disciplines separate a useful system from an expensive one. First, match the tool to the shape, and leave the rest in the cupboard - the worth of this method is not how many fashionable models it stacks but how exactly each one answers a shape your data actually has; add a graph network to truly independent machines, or a hungry Transformer to a short simple signal, and you have spent money and added risk for nothing. Second, build the honesty in, because a confident wrong model is worse than none - hold out real failures and report the misses, flag what you are unsure of instead of guessing, prove your gain against a control group rather than claiming the weather's luck, and watch for drift; a warning is only worth acting on if the people in the control room can believe it, and they can only believe what has been tested, caused, and kept current.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your system honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Match the tool to the shape. Time wants a Transformer, a network wants a GNN, scarce labels want self-supervision. A shape you do not have is a tool you leave in the cupboard.

• Hold out, and report the misses. Never test on data the model trained on. Keep real failures back, then state plainly how many it caught and missed - not a flattering number.

• Flag, do not guess. When the model is unsure - an unclear report, a strange reading - it should raise its hand for a human, not invent a confident answer.

• Correlation is not cause. Before you claim a repair worked, prove it against a control group. Two things moving together can both be moved by a third - the weather, the season.

• Watch for drift, or it rots. The world changes and a model silently goes stale. Check that today's data still looks like the training data, and retrain before the warning light lies.

You came to the control room with smart sensors, a paid-for dashboard, and a burst nobody saw coming. You can leave with a system that reads the slow drift across days and the spreading story across the network, learns from only 31 failures without overfitting them, proves its repairs against a control group, and watches itself for drift - warning early before 5 of 7 held-out bursts, and honest about the 2 it missed. Do not chase one clever model - match the architecture to the shape of your problem, and keep it honest: tested on held-out data, its impact proven by cause, and its accuracy watched for drift, so the warning comes in time and stays true. That is what modern AI is for in asset care: not a cleverer dashboard, but a warning the control room can believe, arriving in time to keep 180,000 people's water in the pipe.
