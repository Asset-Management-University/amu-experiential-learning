The bearing was the last thing to break

T1: The Ache

The raw-water pump has had four bearing failures in two years.

You are Onalenna, and each time the bearing was replaced with the same part, correctly fitted, by people who know what they are doing. Each time it lasted a few months. Nobody has been careless. There is no story here about a bad artisan or a cheap component.

So you have the calculation done properly, and the numbers say the bearing is adequately rated for the load.

Which somehow makes it worse - because now nothing explains it.

Then you stop reading and go and look at the pump. It runs in a wet, gritty pit. The suction pipework pulls the casing very slightly out of line when the system is cold. And the seal is a simple lip seal that has been weeping for months, and is nobody's job in particular.

And you understand what you have been doing for two years. You have been replacing the consequence. The bearing was never the problem. It was the last thing to break.

My bearing is correctly rated, correctly fitted, and it keeps failing in months. How do I specify a bearing and a seal that suit the duty and the environment this machine actually lives in?

Before you read on, decide. Two years of this. The bearing is correctly rated for the load, correctly fitted every time, and it fails within months — so often that replacing it is now a budgeted routine. Standing at the pump today you notice two things you have walked past for two years. The suction pipework pulls the casing very slightly out of line when the system is cold. And the lip seal has been weeping for months, into a wet and gritty pit, and is nobody's job in particular. Write down how you would specify this properly — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the order the selection has to run in

• “Fit a higher-rated bearing.” More capacity against a load that was never the problem. A better bearing in the same conditions fails the same way, slightly later, at more cost — which is how a two-year cycle survives being noticed.

• “Improve the fitting procedure and the fitters' training.” Worth doing in general and irrelevant here: the fitting has been good throughout. You would be correcting the one part of this that has never gone wrong.

• “Shorten the replacement interval — change it before it fails.” Converts an unplanned failure into a planned one and accepts the cause permanently. It also makes the problem invisible in the reliability figures.

• “Fix the pipe strain.” Genuinely one of the two causes, and on its own it leaves a degraded barrier admitting grit and water to a bearing in a wet pit.

• “Replace the seal — it has been weeping for months.” The other cause, and the same objection reversed. Notice why both were missed: each is somebody else's item, and neither is the part that keeps failing.

• What you have been doing is replacing the consequence. The bearing was never the problem; it was the last thing to break. So stop selecting a component and select a system — which changes what you establish first. Not the catalogue rating, but the duty this machine actually lives rather than the one on its nameplate: load with its direction, the shocks that come from starts and upsets rather than steady running, the misalignment that genuinely exists — named honestly, including thermal growth, pipe strain, settlement, and alignment done once and never again — and the dirt, water and temperature it sits in. Two consequences. Direction and misalignment eliminate whole families before any capacity calculation, so doing the arithmetic first means analysing candidates that were never eligible — your cold-condition misalignment rules some out on its own. And the barrier decides the life: a correctly chosen bearing in an environment its seal cannot exclude will fail on schedule, every time. Treat that seal as an accessory rather than as the part of the specification that determines the outcome, and you get exactly the two years you have had.

You stop selecting a component and start selecting a system. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the documented selection - the duty, the families, the check, the seal, and the reasons.

1. Characterise the REAL duty (not the nameplate one)

Everything downstream depends on this, and it is where selections go wrong before any calculation begins - because designers characterise the NAMEPLATE duty and the machine lives the REAL one. Establish: LOAD MAGNITUDE AND DIRECTION - radial, axial or combined - plus any SHOCK from starts, upsets or check-valve slam. SPEED. EXPECTED MISALIGNMENT, and be honest about its sources: thermal growth, pipe strain pulling the casing when the system is cold, foundation settlement, a coupling that is aligned once and never again. And THE ENVIRONMENT: contamination, moisture, temperature, washdown. For this raw-water pump: 8 kN radial at 1,450 rpm, minimal axial, occasional start-up shock, a wet and gritty pit, and suction pipework that pulls the casing slightly out of line when cold. Note that last item. It is not a detail; it eliminates entire bearing families in the next box.

2. Let load DIRECTION and misalignment eliminate families first

Direction and misalignment eliminate FAMILIES; capacity only chooses within them - so do this first or you will spend the analysis on bearings that were never candidates. PREDOMINANTLY RADIAL points to deep groove ball or cylindrical roller. COMBINED radial and axial points to angular contact ball or tapered roller. PREDOMINANTLY AXIAL points to a thrust bearing, an angular contact ball, a tapered roller or a paired arrangement - the choice turning on the size and direction of the thrust, the speed, the stiffness required, the space available and whether a radial load is also present. AND WHERE MISALIGNMENT IS LIKELY, spherical roller or self-aligning ball bearings provide the necessary angular flexibility, because USING A RIGID BEARING IN A MISALIGNED APPLICATION ACCELERATES WEAR DRAMATICALLY. That single sentence explains a great many repeat failures in which the bearing was correctly rated and correctly fitted.

3. Check the speed limit - a bearing that survives the load can fail on heat

A type that comfortably satisfies the load can still be the wrong answer, because SPEED DIRECTLY INFLUENCES HEAT GENERATION AND LUBRICATION FILM FORMATION, and EACH BEARING TYPE HAS A DEFINED SPEED LIMIT BEYOND WHICH HEAT BUILD-UP BECOMES PROBLEMATIC. So check the duty speed against the limiting speed for the type and arrangement, and think about what the heat does: it thins the lubricant, which thins the film, which brings the surfaces closer, which generates more heat. At 1,450 rpm this pump is undemanding and every candidate family clears the limit comfortably - which is exactly why this check gets skipped, and exactly why it surprises people on the machine where it matters. Do it every time, and record that you did, because the next engineer cannot tell the difference between a check that passed and a check that was never made.

4. The ISO 281 check - and why TYPE beats size

ISO 281 gives the BASIC RATING LIFE: L10 = (C/P)^p x 10^6 revolutions, where C is the basic dynamic load rating, P the equivalent dynamic load, and p = 3 FOR BALL bearings but 10/3 FOR ROLLER bearings. In hours, L10h = (C/P)^p x 10^6 / (60n). Run both surviving candidates at P = 8 kN and n = 1,450 rpm. A DEEP GROOVE BALL bearing with C = 52 kN: C/P = 6.50, and 6.50^3 = 274.6, so L10 = 3,157 HOURS - about four months. A CYLINDRICAL ROLLER bearing with C = 93 kN: C/P = 11.625, and 11.625^(10/3) = 3,559, so L10 = 40,906 HOURS - about 4.7 YEARS. THAT IS A THIRTEEN-FOLD DIFFERENCE IN LIFE, on the same shaft and the same duty, from choosing the TYPE - the exponent and the rating together - and not from spending more money. Reach for a different type before you reach for a bigger one.

5. Select the seal against the environment - then document the whole selection

Now the part that usually decides the outcome. SPECIFYING A BEARING WITH INADEQUATE SEALING FOR A CONTAMINATED ENVIRONMENT IS A COMMON OVERSIGHT - and bearings that already incorporate appropriate seals can eliminate the need for external sealing arrangements altogether. Choose against the environment: CONTACT seals exclude contamination well and add friction and heat, so they suit lower speeds and dirty duty; NON-CONTACT seals add almost no friction and suit higher speeds and cleaner service. Remember the failure list - contamination, poor lubrication, misalignment, wrong fit, incorrect clearance, excessive preload, installation damage, shock, corrosion, overheating - and note how many are things a seal governs. Note too that BOTH OVER- AND UNDER-LUBRICATION cause failure. THEN DOCUMENT IT: the duty assumptions, the type chosen, THE ALTERNATIVES REJECTED AND WHY, the seal, the lubrication regime and the life calculation. And match the rigour to the consequence - on this pump a failure costs availability, while on a ventilation fan, a hoist or a steering system it threatens life, and the conservatism of your assumptions must rise accordingly.

Look at box 4 before anything else. Same shaft, same duty, same load - and thirteen times the life, from choosing a different type rather than a bigger one or a dearer one. Then look at box 5, which is what would have decided this pump's fate regardless. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

You do not select a bearing. You select a bearing-and-seal system for a duty and an environment - and the seal usually decides how long any of it lasts. Five questions:

  • What is the real duty? Load magnitude and direction, shock, speed, expected misalignment (thermal growth, pipe strain, settlement), and the environment. Designers characterise the nameplate duty; the machine lives the real one.
  • Which families survive? Direction and misalignment eliminate families; capacity only chooses within them. Radial to deep groove ball or cylindrical roller; combined to angular contact or tapered roller; axial to thrust or paired arrangements. And where misalignment is likely, spherical roller or self-aligning ball - because a rigid bearing in a misaligned application accelerates wear dramatically.
  • Can it run at that speed? Speed governs heat generation and film formation, and every type has a defined speed limit beyond which heat build-up becomes problematic.
  • What does ISO 281 say? L10 = (C/P)p x 106 revolutions, with p = 3 for ball and 10/3 for roller. Compare types, not just sizes - the exponent alone changes the answer enormously.
  • What keeps the dirt out? Contact or non-contact sealing chosen against the environment - and then the documented selection, including the alternatives you rejected and why.

Why the seal deserves equal billing. Read the list of what actually kills bearings prematurely: contamination, poor lubrication, misalignment, wrong shaft or housing fit, incorrect internal clearance, excessive preload, installation damage, shock loading, corrosion, overheating. Count how many of those a seal governs, directly or indirectly. Specifying a bearing with inadequate sealing for a contaminated environment is a common oversight - and bearings that already incorporate appropriate seals can eliminate the need for external sealing arrangements entirely. Note also that both over-lubrication and under-lubrication cause failures: excess grease generates heat and churning losses, while too little allows metal-to-metal contact.

And match the rigour to the consequence. On this raw-water pump, a bearing failure costs availability and money. On a ventilation fan, a crane hoist or a steering system, the same failure threatens life. The depth of your analysis, the conservatism of your assumptions and the quality of your documentation must rise with the severity of what failure would do - that is not perfectionism, it is proportion, and it is a professional obligation rather than a preference.

One honest boundary. You are specifying the types - bearing and seal - and documenting the selection. You are not performing detailed life analysis beyond this selection check, not choosing the lubricant grade, and not diagnosing an existing failure. Any competent engineer can run the ISO 281 calculation; the skill being taught here is asking the right questions before reaching for the calculator, so that whatever you choose has a fighting chance in the place it will actually live.

Does it scale? It is choosing footwear for a journey rather than for a shoe size. The size question is real and it is the easy part; the journey - how far, how fast, over what ground, in what weather - decides the type, and no amount of getting the size right rescues a choice made for the wrong terrain.

T4: Your Turn

A works is refurbishing the drive end of a belt-driven sludge conveyor. The duty:

  • Load: 14 kN radial from belt tension, plus a small axial component; noticeable shock at start-up under a loaded belt.
  • Speed: 320 rpm.
  • Installation: the drive is mounted on a fabricated frame that flexes under load, and the two plummer blocks have never held alignment between overhauls.
  • Environment: open to the atmosphere, sludge splash, hosed down weekly, ambient 5 to 40 degrees Celsius.
  • Candidates: a deep groove ball bearing with C = 62 kN, or a spherical roller bearing with C = 190 kN.
  1. Characterise. Restate the duty in the terms of box 1, and identify which single feature of this installation most constrains the choice.
  2. Eliminate. Say which family survives and why, before doing any arithmetic.
  3. Calculate. Compute L10 in hours for both candidates. Use p = 3 for the ball bearing and 10/3 for the spherical roller.
  4. Seal it. Choose contact or non-contact sealing and justify it against this environment.
  5. Document. Write the four lines of your selection record - duty, choice, rejected alternative and reason, seal.

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, do step 2 before step 3 and notice what happens. The frame flexes and the plummer blocks do not hold alignment - so misalignment is not a risk here, it is a guarantee, and that alone rules out the rigid deep groove ball bearing however the arithmetic turns out. If you calculated first, you will have been tempted by a number for a bearing that was never a candidate.

Second, run the numbers anyway and then think about what they did and did not tell you. Here the arithmetic happens to agree with you - the ball bearing comes out at roughly four and a half thousand hours, about six months, while the spherical roller comes out enormous - so a learner who calculated first would probably have arrived somewhere sensible by luck. That is the danger. The misalignment ruled the ball bearing out regardless of its life figure, and on the next machine the arithmetic will look comfortable and the installation will still destroy it, exactly as it destroyed Onalenna's correctly-rated pump bearing four times. A calculation that agrees with the right answer is not the same as a calculation that found it.

Third, the environment in step 4 is sludge splash and weekly hosing. Ask yourself honestly what a low-friction non-contact arrangement is going to do about a pressure washer, and what the friction penalty of a contact seal actually costs at 320 rpm. Slow, dirty, wet duty is the easiest sealing decision in engineering, and it is still routinely got wrong.

T5: The Guardrails

Bearing selections fail in ways the calculation never sees.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Characterising the nameplate duty. The machine lives the real one - with the starts, the upsets, the pipe strain and the thermal growth nobody wrote down.

• Selecting on load magnitude and ignoring direction. Direction decides the family. Magnitude only chooses within it.

• Ignoring misalignment. Then fitting a rigid bearing into a machine that guarantees it, which accelerates wear dramatically and produces exactly the repeat failure that has no apparent cause.

• Skipping the speed check. A type that satisfies the load can still fail on heat, and the check is cheap.

• Reaching for a bigger bearing of the same type. A different type is often the answer, and the exponent alone can be worth more than any amount of extra capacity.

• Treating the seal as an accessory. Count how many of the standard failure causes a seal governs. In dirty service the seal decides the outcome and the bearing merely records it.

• Inadequate sealing for a contaminated environment. A common oversight with a long history, and often avoidable by choosing a bearing that incorporates appropriate seals in the first place.

• Assuming more lubricant is safer. Both over- and under-lubrication cause failures - excess grease generates heat and churning losses.

• Documenting the choice without the rejected alternatives. The next engineer then repeats your analysis, or repeats the mistake you avoided and never recorded.

• Applying the same rigour regardless of consequence. A conveyor bearing and a hoist bearing deserve different depths of analysis, and knowing which is which is part of the job.

Onalenna replaced the same bearing four times in two years and it was never the problem. Characterise the duty the machine actually lives, including the misalignment its installation guarantees; let load direction and misalignment eliminate whole families before any capacity is considered; check the speed, because heat kills bearings that the load never would; run ISO 281 across TYPES rather than sizes, since a different type gave thirteen times the life on the same shaft; and choose the seal against the environment, because in dirty service the seal decides how long the bearing lasts and the bearing merely records the verdict. Then document it - including what you rejected and why - and match your rigour to what failure would cost.
