Three defensible options and no defensible recommendation

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and there is a committee meeting on Thursday about an ageing critical asset. You have three options and you have done the sums for each of them.

And you cannot make them comparable, because they are not the same kind of number.

The replacement cost is a quotation - firm, dated, signed. The benefit is a service improvement nobody has ever priced. And the risk is a probability that four people estimated in a workshop one afternoon, which is now sitting in your spreadsheet looking exactly as solid as the quotation.

Whichever option you recommend, the first question will be why not one of the other two. And your honest answer is that it depends on assumptions you have not made visible - not to the committee, and not, until this moment, to yourself.

You have three defensible options and no defensible recommendation.

My costs are certain, my benefits are vague and my risks are estimates, and I have to recommend one option to a committee that will ask why not the others. How do I put all three on one footing and make a recommendation that survives challenge?

Before you read on, decide. Three options on the ageing critical asset: leave it, refurbish it for R8m, or replace it for R24m. Each is defensible. The costs are precise; the benefits are described in words; the risks are estimates nobody has quantified. The refurbishment case as written covers the pipework and the replacement case does not. The committee meets Thursday and will ask why not each of the other two — and your honest answer is that it depends on assumptions you have not made visible, not to them and not, until this moment, to yourself. Write down how you would put all three on one footing and recommend one — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the deliverable actually is

• “Recommend the middle option — it is the balanced choice.” Choosing by position in a list rather than by analysis. It also happens to be the one nobody will challenge hard, which is why it is tempting and why it will not survive somebody who does.

• “Present all three and let the committee choose.” Hands them the judgement you were asked for, with less context than you have. They will choose on cost, because cost is the only column that is comparable as things stand.

• “Get better benefit data before recommending.” Reasonable and unbounded — the benefits of a refurbishment are irreducibly vague, and waiting for them to become precise is waiting forever while the asset ages.

• “Recommend replacement — it is the safest.” Possibly right and stated as a preference, which is what you cannot defend. It also spends three times the capital on a judgement you have not shown.

• “Fix the scope mismatch and compare the costs properly.” Necessary — and notice that most arguments about analyses like this turn out to be arguments about incomparable baselines. Fixing it is the beginning rather than the answer.

• Put them on one horizon, one currency basis and one scope boundary first, because as written your options are not comparable at all. Then include the one everybody omits: doing nothing is an option, and not as a courtesy — it is the baseline the others must beat, and without it the analysis can only say which intervention is cheapest, never whether intervening is worth doing. Bring risk onto the same footing by monetising it as expected cost — how likely, times how bad — with the consequence scoped honestly and completely rather than stopping at the equipment: the outage, the emergency replacement at emergency prices, the regulatory exposure. Two honesties must then survive. An expected value describes a portfolio of years and predicts nothing about any single one, and treating it as a forecast turns a sound analysis into a false promise. And the deliverable is not a number but a recommendation with its conditions attached: which assumptions it rests on, and what would have to change for the answer to change. That is what survives Thursday — because the committee's real question is not what the analysis said, but how far to trust it and when to look again.

You stop trying to produce a number and start producing a recommendation with its conditions attached. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the cost-risk-benefit analysis - one basis, monetised risk, and a recommendation that says what would change it.

1. Put the options on ONE horizon and ONE basis (and include doing nothing)

Most arguments about a cost-risk-benefit analysis turn out to be arguments about INCOMPARABLE BASELINES, so fix the basis before computing anything: the same COMPARISON HORIZON (six years here), the same CURRENCY YEAR, and the same SCOPE BOUNDARY - if one option includes the pipework and another does not, the comparison is already decided. Then include the option everybody omits: DO NOTHING. It is not a rhetorical courtesy. It is the BASELINE the other options must beat, and an analysis without it can only tell you which intervention is cheapest, never whether intervening is worth doing at all. The three options for this ageing critical asset: A DO NOTHING; B REFURBISH at R8m capital; C REPLACE at R24m capital.

2. Monetise the risk - expected cost, honestly scoped

EXPECTED ANNUAL RISK COST = PROBABILITY x CONSEQUENCE. State the CONSEQUENCE completely and honestly - here R40m, covering the extended outage, the emergency replacement at emergency prices, and the regulatory exposure, not just the equipment. Then the annual failure probability per option: DO NOTHING 0.25, so 0.25 x R40m = R10.0m A YEAR; REFURBISH 0.06, so R2.4m a year; REPLACE 0.02, so R0.8m a year. Two disciplines. First, this is an EXPECTED VALUE - it describes a portfolio of years, NOT a prediction for any single year, and in most individual years the cost will be zero while in one it will be R40m. Second, resist false precision: a probability estimated in a workshop is a judgement, so carry it as such and test it later rather than reporting it to three decimal places.

3. Monetise the benefit - without double-counting the avoided risk

The benefit line is WHAT IS GAINED BEYOND AVOIDANCE - added capacity, a higher service level, lower energy consumption, extended life. AND THE COMMONEST ERROR IN A CRBA IS COUNTING AVOIDED FAILURE TWICE: once as reduced risk in box 2 and again as a benefit here. It is one thing, and it belongs in box 2. If your benefit line paraphrases your risk line, delete it. Then the harder case. WHEN A CRBA INCLUDES CONSEQUENCES THAT ARE NOT NATURALLY MONETARY, SUCH AS PUBLIC HEALTH OR ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACTS, YOU MUST NOT INVENT A VALUE. YOUR PROFESSIONAL AND ETHICAL DUTY IS TO SOURCE A STANDARD, PUBLICLY-DOCUMENTED VALUATION FROM A RELEVANT AUTHORITY, AND THAT VALUE AND ITS SOURCE MUST BE EXPLICITLY STATED IN YOUR ANALYSIS. THE PURPOSE IS NOT TO CRUDELY PRICE A HUMAN LIFE, BUT TO ENSURE THAT DECISIONS AFFECTING PUBLIC SAFETY ARE MADE CONSISTENTLY, TRANSPARENTLY, AND WITH THE GRAVITY THEY DESERVE.

4. Total on a present-value basis, rank - then try to break it

Undiscounted first, only to see the mechanism: A = 6 x R10.0m = R60.0m; B = 8 + 6 x 2.4 = R22.4m; C = 24 + 6 x 0.8 = R28.8m. NOW DO IT PROPERLY, BECAUSE A MULTI-YEAR ANALYSIS MUST BE DISCOUNTED - money next year is not money today, and only the present-value form is professionally acceptable. At 8 per cent over six years the annuity factor is 4.6229, giving NET PRESENT COSTS of A R46.23m, B R19.09m, C R27.70m. REFURBISH RANKS FIRST. Then the step that makes it defensible: FIND THE ASSUMPTION THE RANKING IS MOST SENSITIVE TO AND COMPUTE WHERE IT FLIPS. Refurbishment stops winning when its post-refurbishment annual failure probability reaches 10.7 PER CENT, at which point its net present cost equals replacement's R27.70m. Note what discounting itself did: the flip point moved from 8.7 per cent undiscounted to 10.7 per cent, BECAUSE FUTURE RISK COSTS ARE WORTH LESS TODAY, which systematically favours the lower-capital option. That is not a trick; it is what discounting means, and you should be able to say so out loud.

5. The recommendation and the conditions that would change it

A recommendation that cannot say what would change its mind is an opinion in a table. So write it conditionally: REFURBISH - CONDITIONAL ON THE POST-REFURBISHMENT ANNUAL FAILURE PROBABILITY STAYING BELOW ABOUT 10 PER CENT. Then complete the sentence with the three things that make it usable. THE ASSUMPTION IT RESTS ON, named plainly, so the committee is deciding on the assumption rather than on your confidence. WHAT YOU WOULD MONITOR to detect the assumption failing - here, the actual failure and near-failure rate after refurbishment, reviewed annually against the 10 per cent threshold. AND WHAT YOU WOULD DO THEN - if the rate exceeds it, the refurbishment has not delivered and the replacement case reopens with the capital already partly spent, which is a real cost of being wrong and belongs in the paper. A CRBA whose conclusion cannot be flipped by any plausible assumption has not tested its assumptions; it has hidden them.

Box 4 holds the move that separates an analysis from an advocacy document: having found the answer, you deliberately try to break it. And box 5 turns the result into something a committee can actually decide on, because it names the assumption rather than asking them to trust yours. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Cost is certain and near-term. Benefit and risk are uncertain and distant. So any comparison that collapses the three into a single number is dominated by how you treated uncertainty - which is why the deliverable is not a number but a recommendation plus the conditions under which it would change. Five questions:

  • Are the options actually comparable? One horizon, one currency year, one scope boundary - and always include do nothing, the baseline the others must beat.
  • What is the risk worth? Expected annual cost = probability x consequence, with the consequence scoped completely. An expected value describes a portfolio of years, not a prediction for one.
  • What is the benefit worth? Only what is gained beyond avoided failure - because counting avoided failure as both a reduced risk and a benefit is the commonest error in a CRBA.
  • Which option wins, and how fragile is that? Total on a present-value basis, rank, then find the assumption the ranking is most sensitive to and compute the flip point.
  • What is the recommendation? Conditional - the option, the assumption it rests on, the flip point, what you will monitor, and what you will do if it fails.

Discounting is not optional. A multi-year comparison must be discounted to present value; an undiscounted total is shown here only to make the mechanism visible, and it is not a professionally acceptable deliverable. Notice what discounting does to this decision: the flip point moves from 8.7 per cent to 10.7 per cent, because future risk costs are worth less today, which systematically favours the option with lower capital and higher future risk. That is not a distortion - it is what discounting means - but you should know it is happening and be able to say so when asked.

And on consequences that are not naturally monetary. When a CRBA includes impacts such as public health or environmental damage, you must not invent a value. Your professional and ethical duty is to source a standard, publicly-documented valuation from a relevant authority, and that value and its source must be explicitly stated in your analysis. The purpose is not to crudely price a human life, but to ensure that decisions affecting public safety are made consistently, transparently, and with the gravity they deserve.

One honest boundary. You are integrating cost, risk and benefit into a defensible recommendation. You are not performing the whole-life costing that produces the cost figures, not selecting the risk analysis technique that produces the probabilities, and not making the decision - the committee does that, and your job is to let them make it on the assumptions rather than on your confidence.

Does it scale? A weather forecast that says "rain" is an opinion. One that says "seventy per cent chance of rain, and it will not rain at all if the front stalls offshore" tells you what to carry and what to watch. The second is not less certain than the first - it is more useful, precisely because it exposed the thing it depends on.

T4: Your Turn

A pumping station's switchgear is at end of life. The comparison horizon is 10 years, the discount rate 8 per cent (10-year annuity factor 6.7101), and the consequence of a switchgear failure is assessed at R25m - an extended outage to a district, emergency hire and replacement, and a regulatory penalty.

OptionCapitalAnnual failure probabilityOther effects
A - Do nothingR00.20-
B - Refurbish and add protectionR6m0.07-
C - Full replacementR19m0.015Saves R0.4m a year in energy and maintenance
  1. Basis. State the three things you would fix before computing anything, and say why do-nothing must be included.
  2. Risk. Compute the expected annual risk cost for each option.
  3. Total. Compute the net present cost of each option over the horizon. Treat option C's energy and maintenance saving correctly.
  4. Break it. Rank the options, then find the post-refurbishment failure probability at which your top two swap places.
  5. Recommend. Write the conditional recommendation in full - option, assumption, flip point, what you monitor, what you do if it fails.

The test of a real answer: three things.

First, option C has a benefit line and the other two do not. It belongs in the total as a negative cost, discounted like everything else - and it must not also be described as "avoided maintenance risk", which would be counting it twice.

Second, do the arithmetic before forming a view. The capital gap between B and C is large and the risk gap is small, and most people's instinct about which wins is wrong at least once in this exercise.

Third, and the one that matters: your step 5 must contain a number that would change your mind, and something you could actually observe to detect it. "We will keep it under review" is not a monitoring plan. What, specifically, would you count, how often, and against what threshold?

T5: The Guardrails

A cost-risk-benefit analysis fails in ways that all look like diligence.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Options on different horizons or scopes. The comparison is then decided before any arithmetic, and the argument that follows will be about the wrong thing.

• Omitting do nothing. Without the baseline you can only say which intervention is cheapest, never whether intervening is worth doing.

• An incomplete consequence. Costing the equipment and forgetting the outage, the emergency premium and the regulatory exposure understates every risk line equally, which quietly favours doing nothing.

• Reading an expected value as a prediction. R10m a year of expected risk does not mean R10m will be spent next year. It means most years cost nothing and one year is very expensive.

• Double-counting avoided failure. Once as reduced risk and again as a benefit. If the benefit line paraphrases the risk line, delete it.

• Leaving the analysis undiscounted. Not a simplification but an error, and one that changes which option wins.

• False precision in estimated probabilities. A workshop judgement reported to three decimals borrows authority it has not earned.

• No sensitivity analysis. Then the assumptions are not absent, merely hidden - including from you.

• A recommendation with no flip point and no monitoring. It cannot be revisited, so it will be defended long after it stops being right.

• Inventing a value for a non-market consequence. Source a published one and cite it. An invented number about public health will not survive its first serious question, and should not.

• Choosing the option first and building the analysis to support it. The commonest failure and the hardest to detect - which is exactly why the flip-point step exists.

Onalenna had three defensible options and no defensible recommendation, because her costs, benefits and risks were not the same kind of number. Put every option on one horizon, one currency year and one scope, and always include doing nothing; monetise risk as probability times a completely-scoped consequence, remembering it is an expected value and not a forecast; monetise only the benefit that goes beyond avoided failure, and never invent a value for a consequence that is not naturally monetary; total on a present-value basis, rank, and then deliberately try to break the ranking by finding the flip point; and deliver the recommendation with its assumption, its threshold, its monitoring and its fallback. A recommendation that cannot say what would change its mind is an opinion in a table.
