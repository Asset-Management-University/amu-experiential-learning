Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Asanda, and you lead a team that keeps the power on for several communities. And you are exhausted. There is one stretch of overhead power line - about 200 metres, running alongside a quiet road - that has become your nightmare. Five times this year, thieves have stripped the copper cable from it. Each time, the same call comes in: the power is out for 500 homes and the local clinic. Each time, you and your team race out and work for hours, sometimes through the night, to string up new cable. You get the lights back on, you feel a brief flash of pride - and then, weeks or months later, it is gone again. The same span. The same loss.

Your repair budget is vanishing into this one hole - each repair costs around R20,000, so that is R100,000 gone on one piece of wire. But the real cost is larger and harder to bear: the clinic running on a backup generator, food spoiling in people's fridges, children who cannot do their homework in the dark. And here is what gnaws at you: a storm or a worn-out part does not learn your schedule. It does not choose you. This feels different. This feels like you are fighting something that is fighting back.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to do your job, just better - to repair it faster. You get your team's response time down, pre-cutting the replacement cable so a crew can be in and out by dawn. But all this does is make the replacement quicker for the thieves to take next time; you are feeding the hole, not filling it. Next you think bigger and try to protect everything - patrols along all 200 of your spans. But your team is small, so you spread your people so thin they see nothing, while the thieves simply wait for a patrol to pass. You are guarding kilometres of cable that are never touched, and your one hot span gets hit again anyway. It is impossible, and it burns your team out. Then a voice born of anger says you should catch them yourself - and you stop, hard. Chasing people in the dark, or trying to confront them, is how someone gets badly hurt, or worse. A length of cable is never worth a life - not your team's, not the community's, not even the thief's. People-safety comes first, always.

Repairing faster does not work. Protecting everything is impossible. Fighting back is dangerous and wrong. A plan built for fixing accidents cannot beat something that chooses you, learns, and comes back. So before you read on: where is the fault really - in the repair, or in the plan?

An accident plan cannot beat an adversary. Wear and weather do not adapt to your repairs; a thief does - so stop just repairing the damage and start making the asset a WORSE TARGET, lawfully. Four steps: (1) SEE it as an adversary, not bad luck. (2) NAME it as a standing, costed condition ('cable theft on the north span') and count its TRUE cost - not just the R20,000 repair but the R150,000+ knock-on to the clinic and homes; that mandate justifies real effort. (3) CONCENTRATE on the few - about 10 of 200 spans carry over 80% of the loss; forget the 190 never touched. It costs money and is never one-and-done - but it breaks the cycle.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

An accident plan cannot beat an adversary. A thief is not bad luck; they are a deliberate person who chooses to attack, learns from what you do, and comes back. To stop them, you must stop just repairing the damage and start making the asset itself a worse target - lawfully, by changing the asset and its surroundings. Five moves:

  • See the trap - an adversary, not an accident: this is the breakthrough. Wear and weather do not adapt to your repairs - a thief is not bad luck but a deliberate person who chooses to attack, learns from what you do, and returns. Repairing the same loss in the same place is like resetting a game for the other player to win again. Your whole plan was built for accidents - rust, storms, worn parts - that do not choose you. An accident-shaped plan cannot beat an adversary-shaped problem, and that mismatch, not any failing of yours, is why the repairs never end it. You must change the game, not just reset it.
  • Name it - a standing, costed condition: stop treating each theft as a brand-new emergency to be cleared by Friday. Name it - 'cable theft on the north span' - and recognise it as an ongoing CONDITION of that asset, the way you would a chronic fault. Then count its TRUE cost. It is not only the R20,000 repair; it is the R150,000-plus in knock-on harm each time - the clinic on a backup generator, food spoiling, work and schooling lost for 500 homes during a 12-hour outage. Seeing that full cost on one line is what gives you the mandate to spend real effort and real money solving it properly, instead of quietly absorbing it as 'just another callout'.
  • Concentrate on the few: you cannot protect all 200 spans, and you do not have to. Look at your own repair records and the pattern is stark: roughly 10 spans - about 5% of your assets - carry over 80% of the theft loss for the year. This is the same focusing discipline as bp-0158: put your limited people and budget where the harm is actually concentrated. Forget the 190 spans that are never touched. Aim everything at the handful of hot spots, because a fix applied there ends most of the bleed, while effort spread evenly ends none of it.
  • Make the asset a worse target - the four levers: for each hot spot ask 'why is this a good target?' then pull four lawful levers to make it a worse one. LESS REWARDING: swap high-value copper for an aluminium line that still does the job, or mark the metal with a traceable fluid so a scrap dealer cannot buy it without being caught. HARDER: bury that span instead of leaving it overhead, or sleeve it so it is too slow to cut. RISKIER, LAWFULLY: 'riskier' means a higher chance of being CAUGHT BY THE AUTHORITIES, never harmed - a silent alarm or hidden camera that alerts the police or a security firm the moment the cable is cut. This is never about confrontation: if it triggers, your team stays away and lets professionals act, because no cable is worth a life. ENGAGE THE PEOPLE AROUND IT: the legitimate scrap dealers who can refuse stolen metal, and the community the line serves - they lose the most from the outages, often know the most, and are partners, never suspects.
  • Accept the honest cost: this is real work, not a magic fix. Hardening and detection cost money up front - though far less than one more year of repeat theft and outages. Detection brings false alarms you must tune out patiently. Community trust is slow to build and easy to break, so you engage with respect, never suspicion. And because the adversary adapts, protection is a standing programme, not a one-and-done job - you watch, you adjust, you keep the few hot spots covered. But this is the only approach that actually breaks the cycle: a loss you merely repair returns forever, while an asset you make a genuinely worse target is one the adversary finally walks past.

She names it - 'cable theft on the north span' - and writes its true cost on one line: not R20,000 but R170,000 a hit, once the dark clinic and 500 cut-off homes are counted. That number wins her the budget to act. She checks the records: 10 of her 200 spans carry over 80% of the loss, so she forgets the 190 and aims everything at the few. On each hot span she pulls the levers - aluminium in place of copper, the line buried and sleeved, a silent alarm that brings the police (never her team) if it is cut - and she sits down with the local scrap dealers and the community the line serves, as partners. You do not just repair the loss; you end the cycle.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This is not only about power lines - any repeated, deliberate loss has the same shape. Think of a school that keeps losing its new tablets, stolen again and again from the same ground-floor classroom.

See the same moves at the school

The school stops just claiming on insurance and buying new ones (repairing the loss) and realises someone is deliberately targeting them - an adversary, not an accident. So it manages the loss instead:

• Name and cost it: 'tablet theft - Room 3', true cost not just R50,000 for new tablets but the lost teaching time for 100 pupils.
• Concentrate on the few: it is always Room 3, near the back fence - so focus there, not on the whole school.
• Less rewarding: install software that locks the tablets and reports their location off the school network - useless to a thief.
• Harder: store them in a secure upstairs room at night; fit stronger locks on Room 3.
• Riskier, lawfully: a visible camera and a silent alarm that calls a security firm - never a teacher confronting anyone.
• Engage people: ask parents and neighbours to report anything suspicious after hours, as partners.

The school stopped replacing the asset and started making it a bad asset to steal - the same five moves, a different world.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think about your own work and your own life. Is there a place where you keep losing something, again and again - not to an accident, but to a choice someone is making? Stock vanishing from the same corner of a store, a tool repeatedly 'borrowed' and never returned, a digital asset misused. Stop just repairing the loss and getting exhausted.

  1. See it for what it is - an adversary, not an accident. Your old plan for fixing things will not work against someone who chooses, learns and returns.
  2. Name it and count the TRUE cost. Give the problem a name, and add up the full cost - time, trust, knock-on harm - not just the price of replacement. That number is your mandate to act.
  3. Find the few hot spots and aim there. Where is the loss concentrated? Put your effort on the handful that carry most of it; do not try to protect everything.
  4. Make it a worse target - people's safety first. Less rewarding, harder, and lawfully riskier (detection and reporting that brings the authorities - never confrontation), and engage the people around it as partners.

None of this was a failing on your part - you were handed an accident-shaped plan for an adversary-shaped problem, and no amount of faster repairing could ever have closed that gap. The exhaustion was the plan's fault, not yours. Yes, hardening and detection cost money, and trust with a community is slow to earn - but it is far cheaper than losing the same span forever, and it is the only thing that actually stops the bleed. An accident plan cannot beat an adversary; a worse target breaks the cycle.
