Step 1 of 5

The documents existed

T1: The Ache

You are Ntsika, fourth year, and you have been given the internal audit file from two years ago to learn from.

It is careful work. Three clauses, forty-seven documents examined, every reference recorded, a clean report. No findings.

Stapled behind it is the external assessor's report from six months later. Eleven findings.

You read them both twice, because you assume you have missed something. You have not. Every one of the assessor's eleven findings was already true on the day of the internal audit. Nothing changed in between.

The internal auditor was trained, certified, and did exactly what they had been taught. They checked whether the documents existed and said the right things.

The assessor checked whether the plant did what the documents said.

Two competent audits of the same system, six months apart, and one found nothing while the other found eleven. How do I plan an audit that finds out what is actually true?

Before you read on, decide. The internal audit in March found nothing. The external assessment in September found eleven. You have checked both twice, assuming you have missed something, and you have not: every one of the eleven was already true in March. Nothing changed in between. The internal auditor is trained, certified and conscientious — she read the procedures, confirmed they said what the standard requires, and asked the quality officer for example records, which he selected and provided. The assessor asked for the records of three specific jobs he named himself, and then went and looked at the jobs. Write down how you would plan an audit that finds out what is actually true — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two audits that share a name

• “Retrain the internal auditor.” She did exactly what she was taught, competently. Retraining her to be more diligent at the wrong question produces a more thorough version of the same result.

• “Use the external assessor's checklist.” It was not a better checklist. He asked for records nobody offered him and then went to look at the thing itself — a difference of method rather than of paperwork.

• “Audit more often.” Twice the frequency of an audit that cannot see this finds nothing twice as often, and would have reported clean in March, June and August.

• “Have someone more senior do it.” Seniority does not change what is being asked. A director reading the same procedures and accepting the same offered examples reaches the same conclusion with more authority.

• “The external assessor was being unreasonable.” You have checked twice. All eleven were true in March, which means the March audit was wrong and the September one was right.

• Two audits share a name and ask different questions. One asks whether a documented system exists and says what is required. The other asks whether the organisation does what its documents say, and whether doing it produces what is needed. Both are legitimate, they find completely different things, and an organisation that has only ever had the first will believe it has been audited. So write the objective first, and check its verbs: implemented and achieving, never documented. State the scope specifically enough that somebody could disagree with it. Then sample to find out rather than to confirm — which is the difference you have just watched: samples chosen by the auditor, where trouble would show, rather than the tidy examples an obliging host provides. Then trace one thread all the way down: from the statement in the manual to the record, to the person who made it, to the thing itself — because systems fail at the joints, and only following one line end to end shows where the chain stops being real. And record evidence somebody else could check — a reference, a record number, a date — because a finding resting on your impression will be argued away by whoever is most confident in the room.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the audit - deciding what you are testing, the clause-evidence matrix, sampling to find out rather than to confirm, tracing one thread all the way down, and recording evidence somebody else could check.

1. Decide what you are testing - that the documents exist, or that the plant does what they say

<strong>Write the objective before anything else, because it decides everything and it is almost never written down.</strong> There are two audits and they share a name. <strong>The conformance audit asks: does a documented system exist and does it say what the standard requires?</strong> <strong>The effectiveness audit asks: does the plant actually do what the documents say, and does doing it produce what the organisation needs?</strong> <strong>Both are legitimate. They find completely different things, and an organisation that has only ever had the first will believe it has been audited.</strong> <strong>Mine, stated plainly:</strong> 'To determine whether the arrangements described in clauses 8.1, 9.1 and 10.2 are implemented in practice and are achieving their intended outcomes.' <strong>Note the two verbs - implemented and achieving - and note that neither of them is 'documented'.</strong> <strong>Now the scope, and be specific enough that somebody could disagree with it:</strong> three clauses, the water treatment works only, the twelve months to date, and the maintenance and operations functions. <strong>What is out of scope is stated too</strong>, because an audit that quietly declines to look somewhere and does not say so has told the reader something false by omission.

2. Build the clause-evidence matrix - for each clause, what would actually demonstrate conformance

<strong>This is the instrument that would have prevented the whole failure, and it is a table.</strong> <strong>Down the side, the clauses. Across the top, the four kinds of evidence.</strong> <strong>Documents</strong> - what the organisation says it does. <strong>Records</strong> - traces that it did. <strong>Observation</strong> - watching it happen. <strong>Interview</strong> - what the people doing it say occurs. <strong>Three clauses by four types is twelve cells, and every one should have something in it before the audit starts.</strong> <strong>Worked, for clause 8.1:</strong> the document is the maintenance planning procedure; the records are the work orders showing the required checks were completed; the observation is watching a planner build next week's schedule; the interview is asking a technician what happens when a job cannot be completed. <strong>Four different things, and only the first is a document.</strong> <strong>Now look at what the failed audit did.</strong> It filled <strong>three cells of twelve</strong> - the document column, once per clause - and left <strong>nine empty</strong>. <strong>Twenty-five per cent of the available evidence, all of it from the one source that cannot tell you whether anything happens.</strong> <strong>The auditor was not careless. The matrix did not exist, so nobody could see that the audit had a shape</strong>, and a shape is exactly what the matrix makes visible before it is too late to change.

3. Sample to find out, not to confirm - and size the sample so the answer means something

<strong>Sampling is where an audit is decided, and there is one habit that destroys it: choosing the examples somebody hands you.</strong> <strong>State the population.</strong> For clause 8.1 there are <strong>340 work orders</strong> in the twelve months in scope. <strong>State how many and how chosen.</strong> Fifteen, selected by me from the full list before I arrive, with a deliberate spread - some routine and some breakdown, both shifts, across the whole period rather than the most recent month. <strong>Then say what a clean result would prove and what it would not.</strong> <strong>Fifteen clean from 340 does not mean the population is clean; it means a problem affecting a large share of the work would probably have appeared.</strong> Say that in the report rather than letting the reader infer certainty. <strong>What the external assessor found doing exactly this: 6 of 15 work orders with no evidence the required check was done - 40 per cent.</strong> <strong>At forty per cent, a sample of fifteen was almost certain to find it, and a sample of zero was certain not to.</strong> <strong>That is the entire difference between the two audits.</strong> Not competence, not diligence, not standards knowledge. <strong>One of them looked at work orders and one of them looked at the procedure that describes work orders.</strong>

4. Follow one thread all the way through - from the requirement to the person doing the work

<strong>Sampling tells you how often. Tracing tells you why, and it is the technique that turns an audit from a count into an understanding.</strong> <strong>Take one requirement and follow it in one direction, without stopping when it gets easy.</strong> <strong>Mine.</strong> The procedure requires a criticality check before a job is scheduled. <strong>Does the procedure say so?</strong> Yes, section 4.3. <strong>Is there a field for it?</strong> Yes, in the work order. <strong>Is it populated?</strong> In 9 of my 15. <strong>What happens when it is not?</strong> Nothing - the system accepts the work order either way. <strong>Does the planner know it is required?</strong> Yes. <strong>Can they do it?</strong> - <strong>and here the thread stops.</strong> The criticality register was last updated three years ago and the planner has no way to look up an asset added since. <strong>So they leave the field blank rather than enter something they cannot support, which is the more honest of the two options available to them.</strong> <strong>Notice what tracing gave you that sampling could not.</strong> Sampling said 40 per cent. Tracing said <strong>the gap is a three-year-old register, and the people at the end of the thread have been behaving reasonably throughout.</strong> <strong>One is a number. The other is a finding somebody can fix.</strong>

5. Record the evidence as you collect it - so that every later finding can be traced back to something specific

<strong>Write the evidence record while you are standing there, not afterwards from memory, and make every entry specific enough to be checked by somebody who was not present.</strong> <strong>Not 'reviewed work orders' - that is a note to yourself and it will support no finding.</strong> <strong>Instead: 'WO-2024-1187, raised 14 March, criticality field blank; WO-2024-1203, raised 19 March, criticality field blank.'</strong> <strong>Every finding you eventually write will be challenged by somebody who has an interest in it being wrong, and they are entitled to challenge it.</strong> <strong>What survives that conversation is a reference. What does not survive is a recollection.</strong> <strong>Record what you saw AND what you were told, and keep them apart.</strong> 'The planner stated that the criticality register has not been updated since 2021' is a different kind of evidence from 'the register's last modification date is 4 August 2021', and a report that blurs the two is weaker than one that carries both. <strong>And record what you could not obtain</strong> - 'the shift handover records for January and February were not available during the audit' is a real result, and leaving it out to keep the record tidy is the beginning of an audit that finds nothing.

Box 2 is the instrument that would have prevented the whole failure, and box 4 is where a number becomes something somebody can fix. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

An audit is not a check that a system is documented. It is a structured attempt to find out whether the organisation does what it says and gets what it needs - and it is designed, before it starts, to be capable of finding out. Five moves:

  • Decide what you are testing. Conformance or effectiveness. Both legitimate, entirely different findings, and an organisation that has only ever had the first believes it has been audited.
  • Build the clause-evidence matrix. Documents, records, observation, interview. Twelve cells for three clauses; the failed audit filled three.
  • Sample to find out. Chosen by you, spread deliberately, sized so a clean result means something.
  • Trace one thread all the way down. From the requirement to the person doing the work - and keep going when it gets easy.
  • Record as you go. Specific enough to be checked, keeping what you saw apart from what you were told.

The arithmetic of the failure. Three clauses, four evidence types: twelve cells. The internal audit filled three - the document column, once per clause - leaving nine empty. Twenty-five per cent of the available evidence, all of it from the one source that cannot tell you whether anything actually happens. Then the sampling: for clause 8.1 there were 340 work orders in scope. The internal audit examined none, because it examined the procedure describing them. The external assessor examined fifteen and found six with no evidence of the required check - 40 per cent. At forty per cent, fifteen was almost certain to find it and zero was certain not to.

Sampling tells you how often; tracing tells you why. The 40 per cent is a number. Following the thread produced the finding: the criticality register was last updated three years ago, so a planner has no way to look up an asset added since, and leaving the field blank is the more honest of the two options they have. One of those you can put in a report. The other somebody can fix.

What the matrix really does. It makes the shape of your audit visible before it is too late to change it. Nobody chose to build a document-only audit. The matrix did not exist, so nobody could see that was what they had.

An honest boundary. This plans and conducts an audit that can find things. It does not classify the findings or write the report - that is the next unit, and it is where most audits are actually lost. It does not make you a certified lead auditor; certification bodies have their own scheme and their own examinations. And an audit is a sample of a moment: a clean result is evidence, never proof, and the report must say so.

T4: Your Turn

You are auditing clause 9.1 - monitoring, measurement, analysis and evaluation - at a different site. Before you arrive you are sent a pack.

ItemDetail
The procedureComplete, current, signed. Requires monthly performance reporting against nine indicators
What they have sentTwelve monthly reports, all nine indicators populated, all submitted on time
Their message"We've pulled everything for clause 9.1 so you don't have to go looking. Let us know if you need anything else"
What you notice in the reportsThree of the nine indicators show the identical value in all twelve months
Also available, not offeredThe source data behind the indicators; the minutes of the meeting the reports go to; the operators who take the readings
Time on siteOne day
  1. The message is friendly and helpful. Say what is wrong with accepting it as offered - and be careful, because they are not doing anything wrong.
  2. Build the evidence matrix for this one clause. Four types. What goes in each?
  3. The three identical indicators. Give two explanations that are entirely innocent and one that is not, and say what evidence would separate them.
  4. Sampling. They have given you twelve reports. What is the population you actually care about, and what would you sample?
  5. Trace one thread. Pick the most useful thread in this clause and write the chain of questions, ending at the person who does the work.
  6. One day. You cannot do everything. Say what you drop and why.

Work it before you turn over. The point of this one is that the most dangerous audit pack is the helpful one.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. The helpful pack. They are genuinely trying to help, and most sites that do this are being courteous rather than clever. But a sample somebody else selected is not a sample - it is a presentation, and it can only tell you about the material they thought to include. Accepting it means your audit's scope was set by the auditee. The reply is easy and not confrontational: thank them, use the pack as background, and select your own examples from the full list.

2. The matrix for clause 9.1. Documents - the procedure, and the definitions of the nine indicators. Records - the twelve reports, and the source data behind at least a few of the numbers. Observation - watch somebody take a reading and enter it. Interview - ask the person who compiles the report what they do when a number is missing, and ask somebody who receives it what decision it changed.

3. The three identical values. Innocent one: they are genuinely stable - a design capacity or a fixed asset count that does not move. Innocent two: the indicator is calculated annually and repeated monthly for continuity, which is a presentation choice rather than a fabrication. Not innocent: nobody is measuring them and last year's figure is being carried forward. What separates them: the source data for those three, for two different months, and the question "show me where this number comes from" asked of the person who compiles it.

4. The population. Not the twelve reports - they are the output, and the output is the part somebody has already checked. The population you care about is the readings and source data behind the indicators, and separately the decisions the reports were supposed to inform. Sample perhaps six indicator-months across different indicators and different months, and trace each to its source. Also sample the meeting minutes - if the report goes to a meeting and no minute ever refers to it, clause 9.1's evaluation requirement is not being met however good the reporting is.

5. The thread. Does the procedure require monthly reporting? Yes. Are the reports produced? Yes, all twelve. Where does indicator four's number come from? A spreadsheet. Who maintains it? The shift supervisor. Where do they get the figure? From the daily logs. Are the daily logs complete? Ask, and go and look. And then the question that matters most: when indicator four moved outside its target in month seven, what happened? If the answer is "nothing", you have found the real finding, and it is not about data quality at all.

6. What you drop, with one day. Drop breadth, keep depth. Do not attempt all nine indicators - audit three properly, including the three identical ones, and say in the report that three of nine were examined and which. A report that says "three of nine indicators were traced to source" is honest and useful. One that implies all nine were examined because all nine were mentioned is the failed audit from the opening of this unit, arriving by a different route.

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Testing conformance and calling it an audit of effectiveness. Documents-exist and system-works are different findings. Decide which one you are testing before you start, and say so.

• Filling the evidence matrix from one source and treating that as coverage. A matrix with documents, records, observation and interview has four columns - filled in one, it is a quarter of the available evidence.

• Accepting a sample somebody else selected for you. A sample chosen by the auditee is a presentation, not a sample - select your own, and size it so a clean result means something.

• Stopping at the document instead of tracing the requirement to the person doing the work. Sampling tells you a rate. Tracing one thread all the way down tells you why.

What you can do now that you could not before. State which of the two audits you are conducting. Build the matrix that makes your audit's shape visible before it starts. Select your own sample and size it so a clean result means something. Trace a requirement to the person doing the work. And record evidence somebody else could check.

The internal auditor who found nothing did their job as they had been trained to do it. Nobody had ever asked them to check whether the plant did what the documents said, and the file contains no instrument that would have shown them the question was missing.

The documents existed. They had always existed. That was the one thing the audit was capable of discovering.
