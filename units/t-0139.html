I handed over the work and kept the risk

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and a reservoir needs cleaning and inspecting inside - a confined-space entry, one of the most dangerous jobs on any plant. To save time and money, the work was given to the cheapest contractor who could start soonest. Nobody checked their safety record. Nobody asked to see a method statement.

You happen to walk past the site on the morning of the job, and your blood runs cold. The crew is about to climb into the tank - no gas monitor, no rescue plan, no permit, improvising the whole thing. Minutes, at most, from someone not coming back out.

And here is what stops you dead: it is your reservoir. Your site. Your permit system they never asked for. It would have been your fatality, and your name in the report. You handed over the work - and you kept every single gram of the risk, without even realising it. You had no process at all for the people you let onto your plant, and you only learned how bad it was by pure chance, standing at the tank.

I gave the work to someone else, but the danger, the duty and the accountability never left my site. How do I manage a contractor on high-risk work so I stay in control of a hazard that is now in someone else's hands?

Before you read on, decide. The reservoir needed cleaning and inspecting inside. You gave it to the crew who quoted lowest and could start soonest — the only two things anybody asked about. You happened to walk past the tank this morning, and what you saw was a man going down through the hatch with no gas test, no attendant at the opening and no rescue arrangement of any kind. Had you not walked past, nobody here would have known. It is your site, your hatch, your duty and your name on the report. Write down how you would manage a contractor on dangerous work — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what actually got transferred

• “Stop the job and get them off site.” Correct for this morning, and it is not a system. Tomorrow you appoint somebody else on the same two criteria and find out about them the same way, if you happen to walk past.

• “Put safety requirements in the contract.” Necessary and, on its own, paper. A clause obliges a crew who could not meet it to sign that they will, and it is enforced after somebody is hurt — which is not when you need it enforced.

• “Do this work ourselves — never contract it out.” Sometimes right, often impossible, and it concedes something false: that dangerous work given to competent people is inherently uncontrollable. It is controllable. It was simply not controlled.

• “Insist they carry insurance and hold them liable.” Money moves and nothing else does. Insurance settles who pays afterwards; it has never once kept anybody out of a tank they should not have entered, and it will not stop the consequence landing on your site.

• “Watch them more closely.” Better than not watching, and you found this by luck rather than by design. Supervision that depends on somebody happening to walk past is not supervision — and you cannot stand at every hatch.

• The thing to see is that you handed over the work and kept every gram of the risk. The hazard, the duty and the accountability never moved; what you actually transferred was control over them, which is the opposite of the intention. And the reason it was possible is that you had no process at all for who is allowed onto your plant — price and availability are the two criteria that say nothing about whether these people can do this work safely. So: establish capability before award, because the alternative is establishing it at the tank on the day. Have they done this specific kind of work, what is their record, can they give a credible account of how they intend to do it. Write the standards and the measures down before day one — the same things agreed afterwards are an argument, and a measure you never set is one you can never enforce. Then keep control of your own site while their hands do the job: induction to the hazards they cannot know about, the method checked against what is actually happening, and presence. And review it afterwards, so the next appointment knows more than this one did.

There is a plan for exactly this, and it runs from before they are chosen to after they leave. Here it is, for the reservoir job.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the contractor management plan that turns "the cheapest crew who could start soonest" into a controlled, safe piece of work.

1. Is this contractor competent and safe - BEFORE I award? (pre-qualification)

Before anyone is chosen for the reservoir entry, pre-qualify: their SAFETY RECORD (incident history, any prohibitions), their COMPETENCE for confined-space work specifically, REFERENCES from similar jobs, and their ability to produce a proper confined-space METHOD STATEMENT. The cheapest, soonest-available contractor is not the answer to 'who is safe enough to put in my tank'. You select up front on capability - because the alternative is discovering the answer at the tank, on the day, the hard way.

2. What exactly will they do, to what HSE standard, measured how? (set the terms)

In writing, before day one: the SCOPE (clean and inspect the reservoir, defined boundaries), the HSE REQUIREMENTS they must meet (confined-space controls, gas testing, a rescue plan, PPE), and the PERFORMANCE METRICS you will hold them to - safety (zero recordable incidents, near-misses reported), quality (inspection passed first time, no rework), and schedule. Agreeing these afterwards is arguing; agreeing them before is managing. The metrics you never set are the ones you can never enforce.

3. How do I control my site while their hands do the work? (on-site HSE management)

On site: INDUCTION to the works' hazards and rules; the task-specific RAMS reviewed and ACCEPTED (not a generic template - this tank, this task); a PERMIT TO WORK issued for the confined-space entry by the authorised person, with isolations and a rescue plan in place; and active SUPERVISION while the work is done. Note the boundary carefully: the permit is signed and the gas test is done by QUALIFIED people - your job is to ensure that system is running and being followed, not to run the hard controls yourself.

4. Did they perform - to the metrics? (performance verification)

Against the metrics set in box 2: any incidents or near-misses? Did the inspection pass first time, or was there rework? Was the schedule met? INSPECT the actual work - the reservoir is clean and the inspection report is sound - and formally CLOSE OUT the job, confirming HSE and quality before you release them and pay. 'It looks done' is not verification; measuring against what you agreed is.

5. How do I make the next selection smarter? (the scorecard loop)

Record a SCORECARD - safety, quality, schedule, behaviour - and feed it into the next pre-qualification (box 1 of the next job). That closes the loop: the contractor who worked safely and well is re-selected; the one who cut corners is screened out before they are ever near your tank again. And the scale note: a TURNAROUND brings a whole workforce of contractors under a compressed timeline with simultaneous operations - this same five-step plan is not less important then but MORE, because the risk is multiplied across dozens of crews at once.

Read it from left to right and notice where the control lives: almost all of it is before the crew ever arrives. By the time they are at the tank, the dangerous decisions - are they competent, is there a permit, is there a rescue plan - have already been made, in daylight, by you. The ache happened because every one of those decisions was left to chance on the morning. Let us name the steps.

T3: The Blueprint

Managing a contractor on high-risk work is a lifecycle, and it starts long before anyone lifts a tool. Five moves, each a question:

  • Is this contractor competent and safe - before I award? Pre-qualification: assess their safety record, their competence for this kind of work, their references, and whether they can produce a proper task-specific method statement - and decide then, on capability, not on price alone. The ache existed because nobody asked these questions until the crew was at the tank.
  • What exactly will they do, to what standard, measured how? Set the terms in writing before day one: the scope, the HSE requirements they must meet, and the performance metrics - safety, quality, schedule - you will hold them to. A metric you did not agree in advance is one you cannot enforce later.
  • How do I control my site while their hands do the work? On-site HSE management: induct them to your hazards and rules; review and accept their site- and task-specific RAMS (a risk assessment and method statement - the step-by-step safe method, not a generic template); ensure a permit to work is issued for the high-risk activity; and supervise actively while it happens. Read one boundary very carefully: the permit is authorised, and the gas test done, by qualified people. Your job is to make sure that system of control is running and being followed - not to run the hard controls yourself.
  • Did they perform - to the metrics? Performance verification: measure against what you agreed - incidents, rework, schedule - inspect the actual work, and formally close it out before you release and pay. "It looks finished" is not verification; measuring against the agreed metrics is.
  • How do I make the next selection smarter? A contractor scorecard from this job feeds the next pre-qualification - so the crew that worked safely and well is re-selected, and the one that cut corners is screened out before they are ever near your tank again. The loop is what turns one good decision into a lasting standard.

And it scales. A turnaround - a planned shutdown - brings a whole workforce of contractors onto your site at once, under a compressed timeline, with many jobs running simultaneously. This same five-step plan does not become less important then; it becomes the thing standing between an orderly shutdown and a chaotic, dangerous one, applied across dozens of crews.

One honest boundary, and it is a safety boundary, so hold it exactly. You build and run the system of control - the plan, the supervision of the process. You are not the certified safety officer who signs the permit, the person who does the gas test, or the lawyer who writes the contract's legal clauses. You are the engineer who ensures those experts are engaged and their controls are in place and working, to your plan. And you cannot delegate accountability: the work goes to someone else; the duty stays with you.

Does it scale? When you hire a contractor for high-risk work you are like a passenger hiring a private pilot - you do not fly the plane yourself, but you still check the licence and see the flight plan before you get on board.

T4: Your Turn

A different high-risk job: a contractor must weld a new branch onto a pipeline close to a live main - hot work, near flammable and pressurised systems, on your site.

  1. Pre-qualify. Write one pre-qualification question whose answer would screen out a contractor you should not let near this job.
  2. Permit. Name the high-risk activity here that must be controlled by a permit to work - and say who issues it (not you).
  3. Supervise. State one on-site step you would take to keep control while their crew does the welding.
  4. Measure. Define two performance metrics - one safety, one quality - you would hold them to.

The test of a real answer: in step 2, the issuer of the permit must be an authorised, qualified person - not you, the managing engineer. If you wrote yourself in as the permit authority, re-read the boundary: you run the plan that ensures the permit exists; you do not sign it.

T5: The Guardrails

Contractor management fails in a handful of predictable ways, and on high-risk work the failures are the ones that end careers and lives.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Selecting on price, pre-qualifying nothing. The exact cause of the ache. The cheapest crew who can start tomorrow is not the same as the crew safe enough to do the job. Pre-qualification is the cheapest safety control you will ever buy.

• Accepting a generic method statement. A RAMS that is not specific to this task and this site is a form filled in, not a plan for staying alive. If it could describe any job, it describes none of them.

• Letting high-risk work start with no permit. Confined space, hot work, working at height, isolation - these are exactly the activities a permit to work exists to control. No permit is not a delay; it is a decision to work uncontrolled.

• "Supervision" that means signing them in and walking away. Presence is not supervision. If nobody is watching the work as it happens, nobody catches the shortcut before it becomes an incident.

• Measuring only cost and schedule. A job delivered on time and on budget with a near-miss buried in it is not a job well done. If safety and quality are not measured, only speed is rewarded - and speed is what hurt people.

• No scorecard. Without feedback, the contractor who cut corners this year is a fresh face at pre-qualification next year. The loop is how a bad experience becomes a permanent screen.

• Believing the risk left with the work. It did not. Contracting out the task does not contract out the hazard on your site or the accountability for it. That belief is the one that has to be un-learned before any of the rest matters.

Onalenna nearly lost someone in a tank because she gave away a job and kept a risk she did not know she still held. Now you can hold it properly: pre-qualify before you award, set the terms and the metrics in writing, control your site while another crew works it, verify against what you agreed, and feed every job into a smarter next choice. You will never fly the plane - but you will always check the licence and the flight plan first.
