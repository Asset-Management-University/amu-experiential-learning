Step 1 of 5

A good system, asked to do something it was never built for

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and your permit-to-work system has run faultlessly for eleven years.

Then the major shutdown starts at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and by 06:30 on the second day there are forty people queuing outside a permit office built for a dozen a day. A supervisor asks you to just sign these four, we will fill in the details later. Two crews are working one above the other in a space where neither knows the other is there. And a contractor you have never met is standing at an open flange asking who has the key.

Nothing about your system is wrong. Every rule in it is sound. Every form is the right form.

It is simply being asked to do something it was never built to do - and you can feel the shape of what happens next. Somebody is going to be hurt not because anyone was careless, not because a rule was missing, but because a good system was overwhelmed. And afterwards the investigation will find a permit that was signed rather than checked, and it will have your signature on it.

My everyday safety system is sound and it is drowning. How do I build a safety plan for a turnaround - for the volume, the crowding and the transitions that normal operation never has to survive?

Before you read on, decide. Your permit system is sound. On an ordinary day it handles about 12 permits, each taking roughly 8 minutes to raise, check and issue — a hundred minutes of work that one office absorbs without anybody noticing. Next month the plant opens up: several hundred contractors who have never been here, four trades working in the same vessel space, twelve-hour shifts and night work, a schedule already compressed — and around 380 permits a day. This morning there was a queue at the permit office, and a supervisor waited eleven minutes and then went to find someone who could just sign it. Write down how you would plan safety for this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what happens to a control it cannot execute

• “Add more permit issuers.” Necessary, and do the arithmetic before you decide how many — 380 at eight minutes is over fifty hours of issuing in a single day. Guess at the number and you will be short, and being short is not a visible failure.

• “Brief everybody thoroughly on the existing system.” The system is not misunderstood; it is oversubscribed. A better-briefed queue is still a queue, and the supervisor who walked away this morning knew the rules perfectly.

• “Simplify the permit for the turnaround so it is quicker.” Reduces the time per permit by thinning the check — during precisely the period when the plant is open, the people are unfamiliar and the checking matters most.

• “Enforce it strictly — nobody works without a permit.” Everyone will have a permit. The question is what a permit will mean by the second week, when the only way to issue enough of them is to sign rather than check.

• “Our system is sound — it has worked for years.” True, and it is answering a different question. A period like this does not scale the risk; it transforms it in kind.

• Name what actually changes. The plant is opened, exposing stored energy where there normally is none. Hundreds of people who have never been here arrive at once, unfamiliar with the layout, the alarms and the habits. Many trades work in the same space, each entirely safe alone. Hours extend and night work begins, so fatigue enters the system. And the schedule compresses — which matters more than it sounds, because compression correlates with cost, delay and harm, through the same mechanism of inadequate preparation. So size the control before you trust it: a control that cannot be executed in the hours available does not fail visibly — it quietly becomes a signature, which is worse than no control at all, because everybody goes on believing it works. Then plan for what only exists in these conditions: the interactions between trades, which nobody owns, and the transitions in and out of the abnormal state, where the rules change and nobody is certain which set applies.

You stop stretching the everyday system and design one for the conditions. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the turnaround safety plan - built for volume, for interaction, and for the moments in between.

1. What actually changes in a turnaround? (the hazard changes in kind)

A turnaround does not scale up the risk of normal work; it TRANSFORMS IT IN KIND. Name what changes. THE PLANT IS OPENED and its stored energy exposed - pressure, chemical, electrical, mechanical, gravitational - in places that are normally sealed. HUNDREDS OF PEOPLE WHO HAVE NEVER BEEN HERE arrive at once, unfamiliar with the site, its layout, its alarms and its habits. MANY TRADES WORK IN THE SAME SPACE simultaneously, each safe alone. SHIFTS EXTEND and night work begins, so fatigue enters the system. And THE SCHEDULE COMPRESSES - which matters more than it sounds, because compressed timelines correlate consistently with cost overruns, schedule delays AND SAFETY INCIDENTS, all through inadequate preparation. A safety plan designed for the steady state is not merely stretched by this. It is answering a different question.

2. Design the permit system for the VOLUME (do the arithmetic)

Size it before you trust it. NORMAL OPERATIONS: 12 permits a day at 8 minutes to raise, check and issue = 96 MINUTES a day. One office absorbs that without anyone noticing. DURING THE TURNAROUND: 380 permits a day at the same 8 minutes = 3,040 MINUTES = 50.7 HOURS OF ISSUING IN A SINGLE DAY - a 31.7-FOLD INCREASE on a system nobody redesigned. A 10-hour issuing window gives 600 minutes per issuer, so that is 5.07 ISSUERS FLAT OUT WITH NO SLACK, which is exactly how a queue of tired trades forms at 06:00 and how a queue turns into permits that were SIGNED RATHER THAN CHECKED. So redesign: put 40 per cent (152) of permits onto PRE-APPROVED TEMPLATES - developed and risk-assessed DURING THE PLANNING PHASE, never on the day, and only for highly repetitive, well-understood, low-risk tasks whose hazards and controls are stable - at 3 minutes each = 456 minutes; the remaining 228 at 8 minutes = 1,824; total 2,280 MINUTES = 3.8 ISSUERS across 4 ZONE PERMIT OFFICES. And GROUP ACTIVE PERMITS BY AREA in one place, so that simultaneous operations become visible on a board instead of being discovered by a welder.

3. The SIMOPS matrix - what is safe alone and lethal together

SIMULTANEOUS OPERATIONS are the hazards created by CONCURRENT activities in the same work area - each individually safe, jointly lethal. Hot work above an open vessel entry. A lift travelling over a live line. Hydro-jetting beside exposed electrical work. The tool is an INTERACTION MATRIX mapping EVERY active work front against EVERY other AND against LIVE OPERATIONS, traffic-lighted: GREEN proceeds; YELLOW proceeds ONLY with stated instructions and control measures written down; RED is RESTRICTED OR RESCHEDULED - moved in time or space so the two never meet. Name an AREA AUTHORITY who owns each zone, and hold a DAILY COORDINATION MEETING that resolves tomorrow's interactions before they meet each other in a confined space. And keep the matrix LIVE: the schedule moves every day, so a matrix built once at the start describes a turnaround that no longer exists.

4. Mass energy isolation - and every worker's right to verify

Hundreds of isolations cannot each carry every worker's personal lock, so use a GROUP LOCK BOX. The isolations are made, and the keys to the equipment isolation locks go into the box. THE PRIMARY AUTHORISED PERSON - the one in charge of the isolation - FITS THEIR OWN LOCK TO THE BOX FIRST, securing those keys and holding the chain of control, so that no individual worker can ever reach them. Only then does each worker fit their OWN PERSONAL PADLOCK to the box as well. The keys, and therefore the ability to restore energy, remain inaccessible until EVERY padlock has been removed. THE GOVERNING PRINCIPLE IS ABSOLUTE, not a guideline: a group procedure is acceptable ONLY if it is demonstrably AS SAFE FOR EVERY INDIVIDUAL as placing their own lock directly on the isolation point. And then the part that is a right rather than a step: EVERY AUTHORISED EMPLOYEE HAS THE PERSONAL RIGHT AND RESPONSIBILITY TO VERIFY THAT ENERGY ISOLATION IS EFFECTIVE BEFORE STARTING WORK. THIS IS A NON-NEGOTIABLE RIGHT OF SURVIVAL, NOT A PROCEDURAL OPTION. THE SAFETY PLAN MUST EMPOWER AND PROTECT THIS RIGHT, AND LINE MANAGEMENT MUST COMMUNICATE IT TO EVERY WORKER ON THE JOB. The sequence: fit your personal lock to the box, THEN verify the isolation yourself, THEN begin work.

5. Plan the transitions - especially putting it back

The dangerous moments are the TRANSITIONS - shift handover, and above all DE-ISOLATION AND START-UP, when the work is nearly done, everyone is tired, and the schedule is screaming. REINSTATEMENT IS NOT ADMINISTRATIVE CLEAN-UP. It is a planned, high-risk activity, the mirror image of isolation, and it demands the same rigour, in this order every time: every personal lock removed BY THE PERSON WHO FITTED IT; the area walked and verified CLEAR OF PEOPLE and tools; guards refitted; affected people notified; and only then the PRIMARY AUTHORISED PERSON REMOVES THEIR OWN LOCK LAST, retrieves the keys and de-isolates the equipment. That last lock is the point of the whole arrangement - it means energy cannot be restored by anyone but the person accountable for it, and only after everybody else has signed themselves out. Then the two authorities that hold the whole plan up. STOP-WORK AUTHORITY BELONGS TO EVERY SINGLE PERSON ON SITE, regardless of rank or employer, because the person with the best view of the risk is usually the one doing the work - with a NO-BLAME process for raising it, resolving it safely and resuming. And SCHEDULE PRESSURE IS NEVER A VALID REASON to override a stop-work or to shorten a safety process.

Box 2 is the one that changes how people think. Ninety-six minutes a day becomes fifty hours a day, on the same system, with the same office and the same person signing. Nothing was neglected. The arithmetic simply moved. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A turnaround does not scale up the risk of normal work - it transforms it in kind, demanding a safety system designed not for the steady state but for overwhelming volume, chaotic interaction and dangerous transitions. Five questions:

  • What actually changes? The plant is opened and its stored energy exposed; hundreds of unfamiliar people arrive; many trades share one space; shifts extend into night work; and the schedule compresses - which correlates with incidents through inadequate preparation.
  • Can the permit system carry the load? Do the arithmetic before trusting it. Then design capacity: zone permit offices, pre-approved templates (developed and risk-assessed during planning, never on the day, and only for highly repetitive, well-understood, low-risk tasks with stable hazards and controls), staggered issue windows, dedicated coordinators - and group active permits by area, so simultaneous operations appear on a board rather than being discovered by a welder.
  • What is safe alone and lethal together? SIMOPS - the hazards of concurrent activities in one area - managed with an interaction matrix mapping every work front against every other and against live operations, traffic-lighted: green proceeds, yellow proceeds only with stated controls, red is restricted or rescheduled. With a named area authority and a daily coordination meeting. Keep it live, because the schedule moves daily.
  • How do you isolate at scale? The group lock box. The keys to the equipment isolation locks go into a box; the primary authorised person fits their lock to it first, holding the chain of control so no individual worker can reach those keys; each worker then adds their own personal padlock. Energy cannot be restored while any padlock remains. The governing principle is absolute: a group procedure is acceptable only if it is demonstrably as safe for every individual as their own lock on the isolation point. The sequence for each worker is lock on, then verify, then work - your lock is what protects you, so it goes on before you go and check, never after.
  • What about putting it back? Reinstatement is a planned high-risk activity, the mirror image of isolation, deserving the same rigour - not administrative clean-up at the end of a tiring job.

Two rights that hold the whole plan up.

The right to verify. Every authorised employee has the personal right and responsibility to verify that energy isolation is effective before starting work. This is a non-negotiable right of survival, not a procedural option. The safety plan must empower and protect this right, and line management must communicate it to every worker on the job. It is not a check performed on someone by a system; it belongs to the person whose life the isolation is protecting.

Stop-work authority. An effective safety plan grants it to every single person on the site, regardless of rank or employer - because the person with the best view of the risk is usually the person doing the work, not the person in the office. The plan must include a no-blame process for exercising it, resolving the issue safely and resuming. And schedule pressure is never a valid reason to override a stop-work, or to shorten any safety process.

One honest boundary. You are the architect of the safety plan, not the general contractor running the turnaround. You design the systems that prevent chaos; you do not manage the chaos itself. Nor are you selecting or vetting the contracting organisations - that is its own discipline. Think of it this way: this technique builds the fence; contractor management ensures you hire people who know not to climb it. The plan you write here binds everyone on site equally, employee and contractor alike.

Does it scale? A hospital that treats twelve patients a day and one that treats four hundred are not the same hospital with a longer queue. The second needs triage, separate streams, a different layout, and a way to see the whole floor at once - and a hospital that simply asked its twelve-patient system to work harder would not be under-resourced, it would be dangerous. Your permit office on day two is that hospital.

T4: Your Turn

A smaller works is planning a nine-day shutdown. Normal operations run 8 permits a day. During the shutdown the planners expect 150 permits a day, at an average of 10 minutes each to raise, check and issue. The issuing window is 10 hours. Three work fronts run concurrently:

  • Front A: hot work - welding a replacement section of pipework on the upper gantry.
  • Front B: confined space entry - cleaning the clarifier directly below that gantry.
  • Front C: electrical work - re-terminating the motor control centre feeding the clarifier scraper drive.
  1. Size it. Calculate the daily issuing load in minutes and hours, the increase on normal operations, and the number of issuers needed. Say what you would change.
  2. Matrix it. Build the three-by-three interaction matrix for fronts A, B and C. Traffic-light each interaction and justify every yellow and red.
  3. Isolate. Front C requires the scraper drive isolated. Describe the group isolation, and state what every worker on front B must be told.
  4. Put it back. Write the reinstatement sequence for the scraper drive, in order.
  5. Authority. State who may stop this job, and what happens next when they do.

The test of a real answer: three things separate a plan from a wish here.

First, if your answer to step 1 is "increase supervision" or "brief the team", you have not planned anything - you have expressed a hope. Size the load, then design capacity for it.

Second, look hard at A above B. Hot work on a gantry directly above a confined space entry is the textbook lethal interaction: sparks and slag fall, and the people below are in a space they cannot quickly leave. That is not a yellow to be managed with a fire watch and good intentions - ask yourself honestly whether any set of instructions makes it acceptable, or whether the honest answer is red, and one of them moves.

Third, and most missed: step 4 is not the end of the exercise. Reinstating the scraper drive while people may still be inside the clarifier is the single most dangerous act in this shutdown, and it happens on the last day when everyone is exhausted and the plant is wanted back. If your sequence does not verify the space is clear of people before energy is restored, you have written the accident.

T5: The Guardrails

These failures have a body count in this industry. None of them look reckless at the time.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Assuming the everyday permit system scales. It does not. Ninety-six minutes a day becomes fifty hours a day, and nobody notices until the queue forms.

• Measuring the permit system by throughput. Permits issued per hour is a productivity measure. The only question that matters is whether the checks were actually made.

• One permit office, no zoning. Trades cross the site, queue in one place, and arrive at the face already behind.

• Templates written on the day. A pre-approved permit is only safe if it was risk-assessed during planning, for a repetitive task whose hazards and controls are genuinely stable.

• Permits not grouped by area. Then simultaneous operations are discovered rather than planned - usually by whoever is underneath.

• A SIMOPS matrix built once. The schedule moves daily; a matrix from day one describes a turnaround that no longer exists.

• Treating yellow as green because the schedule slipped. This is the specific mechanism by which compressed turnarounds hurt people.

• Group isolation that protects less than a personal lock. The standard is absolute: as safe for every individual as their own lock on the isolation point, or it is not acceptable.

• Workers not told of their right to verify. An unexercised right that nobody knows about is not a right. Line management must communicate it to every worker on the job.

• Reinstatement treated as paperwork. It is the mirror image of isolation, performed by tired people under pressure, and it is where energy meets someone who thought the job was over.

• No named stop-work authority, or one that carries a cost. It belongs to every person on site regardless of rank or employer, with a no-blame process - and schedule pressure never overrides it.

Onalenna's system was not wrong; it was overwhelmed, and overwhelmed systems produce permits that were signed rather than checked. Name what a turnaround changes in kind; size the permit load and design real capacity for it, grouping permits by area so interactions become visible; matrix every work front against every other and reschedule the reds instead of managing them; isolate at scale through a group lock box that protects each person as well as their own lock would; and plan reinstatement with the same rigour as isolation, because putting it back is when energy meets someone who thought the job was over. Every person on that site may stop the job, and every person has the right to verify the isolation keeping them alive. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take your organisation's last shutdown and find out two things: how many permits were issued on the busiest day, and how many people were issuing them.
