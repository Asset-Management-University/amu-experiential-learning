Are we actually doing well? Nobody can say

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and you're sure Tank You is thriving. Look at it - deliveries every day, the bakkies always out, everyone rushing! Ibraaheem isn't so sure. He frowns over the books and says something just feels... off. You disagree. He can't quite say why, and you sound more confident, so the matter is settled the way it always is: the louder, surer voice wins. We're busy, so we're fine.

Then the off feeling turns out to be real. Going carefully through the numbers, Ibraaheem finds it: one of the bakkies - the older one on the township route - has quietly cost more than it earned for three months straight. Repairs, fuel, days off the road - it's been bleeding money while you celebrated how busy the whole fleet was. Nobody saw it, because "we're busy" was the only thing you ever measured, and "busy" was just a feeling.

It lands hard: you've been flying blind. You mistook motion for progress. A slow rot set in right under your nose and you couldn't see it, because you had no honest way to look. And if one bakkie could hide a three-month loss, what else can't you see?

We argue about whether we're doing well and it's just opinions - meanwhile something was quietly rotting and we never saw it. How do I actually KNOW if my business is performing, instead of guessing?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your old way of knowing was a feeling - "we're busy, we're fine" - and it just failed you badly. The opposite temptation is to panic and start tracking everything: every number you can think of, a wall of figures. But that drowns you, and you'd end up ignoring all of it. Neither a gut feeling nor a flood of data tells you the truth.

Before you read on: think about that one money-losing bakkie. What single honest number, written down each month, would have shown you the rot while it was small? And what makes a number worth tracking versus just noise?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The cure for "is it doing well?" being an argument is to turn it into evidence - with a few honest numbers, not a feeling and not a flood. Three moves:

  • Opinion can't see a slow rot: when 'are we doing well?' is settled by gut and the loudest voice, two things go wrong: nobody can actually prove it either way, and slow decline hides until something breaks or a customer complains. Busy is not the same as doing well.
  • Choose a FEW honest numbers: not a wall of metrics - two or three numbers that reveal whether each thing is doing its JOB. For one bakkie: does its monthly money-in beat its true running cost? days off the road? Fewer numbers you actually use beat dozens you ignore.
  • Measure truthfully - your honest baseline: write down where you really stand right now, without flattering yourself. You can't fix, or even see, what you won't look at squarely. The honest number is the starting point.
  • Compare to good: a number alone means little - set it against something: your best bakkie, last month, a sensible target. Now decline shows up EARLY, while it's still cheap to fix, instead of as a breakdown.
  • Guard the number - it serves the goal, never replaces it: any measure made into a blind target gets gamed: push drivers on 'deliveries' alone and you'll get reckless driving and skipped checks. A gamed number is WORSE than none - it fakes success while wrecking the real goal. Keep measures honest and few.

Notice this isn't the same as the records you already keep. Your true-cost-per-delivery and your written-down figures are the raw materials; your breakdown log is one kind of clue. This is the step that turns all that into a verdict - a clear answer to the one question that matters: is this thing winning or losing? You're not collecting data for its own sake; you're choosing the two or three numbers that let you SEE.

And carry the warning loudly, because it's where this goes wrong: a number must serve the goal, never replace it. The moment you make a single measure a blind target - "just get the deliveries up!" - people will hit that number by wrecking what actually matters: driving recklessly, skipping safety checks, rushing customers. A gamed number is worse than no number at all, because it looks like success while quietly destroying the very thing you were trying to grow. Keep your measures few, honest, and pointed at the real goal.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad and Ibraaheem stop arguing and pick one honest number per bakkie.

See the one number that ends the argument - and the trap to avoid

Choose the few: for each bakkie, one number that shows if it's doing its job - money it brought in this month, minus what it truly cost to run (fuel, repairs, a slice of wear, the driver's time). Does it earn its keep? Yes or no.

Measure truthfully: they work it out for every bakkie, honestly. The newer two earn well. The old township-route bakkie: minus money, three months running. The truth that "busy" had hidden, now plain on one line.

Compare to good: against the best bakkie (which clears a healthy surplus), the old one's gap is obvious - and so is the moment it started slipping. Now they can act early: investigate the route, repair-or-replace, reassign - a real decision instead of an argument.

The trap they DON'T fall into: they're tempted to just tell every driver "do more deliveries!" But they realise that one blind target would push drivers to speed, skip the seal-check, and cut corners - winning the number while losing the customers and the safety. So they keep the honest measure (earns-its-keep), not the gameable one (raw deliveries). The number serves the goal.

For the engineers: a plant does exactly this for a critical machine - a FEW real numbers (is it available when needed? does its output meet spec? cost per hour run?), measured honestly, compared to a benchmark - and guards each one against being gamed. A bakkie or a turbine: see the truth with a few honest numbers.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The argument ended the moment you had one honest number to look at. That's the whole gift: not a fancy system, but the clarity to see what's true and catch trouble while it's small. For anything you run - a business, an asset, a project, even a personal goal:

  1. Choose a few honest numbers. Two or three that show whether the thing is doing its JOB - no more. Fewer numbers you actually look at beat a wall of ones you don't.
  2. Measure truthfully, then compare to good. Write down where you really stand right now (no flattering), and set it against something - your best, last month, a fair target - so a slow slide shows up early.
  3. Keep every number honest - it serves the goal. Never make a single measure a blind target, or it'll get gamed and quietly wreck what matters. A measure is a window to see through, not a prize to chase.

You can't improve - or even protect - what you can't see. The people who get ahead aren't the busiest; they're the ones who chose a few honest numbers, looked at them squarely, and so could act on truth while everyone else was still arguing about feelings. Trade the loudest opinion for one honest number, and you'll never fly blind again.
