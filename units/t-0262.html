One page, and the cost of doing nothing

T1: The Ache

You are Ntsika, and your report is finished.

It is good. It found that the tank has overflowed four times in three years, with four different operators, and that the reason nobody can see the problem is a flow transmitter removed in a modification and never replaced. It recommends putting the indication back.

You sent it three weeks ago. Nothing has happened.

Not because anybody disagreed. You asked, and the answer was "it's on the list" - said kindly, by somebody genuinely busy, who has not read past page two of a nine-page document and is not going to.

So you know exactly what is wrong, and you know exactly how to fix it, and you have no way to make it happen. Somewhere in the next nine months that tank will overflow again, and everybody will be surprised except you.

I know what the problem is and how to fix it, and I have no money and no authority. How do I ask for R180,000 in a way that somebody will actually read and decide on?

Before you read on, decide. You wrote nine pages. It opens with the history of the modification, moves through the panel architecture, and reaches the actual request on page six. You need R180,000 to reinstate flow indication and valve position on the two transfer pumps. The tank has overflowed four times in three years, costing 44 hours of lost production. Nobody disagreed with you. The answer was “it's on the list”, said kindly, by somebody genuinely busy who did not read past page two and is not going to. Somewhere in the next nine months it will overflow again and everybody will be surprised except you. Write down how you would ask — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the document is actually for

• “Send it again and push harder.” The same nine pages arriving a second time, at somebody with the same amount of attention. Persistence against a document that cannot be read produces irritation rather than a decision.

• “Add more evidence — make the case stronger.” Page ten. Every true and unnecessary paragraph is a paragraph in which a busy reader stops, so strengthening it this way makes it less likely to work.

• “Go over their head.” Spends real credit to hand somebody more senior the same unreadable document, and damages the person you will need for the next request.

• “Wait for the next overflow — then they will listen.” Probably true and it costs another overflow, and you would be trading an event you can foresee for the argument you cannot currently make.

• “Ask for less so it is easier to approve.” Solves affordability, which was never the objection. Nobody rejected the amount — nobody reached it.

• The reframe: you wrote a document to be understood, and you needed one to be decided on. So make the first sentence the whole thing — what you want, what it costs, what it prevents — so a reader who stops there already knows what they are being asked. The money goes at the top, because they will look for it and making them hunt costs the attention that was your scarce resource. And what it prevents is a thing that has actually happened, four times, with hours attached — not a thing that might. Then the hard part: leave things out. The modification history, the panel architecture, why the valve is shut after the test — all true, all yours, all belonging somewhere else. A nine-page document is not more rigorous than a one-page one; it is a document that will not be read. Then state the cost of doing nothing as a positive number, because your reader is not comparing R180,000 against zero — they are comparing it against continuing as they are, and 44 hours makes that comparison itself. And make approval easy rather than brave: answer the objections before they are raised, and collect the agreements you would otherwise be asked to go and get.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the case - the ask in one sentence, the cost of doing nothing, the cut to one page, what happens if it is declined, and who has already agreed.

1. Write the ask in one sentence, at the top - what you want, what it costs, and what it prevents

The first sentence is the whole document. Everything below it exists to support that sentence, and a reader who stops after it should already know what you are asking for. Mine: 'I am asking for R180,000 to reinstate flow indication and valve position on the two transfer pumps, which will stop a tank overflow that has happened four times in three years and cost 44 hours of lost production.' Look at what is in that sentence and be strict about it. The ask - a specific thing, not 'improvements to instrumentation'. The money - a real number, at the top, because the reader is going to look for it and making them hunt costs you their attention. What it prevents - and note this is a thing that has actually happened, four times, rather than a thing that might. Now what is deliberately NOT in it. Nothing about the flow transmitter that was removed in the year-two modification. Nothing about the panel. Nothing about why the valve is closed after the monthly test. All of that is true, and all of it is the reason you understand the problem, and none of it belongs in the first sentence of a document addressed to somebody with eleven of these to read this week.

2. Cost what happens if nobody approves this - and use what has already happened, not what might

This is the box that decides whether you are approved, and most engineers leave it out entirely - they cost the fix and forget to cost the alternative, which is not zero. Do it from what has already happened, because a reader will argue with a forecast and cannot argue with a record. Four events in three years. Eleven hours of lost production each. 44 hours. At R14,000 an hour of lost production that is R616,000 over three years - about R205,000 a year, every year, until something changes. Now put the ask beside it. R180,000 against R205,000 a year is a payback of about ten and a half months. State the assumption openly, in the document: the R14,000 an hour is the plant's own figure for lost production and I have used it as given; if it is wrong the payback moves, and I would rather the reader correct my input than distrust my conclusion. And say what is NOT in the number. Mine excludes the 48 hours of retraining that followed the four events, the containment clean-up, and any regulatory consequence had the overflow left the bund. Naming what you left out makes the number you kept more believable, not less - it tells the reader you were counting carefully rather than assembling a case.

3. Cut to one page - and be deliberate about what goes, because it is all true

My first version was six pages and every page was true. That is exactly the problem. One page is not a length limit. It is a decision about what the reader needs in order to say yes, and it forces you to make that decision rather than leaving it to them. What went, and it was the part I was proudest of: two pages explaining how the flow transmitter came to be removed, the modification history, the closed action list. That is the analysis that made me right, and the reader does not need to follow my reasoning in order to act on my conclusion. What stayed: the ask, the cost of doing nothing, the cost of the fix, the payback, what happens if it is declined, and who has agreed the technical approach. What I did with the rest, and this matters: a single line at the bottom - 'Full analysis, modification history and quotations available on request' - which keeps the work alive and available without spending the reader's attention on it. The test for every paragraph: does removing this change the decision? If not, it is background, and background belongs behind the document rather than inside it.

4. Say plainly what happens if this is not approved - without threatening anybody

Write this paragraph carefully, because there are two ways to get it wrong and they are opposite. The first is to leave it out, which lets the reader believe declining is free. It is not; it costs about R205,000 a year, and if they choose that, they are entitled to be choosing it knowingly. The second is to write it as a threat - 'if this is not approved I cannot be responsible for the consequences' - which is a sentence that ends conversations and makes an ally into an opponent. Write it as information. Mine: 'If this is not approved, the current arrangement continues. On the pattern of the last three years that means approximately one overflow every nine months, contained within the bund, at about eleven hours of lost production each. The operators will continue to have no indication of pump discharge at the panel.' That is the whole paragraph. No adjectives, no warning, no appeal. And notice the last sentence, which is not about money at all. Some consequences of declining are not financial, and stating one plainly - the people running this plant will still be unable to see something they need to see - is honest and it is often the sentence that is remembered. Give the reader a real choice and enough to make it with. That is the whole job.

5. Show who has already agreed, and make saying yes a single small action

Two things close the page, and both reduce the work of saying yes. First, who has already agreed. 'The instrument engineer has confirmed the technical approach and provided the quotation. The maintenance planner has confirmed the installation can be done within a normal outage window.' That sentence answers the two questions the reader would otherwise have to go and ask - is this technically sound, and will it disrupt production - and it means approving does not commit them to finding out. Do not overstate it. If somebody has not agreed, do not imply they have; a case that unravels on one checkable claim loses the reader for every claim. Second, say exactly what you need and what follows. 'I am asking for approval of R180,000 from the maintenance capital budget. On approval the instrument engineer will raise the order and the work will be scheduled into the next planned outage in March.' The reader now has one decision to make and can see what happens after they make it. A business case that ends without naming the next step leaves the reader to invent one, and the easiest thing to invent, at the end of a long day with eleven documents to read, is to think about it later.

Box 2 is the one most engineers leave out, and box 3 is the one that hurts. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A business case is not a summary of your analysis. It is a document that makes one decision easy to take - and the person taking it does not need to follow your reasoning in order to act on your conclusion. Five moves:

  • The ask in one sentence, at the top. What, how much, what it prevents. A reader who stops there should already know what you want.
  • Cost the doing-nothing. From what has happened, not what might. A reader argues with a forecast and cannot argue with a record.
  • One page means choosing. Does removing this change the decision? If not, it is background.
  • Say what declining costs. As information, never as a threat.
  • Show who agreed, and name the next step. Make saying yes one small action.

The arithmetic, and why it persuades. Four events, eleven hours of lost production each: 44 hours. At the plant's own figure of R14,000 an hour, that is R616,000 over three years - about R205,000 a year, continuing until something changes. Against an ask of R180,000, the payback is about ten and a half months. Every number in that comes from events that actually occurred and a rate the organisation set itself. Nobody has to accept a projection.

Two disciplines that make a number believable. State the assumption: the R14,000 an hour is the plant's figure and I used it as given; if it is wrong the payback moves. State what is excluded: the 48 hours of retraining, the bund clean-up, any regulatory consequence had the overflow escaped containment. Naming what you left out makes the number you kept more credible, not less - it shows a person counting carefully rather than assembling a case.

The cut, and why it is the hard part. Six pages to one, and what went was the two pages explaining the modification history - the analysis that made you right. That is painful and it is correct: the reader does not need to retrace your reasoning to act on your conclusion. It survives as one line at the bottom - "full analysis available on request" - which keeps the work alive without spending the reader's attention on it.

On the person you are writing to. They are not an obstacle and they are not withholding the money. They have eleven of these this week, a budget that is already committed, and no way of telling a real R180,000 problem from an enthusiastic one except by what is in front of them. Your job is not to defeat them. It is to be the document that makes a good decision possible in four minutes.

An honest boundary. This gets your case read and decided. It does not guarantee approval - the money may genuinely not exist this year, and a well-made case can be declined by somebody who is right. It is not a full investment appraisal; a large project needs whole-life costing and options analysis, and this page may be the thing that gets you permission to do that work. And it cannot rescue a proposal that is not actually worth doing.

T4: Your Turn

Dineo has a harder case than yours, and she has three weeks before the budget closes.

ItemDetail
What she wantsR240,000 for a standby dosing pump on the chlorination line
WhyThere is one pump. If it fails, treated water cannot be dosed
History of failureNone. The pump has run for nine years without failing
What happens if it failsSupply to about 40,000 people must be stopped, or water goes out undosed. Neither is acceptable. Repair lead time for that pump is quoted at 6 to 9 days
Her draft opening"This proposal addresses the significant risk associated with the single point of failure in the chlorination dosing system."
Her cost section"The consequences of a failure would be severe."
The readerThe works manager, who has R900,000 of capital and eleven requests
  1. Her hardest problem is the one that is not in the table. Name it - it is the exact opposite of yours.
  2. Rewrite her opening sentence to the standard of box 1.
  3. "The consequences would be severe." Say what is wrong with this, and what she must do instead given she has no failure history.
  4. Cost the doing-nothing without a record of failures. Describe the method - you do not need her numbers, you need the approach.
  5. The nine years. A reader will say "it has run nine years, why now?" Answer it in the case, in two sentences.
  6. Some consequences are not financial. Write the one sentence for her declined-paragraph that is not about money.

Work it before you turn over. The point of this one is that the strongest cases are often for things that have never gone wrong.

Compare your thinking - one way through

1. Her hardest problem. She has nothing that has happened. Your case rested on four events in a record nobody could argue with. Hers rests on an event that has never occurred in nine years - and against ten other requests, several of which will be fixing things that broke last month, "it has never failed" reads as a reason to keep waiting.

2. The opening rewritten. "I am asking for R240,000 for a standby chlorination dosing pump. The current single pump has a 6 to 9 day repair lead time, and during that period supply to 40,000 people would have to be stopped or the water sent out undosed."

3. "The consequences would be severe." It is an adjective doing the work a number should do, and the reader has read it in every one of the eleven documents. It also tells them nothing they can weigh. Without a failure history she must build the cost from the exposure: the lead time is real and quoted, the population is known, and the consequence of each of the two available responses can be described concretely. Severe is a word. Six to nine days is a fact.

4. Costing without a record. Take the consequence and price it once, then state the likelihood honestly and separately rather than multiplying them into a single figure that hides both. What one failure would cost: days of interrupted supply, tankering or alternative provision, the regulatory position, the repair itself. How likely: she has no failures in nine years, and she should say so plainly - it is the weakest part of her case and hiding it will be noticed. But nine years is also the manufacturer's typical service life, and a pump that has run that long is more likely to fail next year than it was in year two, not less. Present the cost of one event and the ageing argument as two separate things, and let the reader combine them. A reader trusts a case that hands them the pieces more than one that hands them a conclusion.

5. The nine years, answered in two sentences. "The pump has run for nine years without failure, which is at the upper end of its expected service life. The question is not whether it has been reliable - it has - but whether we are prepared to be without dosing for six to nine days at the point when it stops."

6. The non-financial sentence. "If this is not approved, the works has no means of continuing to disinfect water during a dosing pump failure, and the decision at that point would be between stopping supply to 40,000 people and sending out water we have not treated."

No adjectives. No warning. It simply says what the choice would be, and lets the person whose job it is to make it, make it.

T5: The Guardrails

The method is sound. A few traps catch almost everyone applying it for the first time. Glance through these before you trust your own draft.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Burying the ask in the third paragraph. A reader who stops after the first sentence should already know what you want, how much, and what it prevents.

• Using a forecast instead of what has already happened. A reader can argue with a projection. They cannot argue with a record - cost the doing-nothing from events that occurred.

• Padding the page with the analysis that made you confident. If removing a paragraph does not change the decision, it is background - it can survive as a line offering it on request.

• Leaving out what was excluded from the number. Naming what you left out makes the number you kept more credible, not less - it shows a person counting carefully.

What you can do now that you could not before. Put the ask in the first sentence. Cost what happens if nobody acts, from the record rather than a forecast. Cut to one page by asking of each paragraph whether it changes the decision. State the consequence of declining as information rather than as pressure. And make saying yes a single small action with a named next step.

Your nine-page report was right, and being right is not the same as being acted on. The page you write next is not a lesser document than the report - it is the part that turns the report into a pump that gets a valve position indicator fitted in March.

The cost of doing nothing is never zero. It is just the only cost nobody writes down.
