Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Siyabonga, the workshop manager for the city's bus fleet. Your job is to keep the buses on the road - getting people to work, to the clinic, to school. This morning Bus 27 is up on the lift. The job card is perfect, the mechanics are ready, the diagnosis is clear: a failed gearbox sensor, a small and simple part. But your lead mechanic, Phumzile, stands with her arms crossed. "Boss, the part is not in the stores."

You call the storeman. He confirms it. "Sorry, Siya - we don't stock that one. It's R950, we thought it too expensive to keep on the shelf." You phone the supplier. "Yes, we can get it - it comes from the factory overseas. Air freight, about four weeks. Maybe five with customs."

Five weeks. For five weeks a R2 million bus that carries 80 people at a time will sit in your workshop gathering dust - one less bus on the busiest route in the city, longer queues, more crowding, people late for work. All because of a R950 part. You look at the useless, waiting bus and the frustrated faces of your crew.

Everything is ready - the job card, the crew, the diagnosis - but the one part we need is a month away; how do I stock the right spares so planned work stops stalling?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first reaction is anger - "never again". So you tell the storeman to stock everything: two of every part for every bus model. A month later the finance director is in your office. "Siyabonga, you have tied up R3 million in spare parts - shelves full of parts we might not use for ten years, some obsolete by the time we buy new buses. We are a workshop, not a warehouse. You are drowning our cash." So you swing the other way and stock almost nothing - just-in-time, order only when needed. Finance is happy for a week. Then every breakdown becomes a long wait, your workshop fills with stranded buses, and fleet availability drops to 60%. The operations manager is furious and the public is complaining. Finally you try a middle ground: only stock the cheap parts, the ones under R500. You end up with plenty of fuses and lightbulbs - and still no R950 gearbox sensor, because the cheap parts are rarely the ones that strand a bus for a month.

Stocking everything is too expensive. Stocking nothing is a disaster. Stocking only cheap things misses the point. So before you read on: the real question is not HOW MUCH to stock - it is WHAT to stock, and on what basis?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop thinking about the cost of the part and start thinking about the cost of the STOCKOUT. That bus waiting five weeks for a sensor did not cost you R950 - it cost tens of thousands in lost service, penalties and public trust. A spare on the shelf is not idle cash; it is bought-down downtime. With that mindset you build a new system on five moves:

  • See the real trade-off - the cost of the stockout, not the part: the breakthrough is to stop thinking about the price of the part and start thinking about the cost of the STOCKOUT. The bus waiting five weeks for a sensor did not cost you R950 - it cost tens of thousands in lost service, penalties and public trust. A spare on the shelf is not idle cash sitting useless; it is bought-down downtime - an insurance you have already paid. So every stocking decision flips from 'how much does this part cost to hold?' to 'how much does it cost us if we do NOT have it when we need it?'. Holding a critical R4,000 fuel pump for a year might tie up R800; the same pump missing puts a bus off the road for three weeks at R150,000. Once you cost the downtime, most of the hard decisions answer themselves.
  • Sort by criticality x lead time: the decision is not HOW MUCH to stock but WHAT - and the two questions that decide it are: how CRITICAL is this part to running the asset, and how long is the LEAD TIME to get it? Draw the simple grid. HIGH criticality + LONG lead time = MUST STOCK (the gearbox sensor that is weeks away). HIGH criticality + SHORT lead time = hold few or none (a battery you can fetch in an hour). LOW criticality + LONG lead time = order when needed (a trim clip the bus runs fine without). LOW criticality + SHORT lead time = do not stock (a standard wiper). Price is not on the grid - a cheap part can be the one that strands the asset, and an expensive part you can get next-day need not sit on your shelf. Concentrate your stock on the few squares that actually stop the mission (the same focusing discipline as bp-0158).
  • Consumable spare vs insurance spare: split your must-stock parts into two kinds that are managed completely differently. A CONSUMABLE is a part you use regularly - brake pads, filters - so manage it with a reorder level: set a minimum, and when stock falls to it, reorder automatically so it is replenished before it runs out (a simple min-max). An INSURANCE spare is a part you might never use but cannot wait twelve weeks for if you do - a major control unit, a long-lead assembly - so you hold exactly one, as insurance against a rare disaster, and you do not agonise that it sits unused: that is the point of insurance. Confusing the two is how stores end up over-stocked on the wrong things and bare on the things that matter.
  • Attack the lead time for the critical few: the lead time is not a fixed law of nature - for the critical few parts, you can shorten it. Rather than accept five weeks for the must-stock sensor, negotiate a VENDOR-HELD-STOCK or guaranteed-supply agreement: pay the supplier a small fee to always keep one on their shelf in your city, reserved for you, so the lead time drops from weeks to a day. Other levers: a local alternative part, a pooled spare shared with a nearby operator, or a service-level promise from the maker. Shortening the lead time can be cheaper than holding the spare yourself - and it moves a part out of 'must stock' altogether. Spend the effort only where the criticality-times-lead-time grid says it matters.
  • Tie it to early warning: the cruellest stockouts are surprises - the part fails today and the lead time starts today. But a failure you can SEE coming changes everything. Condition monitoring that warns 'this alternator will likely fail in about three weeks' (the discipline of bp-0096) gives you a head start: if the warning is longer than the lead time, an emergency stockout becomes a calm, planned parts order placed in good time. So the spares strategy and the early-warning strategy work together - watching the critical few for the whisper of failure buys you the very lead time you need to have the part ready. Predict, then order, then repair on your terms.

See it transform Siyabonga's store. A year on, the shelves are not full - they are full of the RIGHT things. The gearbox sensor that once meant five weeks off the road is now a vendor-held spare, in his city, a day away. The brake pads reorder themselves at their minimum; the one rare control unit sits as insurance, hopefully never needed. And the newer buses now whisper their warnings - "alternator voltage fluctuating, likely failure in three weeks" - longer than the one-week lead time, so an emergency becomes a planned order. When a bus comes in with a critical failure, the part is there. Fleet availability is at a record high; he is not just fixing buses, he is running a system that guarantees the mission. Your storeroom is no longer managed by the price tags on the parts - it is managed by the cost of downtime. Stop stocking 'just in case' and start stocking strategically for 'when it matters': weigh the cost of the stockout not the part, sort by criticality times lead time, split consumables (a reorder level) from insurance spares (hold one), attack the lead time on the critical few, and let early warning turn surprises into planned orders. A spare on the shelf is not a cost; it is a guarantee that your people can keep their promises to the public.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already run this system at home - think of your medicine cabinet. You do not run it like a pharmacy (stock everything), and you do not keep it empty (stock nothing). You use exactly this logic.

See the same moves in a medicine cabinet

• Consumables: you keep plasters and headache tablets, and when they run low they go on the shopping list - a simple min-max reorder.
• Insurance spare: if someone in the family has a life-threatening allergy you keep an adrenaline auto-injector - costly, and it expires, and you hope never to use it. But you hold it because you cannot afford the 'lead time' of waiting for an ambulance: a high-criticality, cannot-wait item.
• Attack the lead time / early warning: you do not stock every medicine - when you feel ill (early warning) you see a doctor (the diagnosis and plan) and collect the prescription from the pharmacy in an hour (a short lead time you have arranged).

You already know how to do this - you just apply the same thinking to the workshop: stock the few critical, hard-to-replace things, and arrange fast supply for the rest.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think about your own workshop or plant. Find one job in the last three months that stalled, waiting for a part that was not in stock.

  1. Identify the part. What was it, and what machine did it belong to?
  2. Calculate the real cost. Not the part's price - the cost of the downtime: the idle crew, the lost production or service, the missed deadlines, the people let down. Put a number on it, even an estimate.
  3. Map the risk. How critical is that machine to your mission (high / medium / low)? And what is the true lead time to get the part (hours / days / weeks / months)?
  4. Decide. On that risk map, should the part have been a 'must-stock' item? If so, you have found your first target - then split it (consumable or insurance), and ask whether you could shorten its lead time.

This is not about blaming the storeman or the finance department - both were being reasonable with the rule they were given ('keep the cheap things, save the cash'). It is about changing the rule: decide what to stock by the risk to the mission, not by the price tag, and the storeroom turns from a cost centre into a tool that keeps your promises to the people you serve. A spare on the shelf is bought-down downtime - so that when the job is ready, the parts are too.
