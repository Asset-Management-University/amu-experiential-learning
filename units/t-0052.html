My schedule compliance is 79% for the third time and the boss wants R40,000 of overtime to fix it - but the number always bounces around. Is this a real problem, or just noise I would be wasting money chasing?

T1: The Ache

It is Monday morning at a ferrochrome smelter, and you are the maintenance planner for the crushing and screening section - fourteen months in the job. In your hand is the weekly maintenance report you print every Monday. Near the top is the one number everyone watches: schedule compliance - the share of planned jobs that actually got done in the week they were planned. Head office wants it above 85%. This week it reads 79%.

Across the desk, your section manager, Bonginkosi, taps the page with one finger. He is not shouting - he is worse than shouting, he is calm. "That's the third bad week," he says. "Sort it out, or I send people overtime to catch up the backlog, and that comes off your budget." Your stomach drops: the catch-up weekend he means is about R40,000.

Here is what nags at you. You pull the last twelve weeks from your file: 88, 84, 91, 86, 79, 90, 87, 82, 79, 85, 88, 79. The number jumps around - some weeks great, some bad. Was this 79% a real problem, something that broke in how you work, or just the normal bounce this number always does? Your first instinct is to react right now - call a meeting, blame the contractor who was late, change the plan. But you have done that before, and the number bounced back up on its own the next week anyway. You are not sure whether you would be fixing a real problem or just chasing noise - and chasing noise wastes the team's time and your credibility.

Was this 79% a real signal worth chasing, or just the normal bounce of the process? If I react now, am I fixing a problem - or wasting my team's time and credibility on noise?

Before you read on, decide. Twelve weeks in your file: 88, 84, 91, 86, 79, 90, 87, 82, 79, 85, 88, 79. The target is 85%. This week came in at 79, and the catch-up weekend he is proposing is about R40,000 of your budget. Write down what you would do on Monday morning. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one costs when you are wrong

• “Call a meeting and find out what went wrong this week.” The instinct, and you have done it before — and the number came back up on its own the following week anyway. Reacting to ordinary movement is not neutral: it costs the team a morning, it costs you credibility, and if the process was fine you have just adjusted something that did not need adjusting.

• “It’s the contractor who was late — that’s the cause.” There is always a cause available for a bad week, and one can be found for a good week too if anybody looks. A story that fits is not evidence that anything changed, and a blamed contractor is a real cost to a relationship you will need next month.

• “Three bad weeks in twelve — that’s a trend.” Your eye says so. Read the twelve again: 79 has now appeared three times, spread out, alongside a 91 and a 90. Whether that is a pattern or the ordinary bounce is exactly the question — and eyeballing a list is how people convince themselves of both answers.

• “Approve the overtime — better safe than sorry.” R40,000 buys back the backlog and buys no information at all. Next month the number bounces again, and you will be in this chair with the same question and R40,000 less.

• “Do nothing — it always bounces back.” Closer than the others, and it is the same guess wearing the opposite coat. You would be right this week and wrong the week something genuinely breaks, because nothing in “it usually recovers” can tell the two apart.

Now look once more at the twelve numbers and add them up in your head. The average of what this process actually does is not the same as the target it is being held to — and that is a different problem from the one everybody is about to have a meeting about.

You need a way to tell, from the numbers alone, whether a week like 79% is a genuine signal that something went wrong - or the ordinary up-and-down every process has. You need to know when to act and when to leave it alone. Here is that tool, worked on your twelve weeks.

T2: The Tool in Action

Every number you track moves up and down. The hard question is not "is it high or low?" but "is this movement normal, or a signal that something changed?" Reacting to normal movement is called tampering, and it makes things worse - you keep adjusting a process that was fine. Statistical Process Control (SPC) uses a process's own past numbers to draw a "normal range" on a control chart, so you can tell the two apart. Watch it answer your Monday problem.

Step 1 - Plot the numbers and find the centre line

Plot the twelve weeks of schedule compliance in time order: 88, 84, 91, 86, 79, 90, 87, 82, 79, 85, 88, 79. The centre line is the average: the twelve add to 1,018, divided by 12 = 84.8% - the middle of the normal bounce. Note it already sits BELOW the 85% target, an early clue.

Step 2 - Draw the control limits (NOT the target)

The absolute gaps between consecutive weeks (4, 7, 5, 7, 11, 3, 5, 3, 6, 3, 9) average to 5.73. Distance = 2.66 x 5.73 = 15.2. UCL = 84.8 + 15.2 = 100.0% (capped at 100); LCL = 84.8 - 15.2 = 69.6%. The normal range is about 70% to 100% - this is how the process behaves, not the 85% head office wants.

Step 3 - Read the chart: signal or noise?

All twelve points - including all three 79% weeks - sit INSIDE the band; not one falls below 69.6%. There is no special cause here: every point is common-cause noise, the ordinary up-and-down the process always does. The 79% that triggered the alarm is squarely inside the normal range.

Step 4 - Make the call: act, or improve the process

The 79% is noise, not a signal - do NOT tamper: do not burn R40,000 of overtime chasing one normal week (last time it bounced back on its own). But the centre line, 84.8%, misses the 85% target - so the PROCESS is the real problem. To beat 85% consistently you must change how the work is planned (raise the average, shrink the bounce), not punish single weeks or blame the late contractor.

See what the chart did. Your eye saw "three bad weeks" and panic; the chart shows every point - all three 79% weeks included - sitting inside the normal range. The 79% is not a signal; it is common-cause noise, the ordinary bounce a process makes from a hundred small things. Reacting to it - the meeting, the blame, the R40,000 - would be tampering, and would buy nothing. But the chart also tells a harder truth you cannot dodge: the centre line, 84.8%, sits below the 85% target. The process as it stands cannot reliably hit 85% - so the fix is not to punish a single week, it is to improve the process itself. The chart told you both when to leave it alone, and what the real problem is.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the chart and read it in four steps.

  • 1. Plot the numbers and find the centre line. Plot a measure you track repeatedly, in time order, and find the centre line - the average, the middle of the normal bounce.
  • 2. Draw the control limits - not the target. Measure the week-to-week movement (the average moving range - the absolute gaps between consecutive points); for an individuals chart, multiply it by 2.66 to get the distance, and set the upper and lower control limits that far above and below the centre line. These describe how the process actually behaves - they are not your target. Confusing the two is the most common SPC mistake.
  • 3. Read the chart: signal or noise? A point outside the limits is a special cause - a real, findable jolt (a no-show contractor, a software change, a strike). Points inside are common-cause noise - the ordinary bounce.
  • 4. Make the call: act, or improve the process. Chase a special cause; never tamper with noise; and if the centre line itself misses target, fix the process, not single points.

Two ideas carry the whole tool: common-cause variation (the built-in bounce of many small things - you can only reduce it by changing the process) and special-cause variation (an unusual, findable jolt - a real signal). The control limits are usually set three standard deviations (a measure of how spread out the numbers are) from the average, because for a stable process about 99.7% of normal points land inside - so a point outside is very unlikely to be mere noise. SPC fits a measure you take repeatedly over time on a roughly stable process (aim for ~20 points to set trustworthy limits - with only twelve, treat the limits as provisional and say so); it does not fit a one-off, or a brand-new process still settling. Common chart types: the individuals chart (one number per period - the one here), and the p-chart (a proportion from a count).

The discipline that saves you is the pause before the reaction: when a number looks bad, ask "is it outside the limits?" before you call the meeting. If it is inside, you are looking at noise, and the kindest, cheapest thing you can do is nothing - do not tamper. And keep the two lines straight in your head: the control limits are how the process behaves; the target is what you want. When the behaviour cannot reach the want - when the centre line misses the target - that is not a bad week to punish, it is a process to redesign, calmly.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your SPC honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not tamper. Reacting to a single point inside the limits - a normal bounce - adds variation and wastes effort; you are adjusting a process that was fine. The hardest, most valuable SPC move is to look at a "bad" number and correctly do nothing.

• Control limits are not the target. The limits describe how the process behaves; the target is what you want. Draw the target as a separate line if you like, but never set your control limits to it - that is the single most common SPC mistake.

• A point inside is not "good" - it is "normal". Common-cause noise inside the limits is just the process being itself. If "itself" is not good enough (the centre line misses target), no amount of reacting to single weeks will help - only changing the process will.

• You need a run of history. A handful of points cannot set trustworthy limits; aim for about twenty. With fewer, build the chart to learn, but call the limits provisional and firm them up as data arrives - do not pretend to a precision you do not have.

• Find the special cause before you "fix" it. A point outside the limits is a real signal - but it points to something specific. Go and find that thing (the new contractor, the changed setting); do not just exhort the team to "try harder".

The 79% felt like failure and smelled like a R40,000 overtime bill. The chart turned it into what it was: an ordinary week in a process that bounces between 70 and 100, doing exactly what it always does. The real work was never that one number - it was lifting the whole process so its normal clears 85%. A control chart separates signal from noise: chase the signal, improve the process, and do not tamper with the noise. Get that right and you stop spending money and credibility chasing every twitch, and start fixing the thing that actually moves the average. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
