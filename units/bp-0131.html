Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Liam, the depot coordinator, and your whole job is to make the week run smoothly for everyone else: order the parts, schedule the jobs, set the work up so the team can just get on with it. Your purpose is to get ahead of the problems. But your day never goes like that. From the moment you walk in you're putting out fires - a delivery hasn't arrived, a technician's stuck and needs a different part, a customer's on the phone with an urgent change - and every one lands on your desk, because you're the one who "sorts things out".

You react all day, go home wrung out, and realise you've run flat out without moving forward an inch - you never once got to the planning that's meant to stop these fires starting. And you know, with a sinking feeling, that tomorrow will be worse: everything you didn't plan today becomes tomorrow's fire.

My job is to get us ahead of the work, yet I spend every day fighting fires - and the not-planning is exactly why there are so many fires. How do I break the cycle?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

First you try to work harder: come in an hour early to "get a head start" - but the fires just start an hour earlier too. You stay late to catch up on planning - but your brain's fried and you only absorb more of the day's chaos. You're working longer, not smarter, heading for burnout. So you try to plan in the gaps - five minutes between calls - but before you can get your head in, someone's at your elbow with an "urgent" question, and the plan scatters. Finally you start to think this just is the job now, and give up on getting ahead - which feels like total failure: the team's stressed, the work's choppy, and you're trapped.

You keep trying to fit planning into a day that's designed to interrupt you. So before you read on: what would it actually take to make the getting-ahead work happen?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The work that prevents the fires is the very work the fires won't let you do - so you must protect it on purpose. The problem isn't any single interruption; it's that your most important work has no plan, no protected time, and no rule of its own, so it always loses to whatever shouts loudest. Five moves:

  • Name the real failure: it isn't any single fire - it's that your getting-ahead work has no plan, no protected time, and no rule of its own, so it always loses to whatever shouts loudest. Until you name THAT as the failure (not the latest interruption), you'll keep treating symptoms while the cause runs free.
  • See the self-consuming trap: the less you get ahead, the more chaos arrives to interrupt you; the more fires you fight, the less you get ahead. Your role is quietly eating itself - doing reactively the exact thing it exists to prevent. This isn't a personal failing; it's a vicious cycle, and naming it is the first step to breaking it.
  • Sort your interruptions: for a few days, jot every time you're interrupted. The pattern is stark: of, say, 25 interruptions in a day, maybe 2 were true 'stop-everything-now' emergencies - the other 23 landed on you simply because you were there and known as the one who sorts things. Most interruptions are about your availability, not their urgency.
  • Ring-fence a protected block: the crucial move: defend one small daily block - even 60 minutes - as your 'getting-ahead time', where you're genuinely unavailable for anything that isn't a true emergency. Put it where everyone can see it. It feels strange, even selfish, at first - but that one calm hour is what prevents tomorrow's ten; it's the most mission-critical work you do.
  • Build a rule for what truly interrupts: you can't just vanish - so agree, with yourself and the team, what counts as a real emergency that may break the block ('the whole line is down', 'someone's in danger') versus what waits. Give the 'everything else' a place to go - a list, a board, 'catch me right after 11am' - so it's caught and handled, just not right now. The non-emergencies are still served; they simply stop devouring the prevention.

See it turn for Liam. He logs three days: of about 25 interruptions a day, only 2 are true stop-everything emergencies - the other 23 land on him purely because he's there. So he carves out one defended "Getting-Ahead Hour", in the calendar, known to all: during it he's unavailable for anything that isn't a real emergency. And he sets the rule - a true emergency ("the whole depot's down", "someone's in danger") may break it; everything else goes to a list and "catch me right after 11am". It feels selfish for about a week - until the fires start falling, because the hour he spends getting ahead is the hour that stops tomorrow's ten. The absence of getting-ahead work was producing the very chaos that ate it - the role was eating itself; protecting that one block, on purpose, is how you break the cycle. It was never about ignoring the team - it's about building the calm that lets you serve them, by stopping the chaos that makes everyone's job harder.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

The same trap eats any get-ahead work, not just a depot's. Watch a teacher.

See the same trap eat a teacher's lesson prep

A teacher's real job is to plan and deliver good lessons - the getting-ahead work. But her day is consumed by fires: a corridor squabble, a flood of parent emails, chasing missing paperwork. Lesson-planning is the first thing to go - so her lessons are less prepared, the class less engaged, behaviour worse, which makes more fires for her to fight. The role eats itself.

She ring-fences 45 minutes after school behind a closed door for "deep prep", and tells colleagues: "if the school isn't on fire, can it wait till 4:15?" A box outside her door catches the non-urgent notes. The fires don't vanish - but they stop devouring the one activity that actually prevents them, and slowly there are fewer of them.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

What's the "getting-ahead" work in your world that "right now" keeps eating - planning the month, writing the handbook, organising the stockroom, preparing properly?

  1. Name it, and see the cost. Give the get-ahead work a name - then admit that its absence is creating the very fires that keep interrupting you. The cycle is the enemy, not any one fire.
  2. Sort, then ring-fence. Notice how few of your interruptions are true emergencies - then carve out one small, defended block of time (daily or weekly) for the get-ahead work. It doesn't need to be long, but it must be protected.
  3. Build your rule. Decide what a real, drop-everything emergency is, and where everything else waits until you're free - so the rest is caught and handled, just not right now.

Being the reliable one - the person who sorts things - is a real strength, not a flaw; it's just been left unprotected. The cruelty of the fire-trap is that it turns your best quality against you, spending all your helpfulness on today and leaving none for the planning that would make tomorrow calm. Defending one small block isn't stepping back from the work; it's the truest way to do it. Stop the fires before they start - that is how you serve the people who depend on you, far better than any amount of heroic firefighting ever could.
