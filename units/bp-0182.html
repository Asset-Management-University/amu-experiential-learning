I was the best - so why am I drowning?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Sipho. Not long ago you were the best driver Tank You had - fastest routes, never a missed delivery, the one Muhammad trusted most. That is exactly why he made you Operations Lead. It felt like the reward you'd earned.

So why are you here at the depot at eleven at night, alone, redrawing tomorrow's delivery routes by hand - because the ones your drivers planned "aren't right"? It's the third night this week. You check everyone's work, you catch every mistake, you quietly fix it all yourself so the standard stays as high as it was when you did the job. You are working harder than you ever did as a driver, and you are exhausted.

And here is the part that scares you: it isn't getting better. Your drivers don't improve - why would they, when you silently fix everything? They wait for you to approve every little thing. The whole operation jams up behind you. You were brilliant at the work, and now, in charge, you feel like you're failing - and you can't even say why.

I was the best at the work - so why am I drowning now that I'm in charge, and why isn't my team getting any better?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your instinct is the one that made you great: keep the standard high, no matter what it costs you. Be the best, catch every error, hold the line. But sit with tonight - that very instinct is what has you alone at the depot at eleven, with a team that's no stronger than last month. The thing that made you a brilliant driver might be the wrong tool for the job you have now.

Before you read on, ask yourself: when they made you Operations Lead, what actually became your job? Is it still to draw the best routes - or has it quietly become something else?

I keep fixing everyone's work myself to keep the standard up. But that's why I'm drowning - and why nobody's learning. My old skill (doing it best) isn't the job anymore; the job changed when they put me in charge.
If I redo it at night, the standard lives only in my head and the team stays helpless. What if I got the standard OUT of my head - showed them clearly what 'good' looks like - so they could hit it without me?
Show what good looks like, let them do it, then give feedback and hand it BACK to fix - never silently redo it. And judge myself by what the team can do without me, not by how much I do with my own hands.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The promotion didn't just give you more of the old job - it gave you a different job, and nobody told you the rules had changed. Here they are:

  • The new role is a DIFFERENT job: being the best doer earned you the team - but leading isn't a reward for the old job, it's a new one. The work shifted from DOING it yourself to MAKING OTHERS able to do it well. One pair of hands doesn't scale; a team does.
  • Why gripping harder fails: redoing everyone's work to your standard keeps it right for one night - and guarantees you become the bottleneck, burn out, and your people never grow. (The urge comes from CARING about quality - good instinct, wrong tool now.)
  • Move 1 - delegate with a VISIBLE standard: get 'good' out of your head and into the open: show one strong example, write a simple 'what good looks like'. Now they can hit the bar without you hovering.
  • Move 2 - give feedback and HAND IT BACK: when it's not right, don't silently redo it - point out what's off and hand it back for THEM to fix. That's how they learn. This is the uncomfortable, essential move.
  • Move 3 - measure the TEAM, not your hands: your success is no longer what you produce yourself - it's what your team produces WITHOUT you. That's the whole shift, and the freedom in it.

Now, the trap most new leaders fall into when they hear this - so hear it carefully. This is NOT "lower your standards." The bar stays exactly as high. What changes is HOW you enforce it. You used to enforce the standard with your own two hands, by redoing the work. Now you enforce it with your coaching - by showing what good looks like, and handing work back until it meets the bar. That doesn't weaken the standard; it's the only way to make the standard live in five people instead of one. A route your driver plans differently from how you would - but that still gets the gas there full, sealed, and on time - meets the bar. Different isn't wrong. Only below the bar is wrong.

And be gentle with yourself about the gripping: it came from caring about quality, which is a good and rare thing. You're not being asked to care less. You're being asked to care at a bigger scale - about your people's growth, not just tonight's routes.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So the next morning, instead of handing out the routes he drew at midnight, Sipho tries the new job.

See the old way and the new way, side by side

The old way (this week): Sipho takes the drivers' route plans, says nothing, and quietly redraws them all himself at night. The deliveries go out to his standard. He's wrecked, the drivers learned nothing, and tomorrow it'll be the same - because the only place the standard lives is inside Sipho's head.

The new way:

• He makes the standard visible. He shows the drivers ONE excellent route and writes a simple list: "a good route - shortest fuel, the call-first customers first, no backtracking, finished by 2pm." Now "good" isn't a secret in his head; it's on the wall.

• He lets a driver plan it. Thabo draws tomorrow's route. It's not how Sipho would draw it - and Sipho's hand itches to grab the pen.

• He gives feedback and hands it BACK. "Good - but you've sent yourself across town twice; check it against the list and fix it." Thabo fixes it himself. It now meets the bar - by a slightly different path than Sipho's.

• It compounds. The first week is slower and Sipho has to bite his tongue daily. But within a month the drivers plan their own routes to standard, Sipho's nights are his own again, and he's finally doing the actual job of a lead: thinking ahead, growing his people, solving the bigger problems only he can.

The shift: the standard now lives in the whole team, not just in Sipho - which means it can finally grow past one exhausted man.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The drowning wasn't a sign you're bad at leading - it was a sign you were still doing the old job in the new role. The moment you start building people instead of just doing the work, the water drops to your knees. Whenever you lead anyone - a team, a group, even one junior person:

  1. Measure the new thing - your team's capability. Stop scoring yourself on what you make with your own hands; start scoring yourself on what your people can do well WITHOUT you. Each week, ask plainly: "what can they do now that they couldn't last month?" That number is your real output now.
  2. Make the standard visible, then let go. Show what "good" looks like (an example, a short list) so they can hit it without you - then actually let them do it.
  3. Give feedback and hand it back - never silently redo. Keep the bar high, but enforce it by coaching, not by grabbing the pen. Different-but-good is good; only below the bar gets handed back.
R

Pick one thing you keep redoing, write down what "good" looks like, and hand it over this week - feedback, not rescue. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

The best leaders aren't the ones who can do everything themselves - they're the ones who make everyone around them better. Your skill got you the team. Growing your team is the new skill - and it's the one that turns a tired expert into a leader who multiplies.
