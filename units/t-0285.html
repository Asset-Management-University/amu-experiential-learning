Step 1 of 5

The worst-case assessment says dig up the pipeline and the town loses gas for a fortnight - but it assumed every unknown was at its worst all at once. How do I get an answer that carries the uncertainty instead of stacking it?

T1: The Ache

The inline inspection of the Wolmaransstad transmission line came back three weeks ago. It found a dent with a gouge in it at kilometre 61, in pipe laid in 1974, wall thickness 7.1 mm, running at between 42 and 58 bar. The line is the only gas supply to a town of 34,000 people and to the two bakeries and the hospital laundry in it.

The assessment report is on your desk and its conclusion is one word: fail. The recommendation is excavation and replacement of the section - R41 million, and the town off gas for a fortnight in July.

You are the integrity engineer, and you have read the report properly, which apparently nobody else has. The arithmetic is right. The method is standard. And on page four, in a table nobody would look at twice, are the three inputs it used: the deepest depth the inspection tool could be hiding at the far end of its tolerance, the lowest toughness ever published for pipe of this grade and era, and the highest pressure the line saw all year.

Every one of those numbers is defensible on its own. The report has not done anything improper. But the scenario it has actually assessed is one where the tool is wrong in the worst direction, and this particular 1974 steel is the softest of its whole generation, and the line is at peak pressure - all at the same moment. Nowhere does it say that. It says "fail".

And you cannot simply dismiss it, because the opposite mistake is the one that kills people. Put the friendly middle value in every box instead and you will get a comfortable pass, and the pass will be just as much a scenario as the fail was.

The worst case is possible and stacking every worst value together is not the same as likely. How do I get an answer that carries the uncertainty honestly, instead of piling it all up or wishing it away?

Before you read on, decide what you would put in your memo. R41 million, a town off gas in July, a correct assessment resting on three simultaneous pessimistic assumptions, and no toughness certificates from 1974. Write down what you would actually recommend - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five recommendations, and what each one rests on

• "Accept the report. Excavate." The safe answer for your career and it is not obviously the safe answer for anyone else: R41 million that cannot then be spent on the rest of the network, and a town off gas, on the strength of three worsts landing together. It also sets the precedent that will condemn the next forty defects on 1970s pipe.

• "Re-run it with realistic middle values." The instinct once you have spotted the stacking, and it swaps one scenario for another. A pass built on central estimates says nothing about the tail, and the tail is the entire reason anybody assesses a gas pipeline.

• "Reduce the operating pressure and carry on." Genuinely useful, often the right move, and here it is being chosen without knowing whether pressure is what the answer depends on. It might buy very little for a real cost in delivery.

• "Get a second opinion on the assessment." Reasonable, and the assessment is not wrong. Another competent engineer using the same convention will produce the same word.

• "Split the difference - monitor it closely and review in a year." Sounds prudent and decides nothing, while a defect that may or may not matter stays in a line feeding a hospital laundry through a winter.

• What every one of them does is argue about which single value to put in each box. That is the wrong argument, because the honest answer to "what is the toughness of this steel" is not a number at all - it is a range, and the same is true of the depth and the pressure. An assessment that can only accept one value per input will always force you to choose between a scenario that frightens and a scenario that comforts. The move is to let each input be the range it actually is, and see what comes out.

T2: The Tool in Action

List every input and what you actually know about it. Run the worst case deliberately, and write down the scenario it assumed. Then give each input its real range, run the same assessment thousands of times, and count the failures. Compare that with the frequency somebody has agreed is tolerable - and then ask which unknown is driving the answer. Watch it run on kilometre 61.

Step 1 - List every input and be honest about which ones you actually know

Elmarie Grobler writes six inputs on the board with what she really has beside each. Defect depth: measured by the inline tool at 3.4 mm, with the tool's stated accuracy of plus or minus 0.6 mm. Wall thickness: 7.1 mm, measured directly, solid. Operating pressure: varies between 42 and 58 bar across the year, from the SCADA record - not one number at all. Defect length: measured, good. Grade: from the 1974 line list, reliable. And toughness: nothing. No certificates survive for this vintage, and the published range for pipe of this grade and era runs from about 20 to 70 joules. One input out of six is a genuine blank, and it is written down as a blank rather than filled with a cautious number.

Step 2 - Run the worst case first, and see exactly what it assumed

Deepest defect the tool could be hiding at 4.0 mm, lowest published toughness at 20 joules, highest annual pressure at 58 bar: the assessment predicts failure. That is the answer currently on the table, and it is the basis of the recommendation to excavate - R41 million and the town off gas for a fortnight. Elmarie writes underneath it the scenario it assumed: the tool reading is at the very worst end of its tolerance, and the steel is the softest of its whole era, and the line is at peak pressure, all at the same moment. Every one of those is possible. All three together is not the same claim, and nothing in the report had said so.

Step 3 - Give each input its range, and run the assessment thousands of times

One hundred thousand runs. Each one draws a depth from the tool's tolerance band, a toughness from the published range for 1970s pipe of this grade, a pressure from the year's actual SCADA distribution, and the measured values for the rest. Twenty-three runs out of the hundred thousand fail. That is a probability of about 2.3 in ten thousand per year - and it is a different kind of statement from the worst case, which was not a probability at all but a scenario.

Step 4 - Compare it with the frequency somebody has agreed is tolerable

For a transmission line at this distance from a settlement, the criterion in the code is 1 in ten thousand per year. The result is 2.3 in ten thousand. So it still fails - and it fails by a factor of about 2.3, not by the margin the worst case implied. Elmarie is careful in the report: the probabilistic answer has not rescued the pipeline. It has changed the finding from 'this will fail' to 'this is about twice as likely to fail as we accept', and those two findings lead to different next steps.

Step 5 - Ask which unknown is driving the answer, and go and reduce that one

Hold the toughness at its central value and the probability collapses; hold the depth or the pressure at theirs and it barely moves. About 78 per cent of the failure probability comes from not knowing the toughness of the steel - not from the defect at all. So the recommendation is not to excavate. It is to cut a sample from a redundant section of the same 1974 pipe and test it: R120,000 and three weeks. If the toughness lands anywhere in the middle of its range, the probability falls to about 4 in a hundred thousand and the line passes comfortably. If it lands at the bottom, the excavation is justified and now genuinely justified. Either way the R41 million decision gets made on a measurement rather than on an assumption, for less than a three-hundredth of the cost - R120,000 against R41 million.

See what the last step did to a R41 million decision. The probability is 2.3 in ten thousand a year against a tolerable 1 in ten thousand, so the line still fails - the arithmetic did not rescue it - but about 78 per cent of that failure probability comes from not knowing the toughness of 1974 steel, and almost none of it from the defect everybody had been looking at. So the recommendation stops being excavation and becomes a R120,000 sample cut from a redundant length of the same pipe. Notice what has and has not changed. The engineering is identical - the same assessment, run many times instead of once. What changed is that the answer now says which of the six things you do not know is the one your decision rests on, and that turns out to be a question you can settle for less than a three-hundredth of the cost of acting on your own ignorance. And if the toughness comes back at the bottom of the range, the excavation goes ahead - justified this time by a measurement rather than by a convention.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the fifth is the one that pays for the whole exercise.

  • 1. List every input with what you actually have - a measurement, a tolerance, a published range, or a blank. Write blanks as blanks.
  • 2. Run the worst case deliberately, and write down the scenario it assumed rather than only its verdict.
  • 3. Give each input its range and run the assessment thousands of times, drawing one value from each range per run. Count the failures.
  • 4. Compare with the tolerable frequency somebody else has published, and state the factor rather than just pass or fail.
  • 5. Hold each uncertain input at its centre in turn to find which one the answer depends on - then go and reduce that one.

This is a probabilistic assessment, and the way you ran it - drawing at random from each input's range, many times over, and counting outcomes - is a Monte Carlo simulation. What each input's range describes is its distribution; the share of runs that failed is the probability of failure; and the frequency your code accepts for that consequence is the tolerable failure frequency. The single-value version you ran first is a deterministic assessment, and its worst-case form is doing something specific worth naming: it is stacking compounded conservatism, taking the pessimistic end of several independent unknowns at once, which produces a scenario far rarer than any of its parts and reports it as if it were the expected outcome. And the last move has a name too - a sensitivity analysis - which is the part practitioners skip and the part that changes what anybody does on Monday.

It fits any assessment with several genuinely uncertain inputs and a decision expensive enough to be worth the effort - fracture and fatigue assessments, structural checks on old assets, foundation capacity, flood levels, dose assessments, project cost ranges. Its limits are worth being blunt about. It cannot invent information: the output is only as good as the ranges you fed it, and a range chosen carelessly will produce a confident probability that is wrong in a way nobody can see. It says nothing about failures that are not in the model at all - the wrong steel delivered in 1974, the excavation that damages the coating. And it does not tell you what to do: a probability meets a tolerable frequency somebody else set, and that comparison is the whole of its authority.

Two disciplines decide whether this is honesty or a nicer answer. First, run the worst case anyway, keep it in the report, and say what it assumed. A probabilistic result presented instead of the conservative one looks exactly like an engineer who went looking for a friendlier number, and that impression will follow you into every future assessment - deservedly, because it is sometimes true. Presented beside it, with the compounding named, it is a stronger document than either alone. Second, be as careful about the ranges as you would be about a single value, and say where each came from. This method's whole vulnerability is that a range is easier to nudge than a number and much harder for a reviewer to challenge - a slightly generous toughness distribution will move the answer more than any defect measurement, and nobody will spot it. Cite the source of every distribution, and if you had to guess one, run the answer both ways and show both.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the answer honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A stacked worst case is a scenario, not a probability. Taking the pessimistic end of several independent unknowns at once describes something far rarer than any of its parts. That is not wrong, but reporting it as the expected outcome is.

• Central values are a scenario too. The mirror-image error, and the dangerous one. A comfortable pass from middle-of-the-road inputs says nothing whatever about the tail, which is the only part that hurts anybody.

• Keep the conservative answer in the report. Replacing it looks like shopping for a number. Beside it, with the compounding named, it makes the case rather than weakening it.

• Cite every distribution. A range is easier to nudge than a number and far harder to challenge. If you had to guess one, run it both ways and publish both answers.

• Do the sensitivity step; it is the point. A probability alone tells you where you stand. Knowing which unknown drives it tells you what to do - and it is almost never the thing everybody is looking at.

• Reducing an uncertainty is usually cheaper than acting on it. A sample, a test, a survey, a week of data. Compare that cost against the decision before you spend the decision.

• It cannot see what is not in the model. The wrong material delivered decades ago, damage done during the repair itself, a mechanism nobody listed. A low probability of the modelled failure is not a low probability of failure.

• A probability is not a permission. It has to meet a tolerable frequency somebody with the authority to set one has published. If nobody has set one, that is your finding, and it belongs in the report rather than in your own judgement.

You had a correct report recommending R41 million and a town off gas in July, resting on three pessimistic assumptions landing together and never saying so. You can put a different memo up: the worst case with its scenario written out, the probability of 2.3 in ten thousand a year against a tolerable 1 in ten thousand so the line genuinely does not pass, and the finding that matters - 78 per cent of that risk is not the defect, it is not knowing what 1974 steel this is, and a R120,000 sample settles it in three weeks. This is how an assessment stops choosing between a number that frightens and a number that comforts. And if the steel comes back soft, the excavation happens - and this time everyone will know why.
