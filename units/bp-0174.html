Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

Before you begin - a note of care. This unit is about a separation and the dividing of what two people built together. It also touches on being controlled or made unsafe by a partner, including through money. If any of this is close to your life right now, go gently, and take a break whenever you need to. If you are afraid for your safety, that comes before everything else in this unit: South Africa's Gender-Based Violence Command Centre is free and confidential, 24 hours a day.

  • To speak to someone, call 0800 428 428.
  • If you cannot speak safely, you can ask them to call you. Dial *120*7867# (free) or SMS help to 31531.

A warning that matters: these services call you back, so your phone will ring. Be somewhere safe before you ask for a call. Dialling or texting may also leave a trace in your phone's call and message history.

You are Simphiwe. It is 1am and the house is quiet. Too quiet. On the kitchen table is a laptop, a cold cup of tea, and a spreadsheet that feels like the end of the world.

For fifteen years, every decision was "we" and "our". Our home, our savings, our plans. Now the screen shows a list of those things, but the words have changed: assets, liabilities, division. A separation is splitting the wealth you built together, and a cold panic grips you - "I could lose half of everything."

You think of the deposit for this house, the late nights you worked, the sacrifices. A hard, angry knot forms in your stomach, and an instinct screams at you to fight, to protect what feels like yours, to make sure you are not left with nothing. The children are asleep upstairs, and the thought of their lives being turned upside down by this makes the panic even sharper.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your mind races through the options, the natural instincts that rise up from the fear. "I will get the best lawyer and fight to win." You imagine a courtroom battle where your side is proven right and you keep the lion's share - but a colder thought follows: a court war is not a victory, it is a process of turning your shared money into legal fees, and a long, bitter fight almost always leaves BOTH of you poorer, angrier, and with a co-parenting relationship in ashes; the only guaranteed winners are the lawyers. "I will move some money - hide that small investment." It feels like simple self-protection, but this is not a game: hiding assets is dishonest, often unlawful, and destroys any chance of a fair, trusted process; if it is discovered - and it often is - it shatters your credibility, can rebound badly, and the trust you break is also with your future self. "They need to understand how this affects the children." But using children as messengers or emotional leverage is the most damaging move of all - it wounds the people you love most, forcing them to carry adult burdens and poisoning the years ahead.

These are not solutions; they are just different ways to set your own life on fire. So before you read on: the real question is not "how do I win?" - it is "how do we divide this without burning everything down?"

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you change the goal. A separation is not a war to win but a shared life to divide with the least possible harm. The strong move is not to fight harder, but to choose a wiser path: the goal is not victory, it is fairness-with-least-harm. Five moves:

  • Steady yourself and protect the children first: before a single decision about money, steady the hand that will make it: choices forged in raw fear and anger are almost always the ones you regret, and the fear of 'losing half' is precisely the state in which people do the most damage. So first, do the human things - breathe, sleep if you can, lean on a trusted friend - because a calmer you will protect far more than a frightened one. And if there are children, they come before any asset: their stability, their routine, their sense that they are still loved by both parents is worth more than any share of the house. That means the hardest discipline of all - never use them as weapons, messengers or bargaining chips, never make them carry the adult fear, and shield them from the conflict. Protecting the children is not separate from protecting the wealth; the years of family life ahead are the most valuable thing on the table, and they are the easiest to destroy.
  • Get clarity before you negotiate: you cannot divide fairly what you have not honestly seen, so before any negotiation, calmly build a full and truthful picture of the whole: every asset, every debt, every income - what is shared and what each of you brought in. Do this NOT to hide things or to grab first, but for the opposite reason - because a fair division is impossible without an honest list, and because the clarity protects YOU as much as anyone: it stops you being quietly short-changed, and it stops you, in panic, doing something dishonest you cannot undo. Write it down plainly, without yet deciding who gets what; the deciding comes later and goes far better once both of you can see the same true picture. Honesty here is not naivety - it is the ground that any fair, durable settlement has to stand on, and the thing a court or a mediator will most respect.
  • Aim for fairness, not victory: this is the heart of it: change the goal from winning to dividing with the least harm. The instinct is to fight to 'not lose half' - but a scorched-earth court war is not how you keep more, it is how you turn the shared pot into legal fees while both of you grow poorer, angrier and more damaged, and any co-parenting relationship burns down with it. A negotiated or MEDIATED settlement - two people, with help, agreeing a fair split - almost always preserves far more of the wealth, the dignity and the future than a battle handed to a judge, and it keeps the decision in your own hands rather than a stranger's. Choosing fairness over victory is not weakness or giving up; it is the strongest, wisest move on the board, because it is the only one where the thing you are dividing is not destroyed in the fight over it.
  • Get proper independent advice: do not navigate this by guesswork, by what a friend's cousin got, or by rules of thumb from the internet, because family and property law varies enormously by country, by region, and by the exact type of union you were in - and your real rights and responsibilities may be very different from what you assume. Get sound, INDEPENDENT, local advice: a qualified family-law specialist to tell you where you genuinely stand, and a financial adviser to help you see the long-term shape of any settlement (a paid-off house you cannot afford to run is not the win it looks like). Good advice is what keeps you from being cheated and, just as importantly, from unknowingly cheating - and it lets you negotiate fairness from a place of knowledge rather than fear. This is not the place to save money by staying uninformed; understanding your true position is what makes a fair, final settlement possible.
  • Protect your future self - separate money from emotion: the relationship and the settlement are two different things, and the great danger is letting the grief of the first wreck the wisdom of the second. So grieve the relationship as a relationship - that pain is real and normal and deserves care and time - but refuse to let the urge to punish your ex, or to cling to what is ending, bankrupt the person you will be in five years. Practically, protect your future self now: make sure you have a way to live going forward, a bank account and credit in your own name, and a clear eye on your long-term security (your retirement, your earning power, a roof you can actually sustain). The money you are dividing is not a scoreboard for who was wronged; it is the foundation each of you will rebuild a life on. Spend your energy securing that foundation, not winning the argument - the future you will thank you far more than the present you wants to be proved right.

A critical safety note, first: if your partner is controlling, abusive or dangerous, the advice to "sit down and be fair" does NOT apply. Your safety, and your children's, is the only priority - do not seek mediation; instead contact the Gender-Based Violence Command Centre - free and confidential, 24 hours a day, on 0800 428 428, or SMS the word help to 31531 if you cannot speak safely - and a family lawyer, to make a safety plan.

For the ordinary, painful, non-dangerous split, see it transform things for Simphiwe. She takes a deep breath and closes the spreadsheet. The anger is still there, and so is the fear - but the path is clearer. The goal is not to win; it is to get through this with enough dignity, fairness and resources for everyone to build a new life - especially the children, and especially the Simphiwe of the future. The wisest way to handle the fear of 'losing half' is to realise that the fight itself is what destroys the most value: a scorched-earth battle to 'win' usually costs you far more than half in legal fees, emotional damage and lost time. A separation is not a war to win but a shared life to divide with the least harm. So steady yourself and protect the children first, get an honest picture before you negotiate, aim for fairness and mediation over war, get proper independent local advice, and protect your future self - and you preserve the most wealth, dignity and hope for everyone, including you.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - picture dividing a single cake you baked together.

See why the fight destroys the very thing you fight over

The "fight-to-win" approach is for both of you to grab for the biggest piece at the same time. The result? The cake is crushed, icing smeared everywhere, and you both end up with a handful of crumbs and a sticky, angry mess. In fighting for more than your share, you have destroyed the very thing you were fighting over - and both of you get less.

The "fairness-with-least-harm" approach is to pause, find a knife - the right tool for the job, like a mediator or a clear, fair process - and agree where to make a clean cut. The result? You each walk away with a clean, whole slice. It might not be as big as you dreamed, but it is a real, undamaged piece you can actually enjoy. The fight itself destroys the value that cooperation preserves - and that is just as true of a home, a pension and a family's future as it is of a cake.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This feels huge, but the first steps are small and within your control - you do not have to solve everything tonight. If a separation is something you are facing, or fear facing:

  1. Breathe, and protect the children. Walk away from the spreadsheet; go and check on those who are sleeping. Their stability is your top priority, so keep their routine as normal as you can, and never make them carry the adult fear or become messengers.
  2. Start a private, honest list. In a notebook or a document only you can access, calmly list what you know is there - home, car, savings, pension, debts. Do not value or divide it yet; this is an act of clarity, not aggression.
  3. Secure your own footing. If you do not already have a bank account solely in your name, make opening one a priority this week.
  4. Look up one name. You do not have to call yet - just search for a "family mediator" or "family-law specialist" in your area and write down one name. Knowing where to get proper local advice is a powerful step.

This is one of the hardest things you will ever do, so be kind to yourself - there is grief and fear here, and they are normal. You do not need to be a warrior; you need to be a wise architect of your own future. By choosing fairness, clarity and good advice, you lay the foundation for a new life. This is an ending, but it is also a beginning - there is life and recovery beyond it, and you have the strength to build it.
