It is 04:12, both pumps are down, the reservoir empties in eight hours, and the hospital is on the line asking for a number I do not have - how do I work out in advance which services must keep running and exactly what to do when one fails?

T1: The Ache

It is 04:12 on a Tuesday. You are the operations manager at the Riverstone Water Treatment Works, which supplies clean drinking water to 180,000 people in the town of Riverstone and three nearby settlements. You are standing in the high-lift pump house. The floor is wet to your ankles; the air smells of hot insulation and burnt rubber.

A power surge in the night storm tripped both high-lift pumps - one is burnt out completely. The standby generator started, but its tank ran dry after ninety minutes because nobody had topped it up since March. Treated water is no longer leaving the plant. The clear-water reservoir that feeds the town holds about eight hours of supply at normal demand, and it is dropping now.

Your phone shows nine missed calls. The plant manager, Mrs Mkhize, is on the line, her voice tight. "How long until water is flowing again? The municipality needs a number. The hospital is asking. Tell me the plan." You do not have a number. You do not have a plan - not a written one. Every answer (who to phone for a replacement pump, where the nearest fuel is, whether the second reservoir can cross-feed) lives in someone's memory, and half those people are asleep. You feel the pull to just grab the phone and start ringing people one by one, hoping you reach the right one before the reservoir empties.

The hospital is on the line: how long until water is flowing again? Tell me the plan - and I do not have one written down. How do I work out, in advance, which services must keep running, how long we can survive without each, and exactly what to do when one fails?

Before you read on, decide. The hospital is on the line and the municipality wants a number: how long until water is flowing again. Every answer — who to ring for a pump, where the fuel is, whether the second reservoir can cross-feed — lives in somebody’s memory, and half of them are asleep. Write down what you would do. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one gives the hospital

• “Start ringing people until I reach someone who knows.” What you are about to do, and it is the same scramble as last time in a different coat. It also cannot answer her question, because a number requires knowing how long the reservoir lasts, and that is not a thing you find out by phoning.

• “Give her an estimate now — say six hours.” She will act on it: the hospital will plan around it, and so will the municipality. A number invented under pressure at four in the morning is a promise made on somebody else’s behalf, and if it is wrong the hospital finds out when the taps do.

• “Tell her I don’t know yet and call back.” Honest, correct tonight, and it is exactly the answer you gave the last time this happened in a smaller way. The repetition is the finding — nothing about tonight will stop the next one being identical.

• “Write down everyone’s phone numbers tomorrow.” A real improvement and it is the smallest part of the problem. A call tree tells you who to ring; it does not tell you which services must keep running, how long the town can survive without each, or what to do when one fails.

• “Buy a spare pump and a generator.” Possibly right, and you cannot yet say — spare for which failure, sized how, and restored within what time? Equipment bought before the timings are known is a guess with an invoice.

The question underneath hers is the one you cannot answer and could have: how long can this town be without water before it stops being an inconvenience? That number exists, and it is knowable on an ordinary Tuesday.

You have been here before, in smaller ways - the same scramble each time. You need a dependable, repeatable way to decide what must keep running, how long you can survive without it, and what to do when it fails - written, agreed and tested before the storm, not during it. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

The discipline is business continuity - staying able to deliver your most important services even when something goes badly wrong - and the written result is a Business Continuity Plan (BCP), built to the international standard ISO 22301. Its engine is the Business Impact Analysis (BIA), which asks of each activity: if this stops, how bad is it, and how fast does it get bad? Watch it built for Riverstone.

Step 1 - Set the scope (one sentence: what service, for whom)

'This plan covers the continuous supply of safe drinking water from the Riverstone Water Treatment Works to its 180,000 customers.' You are not planning for the staff canteen - you are planning for water.

Step 2 - Run the Business Impact Analysis (BIA)

The clear-water reservoir holds 8 hours. High-lift pumping: the town runs dry in 8h, so MTPD 8h and RTO 4h (half the buffer, a safe margin). Power: same 8h buffer, RTO 3h. Treatment (chlorination): untreated water is unsafe almost at once, so MTPD 2h, RTO 1h. SCADA quality records: MTPD 24h, RTO 12h, and you can lose at most 1 hour of logged data (RPO 1h). Every RTO sits safely below its MTPD.

Step 3 - Set the MBCO (the smallest level you restore first)

The MBCO: safe water to the hospital and the two highest-risk suburbs at reduced pressure, restored within the RTO, with full town supply to follow. The hospital and the most vulnerable come first; everything else waits.

Step 4 - Choose recovery strategies matched to each RTO

Pumping (RTO 4h): a pre-wired spare high-lift pump on site (swap-in under 3h) plus a 4-hour-callout contract with a pump-service firm. Power (RTO 3h): keep the generator tank always above 75% (a daily checklist item) = 12h run-time, plus a 2-hour fuel-delivery contract. Treatment (RTO 1h): a 48-hour chlorine store plus a manual dosing procedure two trained operators can run. SCADA records (RPO 1h): automatic off-site backups every 30 minutes, well inside the hour.

Step 5 - Write the response (cards + call tree), then test it

Action cards for 'pump failure', 'power loss', 'treatment failure'; a call tree on the wall, so no one rings people one by one in the dark. The first tabletop exercise finds two gaps - the spare pump's coupling is the wrong size, and one phone number is dead - and you fix both. A live drill every 6 months, a review every year. Now 04:12 is a procedure: the town has 8 hours, pumping must be back in 4, the spare pump and the fuelled generator are ready, and the manager gets her number.

See what the five steps did. They turned 04:12 from a panic into a procedure: the town has eight hours, pumping must be restored in four, the spare pump and the fuelled generator are ready, the call tree is on the wall, and the manager gets her number. This is the heart of it: prevention will sometimes fail, and when it does, the people who depend on you - the 180,000, the hospital - must not be abandoned to a scramble in the dark. A continuity plan is a promise kept in advance: that the most vulnerable are served first, and that the worst night becomes a known, rehearsed response rather than a guess. You cannot stop every storm; you can refuse to let one leave a town without water.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the BCP in five moves.

  • 1. Set the scope. One sentence: what service this plan protects, and for whom.
  • 2. Run the BIA. List the activities the service needs; for each, the harm, how fast it grows, and the numbers - MTPD, RTO, RPO.
  • 3. Set the MBCO. The smallest acceptable level you restore first.
  • 4. Choose recovery strategies. For each activity, how you will restore it inside its RTO - pre-positioned in advance.
  • 5. Respond and test. Action cards plus a call tree; then a tabletop, drills, and a yearly review.

The four BIA numbers, each a plain question: the MTPD (Maximum Tolerable Period of Disruption) is the longest you could survive without the activity before the harm is unacceptable; the RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is by when you must have it back - always set shorter than the MTPD, so you keep a margin; the RPO (Recovery Point Objective) is the most recent data or work you can afford to lose; and the MBCO (Minimum Business Continuity Objective) is the reduced-but-acceptable level you aim to restore first. This technique fits any organisation or asset that delivers a service people depend on, where a sudden failure causes real harm - a water plant, a hospital, a mine's ventilation, a payroll system, a delivery business - and it is best spent on the few activities that truly matter. It is not for activities whose loss is a mild nuisance, and it is not everyday maintenance - a BCP is your plan for when prevention has already failed.

Two disciplines keep the plan real. First, every RTO must sit below its MTPD, and every recovery strategy must actually meet its RTO - a four-hour recovery target with a next-week spare part is not a plan, it is a wish; match the strategy to the number, or change the number honestly. Second, an untested plan is a guess - the gaps (the wrong coupling, the dead phone number, the assumption that someone topped up the fuel) only show themselves in a drill, never on paper; rehearse it, find the holes while it is safe, and keep it current as the plant and the people change.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your continuity plan true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not plan in the storm. Working out who to call while the reservoir empties is too slow and too error-prone. The whole point is to decide it all in the calm and write it down.

• Set the RTO below the MTPD - always. If your recovery target equals the time you have, you have no margin and the first delay sinks you. Leave a safety gap, every time.

• Match the strategy to the number. An RTO is only real if a concrete, pre-positioned strategy can meet it - a spare on site, a fuelled tank, a callout contract. A target with no means behind it is a lie you tell yourself.

• Restore the vulnerable first. The MBCO exists so you do not freeze trying to fix everything. Decide now who gets served first - the hospital, the most exposed - and protect that decision.

• A plan you never test is a guess. Tabletop it, drill it, review it. The gaps live in the details, and they only surface when you rehearse - far better in a drill than at 04:12.

You came into a flooded pump house with a hospital on the line and nothing written down. You can replace that, before the next storm, with something solid: a scope, a BIA that says how long the town really has, an MBCO that serves the hospital first, recovery strategies that actually meet their targets, action cards and a call tree on the wall, and a plan rehearsed until the gaps are gone. That turns a 04:12 panic into a tested procedure - and it keeps the promise underneath all of it: that when the worst happens, the people who depend on this service are not left in the dark. You cannot prevent every failure, but you can make sure none of them abandons a town. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one service you protect and run its Business Impact Analysis together.)
