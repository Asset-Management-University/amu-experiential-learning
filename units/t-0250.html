T1: The Ache

You needed to replace the transfer pump, and you had the numbers to prove it. But the books said otherwise: the pump still had years of life left on paper, plenty of value un-depreciated, so the renewal was deferred and the budget went elsewhere.

Then the pump failed. Not because the books were wrong to add up - they added up perfectly - but because they told a calendar story about an asset that actually wears out with use, and that pump had been run hard, day and night, for two years. The numbers lied to you about the pump's real condition, and you believed them, because no one ever chose them to tell the truth.

My books said the asset had years left, and then it wore out anyway - how do I choose a depreciation method that tells the truth about how an asset actually gives up its value?

Before you read on, decide. The pump ran day and night for two years — near-continuous duty, roughly three times what its sisters do. On the books it was written down evenly over ten years, like everything else, so it still showed about R320,000 of remaining value. Nobody put it in the renewal programme, because the record said it had years left. It is now in pieces, worn out, and the replacement is unbudgeted and urgent. The method was not chosen for this pump; it is simply the one always used here. Write down how you would choose a method that tells the truth — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what the choice actually changes

• “Shorten the life — write it down over five years instead.” Better for a hard-worked pump and still the wrong shape: an even charge over a shorter period assumes the wear is evenly spread, when this pump's life was consumed by how hard it ran, which varies year to year.

• “It is finance's decision, not mine.” They chose a schedule; only you know that this pump runs three times as hard as its sisters. The information that makes the choice honest is yours, and without it they cannot get it right.

• “Use whichever method reduces this year's charge.” Treats it as a device for managing profit — and note that it cannot even do that in the long run, since the total is fixed.

• “Ignore the books and use condition monitoring for renewals.” Right that condition should drive renewal, and it leaves the books telling a false story that will keep contradicting you in every budget conversation.

• “It is only accounting — it does not change the pump.” It changed when somebody planned to replace it, which is why the replacement is now unbudgeted and urgent.

• Here is the fact that makes this honest rather than a game: over the asset's life the total written off is exactly the same, whichever method you use. The method does not change how much — it changes when, how the charge is spread across the years. So there is only one honest basis for choosing: which pattern best matches how this asset actually gives up its service. It is not an accountant's trick for managing profit; it is a story about how a thing wears out, and your job is to tell the true one. The patterns tell different stories. An even charge suits something that gives roughly equal service and wears mostly with time — a building, a structure, a fence. A charge weighted early suits something that loses most of its value when it is new. And a charge that follows use suits something whose life is consumed by how hard it is worked rather than by how long it has existed — which is this pump exactly. And the reason it matters beyond the ledger: the pattern feeds real decisions, so a comfortable story quietly postpones an uncomfortable renewal.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you compare the depreciation methods and choose one - seeing that the total is fixed and only the timing changes, matching the method to how the asset really wears out, and remembering that the pattern you choose feeds real decisions about renewal.

1. The same total, a different story

Depreciation spreads the cost of an asset across the years it serves, so each year carries its fair share and the books show the asset steadily giving up its value. Here is the fact that makes the choice honest rather than a game: OVER THE ASSET'S LIFE THE TOTAL DEPRECIATED IS EXACTLY THE SAME, WHICHEVER METHOD YOU USE. The method does not change how much - it changes WHEN, how the charge is spread across the years. So there is only ONE honest basis for choosing between them: WHICH PATTERN BEST MATCHES HOW THE ASSET ACTUALLY GIVES UP ITS SERVICE. Depreciation is not an accountant's trick to manage profit; it is a story about how a thing wears out, and your job is to tell the true one.

2. Three methods, three stories of wear

STRAIGHT-LINE charges the SAME AMOUNT EVERY YEAR. It is the right story for an asset that gives roughly equal service each year and wears mostly with TIME - a building, a fixed concrete structure, a boundary fence. REDUCING-BALANCE charges MORE IN THE EARLY YEARS and less later. It is the right story for an asset that LOSES MOST OF ITS VALUE WHEN IT IS NEW AND FAST - a vehicle is the classic case, worth far less the moment it is driven. UNITS-OF-PRODUCTION ties the charge to ACTUAL USE - per hour run, per unit made, per megalitre pumped - rather than to the calendar. It is the right story for an asset whose wear is driven by HOW HARD IT IS WORKED, not by how old it is: a high-use pump, heavy plant, a production machine.

3. Match the method to the wear

So you choose by asking the honest question: HOW DOES THIS ASSET ACTUALLY GIVE UP ITS VALUE - WITH TIME, WITH AGE-WHEN-NEW, OR WITH USE? Then you tell that story. An asset that wears steadily with the calendar takes STRAIGHT-LINE. An asset that loses most of its value the moment it is new takes REDUCING-BALANCE. An asset that wears with how hard it is worked takes UNITS-OF-PRODUCTION. The right method is the one that MATCHES REALITY - not the simplest to compute, and not the one that happens to flatter this year's numbers. A use-driven pump put on a calendar method looks healthy on the books long after it is worn out in the field.

4. The pattern feeds real decisions

The choice is NOT cosmetic paperwork, because the depreciation pattern FEEDS REAL DECISIONS: it shapes the asset's BOOK VALUE, the PROFIT reported each year, and - critically for an asset manager - THE POINT AT WHICH THE ASSET LOOKS WORN OUT ON PAPER AND THE SIGNAL TO PLAN ITS RENEWAL. A method matched to reality gives honest signals for when to renew and replace; a METHOD CHOSEN TO FLATTER A NUMBER LIES TO EXACTLY THOSE DECISIONS. That is why a use-driven pump put on a calendar method looks healthy on the books long after it is worn out in the field - and why the books once told you a hard-run pump had years of life left.

5. Choose truth, not flattery

There is a SMALL DISHONESTY TO REFUSE, and naming it is part of the craft: picking a method - or quietly stretching an asset's assumed life - NOT because it tells the truth about the asset, but because it SMOOTHS THIS YEAR'S PROFIT OR DEFERS AN INCONVENIENT CHARGE. It is tempting because the total comes out the same in the end, so it feels harmless. IT IS NOT HARMLESS: the whole point of the number is to inform a decision, and a number bent to look good MISINFORMS the renewal plan, the budget, and eventually the people who depend on the asset being renewed before it fails. CHOOSE THE METHOD THAT TELLS THE TRUTH ABOUT HOW THE ASSET ACTUALLY WEARS OUT, and let the books be a map you can trust.

T4: Your Turn

Onalenna must choose a depreciation method for three new assets at Corathyl, and defend a couple of numbers. Your job is to match each to how it wears, and to see what the wrong method would hide.

The three assets: a small brick office building; a delivery vehicle costing R250 000 with a 5-year life and negligible residual; and a production-line stamping machine whose wear tracks the number of parts it presses.

  1. Choose and justify. Pick a depreciation method for each of the three assets, and justify each choice from how that asset actually wears.
  2. Do the arithmetic. For the vehicle, compute the first year's depreciation two ways: straight-line, and reducing-balance at 40 per cent per year.
  3. See the distortion. Explain how using straight-line for the vehicle could mislead a decision to sell it after two years.
  4. Name the refusal. Name the small dishonesty to refuse when choosing a method.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the choices: the office building wears mostly with time and gives steady service - straight-line. The vehicle loses most value when new and fast - reducing-balance. The stamping machine wears with how much it presses, not with age - units-of-production. Each method is chosen because it tells that asset's true story of wear.

Second, the vehicle arithmetic: straight-line = R250 000 / 5 = R50 000 in the first year. Reducing-balance at 40 per cent = R250 000 x 0.40 = R100 000 in the first year. Same asset, same total over its life, very different first-year charge.

Third, the distortion: after two years, straight-line leaves the vehicle on the books at R250 000 - R100 000 = R150 000. But a two-year-old vehicle has really lost value fast; reducing-balance would carry it at about R90 000 (R100 000 then R60 000 of charge), far closer to what it would actually fetch. So a manager deciding whether to sell, trusting the straight-line book value of R150 000, would believe the vehicle is worth far more than the market will pay - and could turn down a fair offer, or budget a replacement on a fiction. The calendar method flattered the asset and misled the decision.

Fourth, the refusal: refuse to choose a method - or stretch an asset's assumed life - to smooth this year's profit or defer a charge rather than to tell the truth about how the asset wears. The total is the same either way; the only thing you gain by bending it is a number that lies to the next decision.

T5: The Guardrails

The total depreciation is the same whatever you choose - so choose the method that tells the truth about how the asset actually wears out, because that story feeds every renewal decision that follows.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Straight-line by default for everything. The same charge every year is only true for an asset that wears evenly with time. A use-driven pump or a fast-depreciating vehicle needs a method that tells its own story.

• Choosing a method to smooth or defer profit. The total is the same whatever you pick, so bending the method buys nothing real - except a number that lies to the next decision. Choose for truth.

• Forgetting the total is fixed. The method changes the timing, not the amount. So the only thing to weigh is which timing pattern matches how the asset actually gives up its value.

• Ignoring the renewal signal. The depreciation pattern is when the asset looks worn out on paper. A method mismatched to real wear will call for renewal too early or, worse, too late - after the asset has failed.

• Treating depreciation as mystique. It is not accountancy magic; it is an honest story about how a thing wears out. Any asset manager can and should choose it deliberately.

• Bending the useful life to hit a number. Stretching an asset's assumed life to lower the yearly charge is the same small dishonesty wearing a different hat. Set the life to reality, not to the target.
