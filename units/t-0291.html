Step 1 of 5

The report says the line was available 98 per cent of the year and I know it stopped twenty times - which number do I owe the customer?

T1: The Ache

It is the contract review at a juice bottler outside Pietermaritzburg. You are the production manager. On the table is the maintenance contractor's year-end report, and beside it your own production diary - a hard-covered book with pencil marks down the margins, one line per stoppage, in your handwriting.

The report says the filling line was available 98 per cent of the year. Ruan Kellerman, the contractor's account manager, is courteous about it and has every right to be. You have checked his arithmetic twice and it is correct.

Your diary says the line stopped twenty times.

Both are true. The line was scheduled for 4,000 hours and lost 80 of them, and 3,920 of 4,000 is 98 per cent exactly. It also stopped twenty times, each time for about four hours, and each stoppage killed a production run - a run that had a pallet of labelled bottles half-filled, a supermarket order behind it, and two hours of cleaning and restart before anything moved again.

What makes this urgent rather than merely irritating is the letter you have to write this week. The supermarket is renewing, and they want a service level in the contract. Ruan has offered, helpfully, to let you quote his figure. If you promise 98 per cent, you will be held to something you cannot keep - because they will not measure hours, they will measure whether their Tuesday order arrived on Tuesday, and one run in twelve and a half did not.

And you cannot say why, in a sentence that would survive the room. The number is not wrong. You keep coming back to that, and it keeps stopping you.

The contractor's 98 per cent is arithmetically correct and it describes a line I would not sign for. What number do I actually owe this customer?

Before you read on, decide. A correct figure, a diary of twenty stoppages, and a letter due this week that has to contain a promise. Write down what you would put in that letter - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does with those twenty stoppages

• "Challenge the contractor's figure." A common first instinct, and it loses, because the figure is right. It spends the meeting on arithmetic that has already been checked twice, and leaves Tuesday's order exactly where it was.

• "Quote the 98 per cent - it is the audited number." Safe this week and it is the promise you cannot keep. The supermarket does not buy hours; it buys deliveries, and one run in twelve and a half did not happen.

• "Promise nothing measurable - say 'high availability' and move on." It survives the meeting and it hands the argument to whoever writes the first complaint letter, with no agreed number to stand on.

• "Promise 92 per cent of runs and take the hit on the tender." Honest, and possibly the answer - but not yet, because you have not shown what would move that figure, and a promise offered without a plan reads as an excuse for poor service rather than a measurement.

• "Add penalties for stoppages to the maintenance contract." Reasonable and it addresses the contractor, not the customer. The letter is still due, and it still needs a number in it.

• What the first five share is the assumption that there is one number and somebody has it. There are two, they are both true at once, and they answer questions so different that a line can be excellent on one and unacceptable on the other. One counts hours. The other counts whether you got through the run. Twenty short stoppages and two long ones can lose the same 80 hours and be completely different businesses to run - and until you have both numbers side by side, you cannot see which one your customer is actually buying.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the method run start to finish on the bottling line, with the figures out of your own diary.

Step 1 - Write the promise down, and the period it is measured in. Before any arithmetic, say what you are being asked to guarantee and in what unit the customer will check it. The supermarket's promise is "your order, on the day you ordered it", and its unit is the production run - one run per weekday order, 250 in the year. Not the hour. Not the month. The run. This single line decides everything that follows, and it is the step people skip.

Step 2 - Count failures and downtime as two separate columns. Never one. Your diary gives both, and Mpho Tshabalala writes them out for the filling line and, because the contract covers both, for the packing line next door:

LineScheduled hoursStoppagesHours down eachTotal hours down
Filling4,00020480
Packing4,00024080

Step 3 - Compute availability: what fraction of the hours was it up? Availability is the up hours over the scheduled hours. Filling: (4,000 - 80) ÷ 4,000 = 3,920 ÷ 4,000 = 0.98. Packing: identical arithmetic, identical answer - 0.98. The two lines have exactly the same availability, and Ruan's report is right about both.

The same figure comes out of the two quantities a maintenance contract is usually written in. Mean time between failures is up hours divided by stoppages; mean time to repair is hours down divided by stoppages. Filling: 3,920 ÷ 20 = 196 hours between failures, 4 hours to repair. Packing: 3,920 ÷ 2 = 1,960 hours between failures, 40 hours to repair. Availability is time-up over total time either way - 196 ÷ (196 + 4) = 0.98, and 1,960 ÷ (1,960 + 40) = 0.98. One line fails ten times as often and is fixed ten times as fast, and the summary number cannot tell them apart, because it was never asked to.

Step 4 - Compute reliability for the period in the promise: what fraction of runs got through? Now use the unit from Step 1. Of 250 runs, the filling line lost one to each of its 20 stoppages: (250 - 20) ÷ 250 = 0.92. The packing line lost one to each of its 2: (250 - 2) ÷ 250 = 0.992.

There it is. Two lines, the same availability to the decimal, and one of them misses ten times as many orders as the other. Nothing was hidden and nobody was careless: availability answered the question it was asked, which is what fraction of the hours the line was up, and the supermarket is not buying hours.

Step 5 - Quote the number that matches the promise, and improve the lever that moves it. The letter offers 92 per cent of runs delivered on the day ordered, because that is the promise's own unit and it is the truth. And it does not stop there, because a bare 92 reads as an excuse. The two numbers point at two different repairs: reliability is moved by failing less often, availability by being fixed faster. The filling line already gets fixed fast - four hours - and fixing it faster would raise a figure that is already 98 and change the customer's experience not at all. What moves 92 is the twenty stoppages themselves. So the letter offers 92 now, names the three causes that account for fourteen of the twenty, and offers 96 per cent from the second quarter with those causes closed out. Mpho takes the same page to Ruan, and the maintenance contract's target changes from availability, which it was always going to hit, to stoppages per quarter, which is the thing the customer actually feels.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the first one is the one that decides the answer.

  • 1. Write the promise and its period - what is guaranteed, and in what unit the other party will check it.
  • 2. Count failures and downtime as two separate columns, never as one summary figure.
  • 3. Compute availability - up hours over total hours.
  • 4. Compute reliability in the promise's own period - periods that got through, over periods attempted.
  • 5. Quote the one that matches the promise, and improve the lever that moves it.

The two figures have names and they answer different questions. Availability is the fraction of time a thing is up and ready - it counts hours, and it is the right measure when what you owe is capacity over a long stretch: a standby generator, a hospital bed, a hired crane paid by the month. Reliability is the chance of getting through one stated period without failing - it counts attempts, and it is the right measure when what you owe is a discrete thing that either happens or does not: a run, a delivery, a flight, a shift. The two quantities inside availability are the ones your maintenance contract is probably already written in: mean time between failures, the up hours divided by the number of failures, and mean time to repair, the hours lost divided by the number of failures. Availability is up-time over total time, so it can be written from those two directly - and that is exactly why it hides the difference between them, because a big MTBF with a big MTTR and a small one of each can land on the same answer, as your two lines did at 0.98.

Which gives the rule worth carrying out of here: the promise chooses the measure, and the measure chooses the repair. If you owe attempts, quote reliability and spend on failing less often. If you owe hours, quote availability and spend on being fixed faster. Quoting the wrong one is not a technicality - it either commits you to something you cannot keep, or it sends the money at the lever that does not move what anybody feels.

Three limits. This assumes a stoppage costs you the whole period, which is true for a run that must be scrapped and restarted and false where work simply resumes - there, count part-periods honestly rather than rounding in your own favour. It says nothing about the consequence of a failure: two lost runs of a life-saving product and two lost runs of juice are not the same event, and no uptime figure will tell you that. And both numbers are backward-looking counts of what happened; treating them as a forecast is only safe while the plant, the duty and the maintenance stay as they were.

How to combine several parts of a system into one figure - which parts must all work, which have spares, and where the whole thing is weakest - is a different question. This unit is about which of the two numbers you owe, once you have them.

T4: Your Turn

Do each task before reading its feedback. Work the arithmetic on paper - the skill is in doing it, not in reading it.

Task 1 - The same availability, two different businesses. A generator at a rural clinic ran 8,760 hours in the year and was down 175 hours. A delivery bakkie was scheduled for 8,760 hours and was also down 175 hours. Compute each one's availability. Does knowing it tell you which asset to spend on?

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

Both: (8,760 - 175) ÷ 8,760 = 8,585 ÷ 8,760 = 0.98. The figure is identical and it tells you nothing about where to spend, because it does not say whether that was one 175-hour outage or fifty short ones. Good answers say what is still missing: the number of failures. Availability is one number made from two, and you cannot get the two back out of it.

Task 2 - Put the promise's period on it. The clinic's generator matters only during grid outages, of which there were 40 in the year. It failed to start on 2 of them. The bakkie makes 240 delivery runs a year and broke down on 6. Compute each one's reliability in its own period, and say which figure you would put in front of the clinic's matron.

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

Generator: (40 - 2) ÷ 40 = 0.95 - it started on 95 per cent of the outages it existed for. Bakkie: (240 - 6) ÷ 240 = 0.975. Both were "98 per cent available" and neither of those is the number the matron needs: she is not buying generator-hours, she is buying starts when the power goes, and one attempt in twenty failed. Good answers name the promise's period first and derive the figure from it, rather than converting between the two.

Task 3 - Choose the lever. The filling line from the demonstration sits at 20 stoppages of 4 hours. Your contractor offers two improvements at the same price: (a) halve the repair time to 2 hours, or (b) remove half the stoppages, leaving 10 of 4 hours. Compute the new availability and the new per-run reliability for each, and say which you buy if the customer measures runs delivered.

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

(a) Downtime 20 x 2 = 40 h. Availability = 3,960 ÷ 4,000 = 0.99. Runs still lost: 20, so reliability stays 0.92. (b) Downtime 10 x 4 = 40 h. Availability = 0.99 as well - identical. Runs lost: 10, so reliability = 240 ÷ 250 = 0.96. The two offers look the same on availability and are not remotely the same to the customer: buy (b). The lesson is the whole unit in one comparison - faster repair moves availability, fewer failures moves reliability, and you buy the one that moves what you promised.

Now take a promise you are actually making - to a customer, a regulator, a clinic, your own board - and run the five steps on it.

Step 1. Write the promise in one sentence and underline its period: hours, runs, deliveries, shifts, starts, calls answered.

Step 2. From your records, get two columns and keep them apart: how many times it failed, and how long each one lasted. If your records only hold a summary percentage, that is a finding in itself - say so, and start counting both from today.

Step 3. Availability = up hours ÷ scheduled hours. (Or from the contract's own terms: MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR).)

Step 4. Reliability = periods completed ÷ periods attempted, in the period you underlined in Step 1.

Step 5. Write both figures side by side and say which one your promise is in. Then name the lever: fewer failures for reliability, faster repair for availability - and check that the improvement you were about to buy moves the one you owe.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the promise honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• One summary percentage, with the two counts gone. Availability is made from a failure count and a repair time, and you cannot get them back out of it. Keep both columns in the record, always - once they are averaged together the information is destroyed.

• The period taken from the report instead of the promise. Reports arrive in hours because that is how contractors are paid. Underline the period your customer will check in - runs, deliveries, starts - and compute in that, even when it is inconvenient.

• Arguing with a figure that is correct. If the arithmetic is right, saying so first is what buys you the room to show the second number. Attacking it makes the meeting about the contractor rather than about the promise.

• Money spent on the lever you do not owe. Faster repair raises availability and can leave the customer's experience untouched; fewer failures raises reliability. Check which one your promise is in before signing for the improvement.

• A stoppage counted as its clock hours when it costs a whole period. A four-hour trip that scraps a run costs a run, not four hours. Count in the unit the loss actually lands in, and say which you used.

• A bare low number offered with no plan. 92 per cent on its own reads as an excuse. The honest version is the figure, its causes, and what it becomes by when - which is a promise rather than an apology.

• Uptime mistaken for consequence. Neither figure knows what a failure costs. Two lost runs of juice and two lost runs of a medicine are the same number and not the same event, and that judgement stays with a person.

• Last year's count read as next year's forecast. Both are backward-looking. They hold only while the plant, the duty and the maintenance stay as they were - and if any of those changed, say so beside the number.

You sat in that review with a report you could not fault and a diary you could not ignore, and no sentence that held both. You can leave with the two of them side by side: 98 per cent of the hours, 92 per cent of the runs, the same line, both true - and the packing line next door at the same 98 and a genuine 99.2, which is what proves the point. Availability counts hours; reliability counts whether you got through. The promise you are making chooses which one you owe, and the one you owe chooses where the money goes. The letter offers 92 now and 96 by the second quarter, and the maintenance contract's target moves from the figure it was always going to hit to the stoppages the customer actually feels.
