Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Mbali, the centre manager of a large shopping centre. It is 11am on the busiest Saturday of the month when your phone rings - the head of security, tense: "the main glass lift is stuck between floors. There is a family inside."

You rush over. A small crowd is gathering, and through the glass you can see a father trying to calm his two small children. You get your outsourced maintenance contractor on the radio. The duty manager is calm - too calm. "We have logged the call, Mbali. The contract gives us a two-hour response for entrapments. A technician is scheduled." Two hours, on a packed Saturday. "We did the monthly service on Tuesday, as per the schedule," he adds, voice flat. "All boxes ticked. The lift is compliant."

He is pointing at the contract - the paper - not at the terrified family in the glass box. And the full horror lands: you outsourced the maintenance to save R500,000 a year, but you kept all the risk, all the liability, all the public shame. You gave them a contract that asks them to tick boxes, not to deliver a safe, reliable lift. And you no longer have a single lift technician on your own payroll, so you cannot even tell whether that "service" was real work or a pencil-whipped page. You are completely at their mercy.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instincts are about control and punishment, and they all fail. "A new contract with massive penalties for failure!" - but penalties are paid AFTER the disaster, cold comfort to the family in the lift and no help to your reputation; and a contractor squeezed by huge penalties just cuts corners elsewhere to protect its margin, making failure more likely. "Fire them and get a cheaper contractor!" - the same trap with less skill and older equipment, restarting the race to the bottom, buying the same broken model for a lower price. Finally you shout at their management and threaten them - but the threats are hollow: you cannot prove they did the work badly because you no longer have the in-house expertise to judge it. They hold all the technical cards; you gave away the knowledge, and with it your power, and shouting just makes you look weak.

The problem is not the contractor, the price or the penalties - it is the whole way you think about the work. So before you read on: what are you actually trying to BUY?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop seeing the contract as a way to transfer work and start seeing it as a tool to guarantee an outcome. The founding principle is simple: you can outsource the work, but you can never outsource the risk or the accountability. The asset is still yours; the service is still your responsibility. Five moves:

  • Own the outcome - accountability cannot be outsourced: the breakthrough begins by accepting the thing the contract tried to make you forget: you can hand over the WORK, but you can never hand over the RISK or the ACCOUNTABILITY. The asset is still yours, the service is still your responsibility, and when it fails it is your name in the complaint and your reputation in the crowd around the stuck lift, no matter whose technician is scheduled to arrive in two hours. A contract that 'transfers risk to the contractor' transfers blame on paper, not risk in reality - the trapped family does not care whose schedule it was on. So own the outcome first: you are accountable for a safe, reliable experience for the people who use your asset, full stop. Everything else is about building an arrangement that actually delivers that, instead of one that just lets you point at someone else when it does not.
  • Specify the outcome, not bodies-and-hours: the root mistake was buying the wrong thing: you bought 'a monthly service visit' and 'boxes ticked', so that is exactly what you got - activity, not reliability. Stop buying inputs and buy the OUTCOME: not 'hours of maintenance' but '99.8% lift uptime and zero safety incidents'; not 'a visit' but 'the asset performs'. Write the contract around what good actually looks like, with a lower fixed fee and a real bonus for meeting or beating the outcome. This is bp-0073's and bp-0076's fit-for-real-duty discipline applied to a service: when you pay for the result you want rather than the activity you can count, the contractor's effort finally points at the same thing you need - a working lift, not a full checklist.
  • Stay the intelligent client: the deepest trap was hollowing out your own competence to 'save headcount', because once you have no one who understands lifts you cannot specify the work, judge it, or prove it was done badly - you are wholly at the contractor's mercy and your threats are empty. So stay an INTELLIGENT CLIENT: keep (or re-hire) enough in-house expertise not to do the work but to OWN it - to write a good specification, review the reports with a knowing eye, run spot-checks and tell good work from a pencil-whipped page. Even one senior, experienced person restores your grip. This is why bp-0139's warning about naming and keeping the skills your assets need matters here: outsourcing the doing is fine, but outsourcing the KNOWING leaves you unable to hold anyone to account.
  • Verify delivery against the outcome: a contract does not enforce itself, and 'compliant on paper' is not the same as 'working in reality' - so trust, but verify. Make your in-house expert's spot-inspections and the real-world performance data (the actual uptime, the actual incident count) the basis of what you pay: pay for proven performance, not for promised activity or a signed checklist. This is bp-0100's and bp-0150's discipline - never take the reading or the report on trust, watch the few signals that show whether the asset is really being kept well, before a failure announces it for you. Verification is also what makes the outcome-based contract real: the bonus is earned against measured results, so the words on the page actually bite.
  • Align incentives and build a partnership: the last move turns an adversary into a partner: arrange things so the contractor earns MORE when your asset runs perfectly, not when it fails and you pay penalties. Tie their success to yours - a bonus for sustained uptime and safety - and the relationship shifts from an argument over costs and blame into a shared goal of keeping the lifts running. And treat a good contractor with dignity, as a partner for a long-lived asset, not a throat to squeeze: a contractor squeezed to the bone cuts exactly the corners you will pay for in a stuck lift, while one who wins when you win brings you their best people and their early warnings. You can outsource the work, never the accountability - so make the people doing the work want the same outcome you are accountable for.

See it transform Mbali's arrangement. She stops buying 'hours of maintenance' and writes a new contract for outcomes - a lower fixed fee plus a real bonus for every month the lifts hit 99.8% uptime with zero safety incidents. She re-hires one senior facilities technician, not to fix the lifts but to be her expert: to review the contractor's reports with a knowing eye, run random spot-inspections, and tell good work from a pencil-whipped page. Payment now rests on that verification and the real uptime data, not on a signed checklist. And because the contractor earns more when the lifts run perfectly, the relationship shifts from an argument over costs into a shared goal - they bring their best people and their early warnings, because they win when she wins. You can outsource the work, but never the accountability. A contract that only shifts blame on paper does not move risk in reality - the asset, the service and the consequence stay yours whoever turns the spanner. So own the outcome, specify the outcome you need rather than hours and visits, stay the intelligent client who keeps enough knowledge to judge and verify the work, and build a partnership where your contractor wins when you, your asset and your customers win. Stay the intelligent client who owns the outcome.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - think about hiring a mechanic to fix your car's brakes.

See the intelligent client in a brake job

You can hand over the work, but the risk of brake failure on the motorway stays 100% yours. So you do not say "spend two hours working on my car" - you state the outcome you need: "make my brakes work perfectly and safely." You do not just take the cheapest quote from a stranger; you pick a reputable garage. You may not be a mechanic, but you know enough to ask "are you using quality parts? Is there a warranty on the work?" - you stay an intelligent client. And when you get the car back, you test the brakes carefully before you drive your family anywhere.

You owned the outcome, specified it, verified it, and aligned incentives by rewarding good work with your future custom. The same five moves scale all the way up to the lifts in a shopping centre - only the number of zeros changes.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own operation. Where have you handed over the work but kept the risk? It could be IT support, vehicle maintenance, cleaning, security, or critical equipment servicing - you have a contract, but if they fail, you are the one who pays the real price. Start here:

  1. Own the outcome. For one outsourced service, write down in a single sentence what a 'win' actually looks like for your customers and your mission.
  2. Check the contract. Does it buy that outcome, or does it just buy hours, visits and ticked boxes?
  3. Assess your knowledge. Do you have enough in-house expertise to tell whether the contractor is doing a good job or a poor one? If not, who is the one person you could train or hire to be your intelligent-client expert?
  4. Verify and align. Schedule one trust-but-verify spot-check this month, and start a conversation about building performance bonuses into the next renewal, so they win when you win.

By creating a system that rewards the outcomes you both want, you stop managing failure and start building success together - and you keep the real promise, which is not to a contract but to the father in the glass lift and everyone like him who trusts that the things they step into are safe.
