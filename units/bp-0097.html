Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Nkanyiso, operations director at a company that manufactures custom industrial components. You are staring at the monthly performance dashboard, and it makes no sense. The boardroom screen glows with success:

  • Production: green. They made 10,500 components - 105% of their 10,000 target.
  • Sales: green. They secured R11 million in new orders, beating the R10 million target.
  • Procurement: green. They bought raw materials 5% under budget.
  • Maintenance: green. They spent 10% less than their quarterly budget.

Every department head is smiling, ready for a pat on the back. But you feel a cold dread, because you have just seen the other screen - the company's accounts. Cash flow is negative R2 million for the month. The warehouse is bursting with R4 million of unsold components and raw materials. And worst of all, you just got off the phone with your biggest customer. They are leaving. "Nkanyiso, your last three orders were weeks late and incomplete. We cannot run our business like this."

They hit their targets - every single one. Production made things, sales sold things, procurement and maintenance saved money. But you shipped less than half the orders on time, your cash is gone, and your customers are walking away. Every part of the machine reports a win, and the whole thing is grinding to a halt.

Every department hit its targets and the whole company still went backwards - how can everyone be succeeding while the business is failing, and what am I actually supposed to manage?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instincts are to fix the parts, and each one backfires. "We need to work harder!" - you push Production to target 115% and ask Sales for another R2 million. The result is chaos: Production works overtime building even more of the wrong components, mountains of work-in-progress blocking the floor; Sales promises faster dates, creating even more late orders. The problem gets worse. "The targets must be wrong - let me re-tune each KPI." - you give Procurement a "low inventory" target and tell Sales to chase "high-margin only". This just moves the problem: Procurement starves the line and causes stoppages; Sales and Finance argue for weeks over which orders count, and volume collapses. "Someone must be slacking - find them." - you start digging through the reports for a team to blame, and breed a culture of fear and mistrust that breaks the team. Then you realise: everyone is doing exactly what they were asked to do. They are not the problem.

The problem is not the people, and not the effort. So before you read on: if the problem is not any of the parts, what is it - and what should you actually be managing?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop looking at the departments and start looking at the flow of work between them. Your business is not a collection of separate boxes; it is a single pipe, designed to turn a customer's order into delivered goods and a paid invoice. The goal is not to make each piece of the pipe shine, but to increase the flow of value through the whole pipe. Five moves:

  • See the system, not the silos: the breakthrough begins when you stop staring at the departments and start watching the flow of work BETWEEN them. Your business is not a set of separate boxes each with its own scoreboard; it is a single connected pipe whose only purpose is to turn a customer's order into delivered goods and a paid invoice. So gather the department heads and draw that whole process on one wall - from the moment an order arrives to the moment the cash lands - and for the first time everyone sees the truth the dashboard hid: their work is not independent, it is a chain of hand-overs, and a 'win' scored inside one box can be a loss inflicted on the next. You manage what you can see, and a wall of green departmental lights shows you everything EXCEPT the thing that actually pays you: the flow of value through the whole. Draw the flow, and the real system - and its real problems - finally become visible.
  • Accept that local optima do not add up: here is the hard, counter-intuitive law: improving every part of a system separately does NOT improve the whole, and often wrecks it, because the parts are interdependent. Walk the evidence on your own wall. Procurement's 'win' - a cheap bulk buy - became R4 million of the wrong stock, freezing your cash and blocking your warehouse. Maintenance's 'win' - delaying a service to save its budget - became the breakdown that made your biggest order weeks late. Sales' 'win' - more volume - became orders the plant could never deliver on time. Every department did exactly what it was measured to do, and each local victory subtracted from the whole. So 'everyone hit their target' is not evidence the system is healthy; when the whole is failing while every part reports success, it is evidence the system is sick - that you are measuring and rewarding the parts at war with the whole.
  • Define the one system goal: a system with many local scoreboards and no shared goal will always pull itself apart, so name the single outcome the WHOLE organisation exists to deliver, and raise it above every local score. Not an activity (things made, money saved, orders signed) but the final value to the customer: here, On-Time-In-Full delivery - the company wins only when the customer receives exactly what they ordered, exactly when it was promised, and pays for it. This one goal becomes the tie-breaker for every decision and the true measure of health, the number the board should glow or darken by. It re-points every department from 'did MY box win?' to 'did we deliver for the customer?'. You cannot align people you have not given a shared direction; the system goal is that direction, and almost everything else is subordinate to it.
  • Find the constraint and subordinate to it: in any flow, one step is the slowest - the bottleneck that sets the pace of the entire system, no matter how fast everything else can run. Find it. Here it is the final quality-testing station: it can pass about 9,000 components a month while the rest of the factory can make 10,500, so 1,500 pile up in front of it as work-in-progress every month - that is your swollen warehouse and your late orders, made visible. The system can only deliver as fast as its constraint allows, which means two things: protect the constraint (never let it stand idle or starve, because an hour lost there is an hour lost for the whole company), and subordinate everything else to it - a non-bottleneck running flat out does not help, it just builds the pile higher. Improve the whole by strengthening the weakest link; sprinting the strong links only makes the chain heavier.
  • Align targets and break the walls: the silos behaved rationally - they did what they were paid and measured to do - so the lasting fix is not to exhort people to 'think of the whole' but to change what winning MEANS for each of them. Re-write the local targets so a department can only win when the whole system wins: Production's job becomes 'keep the testing station fed with exactly what it needs, never more', not 'maximise output'; Sales promises dates based on the constraint's real capacity, not on hope; everyone is measured on the flow of on-time, in-full delivery they helped create. Then break the walls where the real losses always hide - at the hand-overs between departments - by getting the silos talking across those boundaries instead of optimising blindly behind them. When you reward flow instead of local busyness, cooperation replaces conflict, the work calms, the inventory melts away, the cash returns, and the customer who left calls back - because the whole, at last, is winning.

See it turn around for Nkanyiso. He stops promising what the factory cannot deliver and starts feeding the one constraint - the testing station - exactly what it needs, no more. The piles of useless work-in-progress vanish; the floor becomes calm and orderly. Because the company now only promises what it can truly deliver, orders start going out on time, in full. Cash flow turns positive as the R4 million of stranded stock finally converts to sales and invoices. A few weeks later, the customer who left calls back: "We hear you are delivering on time again. Can we talk?" A chain is not made stronger by hardening every link except the weakest - it only gets heavier. A business is a system with a constraint, and you do not improve it by making every department run faster; you improve it by naming one shared goal, finding the bottleneck, and making the whole system serve the flow through it. Every department hitting its target is not proof the system is healthy - when the whole fails while every part wins, it is proof the parts are being measured at war with the whole. Stop managing the parts. Start managing the whole.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one - picture a busy kitchen plating a meal for a table of four.

See why the parts can all win while the meal is ruined

The grill cooks a steak in 8 minutes, the salad station makes a salad in 3, the fryer makes chips in 5. If each station optimises for itself, they all start the moment the order arrives. The salad is "done" in 3 minutes, the chips in 5, the steak in 8 - each station has hit its own speed target. But the meal is a system failure: the salad wilts for 5 minutes, the chips go soggy under the lamp for 3, and when the waiter finally gathers the plates the "hot" food is lukewarm and the "fresh" food is tired. Every part won; the customer's meal was ruined.

A great head chef does not tell everyone to cook as fast as they can - that is the mistake. The chef manages the system: the salad starts just 3 minutes before the steak is ready, the fryer 5 minutes before, so the whole meal arrives at the pass at the same moment, perfect and ready. The goal is not for each station to be fast; it is for the meal to come together. Manage the whole, not the parts - and your factory, your office, your project is exactly the same kitchen.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

This sickness of local optima is everywhere once you see it - a department that hits its budget by refusing to help others, a team that produces beautiful reports no one reads, a policy that looks good on paper but makes everyone's job harder. Your job is to stop managing the parts and start managing the whole:

  1. Name the one system goal. What does your team, department or company exist to deliver - not an activity, but a final valuable outcome for the customer (a paid invoice, a solved ticket, a working product in the user's hands)? Write it down.
  2. Spot a local win that is a system loss. Find one example this week where one team's "good performance" created a problem for someone else - a cost-saving that caused a delay, a sales "win" that became an operational nightmare. Just seeing it is the first step.
  3. Find your constraint. Which one person, team or step sets the pace for everything else - your bottleneck? If you could improve only one part for the good of the whole, which would give the biggest lift?
  4. Rethink one target. Take one local measure and ask: "Does this push someone to do something that harms the whole system?" Then change it so they win only when the whole system wins.
R

Name the place where the parts are winning but the whole is losing, the one system goal you would set, and the one local target you would change so a team wins only when the whole does. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Map your order-to-cash flow and find the constraint together.)

Making people's lives easier starts by seeing the whole system. When you manage the flow, not just the silos, you replace conflict with cooperation and firefighting with smooth, predictable work - and you stop rewarding people for polishing their own small piece while the customer at the end of the pipe is let down. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link; a business is only as good as the flow through its constraint.
