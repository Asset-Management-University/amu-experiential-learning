I'm working flat out and selling all I make - so why doesn't it ever add up?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Tariq, and you make beautiful, hand-painted wooden signs. Everyone says you have a gift. You've a small stall at the market and you're always busy - early morning to late at night, sanding, painting, selling. You sell almost every sign you make. And yet, somehow, you never get ahead: after the wood, the paints, the stall rent, and trying to pay yourself enough to live on, the money is just... gone.

A nagging thought keeps you awake. "If I'm this busy and still struggling, the only answer must be to work harder - make more, sell more." But you're already exhausted, near the limit of what your hands and hours can do, and a quiet dread is growing that no amount of effort is closing the gap.

I'm working as hard as a person can, and selling all I make - so why doesn't it ever add up, and how hard would I actually have to work to make this live?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your instinct is to push harder. You try to paint faster - but the quality drops and you feel less proud of your work. You stay up later - but so tired you make mistakes. A friend says drop your price to sell more - yet that feels wrong: if you're not making enough now, how does making less on each sign help? You feel trapped: you're giving all the effort a person can give, you believe in your work, and still the gap between hard work and a secure living feels impossible to cross.

So you keep pushing, hoping sheer effort will break through. But before you read on: have you ever actually worked out the number of signs you'd need to sell - and whether that number is even possible?

I make hand-painted signs and I'm busier than ever, selling almost everything I make - dawn to dark. Yet after wood, paint, the stall rent and a wage for myself, the money's just gone. Working longer hits my ceiling; dropping the price puts even less in my pocket per sale. I keep reaching for MORE EFFORT - but have I ever worked out the actual NUMBER of sales I'd need, and whether that number is even possible?

If the needed number is bigger than what's possible, effort can't fix it - redesign the model with one of four levers: raise the price, cut the per-sale cost, cut the fixed cost, or widen the reach.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The problem was never your effort - it's the maths of the model. Before you work harder, do the backwards-maths to see whether your goal is even possible. (This is a kindness: a model that can't support you will collapse and then serve no one - so face the numbers, and build something that lasts.) Five moves:

  • Add up ALL your monthly costs - both kinds: two kinds hide here. The costs that GROW with each sale (wood, paint, packaging), and the costs that STAY THE SAME whether you sell one or a hundred (stall rent, a fair wage for yourself, your phone). You must know the stay-the-same total - that's the mountain every month has to cover before you've earned a thing.
  • Find what each sale really puts in your pocket: you don't pocket the price. From the price, take off what that one sale cost you to make (the wood, paint, packaging). What's left - often shockingly thin - is what each sale actually puts toward your mountain of monthly costs. A R250 sign that costs R130 to make puts just R120 in your pocket.
  • Work out the number you'd need to sell: now the key sum: divide your stay-the-same monthly costs by what each sale puts in your pocket. That's the number of sales you'd need just to break even - before you earn a cent of profit. R6,500 of monthly costs, R120 a sale, means 55 sales a month, every month, just to stand still.
  • Compare the needed number to what's possible: hold that needed-number against reality - both ceilings: what your hands and hours can actually MAKE, and what your market will actually BUY. This is the moment of truth: if you need 55 but can only ever make 40 and sell 35, no effort on earth closes that gap. The relief is real - it was never your diligence; it's arithmetic.
  • If it can't be reached, redesign the model - not your effort: effort cannot overcome arithmetic; only a new model can. Pull a lever, each with an honest trade-off: RAISE the price (trade-off: you might lose some price-sensitive buyers, but you need far fewer sales); CUT the per-sale cost (trade-off: risk to quality if you cut too deep); CUT the stay-the-same costs (trade-off: maybe a less ideal stall or tools); or WIDEN the reach (trade-off: a bigger market takes real time and selling skill). Re-run the maths until the needed-number sits safely inside what's possible.

Watch it become clear. Your sign sells for R250; the wood, paint and packaging for it cost R130, so each sale puts R120 in your pocket. Your stay-the-same monthly costs are stall rent R1,500 + a basic wage R4,500 + phone R500 = R6,500. So the number you'd need is R6,500 ÷ R120 = 55 signs a month, just to break even - but flat out you can make only ~40, and the market wants ~35. You need 55; your world can only ever give you about 40. No effort closes that - and the relief is that it was never your fault; it's arithmetic. So you change the maths: your work is fine quality, so you pull Lever 1 and raise the price to R400 - now each sale pockets R270, and the number you need falls to R6,500 ÷ R270 = 25 signs, safely inside what's possible. Effort cannot overcome arithmetic; only a redesign of the model can - so find the number you'd need, check it's even possible, and if it isn't, change the model, not your effort.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about making things - it catches anyone whose required number meets a hard ceiling. Watch a freelancer.

See the same trap catch a freelance writer

Hana is a freelance writer who charges R1,500 for a day of work. Her stay-the-same monthly costs - rent, software, her own wage - come to R30,000.

• The number she needs: R30,000 ÷ R1,500 = 20 billable days a month.
• The ceiling: a month has ~21 working days, but she can't bill every one - she needs time for admin, finding new clients, the odd sick day. Realistically she can bill 15-17 days.
• The trap: she needs 20, but her world can only give ~17 - and more effort can't add days to the month.
• The redesign: she pulls the levers - raise her day-rate, cut her costs, or sell something not tied to her time (an online course) so income isn't capped by her hours.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Are you working harder than ever but never getting ahead? Stop pushing for a moment and start calculating:

  1. List your stay-the-same costs, then find your pocket. Add up everything you must pay each month no matter what you sell (include a fair wage for yourself). Then take one sale's price and subtract what that one sale costs to make - what's really left over for you?
  2. Calculate the number you'd need, and face your ceiling. Divide your monthly costs by that pocket figure - that's the sales you'd need just to survive. Then be brutally honest about the most you could ever make, and the most your market would ever buy.
  3. Compare - and if needed beats possible, redesign. Is your needed number safely inside your ceiling? If not, your effort is being spent on a broken model. Stop grinding and pull one of the four levers - raise the price, cut the per-sale cost, cut the fixed cost, or widen the reach - until the number you need sits inside what's possible.

Arithmetic can feel harsh, but it is one of the kindest guides you have. It refuses to let you pour your one precious life into a model that was never going to carry you - and it shows you, plainly, what to change so it can. Effort is a wonderful thing, but it cannot beat a sum; aim it at a model that works, and the same hard work that was quietly sinking you will start to build something that serves people - and you - for the long journey ahead.
