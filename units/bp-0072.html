Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Vukile. It is half past six on a cold Thursday morning, and you are standing on the steel walkway above the wet well at a municipal high-lift pump station, just outside town. The station lifts the town's drinking water 140 metres up the ridge to the storage reservoir. Below you, three big high-lift pumps sit in a row, painted a faded green. Two are running. The third, Pump 2, is the one everyone is worried about.

In your hand is a laptop. On the screen is the station's simulation model - a digital copy of these three pumps and their pipes that the board paid a consultant R380,000 to build two years ago. The whole point of it was simple and good: before you do anything risky on the real station, you test it on the model first. You change a setting, you watch what the model predicts, and only if it looks safe do you do it for real. The fitter, Ayabulela, comes up the walkway wiping his hands on a rag; there is a smell of warm oil and chlorine in the cold air. He has worked this station for eleven years and he does not trust the laptop. "You and that computer," he says, not unkindly. "Tell me what it says we must do, and I will tell you if it is talking nonsense."

Here is the decision on you this morning. The power utility has moved the town onto a time-of-use electricity tariff: power between 6am and 9am now costs R3.10 a unit; power at night costs R0.95 a unit. The town uses about 4,200 kWh a day to pump its water. If you could shift most of the pumping to the cheap night hours - filling the reservoir overnight and coasting through the expensive morning - the board's modelling says you would save roughly R31,000 a month. That is real money for a small board. So last night you ran exactly that plan through the simulation: run two pumps hard from 10pm, fill the reservoir by 5am, then idle through the morning peak. The model came back green. Reservoir fills with two hours to spare. No pump runs too hot. Safe. You should just do it tonight.

But something is bothering you, and it has a name: Pump 2. The model shows Pump 2 as a healthy pump, identical to its two sisters, pushing 180 litres a second at full speed, brand-new impeller - what the consultant typed in two years ago from the original purchase papers. "Ask me about Pump 2," Ayabulela says quietly. So you ask, and he tells you what the laptop does not know. Eighteen months ago Pump 2 threw a bearing and was rebuilt in a hurry - the only impeller in the store that week was a slightly smaller spare off a different model, and it has been running with that wrong impeller ever since. It does not push 180 litres a second any more; on a good day it manages 150. It runs hotter. And a hand-written note in the maintenance file - not in the model - says Pump 2 must not run for more than four hours without a cool-down, or the seal weeps. The model that told you the plan was "safe" was simulating three identical healthy pumps. The real station has two healthy pumps and one tired, weaker, hotter pump that cannot run all night - and the plan leaned on two pumps running hard for seven hours straight, one of them Pump 2. The model is confident. The model is also describing a station that does not exist.

The model says my cheap-night-power plan is safe and saves R31,000 a month - but it thinks Pump 2 is healthy, and the fitter has just told me it's run a wrong, weaker impeller for eighteen months the model has never heard of; do I trust the green light and start tonight?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The morning is cold and the saving is real. "The model said green - the consultant was an expert, just run it tonight." But the model is simulating three identical healthy pumps; it is confident about a station that does not exist, and "probably fine" is how you cook a R210,000 seal on the first night. "Trust Ayabulela - he says Pump 2 is weak, so just don't use it." But that's swapping one untested opinion for another; you don't yet know HOW weak, or whether the drift is only Pump 2, and you'd be guessing again. "Rebuild the model from the maintenance file and memory." But memory and a hand-written note are not measurements; you'd bake fresh guesses into the very tool whose guessing is the problem. So before you read on: name what the laptop is really a model OF (and which version of the asset it froze), and the cheapest honest way to find out how far it has drifted from the real pumps before you trust it with a single decision.

In my hand is a laptop running the station's simulation model: a digital copy of these pumps and pipes the board paid a consultant R380,000 to build two years ago, whose whole point is that before you do anything risky on the real station you test it on the model first. The power utility has put the town on a time-of-use tariff - power costs R3.10 a unit in the 6-9am peak and R0.95 at night; we use about 4,200 kWh a day to pump - so shifting most pumping to the cheap night hours (fill the reservoir overnight, coast through the morning) should save about R31,000 a month. I ran exactly that plan through the model last night: two pumps hard from 10pm, full by 5am, idle through the peak. It came back GREEN - fills with two hours to spare, no pump too hot, safe. But the model shows Pump 2 as a healthy pump pushing 180 litres a second, brand-new impeller - what the consultant typed in from the original purchase papers. And Ayabulela, the fitter (eleven years here), has just told me what the laptop doesn't know: eighteen months ago Pump 2 threw a bearing and was rebuilt in a hurry with a slightly smaller spare impeller off a different model; it manages 150 l/s on a good day, runs hotter, and a hand-written note in the file (not in the model) says it must not run more than four hours without a cool-down or the seal weeps. The 'safe' plan leans on two pumps running hard seven hours straight - one of them Pump 2. Do I trust the green light and start tonight?
Every decision you make on that laptop is a decision about an imaginary station, not the one humming under your feet. The model is confident AND describing a station that does not exist. What the laptop holds is a DIGITAL TWIN - a living digital copy of a real asset, built so you can test decisions on the copy before risking the real thing - and it is only worth anything if it is FAITHFUL to the asset's true PRESENT condition: its as-MAINTAINED state, not its as-BUILT state. Yours was built once from the purchase papers and left to drift while the station aged, broke and got repaired with whatever was in the store. A twin that has drifted does worse than fail to help - it gives FALSE CONFIDENCE: it told you the night plan was green when the real station can't survive it. So don't run the plan tonight; first make the twin true - but don't just ASSUME it's wrong because the fitter says so. Set a tolerance line in advance ('the twin is faithful if each pump, run alone, reads within ~5% of the flow and power the model claims; drifted if any reads well outside that'), then find out the cheapest honest way: read the asset's OWN flow and power meters, one pump at a time. The real question is not 'do I trust the green light?' but 'how far has my model drifted from the metal, and what does the decision look like once the model tells the truth?'
Five moves turn a confident-but-lying model into one you can act on. (1) NAME THE DRIFT - the twin is a mirror, and an un-updated mirror reflects an asset that no longer exists; as-built two years ago, never re-connected to as-maintained reality, it gives false confidence. (2) SET THE LINE BEFORE YOU TEST - on the whiteboard, the tolerance that decides faithful-vs-drifted (~5% on flow and power), so the meters, not opinion, return the verdict. (3) LISTEN TO THE ASSET - the cheapest honest probe is already bolted on: run each pump alone for twenty minutes and read real flow and power off the station's own instruments. THE SURPRISE: Pump 1 and 3 read within 4%, but Pump 2 reads 148 l/s AND draws MORE power while moving LESS water, running 9 degrees C hotter - a walk of the line finds a length of pipe was replaced last year with a NARROWER one, raising the pressure every pump fights; the drift is bigger than the impeller, so you calibrate ALL three measured truths in. (4) RE-RUN THE DECISION ON THE TRUE TWIN - now green turns amber then RED: filling overnight on two pumps forces Pump 2 to run seven hours, which would cook its seal in week one; the R31,000 dream run blind buys a R210,000 pump failure and a town with no water on the ridge. The TRUE plan: run the two healthy pumps overnight, use Pump 2 only as a three-hour top-up, save a real R24,000/month, and fit the correct impeller next month for R12,000 to lift it back toward R30,000. (5) KEEP THE TWIN AND THE ASSET IN STEP - every repair updates the twin same-day on the same work order, by the fitter who did the work, and re-measure yearly; a digital twin is not built once, it is kept TRUE.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

That afternoon, you, Ayabulela and the station manager, Nolufefe, sit in the cramped control room with the laptop, the maintenance file, and a flask of coffee between you. Nolufefe puts it plainly: "We did not buy a crystal ball. We bought a mirror. And the mirror is showing us a station that was true two years ago and is not true today." Five moves:

  • Name the drift - the mirror shows a ghost: you, Ayabulela and the station manager, Nolufefe, sit with the laptop, the maintenance file and a flask of coffee, and Nolufefe puts it plainly: 'we did not buy a crystal ball, we bought a mirror - and the mirror is showing us a station that was true two years ago and is not true today.' Naming it changes how you treat it. The laptop holds what the industry calls a DIGITAL TWIN - a living digital copy of a real asset, built so you can test operating and intervention decisions on the copy before you risk them on the real thing - and it is only worth anything if it is FAITHFUL to the asset's true, present condition: its AS-MAINTAINED state, not its AS-BUILT state. Your twin was built once, from the original purchase papers (as-built), and then left to drift while the real station aged, broke, and got repaired with whatever was in the store. The model and reality quietly came apart and nobody re-connected them. A twin that has drifted from reality does not just fail to help - it does worse, because it gives you FALSE CONFIDENCE: it told you the night plan was green when the real station could not survive it. So you do not run the night plan tonight; first you make the twin true again.
  • Set the line before you test: you do not just assume the model is wrong because the fitter says so - you set a line to find out for certain, by the cheapest honest means, before you spend a cent or risk a pump. Before you touch a meter, Nolufefe makes you write down on the whiteboard what would tell you the model is faithful and what would tell you it has drifted: 'the twin is faithful if each pump, run on its own, reads within about 5% of the flow and power the model claims; it has drifted if any pump reads well outside that - and Pump 2 is the one to watch.' That is the yardstick, set in advance, so the meters - not anyone's opinion, not the fitter's hunch and not the consultant's reputation - return the verdict. This is the discipline that keeps the exercise honest: without a line set first, you would either rubber-stamp the model because it is an expert's work, or condemn it because the fitter distrusts laptops; with the line, the asset's own instruments decide, and you have agreed to abide by what they say before you read them.
  • Listen to the asset - the cheapest honest probe: you do not rebuild the model from memory and you do not strip a pump to look. The cheapest honest probe is already bolted to the station: its own flow and power meters. You run each pump on its own for twenty minutes and read the real flow and real power straight off the station's instruments - the asset is telling you its true state, if you bother to listen - small, cheap and reversible. Pump 1 and Pump 3 read within 4% of the model: good. Then you get to Pump 2 and the meters hand you a surprise worse than the fitter warned. You expected the wrong impeller to mean low flow, and it does - Pump 2 reads 148 l/s, not the modelled 180 - but the power reading is the jolt: Pump 2 is drawing MORE power than the model says while moving LESS water, and running 9 degrees C hotter than Pump 1. The smaller impeller was only half the story; the friction in the system has also gone up, and a walk of the discharge line finds why - a length of pipe was replaced last year with a slightly narrower one, raising the pressure every pump has to fight. So Pump 2 is not just weak; it is weak AND working harder than the model could ever know, which is exactly why it overheats, and the four-hour limit suddenly makes complete sense. That surprise forces a real course-correction: you had been ready to 'fix Pump 2's flow number' and move on, but the drift is bigger than one number - the impeller, the heat AND the narrower pipe all have to go into the twin, or it will still lie to you. This is the part the original build skipped: a twin must be CALIBRATED against the real asset's own data, not assumed from a brochure.
  • Re-run the decision - from false green to true red: with the real Pump 2 in the model - weaker, hotter, drawing more, four-hour limit, narrower pipe and all - you run the night plan again, and this time it comes back amber, then RED. To fill the reservoir overnight on two pumps, one of them has to be Pump 2, and Pump 2 cannot legally run seven hours straight; the 'safe' plan would have cooked the seal on Pump 2 inside the first night. Now work the real numbers honestly, because a good idea on a false model is just an expensive accident waiting. THE BLIND PLAN (trusting the drifted twin): predicted saving R31,000 a month, but Pump 2's seal fails in week one - repair and downtime about R210,000, plus an emergency call-out and a day pumping on the morning peak tariff anyway - a net loss, and a fitter who never trusts the laptop again. THE TRUE PLAN (on the calibrated twin): the now-faithful twin finds a plan it can actually stand behind - run the two HEALTHY pumps hard overnight, use Pump 2 only as a three-hour top-up well inside its limit, and accept a slightly smaller reservoir fill - a predicted saving of R24,000 a month, less than the dream number but REAL, and trustworthy because the twin now matches the metal; and you spend R12,000 fitting Pump 2 with the correct impeller next month, which lifts the future saving back toward R30,000 - a decision you could only see clearly because the twin now told the truth. Against the line on the whiteboard the verdict is unambiguous (Pump 2 read far outside the 5% band), so you change course: tonight you run only the plan the true twin approved - not guessing it is safer, but having measured the gap and re-run the decision on the corrected model.
  • Keep the twin and the asset in step: then Nolufefe closes the loop, which is the part that matters most: 'fixing the model today is not the win; the win is making sure it never lies to us again.' So the three of you put in a simple rule, and it has a name: keep the twin and the asset in step. Every time a pump is serviced, rebuilt, or has a part changed, the same work order that records the repair also UPDATES the twin - same day, by the fitter who did the work. This is configuration management for the model: the digital copy is only allowed to drift from reality for as long as it takes to write the work order, never eighteen months. And once a year you re-measure each pump's real flow and re-calibrate, because asset condition keeps changing even when nobody touches it. The concept that ties it all together is the one Ayabulela handed you across the walkway this morning, before you had a name for it: a model of an asset is worth less than nothing if it does not match the real, as-maintained asset, because it will tell you, with great confidence, to do things that are not safe. A digital twin is not a thing you build once; it is a thing you keep true. (A static drawing or data field drifting from reality is a kindred problem; asking the right question of the data you do have is its own problem.)

See where it lands. You close the laptop. Tonight you run the plan the true twin approved - the two healthy pumps overnight, Pump 2 as a three-hour top-up, a real R24,000 a month instead of a R31,000 dream that hid a R210,000 failure - and on Monday you write the rule that keeps the model true. A model of an asset is worth less than nothing if it does not match the real, as-maintained asset, because it will tell you, with great confidence, to do things that are not safe. A digital twin is not a thing you build once; it is a thing you keep TRUE - so set a tolerance line in advance, read the asset's own instruments to find the drift, calibrate the model against measured reality not a brochure, re-run the decision on the true twin, and keep model and metal in step with every repair. The most dangerous model is not the one that admits it is old; it is the confident one quietly describing a station that no longer exists.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You've seen the clean version: a model that was obviously old, and an honest fitter who told you so. The real world is rarely that kind.

See the cafe whose spreadsheet she just updated - and still can't trust

Ntombizodwa runs a small cafe. She prices every item off a costing spreadsheet she built last year - what each cup of coffee costs her in beans, milk, gas, cups - so she knows what to charge and still make a profit. Last week she did something sensible: she opened the sheet and updated the milk price and the rent, because she knew those had gone up. The sheet now says her flat white still makes her R9 profit a cup. She is about to run a "two-for-one" winter special on the strength of that R9. The sheet looks current - she just edited it.

Something here should feel familiar. Look at the two things she changed. She updated milk and rent because she already knew they had moved - the milk man told her, the landlord sent a letter - just like the consultant typed in what he knew two years ago. But the sheet has dozens of other cells: the price of beans, the cost of gas, the cups, the wage she pays her helper, how many cups she actually gets from a bag. Those have not been checked against the real till and the real invoices in over a year. So where is the "Pump 2" of this cafe? At the station, the danger was not the pump everyone watched - it was the part the model showed as healthy when it wasn't. Here it is probably the bean cost or the cups-per-bag figure: gone up quietly, never re-checked - and it is more dangerous because she just edited the sheet, since updating milk and rent makes the whole sheet feel fresh, which is exactly the false confidence the drifted twin gave you. If beans actually cost more than the sheet thinks, her real profit might be R4 a cup, not R9 - and a two-for-one special on a R4 margin loses money on every single sale, all winter. The fix is the same three honest moves you made at the station: find the gaps (open last year's invoices next to the sheet), measure don't guess (read the real bean price and real cups-per-bag off the actual deliveries - the cafe is telling her its true state through its own till and invoices), and re-run the decision (does two-for-one still work at the true margin?) - plus the same standing rule: when a supplier price changes, the sheet gets updated that week, by her, not once a year. The deep lesson: a part-updated model is more dangerous than an obviously old one, because fixing the few cells you know about makes you trust all the cells you haven't checked. Drift hides best behind a fresh-looking edit. Whether it is a R380,000 pump twin or a kitchen-table spreadsheet, the question is the same: which numbers in here is the real world quietly disagreeing with - and when did I last go and look?

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The station runs pumps; you almost certainly do not. But the trap is the same everywhere: you make decisions using a model, a record, or a picture of something - and the picture has quietly drifted away from the real thing it is supposed to describe, so it tells you, confidently, to do the wrong thing. Find it where you are:

  1. Name your "twin". What model, spreadsheet, drawing, record or system do you rely on to make decisions about something real - a machine, a building, a budget, a stock list, a route? Write it down plainly.
  2. Name the metal, and your Ayabulela. Name the real thing the model is supposed to describe. When was it last checked against the real thing? Who is the person who actually touches it and knows what the record does not?
  3. Find one drift. One way the real thing has changed since the model was last updated - a repair, a swap, a near-enough spare, a price that moved, a person who left. Where is the model still showing the old, dead truth?
  4. Price the false confidence, and sketch the keep-it-true rule. Imagine you trusted the model for one real decision and it was wrong because of that drift - put a rough number on it, the way the team put R210,000 on a cooked seal. Then write one simple rule: when X changes in the real world, who updates the model, and within what time?
R

Run the smallest test this week: pick the one number your model most depends on, decide in advance how close to reality counts as "faithful", then go and measure that same thing on the real asset and write down by how much the two differ. The gap, not your opinion, is the verdict - and if it's outside your line, your model has been quietly lying to you. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build your model-vs-metal drift audit and your keep-it-true rule together.)

In your world the "pump", the "wrong impeller" and the "hand-written note" will look completely different. Your job is not to copy the pump-station fix; it is to find the same drift between model and reality in your own world, measure the real thing, and build the small habit that keeps your model honest.
