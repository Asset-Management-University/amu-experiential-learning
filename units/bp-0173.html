Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Busisiwe, a careful person saving for your family's future. Your friend Zandile, someone you have known and trusted for years from your community group, pulls you aside after a meeting, her eyes shining with excitement.

"Busisiwe, I have to tell you about this amazing investment," she says. "It is an exclusive, private group. You are guaranteed to make 20% on your money - every single month. I put in R10,000 just six weeks ago, and I have already been paid out R4,000. Look!" She shows you the payment notifications. It looks real. "The founder is a genius," she continues, "but he is closing the group to new members at the end of the week. You have to act fast. Think what you could do - school uniforms, fix the car, everything."

Your heart beats faster; the money would change everything. You trust Zandile completely, and you can see how happy and certain she is. But a part of your mind feels a quiet alarm bell ringing - it sounds too good, too easy. You feel the pull of hope, the fear of missing this one great chance, and the awkwardness of questioning your excited friend.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instincts are to believe what you see and who you trust - but that is exactly how these traps are set. "Zandile is my trusted friend - she would never hurt me." This is the most painful part: the scam is designed to use your trust against you. Zandile is not a liar; she is almost certainly a victim who believes the dream is real, and the scammers are using her genuine excitement to find their next target - you. Her trust is the tool, not the guarantee. "But I saw the payouts - she is really getting money." Yes, and that is the hook: at the start, scammers pay 'profits' to early investors out of the deposits of new ones, creating happy, convincing messengers who recruit for free; the payouts are real, the profit is not - it is just other people's savings being passed around until the whole thing collapses. "I will just put in a small amount, say R2,000, to test it." That is the bait: they let you win on the small amount, may even let you withdraw it, so you feel safe and put in your real savings - and once the big money is in, it is gone for good.

So before you read on: if you cannot trust your friend, the payouts, or even a small test - what CAN you trust?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough is realising this is not an investment offer; it is a story. A scam does not pick your pocket - it sells you a dream so that you hand over your savings with a smile. To see through the story, you need one simple, unbreakable rule. The Iron Rule of Money: risk and reward are always tied together - a high return always carries high risk, and a genuinely 'guaranteed' return always carries low risk and therefore a low return. So a "guaranteed high return" is a contradiction: it cannot exist in the real world, and it is the single biggest red flag of a scam. With that rule in hand, five moves protect you:

  • See the shape - it sells a dream: the breakthrough is realising this is not really an investment offer - it is a STORY, carefully written to move you. A scam does not pick your pocket; it sells you a dream so that you hand over your savings with a smile. Where an impersonation scam attacks with fear, this one attacks with HOPE and the fear of missing out: financial freedom, the uniforms, the car, a way out - dangled in front of exactly the things you long for, and delivered by someone you trust so the warning bells are muffled. Once you see the shape - a dream, plus urgency, plus a trusted messenger - you can name it for what it is. And there is no shame in being shown it: these schemes are engineered by professionals to fool good, careful, intelligent people, often through friends who are themselves victims. The bigger and more certain the promise sounds, the more certain you can be it is a lie.
  • Hold the iron rule of money: this is the single rule that cuts through every version of the scam: risk and reward are ALWAYS tied together. A high return always comes with high risk; a genuinely guaranteed return always comes with low risk, and therefore a low return. Those are not opinions, they are how money works everywhere in the real world. So put the offer against the rule: 'guaranteed' AND 'high' AND 'every single month' cannot all be true at once - a guaranteed high return is a contradiction, which means it is a lie, full stop. '20% a month, guaranteed' is not a wonderful opportunity you nearly missed; it is the loudest possible alarm that you are facing a scam. Hold this rule like a handrail, and no story, however dazzling or however trusted the teller, can pull you over the edge.
  • Refuse the pressure: every one of these scams runs on urgency, because thinking is its enemy: 'the group closes on Friday', 'only a few spots left', 'act now', 'keep it secret, do not tell anyone'. Those are not features of a great opportunity - they are tools to switch off your slow, careful judgement before you can ask a hard question or get an outside opinion. So invert the pressure: treat it as a confession. A genuine, legitimate investment will still be there next week; it will welcome your questions, encourage you to take advice, and never punish you for thinking it over. If you are being rushed, pushed, flattered or sworn to secrecy, that pressure itself is one of the clearest red flags there is - and the right response to 'you must decide now' is always a calm 'then the answer is no'.
  • Verify independently, before a single rand: never rely on their glossy materials, their testimonials, or their early payouts - verify on your own, before you part with any money. Three checks catch almost everything. First, is it REGISTERED and licensed with the proper financial regulator in your country? Find that regulator independently (not through a link or number the scheme gives you) and check. Second, can you explain, simply and honestly, HOW the money is actually made - 'they lend at interest', 'they buy and sell X' - in a way that makes real-world sense? 'It is a secret formula' or 'special trading' is not an explanation, and if you cannot explain where the returns come from, you must not invest. Third, can you get your money OUT easily - test with a small withdrawal, because 'you cannot withdraw yet, just reinvest' is the classic trap door that closes once the big money is in. If any check fails, walk away.
  • Beware the recruit-others model, and protect people: look hard at where the money actually comes from: if your returns depend on signing up new people - your friends, your family, your church - then it is a pyramid or a Ponzi scheme, and the only money coming in is the deposits of new victims being passed to earlier ones. By the iron law of arithmetic it MUST run out of new people and collapse, and the last and largest group in - often the very people you love and persuaded - lose everything. So recruiting is not a bonus, it is the proof and the harm: bringing others in spreads the damage to exactly the people you would most want to protect. Two final, unbreakable rules guard the rest: never invest money you cannot afford to lose, and never invest simply because a trusted person told you to - their trust is sincere, but it is not a substitute for your own independent check.

And now do the arithmetic, because the scheme's own numbers do not agree with each other. The promise was 20% a month. On R10,000, that is R2,000 in a month, so six weeks should have paid Zandile somewhere near R3,000. She was paid R4,000 - that is 40%, two months' worth of the promised return, in six weeks. Hold that up beside the promise and it simply does not fit. This is a red flag in its own right, and a useful one, because it needs no regulator and no expert: when what a scheme PAYS does not match what it PROMISES, the numbers are not coming from a real investment that earns a real return. They are being set by someone, to look impressive. Money that behaves like that is being moved, not made - and the people it is being moved from are the newest members. You do not need to know where the money comes from to know that it does not add up; you only need to check.

See the rules protect Busisiwe. She feels the pull, but she holds the iron rule: '20% a month, guaranteed' is impossible, so however happy Zandile is, the offer is a lie. She refuses the Friday deadline - a real opportunity would survive her asking questions - and she does her own homework: the scheme is not registered with the financial regulator, no one can explain in plain words where the money actually comes from, and 'you reinvest, you cannot withdraw yet' is the trap door. She sees that the 'returns' depend entirely on bringing in new people, which means the money is just new victims' savings. She thanks Zandile gently, keeps her R50,000 savings, and - kindly - shares what she has learned, because Zandile is a victim too. Months later the group collapses; Busisiwe's family is safe. A scam sells you a dream that is urgent, exclusive and guaranteed - a trap that uses your hope against you. The moment you hear "guaranteed high returns", you know it is a lie, because risk and reward are always tied together. So slow down, hold the Iron Rule, treat pressure and secrecy as confessions, verify with the proper regulator and the test of whether you can actually withdraw, and remember that if your returns depend on recruiting others, it is a Ponzi that will collapse and harm the people you love. Never invest what you cannot afford to lose, and never on someone else's say-so.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know this one in your gut - picture a man in the street holding up a hundred-rand note.

See the trick stripped of its fancy suit

He says: "Give me R100, and I will give you R200 back tomorrow. Guaranteed." You would laugh and walk away - you know, instantly, that it is a trick. Why? Because it breaks the basic rules of how money works: nobody can guarantee to double your money overnight, because if they truly could, they would never need yours.

The investment Zandile is offering is the exact same trick. It is just wearing a fancy suit, has a slick website, an excited friend, and notifications that look real - but underneath, it is still just a stranger promising to magically multiply your money. Strip away the suit and the trust, and the gut feeling you would have on the street is the right one here too.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You can protect your savings and your future. Before you ever hand over money for an investment, stop and apply these rules:

  1. Check it against the Iron Rule. Does it promise high AND guaranteed returns? If yes, it is a scam - the two cannot both be true.
  2. Treat pressure as a warning sign. If you are told you must act NOW, or to keep it secret, the answer should be NO.
  3. Verify, do not trust. Check with the proper financial regulator in your country - found independently, not via a link they gave you - whether the scheme is registered and licensed, and test whether you can actually withdraw your money.
  4. Explain it to yourself. If you cannot say simply where the profit comes from ("they lend at interest", "they buy and sell X"), do not invest - "a secret formula" is not an explanation. And never invest money you cannot afford to lose, or just because a friend told you to.
