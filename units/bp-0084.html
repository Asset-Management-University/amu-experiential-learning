Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Karabo, and you run a brilliant business fixing essential farm equipment - the local farmers depend on you to keep their tractors and water pumps running through planting and harvest. You're careful: you've worked out fair prices in rand, you know your costs, you've made a plan, and you keep a small, healthy profit that lets you buy better tools and hold a good stock of parts.

But this month something is wrong. You needed a specific gearbox imported from overseas. The supplier's price hadn't changed - yet when you paid, it cost you 15% more in rand than last month. Suddenly your profit on that job has vanished; in fact you've made a small loss. You did nothing wrong: your effort didn't change, your supplier wasn't greedy, your customer paid the agreed price. But a number you don't control - the exchange rate - moved, and your careful plan broke. Your income is fixed in rand, while a huge, essential part of your costs floats on an outside number.

My own prices and effort didn't change, yet an outside number I can't control just rewrote my whole plan - how do I run a steady business, and keep my promises to the farmers, when I'm hostage to a number I can't predict?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to react. Just raise your prices? Pass the whole cost to the farmers - but you'd be changing prices every week chasing a number that jumps about, risking pricing yourself out of the market and punishing the very people you're here to serve. Outsmart the number? "I'll buy a huge stock of parts now, before the rate gets worse!" - but that's a gamble; the number could move the other way tomorrow, leaving you with overpriced parts and all your cash tied up. That isn't business, it's betting. Quietly absorb it? The commonest trap: to make this month's figures work you skip the maintenance on your own tools, put off the worn safety goggles, use a cheaper unreliable courier - this period looks fine on paper, but you've paid for today's squeeze by making your future fragile. You've started to cut the floor from under your own mission.

So before you read on: the outside number will keep moving both ways and you can't control it - what CAN you control, and what must you never let the squeeze make you cut?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The skill is to stop reacting to the number and start governing your exposure to it. Your plan is not a fact; it's a bet on an outside number - and the goal is not to guess which way it goes, but to reduce how much it can hurt you. Five moves:

  • See the trap - your plan is a bet on an outside number: your income is fixed in your own unit (rand), but a large share of your essential costs floats on a price you don't control - a foreign currency, an interest rate, a global commodity, one dominant supplier. So every time that outside number moves, your careful plan is silently rewritten, usually for the worse, even though nothing about your own operation changed. The plan was never a fact; it was always a bet.
  • Measure the exposure - it's invisible until you do: you can't manage a risk you can't see, so put a real number on it. For an average month, of every R100 of essential costs (parts, fuel, rent, wages), how much rides on the outside number? Say R60 of it goes on imported parts - now you know 60% of your essential costs are set by a price you don't control. The vague dread becomes a sized, workable figure.
  • Lock a one-off, buffer a repeat: treat the two kinds of cost differently. A known ONE-OFF (rebuilding a fleet of five tractors, where you know the big parts) you can LOCK at a fixed price now - a small fixed fee buys certainty for that purchase. A REPEATING everyday cost (the small parts you'll need, you-don't-know-which) you can't lock, so you keep a BUFFER - a small cash reserve set aside for this, or a little extra stock - a shock absorber that lets a bad month pass without breaking you.
  • Protect your floor - decide in calm and write why: before the next squeeze, write your 'non-negotiables' - the things you must do to serve safely and well for the long term, each with its WHY: the weekly safety check (a failure could be catastrophic); the reliable courier (a lost part risks a farmer's harvest); the annual tool service (good upkeep prevents costly breakdowns). This list, written by your calm self, is a promise to your future panicked self - so you never pay for a price swing by quietly cutting the very things that make your service trustworthy.
  • Buy certainty on purpose - the honest trade-off: locking a price or holding a buffer costs real money, and you may miss a gain if the number moves your way. But you're not guessing the future - you're deliberately paying a small, KNOWN price to remove a large, UNKNOWN loss that could ruin the mission. Choose that certainty on purpose, for the parts you can't afford to have rewritten - not by accident, and never by gambling on which way the number goes.

See it steady Karabo. He measures and finds 60% of his essential costs ride on the outside number - the dread becomes a figure he can work with. He locks the price on the known big job (the five-tractor rebuild) and keeps a small buffer of cash and key stock for the everyday parts he can't predict. In calm, he writes his non-negotiables - the safety check, the reliable courier, the tool service - each with its why, a promise his panicked future-self can't quietly break. And he chooses, deliberately, to pay a little for that certainty where a surprise would be ruinous. Your plan is a bet on an outside number, not a fact; the exposure is invisible until you measure it - so lock a one-off, buffer a repeat, decide in calm what must be protected and write why, and buy certainty on purpose, never by guessing which way the number moves.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about foreign parts - any uncontrolled outside price works the same way. Watch a floating loan.

See the same move with a loan whose interest rate floats

You took a loan for your workshop, and the interest rate floats on the central bank's rate - an outside number.

• Measure the exposure: how much does your monthly repayment rise if the rate goes up by 1%? That figure shows how exposed you are.
• Lock or buffer: "locking" could mean asking the bank to switch to a fixed-rate loan - slightly higher today, but it won't change (that's the cost of certainty); "buffering" means keeping a separate reserve to cover three months of higher payments if rates rise.
• Protect the floor: your floor is making the repayment without cutting salaries or failing suppliers - so you decide, in calm, to fund any increase from the buffer, never from the core of your operation.

Same skill, different outside number: measure it, lock or buffer it, and protect the floor.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own business, project, or household. Is there an essential, repeating cost set by a price you don't control - a key material priced on a world market, a loan with a floating rate, rent tied to an index, fuel for your vehicles? This is not a personal failing; it's a normal part of how the world works - the skill is to govern it, not be its victim.

  1. See it as a bet, and measure it. See your plan as a bet on that outside number, then measure the share of your essential costs it controls - make the risk visible.
  2. Separate, then protect the floor. Can you LOCK the big one-off commitments? How will you BUFFER the small repeating ones? And decide in calm the non-negotiables you must protect, each with its why - your promise to the people who depend on you.
  3. Choose where to buy certainty. Where does it make sense to pay a small, known price to remove the risk of a ruinous surprise?

The unfairness you felt was real - an outside number reached into a plan you had made honestly and rewrote it, through no fault of yours, and almost every small business is secretly hostage to some such number. But being hostage and being helpless are not the same thing. You cannot stop the ground from shaking; you can decide, in the calm before it does, what you will hold onto no matter what - the safety, the upkeep, the care that lets you keep your word to the people who rely on you. This was never about predicting the future. It's about making sure your mission can survive it, whatever the outside number does.
