Right and owned

T1: The Ache

You wrote the standard carefully, and it is right. Technically sound, clear, correct - you checked it against the facts and it holds. And on the floor, it is quietly ignored. People nod, and then do what they always did.

Or the other time: you opened it up to everyone in the name of fairness, and what came back was a vague, watered-down compromise that pleased the loudest voice in the room and protects no one. Either the standard is right but not followed, or it is owned but not sound - never both - and you cannot see why a rule so obviously sensible will not take hold.

My standards are either correct but ignored, or accepted but toothless - how do I set a standard so that it is both technically right and genuinely followed by the people it governs?

Before you read on, decide. Two standards, both yours. The isolation procedure is technically excellent — written by the two people who best understand the risk — and on the floor it is quietly worked around, because following it exactly means a second person walking to the far switchroom and back for every isolation, and on night shift there is no second person. The lifting standard went the other way: a workshop with everybody in the room, and it came out as a compromise shaped by whoever argued longest, so weakened that it now permits essentially what people were already doing. Right but ignored, or owned but toothless. Write down how you would set a standard that is both — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the two ways a standard fails

• “Enforce the isolation procedure properly.” Enforcement against a rule that cannot be followed on night shift produces either a stopped job or a falsified record. The workaround exists because the standard did not know about the walk.

• “Write the lifting standard myself and issue it.” Gets you soundness and reproduces the isolation problem — correct on the page, resented on the floor, and worked around within a month.

• “Consult more widely on both.” More participation of the kind that produced the toothless one. Without a floor beneath the discussion, wider consultation is a larger negotiation about what is comfortable.

• “Discipline the people working around it.” They know something about the work that your standard did not, and punishing them for it removes your only source of that information.

• “Meet in the middle — moderately expert, moderately consulted.” A midpoint on a single dial, which gives you something slightly unsound and slightly resented. As the next paragraph shows, the answer is not a midpoint at all.

• Un-learn the idea that a technically correct standard is enough. A standard that is ignored on the floor has not succeeded, however sound the page is. There are two failure modes and a good process guards both: it can be technically wrong — written by people who do not know the work, so it is unworkable or blind to reality — or technically right and illegitimate, imposed without the voice of those bound by it, so it is resented, worked around and dead. You have produced one of each. Both soundness and legitimacy must be earned, and they pull in different directions, which is why this takes judgement. The poles are real: a few experts writing it buys authority and speed at the cost of ownership; involving the people who live with it buys ownership and practical realism — they know about the walk to the far switchroom — at the cost of time and a drift toward the comfortable. And your lifting standard shows the specific corruption of the second: a compromise shaped by the loudest voice, which is participation without a floor. The resolution is not a midpoint but a division of authority — expertise governs what must be true, and the people who do the work govern how it lives in the work.

A standard succeeds when its creation process balances expert authority on what must be true with participatory ownership of how it will live in the work. Let us learn to set it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you choose a standard-setting process that fits the context - seeing that a standard must be both right and accepted, weighing the expert-led and participatory poles, diagnosing what this particular standard most needs, and guarding the two ways the process can be corrupted.

1. A standard succeeds only when it is both right and accepted

The first thing to un-learn is that a technically correct standard is ENOUGH. It is not. A STANDARD THAT IS IGNORED ON THE FLOOR HAS NOT SUCCEEDED, however sound it is on the page - a rule nobody follows is worthless, however correct. A standard has TWO FAILURE MODES, and a good setting process guards against both. It can be technically WRONG - written by people who do not know the work, so it is unsafe, unworkable, or blind to reality. Or it can be technically right but ILLEGITIMATE - imposed on the people who must follow it without their voice, so it is resented, worked around, and dead on the page. Your job is to earn BOTH soundness and legitimacy - and those two pull in different directions, which is why this takes judgement.

2. The two poles - expert-led and participatory

The setting process runs between two poles. An EXPERT-LED process - a few who know the subject write the standard - buys TECHNICAL AUTHORITY AND SPEED: the people who understand the science and the risk decide, quickly, and the standard is sound. Its cost is LEGITIMACY: the people bound by it had no say and do not own it, so they may not keep it. A PARTICIPATORY process - those affected help shape the standard - buys LEGITIMACY AND BUY-IN, and surfaces REAL-WORLD KNOWLEDGE THE EXPERTS LACK (the people who do the work know why the last standard failed). Its costs are that it is slower, and, done badly, it risks being CAPTURED by the loudest interest or watered down to a uselessly vague compromise. The trade-off: TECHNICAL AUTHORITY AND SPEED VERSUS LEGITIMACY AND BUY-IN.

3. Diagnose what the standard needs

You choose by reading THREE things. THE NATURE OF THE STANDARD: a safety-critical or physical-fact standard - the chlorine limit, the pressure rating, the curing time - is NOT UP FOR A VOTE; it is determined by the science and needs expert authority. A standard about how people WORK, one that must be adopted daily, NEEDS THEIR VOICE, or it will simply be ignored. How much LEGITIMACY AND BUY-IN it needs to actually work: a standard that lives or dies on daily adoption needs ownership far more than a back-office rule does. And WHERE THE REAL-WORLD KNOWLEDGE SITS: if the people who do the work hold knowledge the experts lack - and they usually do - a process that shuts them out will write a standard that fails on contact with reality.

4. The mature version is a blend

The seasoned answer is rarely all one pole. It is a BLEND: be EXPERT AND FIRM on the few things that must be technically true - the non-negotiable safety facts and physical limits - and PARTICIPATORY on how the standard is shaped to live in the real work. FIRM ON THE WHAT MUST BE TRUE; OPEN ON HOW IT LIVES. This gives you a standard that is both sound where soundness is non-negotiable and owned where ownership is what makes it real.

5. Guard the twin dangers - tokenism and capture

Whichever process you choose, guard the two ways it gets corrupted. TOKENISM: asking for input you will ignore, then doing what you had already decided - this destroys legitimacy exactly as a sham consultation does, and it is worse than never asking. A STANDARD THAT GOVERNS A PERSON'S WORK IS A CLAIM ON THEIR TIME AND THEIR SKILL. A GENUINE VOICE IN ITS CREATION IS A MARK OF RESPECT FOR THAT SKILL. A TOKENISTIC CONSULTATION IS THE OPPOSITE - IT IS A PERFORMANCE OF LISTENING THAT DISRESPECTS THE PERSON BY ASSUMING THEY WILL NOT SEE THE DIFFERENCE. THEY ALWAYS SEE THE DIFFERENCE. And CAPTURE: a powerful interest bending the standard to suit itself while calling it consensus. There is one more respect, running the other way. HONESTY IS ALSO A FORM OF RESPECT. FOR A STANDARD BASED ON A PHYSICAL OR SAFETY FACT, THE MOST RESPECTFUL ACT IS TO STATE PLAINLY THAT IT IS NOT UP FOR A VOTE, AND EXPLAIN WHY. A FAKE VOTE IS AS DISRESPECTFUL AS A FAKE CONSULTATION. Legitimacy is not unanimity; it is that the process was fair and that people's voice genuinely counted where it could.

T4: Your Turn

Onalenna must establish two new standards at Corathyl. Your job is to choose a setting process for each - proving the choice is diagnosis, not one favourite method.

Standard one - the concrete curing time. The minimum time a specific concrete repair compound must cure before a load can be placed on it. Get it wrong and the repair fails under load.

Standard two - the near-miss reporting procedure. A new procedure for reporting near-miss safety incidents, meant to increase reporting from front-line teams who currently stay silent about them.

  1. Choose and justify. For each standard, choose a setting process - expert-led, participatory, or a blend - and justify it by diagnosing what that standard needs to succeed.
  2. Firm and open. For each, name what parts are non-negotiable and expert-led, and what parts are open to participation.
  3. Spot the wrong move. Say where a fake consultation would be most damaging, and where a vote would be an irresponsible choice.
  4. Explain the difference. Write the single sentence you would use to explain to your team why the two standards needed two different creation processes.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the choices: the curing time is expert-led - it is a physical fact set by the material and the load, not by opinion; the experts determine it and it is fixed. The near-miss procedure is participatory - it only works if front-line teams actually use it, and they currently stay silent, so their voice in shaping it (and their trust that reporting is safe) is the entire point; a procedure they help write is one they will use.

Second, firm and open: on the curing time, almost everything is expert-firm - the minimum time itself is non-negotiable; participation is limited to how it is communicated and checked on site. On the near-miss procedure, the non-negotiable is that near-misses are reported and that reporting is never punished; open to participation is nearly everything else - how, to whom, in what form, so the teams design something they will actually use.

Third, the wrong moves: a fake consultation would be most damaging on the near-miss procedure - front-line teams already distrust the system, and staging a fake ask would confirm that their voice is theatre and kill reporting for good. A vote would be irresponsible on the curing time - you cannot put a load-bearing safety fact to a show of hands; the material does not care how people voted.

Fourth, the one-sentence explanation - something like: "The curing time is a physical safety fact, so the experts set it and it is fixed; the reporting procedure only works if you actually use it, so you help build it - which is why one is decided for you and the other is decided with you."

T5: The Guardrails

Set the standard so it is both right and owned - expert on what must be true, participatory on how it lives - and never ask for a voice you will not heed.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Writing every standard alone from the office. A correct standard nobody owns is quietly ignored. Bring in the people who must follow it wherever adoption is what makes it real.

• Putting a safety fact to a vote. Some standards are not democratic. A load-bearing or safety limit is set by the science; a show of hands does not change the material. Say so honestly - it is more respectful than a fake vote.

• Tokenistic consultation. Asking for input you will ignore is worse than never asking - it destroys legitimacy and people see through it instantly. Only offer a voice you will genuinely heed.

• Capture by the loudest. Participation can be bent by a powerful interest calling its own preference "consensus". Guard the standard's purpose against being watered down to suit whoever shouts most.

• A vague compromise that protects no one. A standard softened until everyone agrees can end up meaning nothing. Legitimacy is fairness of process, not a mush everyone tolerates.

• Confusing legitimacy with unanimity. People do not need to get exactly what they wanted; they need the process to have been fair and their voice to have genuinely counted. Aim for fair, not unanimous.

• Forgetting a standard nobody follows is worthless. Correctness on the page is not success. The test is whether the standard actually lives in the work.
