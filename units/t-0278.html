Step 1 of 5

Everyone signed the same specification and every one of them is picturing a different system - how do I get the disagreement out of the words and onto a page before anything is built?

T1: The Ache

The clinic at Lekgotla serves eleven villages. It has two consulting rooms, a passage, and a vaccine fridge that holds about R90,000 of stock and the immunisation programme for four thousand children.

You are the engineer on a solar-and-battery installation that is meant to keep it running. It is funded, specified, signed by everyone, and due on site in three weeks. Four months of meetings, one specification document, and genuine goodwill from all three parties.

This morning, over tea, three things were said in the space of about a minute.

The installer said the grid connection was outside the scope, since they were supplying solar and batteries. The funder's representative said of course the grid was in scope - they were funding a clinic that keeps working. And Sister Mahlaba, who runs the clinic, said that the grid is off most of every second day in any case, so she had assumed the system simply ignored it.

Nobody was wrong. Every one of them can point to the sentence in the specification that supports what they said, and every one of them has been reading it that way for four months. The document does not contradict any of them, which is precisely the problem: it is compatible with three different systems, and in three weeks somebody is going to build one of them.

Everyone agrees, in words, and each of us is picturing a different thing. How do I get the disagreement out of the sentences and into the open, before it is built in steel and copper?

Before you read on, decide what you would do this morning. Three weeks, one signed specification, three people who each believe the matter is settled. Write down what you would actually do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four responses this moment usually gets, and what each one settles

• "Rewrite the specification more precisely." The right instinct, and it is where most effort goes. But the sentence that caused this already looked precise to three careful readers; a longer document written in the same medium can hide the next disagreement just as comfortably as this one hid this.

• "Get everyone in a room and talk it through." Necessary, and not sufficient on its own - because talking is the medium that produced four months of agreement. Something in the room has to make each person's picture visible, or the meeting ends with everyone satisfied and still divided.

• "Escalate to whoever owns the budget and let them rule." Fast, and it settles the grid question by authority rather than by understanding - which works until the next ambiguity, and the next, none of which anybody has spotted yet.

• "Just build it and adjust on site." This is the honest default when time is short, and it is what will happen by three weeks from now if nothing else does. Site changes are the most expensive way to discover a design decision.

• Notice what none of them does. Each of these settles the one disagreement that happened to surface over tea. What is needed is something that makes every person's picture visible at once - so the disagreements find themselves, including the several nobody has stumbled on yet, and including the ones that live in what happens at two in the morning rather than in what the parts are.

T2: The Tool in Action

Draw the boundary first, because that is where the quiet disagreement lives. Then a box for every part and an arrow for every real flow, labelled with what flows. Then, for the situation you care about most, draw what happens in what order - with every branch ending somewhere. Watch it run at Lekgotla.

Step 1 - Draw the boundary first: what is inside this thing, and what is merely near it

Nthabiseng Rakoma draws a box on the whiteboard and asks a question nobody has asked out loud in four months: is the grid connection inside our system or outside it? The installer says outside, obviously - they are supplying solar and batteries. The funder says inside, obviously - they are paying for a clinic that keeps working. The clinic manager, Sister Mahlaba, says quietly that the grid is off for most of every second day anyway. Four months of agreement, and the first line drawn on the board takes ten minutes to settle.

Step 2 - Put every part in a box and draw what actually flows between them

Boxes: solar array, charge controller, battery bank, inverter, distribution board, vaccine fridge, lights, the two consulting rooms, the grid. Then the arrows, and the room slows right down. Does the fridge sit on its own circuit from the distribution board, or share with the consulting rooms? Nobody knows. Is the grid an input to the charge controller or a separate feed into the board? The installer had assumed one, the funder's specification implies the other. Neither reading contradicts a single written word.

Step 3 - Draw what happens in what order, especially when things go wrong

The situation: it is two in the morning, the grid has been down since noon, it was overcast all day, and the battery reaches 20 per cent. Nthabiseng draws it step by step - read battery level, is it under 20 per cent, if yes then what? And the room stops. Does the system shed the lights or the fridge? The specification says the system 'shall prioritise critical loads'. Everyone had read that and agreed. Nobody had ever decided which loads were critical, and the drawing will not let the question be avoided, because the arrow has to go somewhere.

Step 4 - Walk each drawing through with the people who will live with it

Walking the two-in-the-morning sequence with Sister Mahlaba, she stops at the arrow labelled shed lights and says: the passage light stays on, always - a person walking to the toilet at night has fallen in that passage before. That requirement exists in no document. Four months of specification and consultation had not surfaced it, and the drawing found it in ninety seconds, because pointing at an arrow and asking 'is this right?' is a question anybody can answer.

Step 5 - Keep the drawings true, or stop calling them the design

The installer changes the inverter model in week three, which changes what the charge controller has to talk to. Nthabiseng's rule, agreed in that first meeting: any change to a box or an arrow comes back to the drawing before it goes to site, and the drawing carries a date and a version in the corner. It takes four minutes each time. Twice in the build it catches something - once a circuit that would have put the fridge back on the consulting-room breaker, which is the exact arrow the room had argued about in Step 2.

Look at what the drawing did that four months of documents and meetings did not. Three findings in one morning: the boundary nobody had agreed, a circuit nobody had decided, and - from a sequence that would not let a fork be left open - the discovery that "shall prioritise critical loads" had been signed by three parties, none of whom had ever said which loads were critical. And then the fourth, which no engineer could have produced: Sister Mahlaba stopping at an arrow and saying the passage light stays on, always, because someone has fallen there in the dark. That requirement was in no document. It took ninety seconds, and it took a picture - because a picture asks a question anybody can answer. A specification asks a question nobody hears themselves answering.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the first one finds more than people expect.

  • 1. Draw the boundary. What is inside, and what you depend on but do not control.
  • 2. A box per part, an arrow per flow, each arrow labelled with what actually flows.
  • 3. Draw the order of what happens for the situation you care about most - every branch ending somewhere.
  • 4. Walk it with the people who will live with it, pointing as you go, hunting for the frown.
  • 5. Say who keeps it true, and date every version - or stop calling it the design.

What you have drawn are the two views that carry almost all the value in system modelling - the discipline behind modelling languages such as SysML. The first is a structure view: the parts as blocks, the connections between them, and the line around what is yours, which the language calls the system boundary. The second is a behaviour view: the same system described as what happens in what order, with the forks drawn as decision nodes that must each lead somewhere. The reason two views are needed rather than one is the thing you just watched happen: the structure view found the argument about the circuit, and it could never have found the two-in-the-morning question, because that disagreement was not about what the parts were but about what the system does when it has to choose.

It fits anything with parts that interact and where more than one party has to agree what is being built - equipment, software, buildings, service processes, organisational structures. Its honest limits are two. A model is not a design: it says what must be true, never how to build it. And it is only worth the effort where ambiguity is expensive - three weeks from a build with three parties, or a system that must be safe. A drawing of something obvious, made because a process demanded a drawing, is a document nobody reads and nobody keeps true.

T4: Your Turn

Take something you are about to build, change or hand over where more than one person has to agree what it is. Draw the boundary first and ask someone else where they would have drawn it. Then boxes and labelled arrows. Then pick the one situation that worries you - the failure, the busy hour, the handover, the person who is off sick - and draw what happens in what order until every fork ends somewhere.

Two disciplines decide whether this finds anything. First, draw it in front of the people who disagree, not alone at your desk - a model made in private and then presented is a document, and it will be nodded at exactly like the specification was. The whole mechanism is that a picture makes each person's private version visible, and that only happens if they are in the room while the arrows go on. Second, hunt the fork nobody has decided, and refuse to let it stay open. Prose is comfortable with "the system shall prioritise critical loads" because a sentence can hold three meanings at once; an arrow cannot leave the page unattached. When you find a fork with no agreed answer, you have found the thing that would otherwise have been decided on site by whoever was holding the screwdriver.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your models useful.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Boxes are easy; arrows are the design. People agree readily about what the parts are. What flows between them, and in which direction, is where the disagreements sit - so label every arrow with what actually travels along it.

• Draw the boundary before the parts. The most expensive misunderstandings are about what is in scope, and they are invisible in prose because everyone reads the same sentence as confirming their own line.

• Every branch must end somewhere. The value of drawing behaviour is that it will not let an undecided fork stay undecided. If you find yourself leaving one open, you have found the work.

• A model made alone is a document. Its power is in being drawn in front of the people who will live with it. Presented afterwards, it gets the same nod the specification got.

• A drawing that has gone stale is worse than no drawing. People trust it and act on it. Name who keeps it current and date every version, or say plainly that it is a record of one conversation.

• Do not model for the sake of modelling. This costs real hours. Spend them where ambiguity is expensive or unsafe; a beautiful model of something everybody already understands is a cost with no finding in it.

You started with a signed specification, four months of goodwill, three weeks to site, and three people who each believed the matter was settled. What you can put on the wall instead is one page of boxes and labelled arrows, a boundary everyone argued over and then agreed, a two-in-the-morning sequence with every fork answered - and a passage light that stays on, because somebody was pointing at an arrow when Sister Mahlaba remembered why. This is how agreement in words stops being mistaken for agreement about the thing. For four thousand children on the immunisation register, it comes down to a fridge that keeps running on the night the grid does not. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
