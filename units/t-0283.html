Step 1 of 5

The appraisal says do not build the footbridge because it earns no money, and three children have died crossing that road - how do I get what actually matters into the arithmetic?

T1: The Ache

The R573 runs past Motsepe Primary School with four lanes and no crossing for eleven hundred metres. About 1,900 people cross it on foot twice a day, most of them children.

In five years that stretch of road has killed three of them and seriously injured nineteen.

You are the planner. A footbridge has been designed and costed at R14.2 million. It went through the department's standard appraisal three weeks ago, and the appraisal recommends against building it.

The appraisal is not corrupt and it is not incompetent. You have read it twice. Its arithmetic is correct and its logic is the logic every other project in the department is judged by: what does this cost, what revenue does it earn, what is the return. A footbridge earns nothing. No toll, no fare, no fee. So the column marked benefits contains a zero, and R14.2 million against zero gives the answer it gives.

What stops you is that the zero is not a mistake either. Nobody fiddled it. Every single thing this bridge would do lands on people who will never pay a cent for it - the four minutes a child stands at the kerb waiting for a gap, the mother who walks two children across and loses an hour of her day, and the thing nobody in that document has written down at all.

The committee meets on the fourteenth. Somebody will read out "the appraisal does not support it", and it will be true, and it will be the end of it.

Everything this bridge is for happens to people who pay nothing, so the appraisal counts it as nothing. How do I get what actually matters into the arithmetic - defensibly, not by pleading?

Before you read on, decide what you will take to the committee. A correct appraisal that says no, three dead children, a design ready to build, and eight days. Write down what you would actually do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five things this situation usually produces, and what each one can withstand

• "Tell them about the children." The most human response and the one most likely to be reached for, and it puts the entire case on the committee's feelings in a single meeting. It also loses to the sentence "we sympathise, but the appraisal does not support it" - because that sentence has a document behind it and this does not.

• "Challenge the appraisal - it must be wrong." Reasonable, and it will not survive contact: the arithmetic is right and the method is the department's own. Attacking a correct calculation spends your credibility and leaves the zero exactly where it was.

• "Find a way to make it earn money." Ingenious, and it distorts the thing into something it is not. Advertising panels or a small toll to justify a school crossing is a plan that will be argued about for two years, and the road stays as it is.

• "Get it funded from a safety budget instead, outside the appraisal." This sometimes works and it is worth trying. It also concedes the principle - that the appraisal was right to score this at zero - so the next footbridge, and the one after, will fail in exactly the same way.

• "Petition, press, community pressure." Legitimate, and sometimes the only thing left. It wins occasionally and unpredictably, and it teaches the department nothing about why its own method produced this answer.

• Look at what the zero actually is. It is not a claim that the crossings are worthless. It is a record of the fact that they have no market price, and the method only knows how to count things that do. So the question is not whether to fight the appraisal or go around it. It is whether an effect with no price can be given a defensible value - one that comes from somewhere other than your own conviction, and that survives being read by somebody who does not care about your bridge.

T2: The Tool in Action

List every effect and who bears it, before valuing anything. Count each one in its own natural units. Then value the ones with no market price using figures your government already publishes for exactly this purpose - not figures you choose. Bring the whole life to today at the social discount rate, and put the two appraisals side by side. Watch it run on the R573.

Step 1 - Name every effect and who bears it, before valuing anything

Tumelo Sekano lists them on the wall. Effects on the department: capital cost, annual maintenance. Effects on drivers: none to speak of. Effects on the 1,900 people who cross that road twice a day, mostly children walking to and from the primary school: time spent waiting for a gap in traffic, the risk of being struck, and the fear itself - parents who walk their children across and lose an hour of their own day. Three of those effects fall entirely on people who pay nothing and were never consulted. Nobody had written them down before, which is exactly why the appraisal came back the way it did.

Step 2 - Count each effect in its own natural units first

Crossings: 1,900 a day, counted over a week by two students with clickers, school days only, about 300 a year. Delay: 4 minutes each on average, timed on site at the three busiest hours. Casualties from the traffic register for the last five years at this location: 3 deaths, 19 serious injuries. Every one of those figures has a source Tumelo can name and anybody can re-check, and none of them is in rands yet.

Step 3 - Put a value on the effects that have no price, using published figures rather than your own

For the waiting time, the guidance publishes a value for non-working travel time - lower than a wage, because this is not time somebody would have been paid for - and Tumelo uses it at R18 an hour, citing the clause. For the casualties, the same guidance publishes a value for a prevented fatality and one for a prevented serious injury; he uses the department's current figures, R9.2 million and R450,000, and writes the document reference beside each. He does not adjust either one. And he says the uncomfortable thing out loud to the committee: this is not putting a price on a child. It is using the number the state has already published so that the child appears in the arithmetic at all - because the appraisal that leaves the figure out has not declined to price her. It has priced her at nothing.

Step 4 - Bring the whole life onto one page at the social discount rate

Yearly benefits: time saved is 1,900 crossings x 4 minutes x 300 days, about 38,000 hours, at R18 = R684,000. Casualties are 0.6 deaths and 3.8 serious injuries a year, at R9.2 million and R450,000 = R7.23 million. Total about R7.9 million a year. Over a 40-year life at the published social discount rate of 8 per cent, the factor is 11.9, so benefits are worth about R94 million today. Costs: R14.2 million to build, plus R180,000 a year maintenance which is R2.1 million today - R16.3 million in all.

Step 5 - Put the two appraisals side by side, and be honest about which question each answers

Financially, the footbridge earns nothing at all: no revenue, R16.3 million out, and any commercial test rejects it. Socially, R94 million of benefit against R16.3 million of cost - about R5.80 of value for every rand spent. Tumelo puts both lines on one page and says what each measures: the first is right that there is no money in this, and the second is right that there is a great deal of value in it. What changed between them is not the arithmetic. It is whether the 1,900 people who cross that road appear in it at all.

See where the answer came from, and where it did not. Nothing in the arithmetic was disputed and nothing in it was invented: the same R14.2 million, the same forty years, counted crossings, the traffic register, and values for time and casualties taken from the department's own published guidance with the clause cited beside each. What changed the answer from reject to R5.80 of value for every rand was Step 1 - writing down the effects that land on people who pay nothing. And notice the sentence Tumelo says out loud rather than leaving for somebody else to say: this is not putting a price on a child. It is using the figure the state has already published so that she appears in the arithmetic at all - because an appraisal that leaves the figure out has not declined to price her, it has priced her at nothing. That distinction is the whole ethical weight of this method, and it belongs in the room, not in a footnote.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the first one decides the answer.

  • 1. List every effect and who bears it - especially the effects landing on people who pay nothing and were not consulted.
  • 2. Count each effect in its natural units, from records and observation, with the source written down.
  • 3. Value the priceless ones from published guidance, citing document and date, using the current figure rather than a convenient one.
  • 4. Bring the whole life to today at the social discount rate, not a commercial one.
  • 5. Show both appraisals side by side, and name what each one measures.

This is social benefit-cost analysis, and now that you have run one its vocabulary is just names for what you did. Replacing a missing market price with a value society has agreed for appraisal is shadow pricing - the published rate for an hour of non-working time, the value of a prevented fatality, the value of a prevented serious injury. The rate you brought forty years back to today with is the social discount rate, and it is deliberately lower than a commercial one because society is not an investor seeking a return and the people who will use this bridge in thirty years are not creditors charging you interest. The ratio you ended with is the benefit-cost ratio, and anything above one means the value created exceeds the cost - though a ratio is a comparison and not a permission, and nothing in it decides whether the money exists.

It fits any decision whose main effects fall on people other than the payer - crossings, clinics, water, sanitation, transport, environmental protection, public safety. Three limits travel with the answer, and they matter more than the technique. It cannot tell you whether the budget exists, only whether the value does. It is only as honest as its Step 1 list, so an effect nobody wrote down is invisible no matter how careful the arithmetic afterwards - and the omitted effects are systematically those borne by people who were not in the room. And it is vulnerable in exactly one place: whoever chooses the shadow prices can move the answer, which is precisely why the figures must come from published guidance with a citation and never from the person who wants the outcome.

Two disciplines decide whether this is analysis or advocacy with decimal points, and the first one will feel like working against yourself. Take the shadow prices from published guidance, cite the document and the date, and use the current figure even when a different one would help you. The whole authority of the method comes from the fact that you did not choose the numbers - and a reviewer who finds one adjusted figure will discard the entire submission, correctly, because if one was chosen they must all be suspect. If the published values do not carry your case, that is a finding and you report it. Second, write the Step 1 list with somebody who bears the effects, not only with colleagues. Every honest failure of this method traces back to an effect that was never listed, and the effects that get missed are reliably the ones falling on people who were not asked - the hour a parent loses, the fear that stops an older person going out at all. A brilliant valuation of an incomplete list is a confident wrong answer, and it is the failure this technique is most prone to.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep the analysis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A zero in the benefits column is usually a missing price, not a missing benefit. Ask of every zero: is this genuinely worthless, or does it simply have nobody selling it?

• Never choose your own shadow prices. Cite the published figure, the document and the date. The moment the analyst picks the numbers, the analysis is worth exactly as much as the analyst's opinion - which is what it was trying to replace.

• Count in natural units before you price anything. Hours, injuries, cases, litres. A sceptic attacks the quantities long before the prices, and quantities are where your evidence is.

• Use the social discount rate, not a commercial one. A commercial rate discounts a benefit forty years out to almost nothing, which is a statement about investors rather than about the children who will use the bridge in 2066.

• The Step 1 list is the whole vulnerability. No arithmetic recovers an effect nobody wrote down, and the effects that go missing are those borne by people who were not consulted. Build the list with them.

• Show the financial appraisal too, and say it is right. Replacing it looks like hiding it. Putting both on one page, each labelled with the question it answers, is what makes the finding hard to dismiss.

• A ratio is not a permission. Five-to-one does not mean the money exists, and it does not outrank a safety duty or a legal obligation. It tells you what the value is; other people decide what happens next.

• Say plainly what the casualty figures are and are not. Using a published value for a prevented death is not pricing a person. Leaving it out is - at zero. Say that sentence out loud in the meeting rather than hoping nobody asks.

You started with an appraisal that was arithmetically correct, methodologically orthodox, and about to end the matter - because everything the bridge does happens to people who will never pay for it. You can take one page to the committee instead: every effect and who bears it, crossings counted with clickers and casualties from the traffic register, values for time and for prevented death taken from the department's own guidance with the clause cited, forty years brought to today at the published social rate - R94 million of value against R16.3 million of cost, beside the financial appraisal which is still perfectly right that there is no money in it. This is how something with no market price stops being counted as worth nothing. On the fourteenth, somebody will still read out what the financial appraisal says. This time it will not be the only number in the room. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one decision whose effects fall on people who are not paying, and build its Step 1 list together.)
