The newcomer who could still see

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and you have two reports on your desk about the same organisation.

The first is the culture survey. 71 per cent of staff feel the culture is healthy. Leadership is relieved.

The second is the behavioural record from the culture work. Near-miss reporting is running at about a tenth of the benchmark, and the last two people who stopped a job for a safety concern were quietly made to regret it.

You cannot share the relief. The two reports describe the same works, the same people, the same year - and they are telling you opposite stories. The survey is not wrong. It is measuring something different from what everyone in that meeting thought it was measuring.

The same organisation just told me, in two measurements, that its culture is healthy and that its culture is broken. How do I read the gap between what people say and what they do - and know which way it points?

Before you read on, decide. Two reports about the same works, the same people, the same year. The culture survey came back strongly favourable and the room was visibly relieved. The other says the last two people who stopped a job over a safety concern were quietly made to regret it. Neither is wrong; neither was dishonestly produced. The only person not relieved is the technician who transferred in six months ago, who said something at the end of the meeting about how quiet people go when a job is stopped, and was not really heard. Write down how you would read this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what a survey can actually measure

• “Trust the survey — it is the bigger sample.” Sample size makes a perception estimate more precise; it does not make perception into behaviour. A larger number of people can all have stopped noticing the same thing.

• “Trust the behaviour — that is what actually happened.” Closer to the truth of this case, and it throws away the more sensitive instrument. As the next paragraph shows, perception moves first, so discarding it means always finding out late.

• “Re-run the survey — something went wrong with it.” Nothing went wrong with it. It measured what it measures, well, and you will get the same result again and be no wiser.

• “Average them — the truth is somewhere in the middle.” Averages two different quantities. The distance between them is not error to be split; the gap is the finding.

• “Report the favourable number — it is defensible and true.” Both true and defensible, and it would tell a board that a works where people are punished for stopping jobs has a healthy culture. That is how a number becomes a lie without anybody saying anything false.

• The resolution is that they measure different things. A survey measures perception — what people say they experience. The culture that actually operates shows up in behaviour: what gets rewarded, what gets punished, what happened to the last person who stopped a job. That is why a favourable percentage on its own feels like an answer and is not one — you cannot tell whether it is a healthy culture accurately perceived, a poor culture people have stopped seeing, or a good culture nobody yet believes in. But laid alongside behaviour it becomes powerful, because perception moves first: people register a change, good or bad, before it hardens into the record. So the survey is your leading indicator and the behaviour is the lagging one, and the direction of the gap carries the meaning — perception better than behaviour says people have normalised something and stopped seeing it, which is your case; behaviour better than perception says real improvement nobody believes yet. Two disciplines: the survey's one job is to measure perception well, because a sloppy one makes the gap unreadable; and it must be safe to answer honestly, or it produces exactly the reassuring number that hides the problem. And listen to the newcomer. She has not yet stopped seeing it.

Here is the technique that answers it.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the say-do gap survey and its analysis - measuring perception well, pairing said with done, keeping it safe, reading the gap against behaviour, and reporting it as a system finding.

1. Decide what you are measuring - perception, and only perception

BEFORE YOU WRITE A SINGLE QUESTION, BE HONEST WITH YOURSELF ABOUT WHAT A SURVEY CAN AND CANNOT DO. A SURVEY MEASURES PERCEPTION - WHAT PEOPLE SAY THEY EXPERIENCE - AND PERCEPTION IS NOT THE SAME THING AS THE CULTURE. The culture that actually IS shows up in behaviour: what gets rewarded, what gets punished, what happens to the person who stops a job. That behavioural evidence is a separate discipline, and it produces the LAGGING indicator - the culture as it has already hardened into consequences. A SURVEY PRODUCES THE LEADING INDICATOR: perception moves first, before behaviour, because people notice a change - for better or worse - before it has worked its way into the record. THIS IS WHY YOU NEVER TRUST THE SURVEY ALONE. On its own it produces a number like '71 per cent favourable' that feels like an answer and is not one, because you have no way of knowing whether that 71 per cent is a healthy culture accurately perceived, a poor culture people have stopped seeing, or a good culture people do not yet believe in. THE SURVEY'S ONE JOB IS TO MEASURE PERCEPTION WELL, SO THAT WHEN YOU LAY IT ALONGSIDE THE BEHAVIOUR, THE GAP BETWEEN THEM MEANS SOMETHING. A sloppy survey and a clean behavioural record produce a gap that is just measurement error. So this unit is not about producing a culture score - it is about producing one honest half of a two-part diagnosis, and knowing, the whole time, that it is only half. THE OTHER HALF - reading the behaviour, diagnosing the underlying assumption, and building the change plan - is a technique of its own, and this unit hands off to it rather than trying to do it.

2. Ask about specific experiences, not abstract values - and pair the said with the done

THE FASTEST WAY TO RUIN A CULTURE SURVEY IS TO ASK ABOUT VALUES IN THE ABSTRACT. 'DO YOU VALUE SAFETY?' - EVERYONE SAYS YES, and the item has measured nothing except that people know which answer is the good one. ESPOUSED VALUES ARE WHAT AN ORGANISATION SAYS IT ASPIRES TO; ENACTED VALUES ARE WHAT ITS DECISIONS AND ACTIONS ACTUALLY DEMONSTRATE - and the whole point of this survey is the distance between them, so you must design the distance INTO the instrument. Do it two ways. FIRST, ASK ABOUT SPECIFIC, RECENT, OBSERVABLE EXPERIENCES RATHER THAN BELIEFS: not 'do you feel safe to speak up', but 'in the last month, did you see someone raise a concern, and what happened to them afterwards'. A specific experience is far harder to answer in the socially-approved way, because it asks what happened, not what should happen. SECOND, PAIR THE ESPOUSED WITH THE ENACTED ON THE SAME SUBJECT: ask whether people believe the stated value ('this organisation stands behind people who stop a job for safety') AND ask about the lived experience of that exact value ('the last time stopping a job cost us time, the person who called it was supported'). Now the gap is inside the survey itself, in the distance between the two answers, before you have even reached the behavioural record. AND WATCH THE NEAR-UNANIMOUS ITEM ESPECIALLY. When 95 per cent agree that the stated values are the right ones, that is not evidence of a healthy culture - it is evidence that the values are agreeable, which everyone already knew. The signal is what sits next to it: near-total agreement on the espoused value, beside hard evidence that living it was penalised, is the say-do gap in its purest and most undeniable form.

3. Make it safe to answer honestly - or you are measuring fear, not culture

A CULTURE SURVEY HAS A METHODOLOGICAL HAZARD AT ITS CENTRE, AND IF YOU DO NOT DESIGN AROUND IT THE SURVEY WILL LIE TO YOU MOST CONFIDENTLY EXACTLY WHERE YOU MOST NEED THE TRUTH. SOCIAL DESIRABILITY BIAS makes people over-report the approved attitudes and under-report the disapproved ones, and it INTENSIFIES precisely where culture matters most: high-stakes topics like safety and ethics, power asymmetry, and low psychological safety with fear of retaliation. Read that carefully, because it has a devastating consequence: A CULTURE SURVEY IS MOST DISTORTED PRECISELY WHERE THE CULTURE IS WORST. In the frightened team, people give the answers they think are safe; in the healthy team, they tell you the truth. So a suspiciously glowing result from a high-pressure, high-consequence area is not reassurance - IT IS ITSELF A SIGNAL, and often the loudest one in the dataset. The defence is real anonymity, believed anonymity, and the discipline that protects it. ANONYMITY RAISES TRUTHFUL REPORTING, and confidentiality assurances and 'there are no right answers' framing reduce distortion - but only if people believe them, which they will not if last year's 'anonymous' survey was somehow traced back to a team. Guarantee it in the design: NO REPORTING CELL SMALL ENOUGH TO IDENTIFY A PERSON. If a cut of the data would leave three people in a box, you do not publish that cut. BEWARE RE-IDENTIFICATION: combining several cuts ('women in the finance team over 50') or quoting a distinctive free-text comment can identify a person just as easily. YOUR DUTY IS TO THE PERSON, NOT THE DATA. AND THE FIRST DUTY OF THE ANALYST IS ABSOLUTE: A SURVEY THAT IS NOT SAFE TO ANSWER HONESTLY MEASURES FEAR, NOT CULTURE. THE FIRST DUTY OF THE ANALYST IS TO PROTECT THE PEOPLE WHO SPOKE. ANY ANALYSIS THAT COULD IDENTIFY A PERSON OR A SMALL TEAM IS A BETRAYAL OF THAT TRUST. IF YOU CANNOT GUARANTEE SAFETY, YOU MUST NOT ASK.

4. Read it against the behaviour - the gap, its sign, and its size

NOW THE DIAGNOSIS, WHICH IS NOT THE SURVEY NUMBER BUT THE GAP BETWEEN THE SURVEY AND THE BEHAVIOUR. LAY THEM SIDE BY SIDE. At Corathyl the survey reads 71 PER CENT FAVOURABLE, and 82 PER CENT AGREE they would be supported if they stopped a job for safety - which taken alone looks like a healthy culture, somewhere around rung 4 of the five-step ladder. The behavioural record says otherwise: in the last year, jobs were stopped for safety three times, and in TWO of the three the person who stopped it was criticised or overruled; near-miss reporting runs at about 10 PER CENT of the benchmark; on the behavioural ladder the organisation sits at RUNG 2. THAT IS A TWO-RUNG SAY-DO GAP - AND THE SIGN OF THE GAP IS THE DIAGNOSIS. WHERE PERCEPTION IS LOWER THAN BEHAVIOUR WARRANTS, the cause is usually a RECENT GENUINE IMPROVEMENT people have not yet come to believe. WHERE PERCEPTION IS HIGHER THAN BEHAVIOUR WARRANTS, as here, the cause is usually NORMALISATION (people have adapted and stopped seeing it) or FEAR (they dare not report otherwise). THIS IS THE MORE DANGEROUS DIRECTION, BUT BEWARE THE EXCEPTION: a genuine, very recent improvement can also cause perception to rise before the lagging behavioural record has caught up. THE CONTEXT - is there a real change programme underway? - tells you which it is. THEN SEGMENT, BECAUSE THE GAP IS NEVER UNIFORM AND THE SEGMENTS ARE THE SHARPEST INSTRUMENT YOU HAVE. Split Corathyl by tenure: under two years returns 58 PER CENT favourable; over ten years returns 83 PER CENT. The naive reading is that the long-serving are the healthy, engaged core. THE GAP INVERTS IT: the newcomers can still SEE the culture and report it close to what the behaviour shows, while the long-serving have normalised it. The 25-point spread is not a story about two groups' morale - it is a measure of how long this organisation takes to teach a person to stop noticing. AND HOLD THIS THE WHOLE TIME: THESE NUMBERS ARE A LENS, NOT A MICROMETER. THEIR PURPOSE IS TO MAKE A PATTERN VISIBLE. THE PATTERN IS THE FINDING, NOT THE EXACT PERCENTAGE. A two-rung gap means 'a large discrepancy', not a calibrated reading, and a learner who argues about whether it was really 71 or 68 has mistaken the instrument for a micrometer.

5. Report the gap as a system finding, not a verdict on people

HOW YOU REPORT THIS DECIDES WHETHER IT HEALS ANYTHING OR DOES HARM, SO REPORT THE GAP AS A PROPERTY OF THE SYSTEM, NEVER AS A CHARGE AGAINST PEOPLE. Two groups will be tempting to blame, and both would be wrong. THE NORMALISED. WHEN A PERSON ADAPTS TO A CULTURE, THEY ARE NOT FAILING TO SEE; THEY HAVE LEARNED WHAT IS REQUIRED TO SURVIVE IN THAT SYSTEM. NORMALISATION IS A SIGN OF A POWERFUL SYSTEM, NOT A WEAK PERSON. The long-serving staff at 83 per cent favourable are not complacent or dishonest - they have spent ten years being taught, by consequence after consequence, that noticing costs more than not noticing, and they learned the lesson the organisation actually taught. AND THE LEADERS. THE GAP BETWEEN WHAT LEADERS SAY AND WHAT THE ORGANISATION DOES IS RARELY A LIE. IT IS THE DISTANCE BETWEEN A GENUINE ASPIRATION AND A SYSTEM THAT CREATES DIFFERENT PRESSURES. THE DIAGNOSIS IS OF THE SYSTEM. A manager who means 'we stop for safety' and then, under a deadline nobody gave them room to miss, lets the criticism of a safety-stopper pass unchallenged, is not a hypocrite - they are the visible point where the system's pressures met the system's words. So the report says: here is the gap the system is creating, here is where it is largest, here is the sign of it - and it hands that to whoever can change the CONSEQUENCES that cause it, because consequences are what move a culture, not posters and not the survey itself. FINALLY, AND ABOVE EVERYTHING: PROTECT THE PEOPLE WHO SPOKE. Report at a grain coarse enough that no individual and no small team can be identified, resist every request to drill into 'which team said that', and if the only honest finding would expose someone, you carry it differently rather than publishing it. The people told you the truth on the promise that it was safe to. Keeping that promise is not a courtesy - it is the condition on which you are allowed to ask again next year.

Box 4 is where two measurements become a diagnosis. Box 5 is where you decide whether it heals or harms. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A culture survey does not measure culture. It measures perception - and the gap between that perception and observed reality is the true diagnosis. Five questions:

  • What am I actually measuring? Perception, and only perception - a leading indicator, one honest half of a two-part diagnosis, never trusted alone.
  • What do I ask? Specific recent experiences, not abstract values - and pair the espoused value with the lived experience of the same thing, so the gap sits inside the survey.
  • Is it safe to answer? Real, believed anonymity, or you measure fear - and a glowing result under pressure is itself a signal, because the survey distorts most where the culture is worst.
  • Which way does the gap point? Read it against the behaviour - the sign and the size. Higher than warranted is normalisation or fear; lower is unbelieved improvement. Segment it.
  • How do I report it? As a system finding, never a verdict on people - and protect every respondent absolutely.

The arithmetic of two reports that cannot both be right. The survey reads 71 per cent favourable, and on the specific item "I would be supported if I stopped a job for safety", 82 per cent agree - which, taken alone, looks like a healthy culture around rung 4 of the five-step ladder. The behavioural record says otherwise: jobs were stopped for safety three times last year, and in two of the three the person who stopped it was criticised or overruled; near-miss reporting runs at about 10 per cent of the benchmark; the behavioural ladder puts the organisation at rung 2. That is a two-rung say-do gap, and its sign is the diagnosis. Where perception is lower than behaviour warrants, the cause is usually a recent genuine improvement people do not yet believe. Where perception is higher than behaviour warrants, as here, the cause is usually normalisation (people have adapted and stopped seeing it) or fear. This is the more dangerous direction, but beware the exception: a genuine, very recent improvement can also cause perception to rise before the lagging behavioural record has caught up. The context - is there a real change programme underway? - tells you which it is.

The sharpest instrument is the person who has not yet learned to stop noticing. Split Corathyl by tenure and the survey inverts its own headline: staff under two years return 58 per cent favourable; staff over ten years return 83 per cent. The naive reading is that the long-serving are the healthy core and the newcomers are malcontents. The gap says the opposite: the newcomers can still see the culture and report it close to what the behaviour shows, while the long-serving have normalised it. The 25-point spread is not a story about two groups' morale - it is a measure of how long this organisation takes to teach a person to stop noticing. Hold one thing the whole time, though: these numbers are a lens, not a micrometer. Their purpose is to make a pattern visible. The pattern is the finding, not the exact percentage. A "two-rung gap" means "a large discrepancy", not a calibrated reading.

And the gap is a fact about the system, not a charge against anyone. When a person adapts to a culture, they are not failing to see; they have learned what is required to survive in that system. Normalisation is a sign of a powerful system, not a weak person. Nor is the leaders' say-do gap a lie: it is the distance between a genuine aspiration and a system that creates different pressures. The diagnosis is of the system. And the safety of the respondent is absolute - a survey that is not safe to answer honestly measures fear, not culture; the first duty of the analyst is to protect the people who spoke; any analysis that could identify a person or a small team is a betrayal of that trust; if you cannot guarantee safety, you must not ask.

One honest boundary. You measure the gap and hand it - its pattern, its sign, its largest place - to the people who can change the consequences that cause it. You do not diagnose the underlying assumption or write the change plan; that is a technique of its own, working from the behaviour.

Does it scale? Anyone who has managed people knows that "everyone here is happy" and "nobody will tell me otherwise" can look identical from the front of the room. The way you tell them apart is never the answer people give - it is whether the answer they give matches what you can watch them do.

T4: Your Turn

A different organisation runs its culture survey and behavioural review together. You have to read them as one diagnosis.

Survey itemResult
Overall favourable78 per cent
"Our company's stated values are the right ones"95 per cent agree
"I would be backed if I refused unsafe work"80 per cent agree
Favourable, staff under 2 years61 per cent
Favourable, staff over 10 years88 per cent
Behavioural record (same period)Detail
Refusals of unsafe work in 18 months4; in 3 of them the person was moved to a less desirable shift within a month
Near-miss reports0.5 per person per year, against a benchmark of about 4
Behavioural ladder rung2 of 5
Recent changeNone - stable for years
  1. Read the headline. What does 78 per cent favourable tell you on its own? What does it not tell you?
  2. The 95 per cent item. Why is near-unanimous agreement here the opposite of reassuring, given the behavioural record?
  3. Sign and size. Which way does the gap point, and what are the two possible causes? Which one fits, and how do you know?
  4. The tenure split. The long-serving are more favourable than the newcomers. Read it two ways, and say which is right.
  5. Report it. Write the one-sentence finding you would hand up - and say what you would refuse to publish.

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, 78 per cent favourable tells you what people say, and nothing more. Read alone it is the comfortable answer, and comfort is exactly what you must not buy here.

Second, the 95 per cent is the undeniable pairing. Almost everyone agrees the stated values are right - of course they do, the values are agreeable - while the behavioural record shows that in three of four cases, living the value of refusing unsafe work got the person moved to a worse shift. Near-total endorsement of the espoused value, beside clear evidence the enacted value is punished, is the say-do gap in its purest form. It is not reassurance; it is the finding.

Third, the gap points perception higher than behaviour warrants. The two possible causes are normalisation-or-fear and unbelieved-improvement - and this one is not improvement, because the record shows no recent change, stable for years. So it is normalisation or fear, and the punished refusals point specifically at fear: people say they would be backed because saying otherwise feels unsafe, which is the very thing the survey is failing to measure.

Fourth, the tenure split. Read one way, the long-serving are the loyal, engaged core. Read against the behaviour, the long-serving have had more years to be taught that noticing costs more than not noticing, and the 27-point spread measures the teaching. The second reading is the right one, because it is the one the behavioural record supports; the first is the comfortable story the headline invites.

Fifth, the finding is a sentence about the system: "Stated safety values are near-universally endorsed, but the enacted consequence of acting on them is penalty, and staff appear to normalise this over tenure - the gap is largest between what the organisation says about safety and what it does to those who invoke it." And you would refuse to publish anything that names the four people who refused unsafe work, or any cut small enough to identify them - because they are the people who told you the truth, and the whole technique depends on that never costing them.

T5: The Guardrails

A culture survey read on its own tells you what people were willing to say.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Measuring satisfaction and calling it culture. A happiness score and a culture diagnosis are different instruments. This one measures the gap between the said and the done.

• Stopping at a single favourable number. "71 per cent favourable" is not an answer. Without the behavioural comparison, you cannot tell health from normalisation from unbelieved improvement.

• Asking about abstract values. Everyone endorses safety in the abstract. Ask what actually happened, recently, to a real person who acted on it.

• A survey that is not safe to answer. Then it measures fear, not culture - and it measures it most confidently exactly where the culture is worst.

• Trusting the survey alone. Perception leads; behaviour is the reality. The gap between them, read against the behavioural record, is the whole point.

• Averaging the gap away. The gap's sign and size are the finding. A mean across the whole workforce hides the two groups that disagree, which are the two groups you needed to hear.

• Reading high perception as health. Higher than the behaviour warrants usually means people have stopped seeing, or dare not say. It is the more dangerous direction, not the safe one.

• Reading a tenure split as morale. When the long-serving score higher than the newcomers against a poor behavioural record, that is adaptation, not engagement. The newcomer who can still see is your clearest instrument.

• Blaming the normalised or the leaders. Normalisation is a sign of a powerful system, not a weak person. A leader's say-do gap is usually pressure, not a lie. The diagnosis is of the system.

• Publishing a cut that identifies someone. If a box holds three people, you do not open it. The people spoke on the promise of safety, and keeping it is the condition of asking again.

Onalenna had two reports that could not both be right in the way she thought. Measure perception and never forget it is only perception, a leading indicator you will read against the behaviour; ask about specific recent experiences rather than abstract values, and pair the stated value with the lived experience of the same thing so the gap sits inside the survey; make it genuinely safe to answer, because a survey that is not safe measures fear and distorts most exactly where the culture is worst; read the result against the behavioural record for the gap's sign and size, remembering that perception higher than behaviour warrants is normalisation or fear while perception lower is improvement not yet believed, and segment it because the newcomer who can still see is your sharpest instrument; and report the gap as a fact about the system, protecting absolutely the people who told you the truth. A survey measures what people will say. Behaviour measures what the culture is. The distance between them is the diagnosis - and its direction tells you whether your people have stopped seeing a problem, or have not yet believed a solution. Take one thing your organisation says it values, find one recent decision where that value cost something, and ask what actually happened to the person who upheld it. That single pairing is a say-do gap in miniature.
