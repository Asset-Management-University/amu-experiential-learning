Repair or improvement

T1: The Ache

The invoice for the big pump overhaul is on your desk: R500,000. It was a major job - the crew stripped and restored the pump to its original condition, new seals and bearings and a re-wind, and while it was open they fitted a larger impeller and a variable-speed drive that lift its capacity and add years to its life.

Your accountant asks what sounds like a small, tidy question: "Is this a repair, or an improvement?" And you realise, standing there, that it is not small at all. The answer decides this year's reported profit, the value of the pump on the books, the tax the company pays, and whether the capacity upgrade you fought for even survives into next year's asset base.

And you realise the easy answer - book the whole R500,000 to one bucket and move on - is a lie, whichever bucket you choose.

One invoice, and how I classify it changes the profit, the asset value, the tax, and the budget - so how do I book it to tell the truth about what the work actually did?

Before you read on, decide. One invoice, R500,000, for the pump overhaul. Part of it restored the machine to the condition it was supposed to be in — bearings, seals, the casing made good. Part of it fitted the larger impeller and the upgraded motor that gave you the extra capacity you fought for. The accounts clerk wants a code by lunchtime and treats it as a small question. Standing there you realise it is not small: the answer moves this year's reported profit, the pump's value on the books, the tax the company pays, and whether that capacity upgrade even exists in next year's asset base. Write down how you would book it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why accounts follow value

• “All of it as maintenance — it was an overhaul.” Charges the whole amount against this year and makes the year look expensive, and it quietly deletes the upgrade: the extra capacity you fought for never appears in the asset base at all.

• “All of it as capital — it was a major job.” Flatters this year's profit and puts restoration work onto the books as though it created new value, which overstates what the pump is worth and understates what running it actually costs.

• “Whatever the accountant prefers.” They will pick by convention or by what suits the year, because they cannot see what the work did to the machine. That knowledge is yours, and it is the only thing that can settle it.

• “It is only a code — it does not change anything real.” It changes four real things you have just listed, including the tax paid and whether next year's budget starts from an asset base that includes your upgrade.

• “Book it when we pay it and keep it simple.” Cash timing rather than classification, and it introduces a second error on top of the first.

• The idea that unlocks it: an honest set of accounts records when value was used up or created, not when money left the bank. Cash and value almost never move together — you pay for a part months before fitting it, do an overhaul weeks before the invoice arrives, buy a pump once and consume its value over twenty years. Record by payment date and every period is distorted by the accident of timing, so nothing can be compared or managed. Line the cost up with the value and you get a figure you can trust. From that, the classification falls out: spend that restores an asset to the condition it was expected to be in is consumed now and belongs to this period, while spend that improves it — more capacity, longer life, a capability it did not have — creates value used over years and belongs with the asset. So the test is not the size of the invoice or who did the work, but what the work did to the machine. And since this one did both, the honest treatment is to split it — because putting it all in one bucket is a lie whichever bucket you choose.

Here are the principles that make the answer honest.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how an asset manager keeps a cost report honest - following value not cash, matching cost to the period, accruing what is incurred, and classifying spend by what the work did to the asset.

1. Accounting follows value, not cash

START WITH THE IDEA THAT UNLOCKS EVERYTHING ELSE IN THIS UNIT: AN HONEST SET OF ACCOUNTS IS NOT A RECORD OF WHEN MONEY LEFT THE BANK - IT IS A RECORD OF WHEN VALUE WAS USED UP OR CREATED. This sounds abstract until you see that CASH AND VALUE ALMOST NEVER MOVE AT THE SAME TIME. You pay for a spare part months before you fit it. You do the overhaul weeks before the invoice arrives. You buy a pump once and consume its value over twenty years of service. CASH ACCOUNTING - money in, money out - IS SIMPLE, AND IT LIES TO AN ASSET MANAGER, because if you manage by when payments happen to fall, every single period is distorted by the accident of timing: a month looks cheap because a big invoice has not landed yet, a month looks dear because two years of insurance was paid up front. You cannot see what anything actually cost, so you cannot manage it. ACCRUAL ACCOUNTING FIXES THIS BY LINING THE COST UP WITH THE VALUE - putting each cost in the period where the value was actually used or created, whatever the cash did. Only that gives you a period figure you can trust, compare and act on. TWO PRINCIPLES DO THE LINING-UP, and they are the next two boxes: the MATCHING PRINCIPLE (which period a cost belongs to) and ACCRUALS (when you record it). Hold the anchor: accounting follows value, not cash - and an honest cost report tells the story of when value was used or created, not when cash changed hands.

2. The matching principle - cost sits in the period of the benefit

THE FIRST PRINCIPLE IS SIMPLE TO STATE AND EASY TO BREAK: PUT A COST IN THE SAME PERIOD AS THE WORK OR BENEFIT IT BELONGS TO, REGARDLESS OF WHEN THE INVOICE IS DATED OR THE CASH IS PAID. Work done in March is a March cost, even if the paperwork lands in May and the money leaves in June. The period is decided by when the VALUE was used or created, not by the dates on the documents. This is the MATCHING PRINCIPLE, and once you have it, a great deal of accounting stops looking arbitrary. THE BIGGEST APPLICATION OF MATCHING IN ASSET MANAGEMENT IS ONE YOU ALREADY KNOW BY ANOTHER NAME: DEPRECIATION IS THE MATCHING PRINCIPLE. When you capitalise an asset and depreciate it across its useful life, you are matching the cost of that asset to each of the years it actually delivers value. You do NOT charge a twenty-year pump entirely to the year you bought it, even though that is the year the cash left - because the pump earns its keep over twenty years, so its cost is spread across those twenty years, a slice matched to each year of benefit. Depreciation is not a tax trick and it is not an arbitrary rule; it is matching applied to a long-lived asset. Understand matching and you understand why the accounts treat a capital asset completely differently from a monthly repair: the repair's benefit is used up this month, so its cost belongs this month; the asset's benefit is used up over decades, so its cost is spread over decades. Same principle, two answers, decided entirely by WHEN THE VALUE IS CONSUMED.

3. Accruals - record when incurred, not when paid

THE MATCHING PRINCIPLE IS A PROMISE ABOUT WHICH PERIOD A COST BELONGS TO; THE ACCRUAL IS HOW YOU KEEP THAT PROMISE IN PRACTICE, DAY TO DAY. THE RULE: YOU RECORD A COST WHEN IT IS INCURRED - WHEN THE WORK IS DONE OR THE BENEFIT IS RECEIVED - NOT WHEN THE CHEQUE CLEARS. This is the exact move that makes a real cost report true, and it is the most common place a report quietly becomes a lie. WHEN WORK HAS BEEN DONE THIS MONTH BUT THE SUPPLIER INVOICE HAS NOT YET POSTED, YOU ACCRUE IT: you book the cost NOW, into the month the work actually happened, because under matching that is the month it belongs to, even though no invoice and no payment has appeared yet. If instead you wait for the invoice and record the cost only when it posts next month, you tell two lies at once - THIS MONTH LOOKS ARTIFICIALLY CHEAP (a cost that belongs to it is missing) AND NEXT MONTH LOOKS ARTIFICIALLY DEAR (it carries a cost that was not its own). Neither month can be managed against, and a variance analysis on either is meaningless. This is why the accrual is not bureaucratic fiddling - it is the practical enforcement of matching, and it is the single discipline that lets a maintenance cost report reflect the work that was actually done in the period rather than the invoices that happened to arrive in it. ACCRUALS ARE SIMPLY THE MATCHING PRINCIPLE, DONE ON THE DAY, ONE COST AT A TIME - and the reason a done-but-unposted job must never be left off this month report.

4. Capital or operating - restore or improve

NOW THE CLASSIFICATION THAT MATTERS MOST TO AN ASSET MANAGER AND IS GOT WRONG MOST OFTEN - AND THE FIRST THING TO UNLEARN IS THAT IT IS DECIDED BY THE SIZE OF THE CHEQUE. IT IS NOT. OPERATING EXPENDITURE KEEPS AN ASSET DOING WHAT IT ALREADY DOES - a repair, a service, a like-for-like replacement of a worn part - AND IT IS EXPENSED IN FULL, THIS PERIOD, because its benefit is used up now. CAPITAL EXPENDITURE CREATES A NEW ASSET OR MATERIALLY EXTENDS THE LIFE, CAPACITY OR CAPABILITY OF AN EXISTING ONE - a rebuild that adds years, an upgrade that raises output - AND IT IS CAPITALISED (added to the asset value on the books) AND DEPRECIATED OVER THE YEARS IT WILL SERVE, which is the matching principle from box 2, because the value it buys is used over those years. SO THE WHOLE TEST IS ONE QUESTION: DID THE SPEND RESTORE THE ASSET TO WHAT IT WAS (OPERATING), OR IMPROVE IT BEYOND THAT (CAPITAL)? A worn bearing replaced with the same bearing RESTORES - opex. A larger impeller that lifts the pump capacity IMPROVES - capex. A re-wind to original specification restores; a variable-speed drive that adds years of controllable service improves. AND BECAUSE A REAL INTERVENTION IS OFTEN BOTH AT ONCE - you restore the pump and upgrade it in the same shutdown - THE HONEST, SOPHISTICATED ANSWER IS USUALLY TO SPLIT IT: the restoration portion to operating, the improvement portion to capital, each treated as its own kind of cost. The unsophisticated move is to shove the whole invoice into one bucket for tidiness; the professional move is to split it by what each part of the work actually did to the asset. (How that capex-vs-opex choice then flows through cash, profit and the balance sheet, and how the depreciation is calculated, is the work of the financial-framing technique; the job HERE is to classify it correctly in the first place, because everything downstream depends on getting this one call right.)

5. The consequences - and the integrity line

FINALLY, WHY GETTING THIS WRONG IS NEVER A MERE TECHNICALITY, AND THE INTEGRITY LINE THAT GOVERNS IT. THE CONSEQUENCES SHOW UP EVERYWHERE A DECISION USES THE NUMBERS. Break MATCHING or the ACCRUAL and you misstate the period - this month cheap, next month dear - so every variance and every comparison is wrong. Misclassify CAPEX AS OPEX and you overstate this year cost (the maintenance budget takes a distorting hit), understate the asset value (the improvement never reaches the books), and may even see the work killed because it looks like a blow-out. Misclassify OPEX AS CAPEX and you flatter this year result, inflate the asset base with spend that bought no new life, and then depreciate a routine repair for years - a fiction carried on the balance sheet that overstates how healthy the position really is. Either direction distorts the picture that managers, auditors and funders rely on, and can bring audit findings. NOW THE LINE, AND IT IS AN INTEGRITY LINE, NOT JUST A TECHNIQUE: YOU CLASSIFY SPEND TO TELL THE TRUTH ABOUT WHAT THE WORK DID, NOT TO HIT A NUMBER. Capitalising a routine repair to protect a stretched maintenance budget, or expensing a real upgrade to slip it past capital approval, is not clever accounting - it is a misstatement that misleads the next person who trusts the books. AND THE BOUNDARY IS A PLACE OF GENUINE JUDGEMENT, NOT A TEST YOU CAN FAIL: a big overhaul really is part-restore and part-improve, and where a cost is honestly ambiguous you do not guess and do not pick the convenient answer - YOU STATE THE BASIS ('classified as capital because it extends the life by an estimated three years, recorded for review'), which is honest, checkable and correctable. AND YOU DO NOT DECIDE IT ALONE: THE ASSET MANAGER AND THE ACCOUNTANT NEED EACH OTHER - the asset manager knows what the work actually did to the asset, the accountant knows the rule, and the truest classification is the conversation between them, not a decree by either.

Box 4 is the classification that changes everything for an asset. Box 5 is the line that keeps it honest. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

An honest cost report tells the story of when value was used or created, not when cash changed hands. Classify spend to tell the truth about what the work did, not to hit a number. Five questions:

  • What does accounting follow? Value, not cash. The question is when the value was used or created, not when the money moved.
  • Which period does a cost belong to? The matching principle: the period of the work or benefit it relates to, whatever the invoice date.
  • When do I record it? Accruals: when the cost is incurred (the work is done), not when it is paid.
  • Repair or improvement? Restore or improve. Restore the asset to what it was is operating (expensed now); improve it beyond that - more life, capacity or capability - is capital (capitalised and depreciated). Split a mixed job.
  • What if I get it wrong? You misstate the period, the asset value, the tax and every decision built on the numbers. Classify to tell the truth; decide it with your accountant.

Accounting follows value, not cash. Start with the idea that unlocks the rest: an honest set of accounts is not a record of when money left the bank - it is a record of when value was used up or created. Cash accounting (money in, money out) is simple and it lies to an asset manager, because the cash and the value almost never move at the same time: you pay for a spare months before you fit it, you do the work weeks before the invoice arrives, you buy an asset once and consume it over twenty years. If you manage by cash, every period is distorted by the accident of when payments happened to fall. Accrual accounting lines the cost up with the value, and only that lets you see what a period truly cost and manage against it. Two principles do the lining-up.

The matching principle - cost sits in the period of the benefit. The first principle is simple to state and easy to break: put a cost in the same period as the work or benefit it belongs to, regardless of when the invoice is dated or the cash is paid. Work done in March is a March cost, even if the invoice lands in May. And the biggest application of matching in asset management is one you already know by another name: depreciation is the matching principle. When you capitalise an asset and depreciate it over its life, you are matching the cost of the asset to each of the years it delivers value - you do not charge a twenty-year pump entirely to the year you bought it, because it earns its keep over twenty years, so its cost is spread across twenty years. Matching is why.

Accruals - record when incurred, not when paid. The matching principle is a promise; the accrual is how you keep it in practice. You record a cost when it is incurred - when the work is done or the benefit received - not when the cheque clears. This is exactly the move that makes a cost report true: when work has been done this month but the supplier's invoice has not yet posted, you accrue it - you book the cost now, into the month the work happened, because that is the month it belongs to. Leave it out and this month looks artificially cheap and next month artificially dear, and both are lies. (This is the same accrual that keeps a cost report honest - the report and the principle are two views of one discipline.) Accruals are simply matching, done on the day.

Capital or operating - restore or improve. Now the classification that matters most to an asset manager and is got wrong most often, and the test is not the size of the cheque. Operating expenditure keeps an asset doing what it already does - a repair, a service, a like-for-like replacement of a worn part - and it is expensed in full, this period. Capital expenditure creates a new asset or materially extends the life, capacity or capability of an existing one - a rebuild that adds years, an upgrade that raises output - and it is capitalised (added to the asset's value) and depreciated over the years it will serve (matching, again). So the test is one question: did the spend RESTORE the asset to what it was (operating), or IMPROVE it beyond that (capital)? A worn bearing replaced with the same bearing restores - opex. A larger impeller that lifts capacity improves - capex. And because a real job is often both, the honest answer is usually to split it: the restoration portion to opex, the improvement portion to capex. (For how a capex-vs-opex choice then plays through cash, profit and the balance sheet, and how depreciation is calculated, that is the work of the financial-framing technique; here the job is to classify it correctly in the first place.)

One honest boundary - and it is an integrity line, not just a technique. The capex/opex boundary is a place of genuine judgement, especially at the margin, and that is not a failing to fear - a big overhaul really is part-restore and part-improve. But the rule that governs the judgement is absolute: you classify spend to tell the truth about what the work did, not to hit a number. Capitalising a routine repair to protect a stretched maintenance budget, or expensing a real upgrade to slip it past a capital-approval process, is not clever accounting - it is a misstatement that misleads the next decision made on those books. Where a cost is genuinely ambiguous, you do not guess and you do not pick the convenient answer: you state the basis - "classified as capital because it extends the asset's life by an estimated three years; recorded for review" - which is honest, checkable, and correctable. And you do not decide it alone: the asset manager and the accountant need each other - the asset manager knows what the work actually did to the asset, the accountant knows the rule, and the right classification is a conversation between them, not a decree by either.

Does it scale? A person who pays R8,000 to fix their car's failed gearbox has an expense - it restored the car to working order, gone this month. A person who spends R8,000 fitting a tow bar and a roof rack that let the car do a new job has bought a lasting improvement - value they will use for years. Same amount, different meaning, because one restored and one improved - and if you tracked your own money by when the value was used rather than when the cash left, you would see your life the way an asset manager must see a plant.

T4: Your Turn

Here is the pump invoice from your desk. Total: R500,000. It has two parts, plus a timing wrinkle:

Part of the jobWhat it did
Strip, re-seal, re-bearing, re-wind to original spec (R200,000)Restored the pump to its normal working condition
Larger impeller + variable-speed drive (R300,000)Raised the pump's capacity and added years to its service life
NoteR50,000 of the restoration work was done this month but the supplier's invoice has not yet posted
  1. Split it. Give the final operating amount and the final capital amount.
  2. Justify the split in one sentence, using the restore-versus-improve test.
  3. The accrual. State the one accrual required and, in one sentence, why - referencing the matching principle.
  4. The distortion, both ways. Describe what specifically goes wrong - to this year's profit and to the asset's value - if the whole R500,000 were booked to capital, and then if it were all booked to operating.
  5. The ambiguous item. Suppose R20,000 of the job could honestly be seen either way. Write the one sentence you would record for it, demonstrating "state the basis".

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, the split: operating R200,000 (the restoration), capital R300,000 (the upgrade) - and they sum to the R500,000 total, because you are classifying the spend, not changing it.

Second, the justification: the R200,000 restored the pump to the condition it was already in (a repair - operating), while the R300,000 improved it beyond that, raising capacity and extending life (capital) - the test is what the work did to the asset, not what it cost.

Third, the accrual: accrue the R50,000 of restoration work done this month, because under the matching principle the cost belongs to the period the work was performed, not the later period the invoice happens to post - leaving it out understates this month and overstates the next.

Fourth, the distortions. All to capital: this year's maintenance cost looks suspiciously low (the R200,000 repair has vanished from it), the asset's book value is overstated by R200,000, and you are now depreciating a routine repair for years as though it bought lasting value - the balance sheet carries a fiction. All to operating: this year's profit takes a R300,000 hit it should not (the upgrade should have been spread over its life), the pump's capacity improvement never appears in its book value at all, and next year the overhaul looks like an unexplained cost spike - and the maintenance budget wrongly wears a capital project.

Fifth, the ambiguous R20,000, stating the basis: something like "Classified the R20,000 realignment as capital, on the basis that it is expected to extend the pump's service life by around two years; recorded with this basis for the accountant to review." Notice it does not agonise and does not pick the convenient bucket - it classifies on a stated, reviewable reason, which is what integrity looks like under genuine uncertainty.

T5: The Guardrails

Your cost report is not a list of numbers. It is the story of your asset's year - and these principles are what make that story true.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Classifying by the size of the cheque. A big repair is still a repair, and a small upgrade is still capital. The test is restore-versus-improve, never the amount.

• Booking a mixed job wholly to one bucket. A real overhaul is often part-restore, part-improve. Split it - the restoration to opex, the improvement to capex.

• Expensing a genuine upgrade. Charging a life-extending improvement to this year hammers the profit and hides the added value from the asset base. Capitalise what lasts.

• Capitalising a routine repair. Parking a repair on the balance sheet flatters this year and carries a fiction you then depreciate for years. Expense what merely restores.

• Classifying to hit a number. Capitalising to protect a budget or expensing to dodge capital approval is not clever - it is a misstatement that misleads the next decision. Classify to tell the truth.

• Recording cost when paid, not when incurred. Cash timing is an accident; the work is the fact. Accrue for work done but not yet invoiced, into the month it happened.

• Forgetting depreciation is matching. You spread an asset's cost over its life because it delivers value over its life. That is the matching principle, not a tax trick.

• Deciding it alone. The asset manager knows what the work did; the accountant knows the rule. Classify together, and where it is ambiguous, state the basis for review.

One pump invoice, and how it is booked changes the profit, the asset value, the tax and next year's budget - because accounting follows value, not cash. Put each cost in the period of the work it belongs to (the matching principle), record it when it is incurred and not when it is paid (accruals, which is why the done-but-unposted work is accrued into its month), and classify each spend by what it did to the asset - restore it to what it was (operating, expensed now) or improve it beyond that (capital, capitalised and depreciated) - splitting a mixed job honestly between the two. Classify spend to tell the truth about what the work did, not to hit a number - and decide it with your accountant, stating the basis where the answer is genuinely a matter of judgement. The next time an invoice asks the small question "repair or improvement?", remember it is not small at all: your answer is the story your asset's year will tell, so make it a true one.
