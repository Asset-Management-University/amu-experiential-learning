Step 1 of 5

The regulation that changed in March

T1: The Ache

Somebody asks which standards and which laws this organisation is actually bound by.

You start assembling the answer and it comes from four people's memories and a folder somebody made in 2019. The person who knew the licence conditions retired. The contracts are with procurement. The environmental permits are with somebody in a different building who has always handled them and always will, until they do not.

Nobody has been careless. The obligations arrived over twenty years, one project and one regulation at a time, each landing with whoever happened to be there. No single person has ever been asked to hold the whole list.

And then the contractor mentions, in passing, that the standard was revised in March.

It is now May.

Nobody in this organisation can say what binds us, and we found out about a change from somebody outside. How do I build something that knows - and that keeps knowing?

Before you read on, decide. The contractor said it in passing, the way you mention the weather: the standard was revised in March. It is May. Nobody in this organisation knew, and nobody in this organisation could have told you the list it belongs to, because that list has never existed in one place — the obligations arrived over twenty years, one project and one regulation at a time, each landing with whoever happened to be there. Write down what you would build so that this organisation can say what binds it and keeps knowing when that changes — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and where each one leaks

• “Make a list of everything we have to comply with.” The right instinct, and a list alone is accurate on the day you write it and silently wrong afterwards. It also flattens things that behave completely differently: how a requirement binds you decides what happens when it changes, who can alter it, and whether you could ever be released from it — and a plain list has nowhere to put that.

• “Set a reminder to review the list annually.” Reasonable, and it would not have caught this one: a March revision found in a May review is still two months late, and found by you rather than by anything. A review looks at the list. The change happened at the source. Those are different places, and only one of them changes first.

• “Give it to one person to own.” Tempting, and it recreates the failure with better intentions — you now have a single point of failure who goes on leave. Notice what is actually true here: no one has ever been asked to hold the whole list, and the fix for that is not asking one person to hold it in their head instead of in several people's.

• “Focus on the statutory ones — those are the real obligations.” The most expensive assumption on this page. Some of what binds this organisation binds it because a contract it signed says so, or because it said in a document that it complies — and those bite exactly as hard, are frequently more specific than the law behind them, and were created by purchasing decisions and tenders rather than by regulators.

• “Record it against the organisation — we comply as a whole.” True and useless. An obligation owned by the organisation in general is owned by nobody in particular, lands on no specific asset, and has no evidence attached to it. The question in an inspection is never whether you believe you comply. It is what shows it, and “we comply” is not an answer to that.

Keep both facts in view: nobody here can say what binds us, and we found out about a change from somebody outside. The second is a symptom of the first, and anything you build has to answer both.

Here is how.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the register - what binds you and by what mechanism, traced to the assets it lands on, owned with its evidence named, watched at its source, and tested against a change that actually happened.

1. Find what actually binds you - and record the mechanism, because the mechanism decides everything else

Start by finding them, and record against each one HOW it binds you, because that single column decides what happens when it changes. Four mechanisms, and they behave completely differently. Statute - it binds you because it is the law, whether or not anybody has told you. Licence or permit condition - it binds you because a regulator wrote it into your authorisation, and it is often more specific and more demanding than the statute behind it. Called up by contract - a standard binds you because a contract you signed says it does, which means a purchasing decision made an obligation. Voluntarily adopted - you said you comply, in a policy or a tender, and having said so you are held to it. Mine came to 63. 11 statutory, 9 licence conditions, 24 called up by contract, 19 voluntarily adopted. Look at that shape, because it is the finding before you have analysed anything. Two-thirds of what binds this organisation was not imposed by a regulator - it was signed up to, most of it by people making a procurement decision who did not experience themselves as creating a compliance obligation. And the folder somebody made in 2019 held 38 of the 63, so 25 had never been in it at all. Not removed. Never captured, because the folder was assembled from what the assembler knew.

2. Trace each obligation to the assets and activities it actually lands on

An obligation nobody has traced to an asset is a title in a list. Trace each one to what it actually touches. Then read the two edges of the result, because both are findings. Obligations that land on nothing you own. I had four - inherited from a plant sold in 2018, from a process no longer run, and one that applies to a vessel size we have never operated. These are not harmless. They consume review effort, they make the register look diligent, and one of them was cited in a tender response last year, which is a claim to comply with something irrelevant. Retire them explicitly, with a note saying why and when - never by deletion, because somebody will otherwise re-add it in three years from the same source. Assets with no obligation traced to them. Read this one carefully and do not celebrate it. Sometimes it is true. More often it means nobody has looked - and in my case one asset class with a blank column turned out to be covered by a licence condition that names it explicitly, which nobody had traced because the licence was filed under permits rather than under standards. The blank column is a question, not an answer.

3. Give each obligation one owner - and say what they must be able to show

Two columns, and the second is the one that is almost always missing. One accountable owner per obligation - a named role, not a department, because an obligation owned by maintenance is owned by nobody at four o'clock on a Friday. Of my 63, 41 had an owner I could name and 22 did not. The 22 are not the ones nobody cares about - they are mostly the contractually called-up standards, because the contract was owned by procurement and the standard inside it was owned by nobody after signature. Now the second column: what you would show. Not what you do - what a person who does not trust you could examine. A calibration certificate. A competence record. A test result with a date. An obligation whose evidence column reads 'we comply' is an obligation you will fail to demonstrate on the day it matters, however genuinely you comply. And the evidence column has a second use that is worth more than the first: it tells you what to keep. Most organisations retain either everything or whatever the last person retained. A register that says which records demonstrate which obligation is also a records-retention policy, derived rather than guessed.

4. Build the watch - decide how you find out something changed, before somebody else tells you

This is the part that makes the register a living thing rather than a snapshot, and it is far cheaper than it looks - because obligations cluster by source. My 63 obligations come from 17 sources. A regulator, a few standards bodies, a licensing authority, several contract counterparties. You are not watching 63 things. You are watching 17, and that is a fortnightly task for one person rather than a programme. For each source, write down three things. How a change would reach us - a subscription, a published amendment list, a notice from the regulator, or the honest answer of 'it would not'. Who checks, by name. And how often, set by how fast that source moves rather than by a uniform quarterly habit - a standards body revising on a five-year cycle does not need the attention a licensing authority does. Then be honest about the sources where the answer is 'it would not reach us'. I had five. Writing 'no mechanism' in that column is not an admission of failure - it is the most valuable cell in the register, because it is the only way anybody will ever decide whether to build one. An obligation you cannot detect a change in is an obligation you comply with by luck.

5. Test the whole thing against one real change - and follow it all the way to the procedures

A register is only as good as its behaviour on a real change, so take one that has actually happened and follow it through. Mine: a standard revised in March. What the register told me. It is called up by three contracts, so three counterparties are affected and one of them has a clause requiring us to notify them. It lands on two asset classes. Its owner is named. And five procedures quote the superseded clause number, which the register knew because the evidence column had pointed at those procedures. Now the uncomfortable measurement, and take it honestly: how long did it take us to notice? Mine was seven weeks, and we found out from a contractor rather than from the watch. Write that number down in the review. It is the single best measure of whether any of this is working, it is far more useful than a count of obligations, and it will be uncomfortable the first time. Then fix the specific thing that failed - in my case a source with no subscription against it, which is a small fix that would not have been found by any amount of reviewing the register itself. A register that has never been tested against a real change is a document. One that has been tested twice is an instrument.

Box 1 holds the finding that arrives before any analysis, and box 4 is what makes this a living thing. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

An obligations register is not a list of standards. It is a working instrument that knows what binds you, what each obligation touches, who owns it, what would demonstrate it, and how you would find out it had changed. Five moves:

  • Find them, and record the mechanism. Statute, licence condition, called up by contract, voluntarily adopted. The mechanism decides what happens when it changes.
  • Trace each to the assets it lands on. Both edges are findings - obligations touching nothing you own, and assets with a blank column.
  • One owner, and the evidence. What a person who does not trust you could examine.
  • Watch the source, not the obligation. Sixty-three obligations, seventeen sources.
  • Test it on a real change. And measure how long it took you to notice.

The shape that is the first finding. Sixty-three obligations: 11 statutory, 9 licence conditions, 24 called up by contract, 19 voluntarily adopted. Two-thirds of what binds this organisation was not imposed on it - it was signed up to, most of it by people making a purchasing decision who did not experience themselves as creating a compliance obligation. And the 2019 folder held 38 of the 63, so 25 had never been in it - not removed, never captured, because a folder assembled from what the assembler knew contains what the assembler knew.

Why the mechanism column does the work. A statute changes and you must comply. A licence condition changes and the regulator will usually tell you, but on their timetable. A contractually called-up standard changes and nothing happens at all until somebody reads the contract - which is why 22 of the 63 had no owner, and why nearly all 22 were the contractual ones: procurement owned the contract, and after signature the standard inside it belonged to nobody.

The evidence column, and its second use. "We comply" is not evidence. A calibration certificate, a competence record, a test result with a date - those are. And the column has a use worth more than the first: a register that says which records demonstrate which obligation is also a records-retention policy, derived rather than guessed at.

Watch the source, not the obligation. Sixty-three obligations come from seventeen sources, so this is a fortnightly task for one person rather than a programme. For each source: how a change would reach us, who checks, how often - and where the honest answer is "it would not", writing "no mechanism" in that cell is the most valuable thing in the register, because it is the only way anybody will decide whether to build one. An obligation you cannot detect a change in is one you comply with by luck.

The measurement that matters. Seven weeks, and from a contractor. Write that number down - it is a far better measure of whether this is working than any count of obligations, and it will be uncomfortable the first time.

An honest boundary. This produces a register that knows and keeps knowing. It does not tell you whether you are compliant - that is what audit does, and this register is the thing audit is conducted against. It is not legal advice, and where an obligation's interpretation is genuinely contested you need somebody qualified to say so. And a register is a claim about a moment: its whole design is to survive that, which is what box 4 is for.

T4: Your Turn

A regional transport operator. Their register looks much better than the one above.

  1. 212 entries and annual review looks like diligence. Say what is actually wrong, and be precise about which of the five moves are missing.
  2. "Every entry says Compliance Manager." Explain why this is the same defect as "no owner", not a better version of it.
  3. The resignation is the real event. Say exactly what leaves the building in four weeks, and what does not.
  4. Four weeks. You cannot do all five moves. Choose the order and justify it - what do you extract first?
  5. The watch. Two bulletins, read by one person. Design the replacement in a form that survives a resignation.
  6. The last change was caught fourteen months ago. Is that reassuring? Answer carefully.

Work it before you turn over. The point of this one is that a register can be comprehensive, reviewed, minuted - and held entirely in one person's head.

T5: One Way Through

1. What is wrong. Move 1 is half done - the obligations are listed but the mechanism is not, so nobody can tell a statute from something a contract signed them up to. Move 2 is absent: nothing is traced to an asset or activity. Move 3 is nominally present and effectively absent. Move 4 exists but as a person rather than a design. Move 5 has never been done. 212 entries reviewed annually is diligence about the list, and the list is the least valuable part.

2. Why one name for everything is the same defect. Because accountability that is universal is not assigned. The Compliance Manager cannot personally hold the calibration evidence for a workshop, the competence records for drivers and the emissions returns for a depot - so in practice each of those is done by somebody else, informally, and the register does not know who. A single owner on 212 lines records where the register was written, not where the obligations live.

3. What leaves in four weeks. The mechanism for all 212 - which are law, which are contractual, which were voluntarily adopted. The real ownership map, which exists in their head as a set of relationships. The watch, entirely. And the judgement about which of the 212 actually matter. What does not leave is the list itself, which is why the organisation will feel fine for about six months.

4. The order, and it is not the order of the five moves. Extract the watch first - it is the only one that fails silently and immediately, and it is a half-day conversation: which sources, how, how often. Then the mechanism column, because it is fast with them in the room and impossible without them. Then the real owners - walk the list and ask "who actually does this", which they can answer and nobody else can. Leave the evidence column and the asset tracing entirely. Those are slow, and they can be built by a successor from the register itself. Spend the four weeks on what only this person can give you.

5. The replacement watch. Group the 212 by source - there will be far fewer, perhaps twenty. For each: the subscription or notification route recorded as a thing the organisation holds rather than an inbox somebody reads, a named checker with a named deputy, and a frequency. Route the subscriptions to a shared address rather than a personal one, which is a ten-minute change that would have prevented the whole exposure.

6. Is fourteen months reassuring? No - and it is the most misread fact in the table. It tells you that one change was detected. It tells you nothing about how many were not, because an undetected change produces no event. A watch that has caught one change in fourteen months and a watch that has missed nine look identical from inside the organisation. The only way to know is to go and check a sample of sources against what they have actually published - which nobody has done, and which is a day's work.

What you can do now that you could not before. Find what binds you and record how. Trace each obligation to what it touches and read both edges. Give each one owner and name the evidence. Watch the source rather than the obligation, and write "no mechanism" where that is the truth. And test the whole thing against a change that really happened, measuring how long you took to notice.

Nobody in the opening was careless. Twenty years of obligations landed one at a time with whoever was there, and the job of holding the whole list was never anybody's.

The register is not the point. Knowing in April that something changed in March is the point.
