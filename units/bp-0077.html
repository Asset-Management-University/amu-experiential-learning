Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Lungisa, the design engineer at a small steel fabrication shop, thirty people. It is 07:20 on a Thursday and you are standing on the steel walkway above the skid bay; the air smells of cut steel and the sharp tang of pickling acid from the line next door. Below you, parked on a low-loader that should have left an hour ago, sits the unit your team designed: a reciprocating-compressor pulsation skid - a welded frame carrying a vessel, two pipe loops and a heavy snubber bottle, all bolted down to go to a gas plant 300 km away.

In your hand is a single A4 page, still warm from the printer. It is a failure report from the LAST skid you built - the twin of this one, shipped fourteen months ago. At 08:00 yesterday their skid cracked clean through a welded pipe support and dumped a loop of high-pressure pipe onto the deck. No one was hurt. This time. The photo shows the break: a bright, flat, beach-marked surface, like rings on a cut tree, with a small final lip that tore at the very end. Your foreman, Bhekani, is leaning on the rail beside you, not looking at the report, looking at you. "Same drawing as that one," he says, nodding at the skid below. "Same welds. I signed it. You signed it. So do we send it, or not?"

You go back through your own design file. Everything checks. You sized that pipe support for the dead weight of the loop - 240 kg - and for the peak pressure thrust, a one-off 18 kN push at the relief setting. Your stress on the weld throat came out at 84 MPa against an allowable of 138 MPa for that steel. A comfortable margin. The vessel itself was pressure-tested to 1.5 times working pressure and held for two hours, dead steady, not a weep. Every static number you can find says this skid is strong enough. By every check your code asked for, it passed. And yet its twin broke - not on the day it was installed, not under a freak overload. It ran for fourteen months, and then a support your sums said was carrying 84 MPa, comfortably below 138, simply let go.

You look harder at the photo. That flat ringed surface is not how steel looks when you overload it once and snap it - that gives a rough, grey, fibrous tear. This is different. The rings march inward from one corner of the weld toe, getting closer together, then a sudden small rough crescent where the last bit gave way. Something grew that crack, a little at a time, then finished it in an instant. You pull the operating data the client attached: the compressor runs at 590 rpm. That is 590 pressure pulses a minute, every minute the machine runs. Fourteen months, running most days. You do the rough arithmetic on the back of the report and your stomach drops: the support did not see one push of 18 kN. It saw a small push-and-relax, over and over - hundreds of millions of times. Your design checked the load that happens once. It never once asked what happens to that weld when a smaller load arrives, leaves, and comes back, a quarter of a billion times over. The static margin you trusted has gone quiet and useless in your hands. Bhekani says it again, softer: "Do we send it?"

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The crane is booked, the client needs both skids by Monday, and your file passes every check. "It passed the code - 84 MPa against 138 allowable - so send it." But the static check answers the load that happens ONCE; the twin proves a stress that is safe once can be lethal when it repeats, and your file never asked that question. "It's a freak - the twin had a bad weld, this one's fine." But the beach-marked surface is the signature of slow, ordinary fatigue, not a freak overload, and this skid runs the same 590 pulses a minute - same drawing, same race. "Just make the weld bigger to be safe." But a bigger weld with the same sharp toe still concentrates stress at the same spot; you would be adding metal without touching the thing that actually starts the crack. I am Lungisa, the design engineer at a small steel fabrication shop, standing over a reciprocating-compressor pulsation skid bound for a gas plant. In my hand is a failure report from the LAST skid we built - the twin of this one, shipped fourteen months ago. Yesterday it cracked clean through a welded pipe support and dumped a loop of high-pressure pipe on the deck; no one was hurt, this time. The foreman, Bhekani, asks the only question that matters: 'Same drawing, same welds, I signed it, you signed it - so do we send it, or not?' My design file checks: I sized that support for the dead weight of the loop (240 kg) and a one-off peak pressure thrust (18 kN); the stress on the weld throat came out 84 MPa against an allowable of 138 - a comfortable margin; the vessel held a 1.5x pressure test for two hours, not a weep. Every STATIC number says it is strong enough. And yet its twin broke - not on install, not under a freak overload, but after fourteen months of running. The break photo is not a rough overload snap; it is a bright, FLAT surface with beach-marks like tree-rings marching in from one corner of the weld toe, then a small rough crescent at the very end - something grew that crack a little at a time, then finished it in an instant. The compressor runs at 590 rpm: that is 590 pressure pulses a MINUTE, every minute it runs, fourteen months. My design checked the load that happens ONCE. It never asked what happens to that weld when a smaller load arrives, leaves and comes back hundreds of millions of times.
The static margin has gone quiet and useless in your hands, because it was answering a question nobody asked correctly. The real question is not 'is this weld strong enough for the load?' but 'how long will this weld survive a load that arrives, leaves and comes back, again and again?' A repeating load is a CYCLIC load, and the slow, invisible, then sudden damage it does has a name: FATIGUE. Each time you load and unload steel - even far below its strength - a tiny bit of damage gathers at the worst stress point, and on a welded joint that is always the weld TOE, the sharp valley where weld metal meets parent steel, which concentrates stress far above your tidy 84 MPa average. Each pulse nudges a microscopic crack open a hair; a quarter of a billion nudges and the hair becomes a crack that runs. What matters is not the peak of any one pulse but the RANGE of the swing and the NUMBER of swings. There are two numbers your file never had: (a) how many cycles until a crack STARTS - read the cyclic stress RANGE against the weld detail's S-N curve (welds are sorted into classes by how sharp the detail is; your sharp-toed fillet is a low Class F); and (b) once a crack starts, how fast it GROWS and whether it will warn you - that is fracture mechanics. The real question is not 'do I send it?' but 'how many of those small pulses can this weld take before it cracks, how fast does the crack then run, and will it leak-and-warn or guillotine like its twin?'
Five moves turn a clean static margin into an honest answer. (1) THE STATIC MARGIN ANSWERED THE WRONG QUESTION - a load that repeats is fatigue; a stress perfectly safe ONCE (84 MPa, well under 138) can be lethal if it repeats enough times. (2) THE FIRST NUMBER - HOW MANY CYCLES UNTIL IT CRACKS: plot stress range against cycles-to-failure and you get the falling S-N curve; your sharp-toed load-carrying fillet is a low Class F detail. The pulse does not swing the support a full 84 MPa - it swings it by a RANGE of only about 8 MPa, up and down, 590 times a minute. Read 8 MPa against the Class F curve (where a 40 MPa range fails at about 2 million cycles) and it lands at roughly 250 MILLION cycles - and the twin saw about that in fourteen months. The curve does not say 'safe'; it says 'you were always going to lose this race, and roughly when.' (3) THE SECOND NUMBER - ONCE IT STARTS, HOW FAST DOES IT GROW: fracture mechanics tracks one real crack; Paris' law says it lengthens a tiny amount per cycle, slowly then faster, until it reaches a critical size (set by the steel's fracture toughness) and goes in one instant - through your 8 mm throat, the last stretch takes only weeks (the rough final crescent). (4) DESIGN THE FAILURE TO WARN - leak before break: size the wall so a growing crack breaks THROUGH and leaks (a hiss, a smell, an alarm) BEFORE it reaches the length that snaps the part; the opposite is a guillotine break with no warning, what killed the twin. (5) BUY THE INFORMATION, FIX THE CAUSE: don't ship and don't scrap - attack the detail class (grind the weld toe smooth, Class F toward D, 2-3x the life; better, change to a smoother detail), add a drain hole for leak-before-break, and put a fatigue check + a leak-before-break check into the design rule permanently. (And when you read the twin's broken face, the crack started not at the toe you blamed but at a buried slag defect - so the fix grows a third leg: an NDT scan of every load-carrying weld.)

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You stop staring at the static margin, because it was never the thing that was wrong - it was simply answering a question nobody had asked correctly. You explain it to Bhekani on the back of the same report. Five moves:

  • The static margin answered the wrong question: you stop staring at the static margin, because it was never the thing that was wrong - it was simply answering a question nobody had asked correctly. The real question is not 'is this weld strong enough for the load?' It is 'how long will this weld survive a load that arrives, leaves, and comes back, again and again?' A load that repeats is a CYCLIC load, and the slow damage it does - invisible, building, then sudden - has a name: FATIGUE. When you load steel once, far below its strength, nothing happens. But each time you load and unload it, a tiny bit of damage gathers at the worst stress point, and on a welded joint that worst point is always the same place: the weld TOE, the sharp little valley where the weld metal meets the parent steel, which concentrates stress far above your tidy 84 MPa average. Each pulse nudges a microscopic crack open a hair, then it closes; hundreds of millions of nudges, and the hair becomes a crack you can see, then a crack that runs. The headline truth of fatigue is brutal and simple: a stress that is perfectly safe ONCE can be lethal if it repeats enough times - and what matters is not how big each pulse is on its own, but the RANGE of the swing and the NUMBER of swings. Your twin did not fail from weakness. It failed from fatigue, and fatigue was sizing your risk because you were not.
  • The first number - how many cycles until it cracks (the S-N curve): engineers learned long ago that for any given detail you can plot how big a stress swing it can take against how many times it can take it, and you get a falling line - the S-N curve (stress range versus number of cycles to failure). Welded joints have standard S-N curves sorted into CLASSES by how sharp and nasty the detail is: a smooth machined bar is a high class; a load-carrying fillet weld with a sharp toe, like yours, is a LOW class - a welding-code Class F detail. Now you go to the real load, and here is the thing your static eye missed: the compressor's pulse does not swing your support by the full 84 MPa. It swings it by a RANGE of only about 8 MPa - a small alternating wobble riding on top of the bigger steady stress - up and down, 590 times a minute. That sounds far too small to matter. But you read 8 MPa against your Class F curve (on which a 40 MPa range would fail at only about 2 million cycles, and the line keeps falling), and the answer lands like a stone: an 8 MPa range predicts failure at around 250 MILLION cycles - and the twin saw about a quarter of a billion in fourteen months. The S-N curve does not say 'safe.' It says 'you were always going to lose this race, and roughly when.' Your static calc was checking the wrong axis entirely - a tiny repeated swing, not the one big push, was the killer all along.
  • The second number - once it starts, how fast does it grow, and will it warn you: knowing when a crack STARTS is only half the safety case; the other half is what happens AFTER, and that is a different discipline called FRACTURE MECHANICS. Instead of an average stress, it tracks one real crack of a measured size and asks two things: how fast does it grow with each cycle, and how big can it get before the part breaks in one go? There is a rule for the growth rate - PARIS' LAW - which says a crack lengthens a tiny, predictable amount per cycle, slowly at first, then faster and faster as it gets longer. And there is a CRITICAL SIZE, set by the steel's FRACTURE TOUGHNESS, beyond which the remaining steel can no longer hold and it goes in a single instant. You run it for your 8 mm pipe-support throat: a crack growing through that throat would, in the last stretch, take only a few weeks to go from 'small' to 'critical.' That is the rough final crescent in the photo - the bit that tore in one motion when the crack reached critical size. So the slow beach-marks are Paris' law writing in the steel, and the crescent is fracture toughness running out: the surface is a recording of exactly how it died.
  • Design the failure to announce itself - leak before break: here is the part that makes Bhekani put his coffee down. On a pressure-containing part, you get to CHOOSE, by design, between two endings. If you size the wall and the steel so that a growing crack breaks all the way THROUGH the wall and LEAKS - visibly, audibly, smellably - BEFORE it reaches the critical length that snaps the part in two, then your failure announces itself: a hiss, a smell of gas, an alarm, time to shut down. That is LEAK BEFORE BREAK. The opposite - where the crack reaches critical length before it ever breaches the wall - gives you a GUILLOTINE BREAK with no warning: exactly what happened to the twin's support, where the part let go before anyone saw a thing. Designing for leak-before-break is not luck; it is a deliberate sum: prove the critical crack length is LONGER than the wall is thick, so the wall is breached and leaks first. You do not only try to stop the failure - you decide, in advance, what KIND of failure you will have if it comes, and you buy yourself a warning.
  • Buy the information, fix the cause - and the surprise the steel held: so you answer Bhekani, and the answer is the third option: you do NOT send it, and you do NOT scrap it - you buy the missing information and fix the CAUSE. The skid does not ship today, not because it is weak but because by your own S-N reading it is on the same losing race as its twin, and you now know it. The fix is at the TOE, not the throat: you do not just make the weld bigger (a bigger sharp toe still concentrates stress) - you attack the detail CLASS, grinding the weld toe smooth (a burr grind) to lift the joint from Class F toward Class D, which roughly doubles to triples the cycles it survives; better still, you change the connection so the support is a smoother, lower-stress detail, not a sharp load-carrying fillet. You add a small drain hole so a through-wall crack leaks where it can be seen, buying leak-before-break. And you add the two missing checks to the design rule permanently: every dynamic skid now gets a FATIGUE ASSESSMENT (an S-N check of the cyclic stress range against the weld's detail class and the machine's cycle count over its life) and, where it holds pressure, a LEAK-BEFORE-BREAK check; the static check stays - it was never wrong, only incomplete. But first you test your paper case against the cheapest honest evidence you hold - the recovered twin's broken face - having written your success line in advance: 'the crack will have started at the weld toe I blamed.' Most of it confirms you. The SURPRISE: under the lens the origin sits a few millimetres along, at a tiny buried slag defect the welder never saw, in a spot your S-N class never modelled - so the true starting flaw was bigger than your sum assumed and a ground toe alone would not have saved it. The static file said 'strong'; your fatigue sum said 'doomed, slowly'; the steel said 'doomed faster, and for a reason your sum could not see.' So the fix grows a third leg - an NDT scan of every load-carrying weld before sign-off - and your fatigue assessment now assumes a realistic starting flaw, not a perfect one. (Finding the flaw that already exists in service, by ultrasonics, is the kindred discipline of bp-0143; a machine that gives off a changing signal as it degrades is bp-0096.)

See where it lands. The skid does not ship today; you grind the toes, change the detail, add the drain hole and the NDT scan, and write the fatigue check and the leak-before-break check into the design rule for good. Strength answers "how hard a push can it take once"; fatigue answers "how many small pushes can it take before it breaks" - and for anything that vibrates, pulses, cycles or repeats, the second question is the one that kills you. A clean static margin is necessary and never sufficient: count the repeats, read the stress RANGE of the swing not the peak of one push, find the sharp detail where the crack will start, and then DESIGN the failure to announce itself - a leak, a hiss, a warning - rather than a guillotine break with no warning at all. The load that breaks you is rarely the big one you braced for; it is the small one you never counted, arriving again, and again, and again.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

The weld made this easy to see: a break surface you could read like tree-rings, and a number - 590 a minute - that did the arithmetic for you. The deep structure is harder to spot when there is no steel and nothing snaps. So take one guided step into a world with neither.

See the household budget that survived the geyser but ran dry anyway

Tshepiso runs a small two-van gas-delivery round and, at home, keeps the household books on a spreadsheet. Last winter a geyser burst - a R14,000 hit, all at once. It hurt, but the household took it: there was a buffer, and within two months the books were level again. By every test she trusts, the budget is sound - it survived the worst single thing that happened all year, with room to spare. And yet this month, for the first time, there is nothing left. No geyser. No big bill. No one event she can point to. The account just quietly ran dry, and the shortfall arrived all at once - a debit order that bounced on a Tuesday morning, out of a clear sky. She is staring at the same kind of clean margin you were staring at on the walkway.

Don't hunt for the big bill - there wasn't one, just as the support didn't see one 18 kN push. Ask instead: what hits this budget the way the pulse hit the weld - small, and again, and again? Look down her statement and there they are: a R99 streaming service, a R59 app subscription, a R45 "convenience" delivery fee, a R30 bank charge, three small top-ups, a R120 standing donation she forgot she set up. Each one, on its own, is nothing - far below any margin she would bother to check. That is the trap. Each is "safe once"; it is the REPEAT that does the damage, exactly the 84 MPa that was fine as a single push and lethal at a quarter of a billion. Now do the back-of-the-report sum: don't read the PEAK of any one charge, read the RANGE times the NUMBER of repeats over the year. The geyser was R14,000, once. The small recurring drains total about R500 a month - R6,000 a year, every year, and climbing as she forgets to cancel things; over three years the "harmless" repeats cost more than the geyser she braced for. Her budget check, like your static calc, was built entirely around the worst single event. It never once counted the load that repeats - which is precisely why it said "sound" right up to the Tuesday the order bounced. And notice she got no early warning: sound budget on Monday, bounced order on Tuesday - a guillotine break, not a leak. So ask her your two questions: how many months of quiet draining until trouble started, and once it started, how fast did it tip from "fine" to "dry"? And where could a leak-before-break warning have gone? A simple one: a phone alert when the balance drops below one month's drains - a hiss she would hear before the order bounces, not after. There was no weld, no crack, no MPa, and it is the same problem: something passes the check for the worst single event, then fails anyway because no one counted the small load that repeats - slowly and invisibly, then all at once, with no warning. The skill is not reading break surfaces; it is refusing to let a comfortable single-event margin answer a repeated-load question, and deliberately building the early warning so the failure speaks before it strikes.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The compressor skid was just the costume. The real shape is this: something passes every check for the load that happens once, then fails anyway - slowly and invisibly at first, then suddenly - because no one counted the load that happens over and over. That shape lives far beyond welded steel. Find it where you are:

  1. Find your repeating load. In something you own, run, design or maintain, name the thing that does not break under one big event but is hit by a small load again and again - every shift, every cycle, every login, every flex, every transaction. What is the "590 times a minute" in your world?
  2. Ask the two different questions. For that thing, write down (a) how many cycles until trouble even STARTS, and (b) once trouble starts, how fast does it get worse and when does it become dangerous? Notice they need different evidence - and that knowing only one of them is not enough to make you safe.
  3. Design for a warning, not a guillotine. When your thing finally fails, will it announce itself in time to act - a leak, an alarm, a wobble, a near-miss, a complaint - or go straight to catastrophe? What small change would convert a silent failure into a noisy, early one?
  4. Hunt the static-margin trap. Find the place where you (or your team) checked the worst single load, found a comfortable margin, and quietly assumed that settled it. Where might the REPEATED load be the real risk the single-load check can never see - and is counting the repeats worth the overhead here, or is it ceremony the risk does not justify? Be honest both ways.

You will know it worked when the evidence either lands where your single-load check assumed, or surprises you - and the surprise is the gap worth fixing. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
