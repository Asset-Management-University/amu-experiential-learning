Do I keep fixing her, or let her go?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, 21. It is dusk, the rain is starting, and you are standing on the side of the road next to the old bakkie - the first one, the one that started everything - with its hazard lights blinking and a full load of cylinders going nowhere. She has died again. And tonight, of all nights, a big client's delivery is sitting in the back, and Sipho is on the phone trying to save it, his voice tight.

The tow-truck driver looks her over, then says the thing you cannot stand to hear: "Chief, maybe it's time to let the old girl go."

Back at the depot the news is bad: the gearbox is gone, a big bill. And something in you digs in. We fix her. We've already replaced the clutch, the radiator, the alternator - we're almost through the worst. If we give up on her now, all that money was for nothing. She is not just a bakkie; she is the one your parents trusted you with, the one that built Tank You.

But you saw Sipho's face tonight. You heard how close you came to losing that client. The breakdowns are getting worse, and one day it will not be a tow - it will be a disaster on the highway. You are torn in half:

Do I keep fixing her, or let her go? I've put so much into her already - how do I decide without just throwing good money after bad?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your whole argument for keeping her is the same: look how much we've already spent - we can't waste that. But Ibraaheem says something quiet that lodges in you: "Muhammad, that money is gone whatever we do tonight. It already bought us its months of service. It can't be un-spent by fixing her again."

Before you read on, sit with that. If the money you have already poured in is gone no matter what you choose now - then it cannot be a reason to keep going. So what SHOULD the decision be based on instead?

My gut says keep fixing her - we've already poured so much in, scrapping her now would waste all of it. But Ibraaheem keeps saying that money is gone whatever I choose. Maybe the past spending isn't the question at all.
If I forget what's already spent, the real question is just: from today, what's the cheapest, most reliable way to keep deliveries running? Keep paying for an old bakkie that breaks - or pay for a sound one that doesn't?
So I compare them on the SAME footing - what each costs me per year going forward: the old one's repairs + lost-delivery days + the risk of a roadside disaster, against a replacement's price spread over its life plus its low running cost. Numbers forward, not feelings backward.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Ibraaheem was pointing at the trap that catches almost everyone: you keep paying because you have already paid. But the past spending is gone whatever you choose - so it must not get a vote. The only honest question is forward. Here is how to answer it:

  • The trap: "I've already put so much in": the money you have already spent (the 'sunk cost') is GONE whatever you choose now. It must NOT weigh on the decision - clinging to it is exactly how you throw good money after bad.
  • Set the past aside - decide FORWARD: the only real question is: from TODAY, what is the cheapest, most reliable way to do the job for the stretch of time ahead?
  • Cost to KEEP (per year): next year's likely repairs + the income lost to breakdown days + the risk and cost of a bad failure. For a worn-out asset, this climbs every year.
  • Cost to REPLACE (per year): the price of a sound replacement, minus what the old one fetches, spread over its life + its (low) running cost. Compare it to KEEP on the SAME footing - per year, not one year against many.
  • The middle path - REBUILD, but judge the WHOLE asset: rebuilding one worn part can win IF the rest is sound. A new engine in a crumbling frame is money wasted - judge the whole thing, not one part.

And one mercy for that ache you feel: the money you spent on her was not wasted. It bought you real months of work AND the information you needed to make this decision. Its job is done. Letting her go is not admitting those repairs were a mistake - it is recognising that her chapter is over.

The other trap is the opposite one: replacing in frustration without doing the sum. So do the sum - forward, per year, honest estimates not prophecies - and compare keep, rebuild, and replace on the same footing. Then the right answer is not a feeling. It is a number you can stand behind.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Ibraaheem goes to the board and lays the three choices side by side - forward, and per year, so they can be compared fairly.

See the three columns, compared on the same footing

(Rough, honest estimates - not prophecies. The clutch, radiator and alternator already paid for? Gone either way - they get NO column.)

KEEP & REPAIR - next 12 months: gearbox now ~R18,000 + likely further repairs ~R15,000 + income lost to breakdown days ~R24,000 + the risk of a bad failure ~R7,500 = about R64,500 for the year - and worse the year after.

REPLACE - a sound used bakkie: ~R95,000, minus ~R10,000 the old bakkie fetches = R85,000, spread over ~4 good years = ~R21,000 a year + low running costs ~R5,000 = about R26,000 a year - and reliable.

REBUILD - a new engine + gearbox: ~R40,000. But Sipho points at the rusted chassis and worn suspension: "We'd have a new heart in a dying body. The next failure is just the frame instead of the motor." A rebuild only wins if the REST of the asset is sound - here it is not.

The verdict: R64,500 a year (rising, unreliable) to KEEP, versus R26,000 a year to REPLACE. Forward, it is not close. Replace.

And then the part that honours the heart, not just the spreadsheet: Muhammad does not scrap her. He sells her to a young apprentice at the garage who loves fixing old bakkies - a good home - and takes one last photo of her outside the first depot. He thanks her for her service, and lets her go. The decision was cold; the goodbye did not have to be.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The thing that nearly trapped you was not the bakkie - it was the pull of all you had already spent. The moment you set that aside and looked forward, the answer became clear. That move works on anything you are tempted to keep pouring money into.

For something of your own you keep repairing:

  1. Set aside what you've already spent. It is gone whatever you choose - it bought you time and information; its job is done. It does not get a vote.
  2. Compare forward, on the same footing. What will it cost to KEEP it for the next year (repairs + the trouble it causes), versus to REPLACE it (price spread over its life + running cost)? Per year, like for like.
  3. Check the rebuild option - and the whole asset. Fixing one big part can win, but only if the rest is sound. Don't put a new heart in a dying body.
R

Do the forward sum, decide, and if it's time to let the thing go, let it go - with thanks, not guilt. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

Holding on because of what you have already spent has a name worth knowing - the sunk-cost trap (some call it the sunk-cost fallacy) - and it is one of the most expensive habits there is. The people who get ahead learn to ask only one question of a tired old asset: not what did it cost me, but what will it cost me now?
