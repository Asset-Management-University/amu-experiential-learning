Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Rosa, and you run a small, busy workshop where your team assembles metal parts. For seven months, the safety guard on the small bench press has been missing. Everyone knew. It was right there on the whiteboard to-do list. People simply got used to being extra careful around that station - it became part of the place, like a step you know to avoid.

Then yesterday Yamkela, one of your best workers, was concentrating hard on a difficult part. For a split second, he forgot. His hand was caught, and he was badly hurt - off to hospital, surgery, weeks of recovery ahead. Now you sit with the crushing weight of it: it wasn't a surprise. You saw that missing guard every single day. Everyone did. And still, nothing was done.

We all knew about it - it was on the list - so how did knowing change nothing, and how do I make sure that from now on, seeing a danger means it actually gets fixed?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to tell everyone to "be more careful" - but that feels hollow; it puts the job of dodging the danger onto people, when the danger itself should simply be gone. You think about putting up a big warning sign - but the missing guard was the warning, and it had long since faded into the scenery. You could call a meeting and stress how important safety is - but everyone already knew it was dangerous; it was on the list; more awareness changed nothing before, so why now? Then a panic to "fix everything, right now!" - except you can't fix every small thing at once, and with no order to it you'd scatter and the worst might still wait.

You're stuck between knowing this can never happen again and not knowing what was truly missing the first time. So before you read on: everyone already saw it, and it was on a list - what was actually missing between seeing it and it being gone?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The hard truth is this: seeing a danger and managing a danger are two different things, and the gap between them is where people get hurt. Five moves close that gap:

  • See the trap - visibility is not management: a danger that 'everyone knows about' or is 'on the list' creates a false feeling of comfort - it feels handled because it's been noticed. But noticing is not fixing: a known, un-owned danger is just as able to hurt someone as a hidden one, sometimes more, because the knowing lulls everyone into stepping around it instead of removing it. Awareness without action is a trap.
  • Name the gap - shared by all is held by none: the missing guard was everyone's problem, so it became no one's job. When a danger belongs to 'the team' or 'someone', it belongs to nobody, and it just drifts month after month. Diffuse awareness spread across a whole group produces no action at all - that's why more meetings and more reminders changed nothing.
  • Score the consequence: for every danger you can see, ask honestly: 'if this goes wrong, how BAD would the harm to a person be?' A loose floor tile that might cause a stumble is not the same as a missing machine guard that could take a hand. Judging the seriousness of the harm is what lets you compare your dangers instead of treating them all as one vague worry.
  • Rank out loud - and choose what waits: put the dangers in order, worst harm first, and say it plainly to your team: 'the press guard is number one - we fix it now; the loose tile is number three - it waits till next week.' This is the hard step: you ARE choosing to leave some dangers for now, but choosing on purpose and out in the open, not letting the worst one sit unnoticed behind a dozen trivial ones.
  • One name, one date: give every high-priority danger a SINGLE named owner and a deadline - not 'the team' but 'Naadia', not 'as soon as possible' but 'by Friday'. One person answerable, one day it will be gone. That single name and date - not awareness, not another reminder - is what finally turns seeing a danger into the danger being gone.

See how it would have saved Yamkela. That guard sat on the list for seven months; a replacement would have cost about R300 and taken an hour to fit. What was missing was never awareness - everyone had that - it was the bridge from seeing to safe: nobody had scored how bad the harm could be (a hand, not a stumble), nobody had ranked it out loud as number one, and nobody's name and date sat beside it. Put those in place and the press guard is fixed in week one, while the loose floor tile waits till next week - chosen on purpose, in the open. Visibility is not management; a danger shared by all is held by none, and awareness creates false comfort - so score the consequence, rank out loud, and put one name and one date on it. Turn "everyone knows" into "one person, by Friday."

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This gap between seeing and managing opens far beyond a workshop. Watch an office.

See the same three steps with a blocked fire exit

In an office, a fire exit is regularly blocked by stacked boxes of paper. Everyone sees it. People squeeze past it every day. It's a known problem - and "everyone knows" changes nothing, just like the guard. To actually close the gap, you don't send another email:

• Score the consequence: people trapped behind a blocked exit in a fire - catastrophic.
• Rank it out loud: this matters more than the broken printer; it's near the top.
• One name, one date: "Saba, these boxes move to the storeroom by 4pm today, and we keep this exit clear from now on."

You turn "everyone squeezes past it" into "one person is responsible for it being clear" - the same bridge from seeing to safe.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look around your own workplace, your home, or your team's work, and find the "missing guard" - the danger you have all seen, all walked past, all grown used to; the thing that's "on the list":

  1. Accept that seeing it isn't managing it. "Everyone knows" and "it's on the list" are not the same as it being gone - they're the false comfort that lets it linger.
  2. Score it, then rank it out loud. How bad would the harm to a person be if it went wrong? Put it in honest order against your other dangers, and say plainly which you treat now and which must wait.
  3. Give it one name and one date. A single owner, a single deadline - turn your team's "everyone knows about that" into "one person will have it fixed by this Friday".

You are not a villain in this, and the weight you feel is the weight of someone who cares - the very thing that will make your workshop safer now. What failed was never your conscience or your team's; it was a system that mistook noticing for doing, and let a danger that belonged to everyone belong to no one. Yamkela was the person that system failed to protect, and he is never the one to blame. So honour him, and everyone who works beside you, by closing the gap for good: see the danger, yes - but then score it, rank it, own it, and date it, so that knowing always, always leads to people going home whole.
