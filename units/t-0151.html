A beginning, a middle, and no end

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and the works is building a new treatment stream. The project is a flurry of activity - drawings being finalised, equipment on order, contractors mobilising. It looks healthy. But something nags at you, and it will not leave.

You start asking questions. Who defined the requirements this stream is being built to? The answer is vague: "we based it on the last one." Who is planning the handover to the operators who will actually run it? Nobody, yet. What about eventual decommissioning - when this thing reaches the end of its life? People laugh; that is decades away, not their problem.

And you see it. The project has a middle - design and build - and almost nothing else. The work that belongs at the very start was skipped, so the whole thing rests on an assumed specification. The work that belongs at the very end is ignored, so an obligation is quietly being created for someone in the future to inherit. It is a pile of activities with no map of the whole life, and things are falling into the gaps between stages that nobody owns. It is busy, and it is not whole.

My project has a beginning it skipped, a middle it is deep in, and no end anyone has planned - a heap of tasks with gaps nobody owns. How do I see the whole life of this system at once, so nothing important falls between the stages?

Before you read on, decide. The build is going well. Drawings, orders, contractors, programme — all of it moving, all of it competent. Two things are not on any list. Nobody ever properly established what this thing actually needed to do, which is why you have already rebuilt one section to suit a requirement that surfaced in month three. And nobody has planned or costed how it ends: when you raised decommissioning, somebody laughed and said that is a problem for whoever is here in thirty years. Meanwhile the commissioning spares fell between the contractor's scope and yours, and nobody has them. Write down how you would see the whole of this — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the gaps are invisible

• “Add the missing items to the project plan.” Adds the two you have noticed, and leaves you with the same instrument that failed to show you those two. Tomorrow's omission is equally invisible today.

• “Tighten the schedule and track tasks more closely.” Better management of what is on the list. Your problem is entirely off the list, and no amount of rigour applied to present items reveals absent ones.

• “Deal with disposal later — it genuinely is thirty years away.” The decision is being made now regardless: what gets built, and how, determines what removing it costs. Deferring it does not postpone the decision, only the knowledge of what was decided.

• “Have a workshop to find what we have missed.” Useful, and it finds what the people in the room happen to think of — which is why the beginning and the end are the two that get missed, since nobody currently in the room is accountable for either.

• “Assign an owner to the gaps we have found.” Right for those, and it treats a symptom. Work fell into that gap because the space between two parts of the work belonged to nobody, and there are others like it you have not found.

• Here is the structural reason: a pile of activities can only show you what is present, never what is absent. Every task on your list is real and being done well, and that is precisely why the omissions are invisible — nothing about a list of work announces the work that is not on it. So use a frame that covers the whole life from beginning to end, and the gaps appear as empty space rather than arriving later as surprises. What it shows you is that decisions belong at particular points and cannot be relocated cheaply: establishing what is genuinely needed belongs at the very start and is nearly impossible to recover afterwards — which is what your month-three rebuild cost you — and ending the thing responsibly belongs at the very end, and is almost always the stage skipped, because its cost lands on somebody who is not in the room. Those omissions are predictable rather than random, which is what makes the frame protective rather than merely tidy. One refinement so it is not applied mechanically: the stages are a frame, not a queue — a thing is operated and maintained at the same time — so what you are after is coverage, and above all somebody owning each part, because things fall into the gaps between stages that nobody owns.

There is a standard frame for exactly this, and the map fits on one page. Here it is, for the new stream.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the lifecycle map - the whole life of the system on one page, with the gaps made visible.

1. Why does a pile of activities leave gaps? (the lifecycle is the frame)

A project treated as a heap of tasks - drawings, orders, construction - has no way to show you what is MISSING, only what is present. And what is missing is predictable: the requirements that were never properly defined (a decision that belongs at the very start), and the disposal that was always going to be someone else's problem (a decision that belongs at the very end). Work falls into the gaps between stages that nobody owns. A lifecycle frame fixes this by making the whole life - beginning to end - visible at once, so the gaps show up as empty space rather than as surprises later.

2. What are the six stages, and what belongs in each? (ISO 15288)

ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288:2015 defines six life cycle stages for any man-made system. CONCEPT: identify the needs and stakeholder requirements, explore options, propose a feasible solution. DEVELOPMENT: refine requirements, design, build and verify. PRODUCTION: manufacture or construct it, and test. UTILIZATION: operate it to deliver its service. SUPPORT: maintain and sustain it. RETIREMENT: decommission, dispose, and meet the end-of-life obligations. One thing to hold: UTILIZATION and SUPPORT run CONCURRENTLY - you operate and maintain at the same time - so the stages are a frame, not a strict queue.

3. Where does each of my project's activities sit? (map the activities)

Map the new treatment stream's activities onto the stages. The needs and requirements analysis -> CONCEPT. The design and its verification -> DEVELOPMENT. The construction and commissioning tests -> PRODUCTION. The operating plan and operator training -> UTILIZATION. The maintenance strategy and spares -> SUPPORT. The decommissioning provision and disposal plan -> RETIREMENT. Now every activity has a home, and the shape of the whole life is on one page - which immediately shows you not just what you are doing, but where you are doing nothing.

4. Which stages are empty or unowned? (find the gaps)

The map's real payoff is the empty space. On the treatment-stream map, CONCEPT is nearly blank - the requirements were assumed from 'the last one', never actually defined, so the whole build rests on an unstated spec. And RETIREMENT is completely blank - there is no end-of-life provision at all, so decommissioning cost and obligation will arrive one day as a nasty surprise. Those two gaps were invisible in the flurry of activity and obvious the moment the life was mapped. Do not paper over an empty stage with a made-up task - flag it honestly as the gap it is.

5. Who owns each stage? (populate and assign)

Close the gaps by giving every stage both activities and an OWNER. CONCEPT: commission a proper requirements definition - owner, the engineering lead - before more is built on an assumption. RETIREMENT: add a decommissioning and disposal provision - owner, the asset manager - so the end-of-life obligation is planned and funded, not inherited. Now the map is not just a picture of the life; it is a check that every stage of it is populated and accountable. A gap that has an owner is a task; a gap that has nobody is a future crisis, and the whole point of the map was to turn the second into the first.

Look at box 4, because that is where the map earns its keep. It is not the populated stages that matter most; it is the empty ones - Concept nearly blank, Retirement completely blank - the two gaps that were invisible in the flurry and obvious the moment the life was laid out. The map does not just show what the project is doing; it shows where it is doing nothing. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A project is not "design it and build it"; it is a whole life, from concept to retirement. Five questions map it:

  • Why does a pile of activities leave gaps? Because a heap of tasks can only show you what is present, never what is missing - and what is missing is predictable: the requirements never properly defined at the start, and the disposal always left to someone else at the end. Work falls between stages nobody owns. The lifecycle is the frame that makes the whole life visible, so gaps show up as empty space rather than as surprises.

  • Where does each activity sit? Map every project activity into its stage - the needs analysis into Concept, the design into Development, the construction into Production, the operating plan into Utilization, the maintenance strategy into Support, the disposal provision into Retirement. Now the whole life is on one page.
  • Which stages are empty or unowned? Read the map for its gaps - the real point of the exercise. An empty stage is a category of work nobody is doing. On the treatment-stream map, Concept is nearly blank (requirements assumed) and Retirement is blank (no end-of-life provision). One honest boundary. You map the project's activities onto the lifecycle stages, to see the whole life and find the gaps. You are not doing detailed systems engineering - the requirements modelling and traceability are a separate, deeper craft - and you are not doing the economics. You are placing activities in stages so that nothing is skipped and every stage is owned. The value is completeness: the whole life visible, populated, and accountable.

Does it scale? It is the difference between a building seen as phases - design, construct, occupy, maintain, demolish - and a building seen as a heap of tasks; only the phased view makes sure nobody forgets the foundations at the start or the demolition at the end.

  1. Concept. Name one activity that belongs in the Concept stage for this generator.
  2. Support. Name one activity that belongs in the Support stage.
  3. Retirement. Name one activity that belongs in the Retirement stage.

The test of a real answer: in step 4, if you did not name Concept or Retirement, look again - those two are the stages real projects skip, because the pressure to "get on and build it" pulls attention to the middle and away from the un-glamorous start and the far-off end. Naming that tendency is half of beating it.

T5: The Guardrails

Lifecycle mapping goes wrong in a few consistent ways, most of them a version of "just build it".

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Starting at Development or Production. Jumping straight to design-and-build skips Concept, so the requirements were never properly defined and the whole project rests on an assumption. The start is a stage, not a formality.

• Ignoring Retirement. Leaving end-of-life out because it is far away turns disposal and decommissioning into a surprise cost and obligation. The end is a stage too, and it is cheapest to plan for at the beginning.

• Treating the stages as a strict queue. Utilization and Support run concurrently - you operate and maintain at the same time. The lifecycle is a frame, not a one-way conveyor.

• Populated stages, no owners. A map with activities but no named owner per stage still lets the gaps fall between people. Every stage needs someone accountable.

• Confusing mapping with engineering. The map places activities in stages; it does not design the system. Do not mistake a lifecycle map for the systems-engineering work that fills the stages.

• Faking completeness. Inventing a task to fill an empty stage so the map looks finished hides the very gap you were trying to find. Flag the empty stage honestly - the gap is the finding.

• Mapping once and filing it. The map is a live planning check, revisited as the project moves through the stages, not a one-off drawing.

Onalenna's new stream was a busy middle with no defined start and no planned end, and the gaps were invisible until the whole life was drawn on one page. Now she can map any project onto the six stages, see the entire life at a glance, find the empty stages nobody was thinking about, and give each one activities and an owner. A project is not a pile of tasks; it is a life from concept to retirement, and the map is how you make sure it has all of both. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take one real project and map it - then look first at whichever stage is emptiest.
