The engineering register says our network is worth R4.81 billion and the finance ledger says R4.34 billion, nine dashboards each give a different answer, the auditors are 23 days away, and the CFO wants me to show what our R81m upgrade actually bought - where do I start before any number on that page can be trusted?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Lwando, the asset performance lead at a regional water utility. It is 06:40 on a Tuesday in late July and you are the only car in the gravel lot at the regional office, frost still on the windscreen, coat still on, hands wrapped around the first coffee. In nine days the board meets. In twenty-three days the external auditors arrive. On your desk is a single printed page the CFO, Sibahle, slid under your door last night with one line across the top in blue pen: "Make these two numbers agree."

The page has two figures circled. The first is from the fixed-asset register - the engineering list of every pump, pipeline, reservoir and treatment works the utility owns. It says the network is worth R4.81 billion. The second is from the general ledger - the money system the accountants close every month. It says the same network is worth R4.34 billion. The gap is R470 million, and nobody in the building can say where it comes from.

You open your laptop and the dashboards bloom open one after another, the way they always do. There are nine of them now. Operations built one in a spreadsheet. The maintenance planner built one in the work-order system. Finance has a glossy one the consultants set up two years ago. The pump-station SCADA throws off its own. Each is beautiful; each is the truth to the person who built it. You pull the pump availability number: the operations dashboard says the eastern pumps ran at 94% availability last quarter; the maintenance dashboard, counting the same pumps, says 88%. A six-point gap on a single number, and you genuinely do not know which one you would defend to a stranger.

Down the corridor, Yanga the maintenance planner drops his keys on a metal desk and puts his head in. "You also got Sibahle's note?" He laughs without joy. "Last year the auditors flagged us - management letter - said our asset records were 'not reliably reconciled to the financial records.' If they say it again this year, it's a finding. A repeat finding. That goes to council. That ends up in the paper." He waits. "You're the one who's supposed to know what the assets actually delivered. So - did they? We spent eighty-one million rand on the network upgrade. Can you stand up at that board table and show it bought us anything?" And under it all, a small, tempting voice: just build a tenth dashboard. A clean one. Pull the best-looking figures from each system, lay them out nicely, present that. It would look complete by Friday. You know, somewhere, that this is exactly how you ended up with nine.

Two asset values R470 million apart, one availability number that exists in two realities, nine dashboards, an R81m upgrade I'm supposed to vouch for - where do I start before any number on that page can be trusted?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

The pull is to make the page look right by Friday. "Build the tenth dashboard - a clean, board-ready one." But a tenth report adds an eleventh number without making the underlying sources agree; build it and it still shows 94% and 88% for the same pumps, because the sources still disagree - it is how you got nine. "Pick the system that looks most official and make the others match it." But finance's glossy dashboard is one of the nine, and you do not yet know which of its numbers are even right - trusting the tidiest just enshrines its errors. "Write 'R470m - timing differences' and move on." But an auditor will ask you to explain that R470m rand by rand, and "timing differences" is a guess wearing a suit; it would not survive one question. Each shortcut spends your credibility before you know what you are vouching for.

So before you read on: stop asking "what should the board see?" Name what every report has to REST on that these nine do not, and the one thing you would write down in advance so you could not later call a half-finished reconciliation "done".

I am Lwando, the asset performance lead at a regional water utility. In nine days the board meets; in twenty-three the external auditors arrive. The CFO, Sibahle, slid a page under my door: two circled figures with 'make these two numbers agree' across the top. The engineering FIXED-ASSET REGISTER says the network is worth R4.81 billion; the financial GENERAL LEDGER says R4.34 billion. The gap is R470 million and nobody can say where it comes from. I have nine dashboards - operations built one in a spreadsheet, maintenance one in the work-order system, finance a glossy consultant-built one, SCADA its own - each beautiful, each 'the truth' to whoever built it. The pump availability is 94% on the operations dashboard and 88% on the maintenance dashboard for the SAME pumps, and I could not defend either to a stranger. Last year the auditors wrote that our asset records were 'not reliably reconciled to the financial records'; a repeat is a finding that goes to council and the papers. And Yanga, the maintenance planner, asks the real question: we spent R81 million on the network upgrade - can I stand at the board table and show it bought us anything? A small voice says: just build a TENTH dashboard, clean and board-ready, pull the best figures from each system. I know, somewhere, that this is exactly how I ended up with nine. Where do I start?
Resist the tenth dashboard. The problem is not that you have too few reports - it is that your reports do not stand on one agreed set of facts: nine dashboards disagree because each defines its terms differently and draws from a different list, and a tenth is just a tenth opinion. So ask not 'what should the board see?' but 'what does every report have to REST on?' Three foundations are missing. (1) Your two big numbers do not RECONCILE: prove the engineering register and the financial ledger describe the same assets in money terms by forcing them to meet line by line - every gap is a MIX (a thing commissioned in engineering but still 'work in progress' in finance; pipes written off in the books but physically pumping; a list double-counted; small errors), never one cause. (2) Your words do not have ONE meaning: 'available' means 'could run' to operations and 'did run and not overdue' to maintenance, which is the whole 94-vs-88 fight - so write one agreed definition, formula, source and owner per metric. (3) You cannot yet trace SPEND to OUTCOME: take the R81m, write what it was meant to deliver, what it actually delivered, and the honest gap. The real question is not 'what number do I show?' but 'can a stranger follow every rand of it?' (To reconcile VALUE you must first agree IDENTITY - that each record is one real asset;.)
Five moves turn nine dashboards into one trustworthy account. (1) RESIST THE TENTH DASHBOARD, FIND THE SOURCE - the problem is not too few reports but that none rest on one agreed, reconciled set of facts. (2) FORCE THE TWO SOURCES TO MEET, LINE BY LINE - the R470m gap is four causes: R210m the upgrade commissioned and running in the register but still 'work in progress' in the ledger; R150m of pipes the books wrote off to zero but the register carries at full value because they still pump water; R80m of double-counted valves (a contractor's list imported twice two years ago); R30m of small errors that net out. The documented reasons must SUM to the gap. (3) SET THE SUCCESS LINE FIRST AND REFUSE TO ROUND OFF - 'the reasons must total R470m exactly, and a stranger must follow every rand'; on day four the reasons stalled at R390m, R80m short, and rounding it off to 'unexplained timing' would have buried the double-counted valves forever - the success line forced the dig that found them. The books then met at about R4.55bn (the R4.34bn ledger lifted by the R210m commissioning it had never recorded; the register corrected down to meet it). (4) DEFINE EACH KEY WORD ONCE - one agreed definition ('available' = in service and capable of running on demand, excluding planned maintenance) plus its formula, source and owner, for the seven numbers the board cares about; the pumps came out at 91%, and now there is one availability number in the building. (5) TRACE SPEND TO OUTCOME, HONESTLY - the R81m promised interruptions down from 14 a quarter to under 5; it delivered 6 - real improvement, but short of target, and you can show exactly why (a recurring seal fault on one new pump). The board did not need a hero story; it needed to trust that when you said a number, it was true. The auditors raised no repeat finding - they followed the four reasons and signed off in an afternoon.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You resist the tenth dashboard. You write on your own whiteboard: the problem is not that we have too few reports - it is that our reports do not stand on one agreed set of facts. So you stop asking "what should the board see?" and ask "what does every report have to rest on?" Five moves:

  • Resist the tenth dashboard - find the unreconciled source: the first move is the one that feels most like giving up: you do NOT build the clean, board-ready tenth dashboard, however tempting it is to pull the best figures from each system and lay them out nicely by Friday. You write on your own whiteboard instead: the problem is not that we have too few reports - it is that our reports do not stand on one agreed set of facts. Nine dashboards disagree because each defines its terms differently and each draws from a different list; a tenth would just be a tenth opinion, and building it is exactly how you got nine. So you change the question. Not 'what should the board see?' but 'what does every report have to REST on?' That single shift turns a presentation problem into a foundation problem - and a foundation you can actually fix, because it has a finite number of sources, while opinions are endless. (Note that reconciling VALUE assumes you have first agreed IDENTITY - that each record points to one real, unique asset; that is a prior discipline.)
  • Force the two sources to meet, line by line: this is the ASSET-TO-LEDGER RECONCILIATION: you put the engineering asset register and the financial general ledger side by side and force them to meet item by item, proving that the list of things the engineers say you own is the same list, in money terms, that the accountants say you own. The R470m gap is never one mistake; it is a mix, and here it was four. R210m: the pump-station upgrade was commissioned and running in the engineering register but still sat in the ledger as 'work in progress', never moved across to a real asset - engineering knew it existed, finance was never told it was finished (a TIMING difference). R150m: pipeline sections the ledger had written off to zero because their accounting life ended, but which the register still carries at full value because the pipes are physically in the ground pumping water every day (a DIFFERENT-RULE difference - the books and the engineers value the same steel by different rules). R80m: DOUBLE-COUNTED valves, a contractor's list imported twice during a consultant project two years ago and never reconciled. R30m: small genuine ERRORS in both directions that netted out once you looked. Four reasons, four kinds - timing, different rule, double-count, error - and every rand of the gap belongs to one of them or you have not finished.
  • Set the success line first, and refuse to round off the uncomfortable part: before opening a single spreadsheet you write down, in advance, what FINISHED looks like, so you cannot later fool yourself into calling a half-job done: we are finished when (a) the documented reasons for the R470m gap add up to R470m, not 'about R470m', and (b) an auditor who has never met us can follow every rand without asking a question. Then reality refuses to behave. On day four the reasons you have found - the R210m commissioning, the R150m written-off pipes, the R30m of small errors - sum to R390m. You are R80m short. The tempting move is to write 'R80m - unexplained timing differences' and move on; the success line forbids it, because an auditor will ask 'explain this R80m' and you will have nothing. So you keep the instrument running and chase the missing R80m specifically - and THAT is what surfaces the double-counted valves nobody would ever have gone looking for if you had let yourself round off. The gap did not close because you were clever; it closed because your success line would not let you stop one cause short. R210m + R150m + R80m + R30m = exactly R470m, and the two numbers meet at about R4.55bn - the R4.34bn ledger lifted by the R210m commissioning the books had never recorded, with the register corrected down to meet it (the R80m double-count removed, the R150m of pipes carried at their zero accounting value) - every rand documented.
  • Define each key word once - one agreed definition: the 94%-versus-88% availability fight had a boring, powerful cause: the two teams were measuring different things and calling them the same word. Operations counted a pump as 'available' if it COULD run; maintenance counted it available only if it DID run and was not overdue for a service. You do not pick a winner - picking a winner just makes the loser keep their own number. You write ONE agreed definition - 'available' means in service and capable of running on demand, excluding planned maintenance windows - and next to it the exact formula, the source system it pulls from, and the person who owns it. You do this for the seven numbers the board actually cares about, and this short, dull document becomes the thing every report now has to obey. With one definition the pumps come out at 91% - sitting between the two old figures, because the agreed rule counts some hours each side had counted differently - and now there is only one availability number in the building. The discipline is not cleverness; it is writing the meaning down once, with an owner, so the argument cannot regrow.
  • Trace the spend-to-outcome thread, honestly: to answer the real question - did the assets DELIVER? - you trace one thread end to end, from the money to the result it was meant to buy. The R81m upgrade was justified two years ago on one promise: cut unplanned supply interruptions to the eastern reservoirs. So you lay the line of evidence out plainly. Money in: R81m, now correctly sitting in the ledger as a commissioned asset, thanks to the reconciliation. What it was meant to deliver: interruptions down from 14 per quarter to under 5. What it actually delivered: 6 per quarter. The honest verdict: the investment worked - 14 down to 6 is 8 fewer interruptions a quarter, real improvement - but it underperformed its business case, because the promise was under 5, so it missed by about 1 a quarter, and you can show exactly why (one of the two new pumps had a recurring seal fault). That honesty IS the point: the board did not need a hero story, it needed to trust that when you said a number, the number was true. What you built underneath all of it was a single source of truth - one reconciled register that agrees with the ledger, one agreed definition per term, one traceable thread from spend to outcome - so a report stopped being one person's opinion and became something anyone could check. When the auditors arrived they did not raise the repeat finding; they asked how the R470m moved, you handed them the reconciliation with its four documented reasons, and they signed it off in an afternoon. The honest trade-off: it is slow and must be maintained every period or it rots back into nine dashboards within a year - but a number an outsider can follow is worth more than nine that only flatter.

See where it lands. The two numbers meet at about R4.55bn with every rand of the R470m movement documented; the seven board metrics each have one agreed meaning, so the building has one availability figure (91%), not two; and the R81m upgrade can be shown for what it honestly was - a real improvement that fell short of its promise, with the reason named. When the auditors arrive they raise no repeat finding: they follow the four reasons and sign off in an afternoon. You cannot show what your assets delivered while many reports rest on no single trusted foundation. So do not build a tenth dashboard: force your operational and financial records to meet line by line until the documented reasons SUM to the gap, define each key word once (its meaning, formula, source and owner), and trace each spend to the outcome it promised - honest shortfall and all. A number becomes trustworthy not when it flatters you, but when a stranger with no reason to trust you can follow every rand of it; and a foundation is only as strong as its least-reconciled pair.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know the clean version - two big numbers, openly at war. The disguise is harder: when one pair of numbers agrees beautifully, and that agreement quietly stops you looking at the pair that doesn't.

See the bakery whose books balance perfectly - and still hide a leak

Athi owns a small bakery: three staff, one shopfront. Tax season is six weeks off and her accountant wants clean books. Athi is quietly proud - every month she reconciles her bank statement to her bookkeeping to the last cent, and they agree. So she tells herself the numbers are sorted. But there is a second pair she has never put side by side. Her stock system - the app where she records flour, butter, sugar bought in - says she should have about R18,000 of ingredients in the storeroom. When her baker counted the shelves last week, the real total was closer to R12,500. A R5,500 gap. Athi shrugs: "Baking's messy, stock's always a bit out," and reaches for a fresh, tidy stock summary to hand the accountant.

Notice the trick. One pair of her sources - bank versus books - reconciles beautifully, and that clean agreement is doing exactly what a pretty dashboard does: it makes her feel the numbers are trustworthy, so she stops looking. But trust earned on the bank-versus-books pair does not transfer to the stock-versus-shelf pair, which has never been forced to meet. That R5,500 is the same shape of problem as the utility's R470m - two sources describing the same things, disagreeing, with nobody owning the join - just hiding behind a part that is sorted. So walk it the way you walked the R470m, and the gap splits four ways: some is timing (a delivery booked into stock but the invoice not yet entered, or the reverse); some is different rules (does "stock" count the half-open bag of flour, or only sealed bags? the app and the baker may answer differently); some is double-counting (the same butter order keyed twice on a busy morning); and some is genuine loss (waste, spoilage, or a staff member helping themselves) - that last slice is the one Athi most needs to see, and the one a tidy summary would bury forever. She needs the same three tools: reconcile the two stock figures line by line, write one agreed meaning of "stock on hand", and - when she next spends on a new oven - trace whether it actually delivered the extra trays she is buying it for. The deep lesson: a foundation is only as trustworthy as its least-reconciled pair; one set of numbers agreeing is not proof the others do, and a clean report over an unreconciled source is the tenth dashboard in disguise.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The water utility's problem was pumps and pipelines, but the problem-type is everywhere: many reports, none of them trusted, because nothing rests on one agreed and reconciled set of facts. Find it where you are:

  1. Pick your two numbers that should agree but don't. Where do two systems describe the same thing and give different answers - your bank balance versus your spending notes, the warehouse system versus the shelf, a project "80% done" versus the budget already spent? Name the two sources and the gap.
  2. Hunt the four reasons, like the R470m. Force the two lists to meet line by line: how much of the gap is timing (one side knows something the other hasn't been told), how much is different rules (each counts it differently), how much is double-counting, and how much is genuine error? You will almost never find one cause - and the reasons must sum to the gap.
  3. Find a word two people use to mean two things. Pick a term everyone uses - "done", "active customer", "available", "in stock", "on time" - and quietly ask two people for the exact definition. Watch them differ. Write the one agreed meaning that ends the argument, and decide who has the authority to make it stick.
  4. Trace one thread from spend to outcome, and catch the tenth-dashboard urge. Take one thing you spent real money on for a specific result: lay out the money in, what it was meant to deliver (agreed before you spent?), what it actually delivered, and the honest gap. And next time a report disagrees with another, notice the urge to build a cleaner one - name what it is hiding from, and fix what is underneath instead.
R

Run the smallest test this week: pick the one gap you could reconcile in an afternoon, write your success line first ("I am finished when my listed reasons add up to the exact gap, and someone else could follow them"), then force the two lists to meet. If your reasons sum to the gap, you have found the truth; if they fall short - like the team's R80m - that remainder is a real cause still hiding. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Build the reconciliation, the agreed definition, and the spend-to-outcome thread for your own numbers together.)

This is the same problem you just felt at the water utility - many reports, no single trusted foundation - wearing your clothes instead of a utility's. The transfer is honest and exact: a number is trustworthy not when it flatters you, but when an outsider can follow every rand of it.
