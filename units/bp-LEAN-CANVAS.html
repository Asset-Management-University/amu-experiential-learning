Your whole business on one page

T1: The Ache

You are Muhammad, and Tank You is real. People are ordering. The first paying customers said yes, and you and Ibraaheem are riding high - but also drowning. Ideas fly between you every night: a second bakkie, a discount for standing orders, branded caps, a deal with the depot, an app one day. Everything feels urgent and nothing feels settled.

Then, over tea, Uncle Yusuf looks at you and asks the simplest question in the world: "So what IS this business, exactly?"

And you open your mouth - and it all tumbles out in a jumble. Gas, and delivery, and the seal, and the elderly, and the fee, and the bakkie, and word of mouth... You hear yourself going in circles. Ibraaheem jumps in to help and you end up talking over each other, telling it differently. Uncle Yusuf just nods slowly. You can feel the business in your bones - but you cannot say it simply, and if you can't say it, how can you steer it, or explain it to a customer, a helper, or a lender one day?

We can feel our whole business - but we can't say it simply, all in one place. How do we get it out of our heads and onto a single, clear page?

Before you read on, decide. Everything about Tank You is true and none of it is written down. Gas runs out mid-cooking and a refill means carrying a heavy cylinder to a depot and queueing, and you can never be sure you got a full one. You deliver a full, sealed cylinder to the door and change it over. Your customers are township households — mostly the elderly, mothers, and anyone without a car. You know the money, you know the round, you know who calls back. Tomorrow somebody who has never met you has to understand all of it in about a minute. Write down what you would put in front of them — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the four answers, and the thing none of them survives

• “Explain it properly, from the beginning.” This is what you did last night, and the three of you told it three different ways without noticing. A full explanation is complete and unshareable: it exists only while you are speaking, and the listener gets whatever order you happened to say it in.

• “Show them the numbers — what comes in, what goes out.” Honest, and it answers a question they have not asked yet. Somebody who does not know what the business is cannot make sense of what it earns.

• “Take them on the round. Let them see it.” The best of the four, and it takes a morning you do not have, and shows them one day rather than the shape of the thing. They will leave knowing how it feels and still unable to repeat it to anybody.

• “Write it all down — everything we know.” Now it is on paper, and it is the same jumble in a different place. Nothing about writing it long makes it possible to take in.

• What the four have in common is that they all treat the difficulty as one of telling, when it is one of shape. You are not short of true things about this business — you are drowning in them, and they arrive in a different order every time because nothing decides which come first or how many there are. So the answer is not a longer account but a severe constraint: one page, a fixed set of boxes, plain words. The page forces the essential apart from the merely true, and the fixed boxes do something an explanation never can — they show you which ones you cannot fill. A blank box is not a tidiness problem. It is a question your business has never had to answer, sitting in plain sight, and finding those is worth more than the page you were trying to write.

There is a humble, famous tool for exactly this: one page, a handful of boxes, your entire business made visible at a glance. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Before we explain a single box, here is what you are working toward: Tank You, the whole business, on one page. Read it like a map. Notice how the messy jumble from last night becomes something you could take in at a glance - and hand to anyone.

1. The Problem

Gas runs out at the worst moment - often mid-cooking. Getting a refill is a heavy schlep: carry the cylinder far, queue at the depot. And you can never be sure you got a truly full one.

2. Your People (who, exactly)

Township households - especially the elderly, mothers, and anyone without a car or who finds a full cylinder too heavy to carry.

3. Your Simple Promise

Gas without the schlep - a full, sealed cylinder brought to your door and changed over for you.

4. How You'll Deliver It

Order on a messaging app. We bring a full, sealed cylinder by bakkie, change it over for you, and take the empty away.

5. How You'll Reach Them

Word of mouth; a flyer at the corner shop; the messaging-app number passed from neighbour to neighbour; standing weekly orders.

6. How You'll Get Paid

Cash on delivery (or a quick phone transfer). R260 per delivered refill - the same price as other outlets; the profit (about R55) comes from buying gas in bulk and packing the routes, not from charging more.

7. What It Will Cost You

Cylinder deposits and refills (your stock), bakkie fuel and upkeep, phone airtime and data, a few flyers.

8. The One Number That Matters

Deliveries completed per trip - the delivery density. Many drops on one route makes it work; one drop per long drive sinks it.

9. Your Secret Weapon

A trusted local face (Muhammad, the people-person), the seal that proves the cylinder is full, and changing it over for you - none of which the depot offers.

That is it. Nine small boxes, plain words, no jargon - and now the business has edges. You could put this in front of Uncle Yusuf, a new helper, or a customer, and they would understand Tank You in under a minute. That clarity is the prize. Now let us walk through it, box by box, so you can build your own.

T3: The Blueprint

Each box answers one honest question. With the Tank You page still fresh, here is what each one is for - and the question to ask yourself when you fill your own.

  • 1. The Problem - What real pain are you solving, and when does it bite? (Tank You: gas dying mid-cooking; the heavy schlep of a refill.) Start here always: no problem, no business.
  • 2. Your People - Who, exactly, feels that pain most? Not "everyone" - a real, named group (the elderly, mothers without a car). The narrower, the clearer.
  • 3. Your Simple Promise - In one plain sentence, what do you promise them? Lead with the outcome ("gas without the schlep"), not the features. This is the heart of the page.
  • 4. How You'll Deliver It - The simplest way you actually keep that promise. (Order on a messaging app; bakkie brings a sealed cylinder; change it over.)
  • 5. How You'll Reach Them - How will they first hear about you? Meet them where they already are (word of mouth, the corner shop, a passed-on number).
  • 6. How You'll Get Paid - How does money reach you, and how much per sale? Be concrete (R260 a refill, cash on delivery).
  • 7. What It Will Cost You - What must you spend to make it work? (Stock, fuel, airtime, flyers.) Promise minus costs is whether you survive.
  • 8. The One Number That Matters - If you could watch only one number, what tells you it's working? (For Tank You: deliveries per trip - the density.) Every business has one; find yours.
  • 9. Your Secret Weapon - What can you offer that others can't easily copy? (A trusted local face, the seal, the changeover - things the depot will not do.)

Notice the shape: the left side is about your people and their problem; the right side is about you and how you'll win and survive. A whole business, honestly faced, on one page.

T4: Your Turn

Now you build yours. Take a business idea you actually care about - even a rough one - and fill the boxes in your own words. Don't aim for perfect; aim for honest and clear. A page you can see is a page you can fix.

Feeling the nine is a lot? Start with just the first three - Problem, Your People, Your Simple Promise. Get those clear and the rest come easier. You can always come back.

T5: The Guardrails

A one-page plan is powerful, but a few honest traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then go back and strengthen any box that needs it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• "Everyone is my customer." If it's everyone, it's no one. Narrow Box 2 to the people who feel the problem most - you can grow later.

• Describing features, not the promise. Box 3 is the outcome for them ("gas without the schlep"), not a list of what you do. Say what changes in their life.

• Too many words. If a box needs a paragraph, you haven't found the simple truth yet. Plain and short beats clever and long.

• Guessing the One Number (Box 8). Ask: what single thing, if it went wrong, would sink me? That's usually your number.

• A "secret weapon" anyone could copy. Box 9 should be hard to imitate - trust you've earned, a relationship, a real skill - not just a low price.

• Treating it as final. The canvas is a living draft. As the real world answers back, change it. That's not failure; that's it working.

That jumble you couldn't explain to Uncle Yusuf? It was never that the business was unclear - only that it had never been put in one place. Now you have the tool to do that for any idea, for the rest of your life: pin it to one honest page, and you can see it, say it, share it, and steer it. The next time someone asks "so what IS this business?", you can simply hand them the page.
