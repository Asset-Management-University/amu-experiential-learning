Nobody knew who could say stop

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and your phone rings at three in the morning. It is the night operator, and his voice is tight. The control system is showing a chlorine-dosing set-point that nobody set, and it is drifting upward. The water going out to a town, right now, may be over-dosed.

Is it a faulty comms module? Has someone got into the SCADA? He does not know, and neither do you. And then the real problem arrives, worse than the incident itself: you do not know what to do. Who decides how serious this is? Is the operator allowed to switch dosing to manual on his own? If you isolate the control system to lock out an intruder, does the whole plant trip? Who calls the water regulator, and by when?

Every one of those questions is being asked for the very first time, at three in the morning, by tired people guessing. Nobody knew who could say stop. And that - not the drifting set-point - is what is turning a controllable fault into a crisis.

Something has gone wrong in the middle of the night and we are inventing the response as we go. How do I write down, in advance and in daylight, exactly who does what, how we stop it safely, and who we must tell?

Before you read on, decide. 03:20. The chlorine dosing set-point is drifting upward, uncommanded. It might be a failing comms module. It might be an intrusion. Either way the town may be getting over-dosed water. In the last ten minutes the shift has asked, for the first time ever: is this a fault or an attack, and does it matter which right now? Should we pull the network cable? Do we have to tell anybody, and by when? And the one that has stopped everything — who is allowed to say stop? Tired people are guessing at all of it. The fault itself is controllable. Write down what should have been written down in daylight — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the inversion that saves the hour

• “Work out whether it is a fault or an attack, then respond.” The most reasonable-sounding answer and the one that costs you the night. Diagnosis takes hours; the water is being dosed now. The consequence is the same either way, and waiting to know the cause before acting is the delay that does the damage.

• “Pull the network cable — isolate it.” The instinct that is correct on an office network and dangerous here. Cut the link to a running controller mid-operation and you may lose the ability to see or command it, leaving it doing whatever it was last told to do, unsupervised.

• “Wake everybody — get all the managers on the phone.” Adds voices, not authority. Five people who each think somebody else can authorise a shutdown is the same paralysis with more noise.

• “Shut the plant down now and ask later.” Sometimes exactly right — and notice nobody on that shift knows whether they are permitted to, which is why it is not happening. The problem is not the decision; it is that its owner is undefined.

• “Sort it tonight and write it up tomorrow.” How the next one gets improvised too. Whatever is invented tonight by tired people evaporates by the weekend unless it is turned into something.

• All of it should have been settled in daylight. Classify the event by its consequence before its cause — anything that threatens the water is the top tier whether it turns out to be a module or an intruder — and let that classification itself be the trigger that wakes people and starts the external clocks, so nobody is debating the diagnosis while the clock runs. Put roles and decision rights on paper, including who may say stop, so at 03:20 that question has an answer instead of a discussion. Work out containment that is safe for this kind of system, in advance, by people who understand the process — because borrowed instincts are exactly the hazard here. Write down whom you must tell and by when, since those obligations run on clocks that started whether or not anyone noticed. And convert the night into a change afterwards. The fault was controllable. It is the improvising that is turning this into a crisis.

It is called an incident response plan, and its whole purpose is that this night should never be improvised. Here is one, worked on the dosing incident.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the one-page plan the shift should have been able to reach for at 03:20.

1. How bad is this, and who must know? (detect, analyse, classify to a tier)

03:20. The chlorine-dosing set-point is drifting upward, uncommanded - the water may be over-dosed for a town. Analyse: it is either a faulty comms module or an intrusion; either way it threatens water safety AND may be a security breach. Classify against the tiers: anything that risks public health or water quality is SEV 1. That single classification is the escalation trigger - Sev 1 means the incident lead is woken now and the clock on external notification has started. We do not debate the cause first; we classify the consequence first.

2. Who does what? (roles and notification, decided in advance)

From the plan, already written: the OPERATOR detects and holds the plant; the SHIFT LEAD classifies and may authorise going to manual; the INCIDENT LEAD (Onalenna) owns escalation, external notification and the shutdown decision; OFF-SITE OT SUPPORT is called for the technical cause. Internal notification chain: operator -> shift lead -> incident lead -> management. The whole point of writing this in daylight is that at 03:20 nobody is asking 'who is allowed to say stop?' - the answer is already on the page.

3. How do we stop it spreading WITHOUT making it worse? (OT-safe containment)

The IT instinct is to pull the network cable. Do that to a running dosing controller and you could stop dosing entirely, which is its own safety event. FIRST, DO NO HARM: revert to the last known-good set-point and switch dosing to MANUAL under the operator, so a human now controls the process. THEN isolate the SCADA's external network access - the path an intruder would use - while leaving the local control loop intact and the plant controllable. And preserve the logs. Safety and availability come before catching the attacker.

4. How do we remove the cause and safely restore? (eradicate, recover)

ERADICATE: identify and close the cause - here, a compromised remote-access account that let the set-point be changed (or, if it was the comms module, replace it). Remove the access path, not just the symptom. RECOVER: do not snap back to automatic. Confirm the dosing is right and the treated water is within the safety limits by test, keep it on manual until it reads true, and only then return to verified automatic control - watching it closely. Recovering to auto before the process is proven safe is how a second incident starts.

5. Who do we tell, and what do we change? (external notification, post-incident review)

NOTIFY: a water-quality event carries a legal duty to inform the safety/environmental regulator within a statutory window - here, within 24 hours - and that clock started at classification, not at resolution. Miss it and a handled incident becomes a compliance failure. LEARN: within days, a blameless post-incident review - what happened, how the plan held, where it did not - and the output is a REVISED plan. An incident that does not change the plan will happen again the same way. This is the box everyone skips and the one that pays.

Read box 3 twice, because it is the one that separates a plant from an office. The instinct - pull the cable, isolate the machine - is exactly wrong here: in a running works, the first duty is to keep the physical process safe, not to catch the attacker. Every decision on this page was made in daylight by people who were calm. At three in the morning, the shift does not decide; it executes.

T3: The Blueprint

A good response follows a lifecycle that is decades old and standardised (NIST SP 800-61): prepare (the plan, before), detect and analyse, contain, eradicate and recover, and learn. The one-page plan turns that into five questions:

  • How bad is this, and who must know? Detect and analyse, then classify against pre-set severity tiers with escalation triggers. Classify the consequence, not the cause - anything threatening public health is Sev 1, whether it turns out to be a fault or an attack. The tier decides who is woken and what clock starts.
  • Who does what? Response-team roles and the notification chain, defined in advance: who detects and holds, who may authorise manual control, who leads and owns external notification, who is called in from off-site. The reason you write this in daylight is so that no one at 03:20 is asking who is allowed to say stop.
  • How do we stop it spreading without making it worse? This is containment, and in operational technology it has a rule that inverts the IT instinct. In IT, a compromised server gets its cable pulled to protect the data. Do that to a running pump or dosing controller and you can trip the physical process and cause real harm. So the first rule of OT response is the first rule of medicine - first, do no harm: stabilise the process first (revert to a known-good set-point, go to manual control under an operator), then isolate the external network path while keeping the plant controllable, and preserve the logs. Safety and availability come before catching the attacker.
  • How do we remove the cause and safely restore? Eradicate the actual cause - close the compromised access path, replace the failed module - not just the symptom. Then recover with discipline: confirm by test that the process is genuinely safe and within limits, hold it on manual until it reads true, and only then return to verified automatic control. Snapping back to auto before the process is proven safe is how the second incident begins.
  • Who do we tell, and what do we change? Notify: internal escalation, and external parties within their statutory windows - a water-quality event carries a legal duty to inform the regulator, and that clock started when you classified, not when you finished. Then the post-incident review, blameless, whose output is a revised plan. An incident that does not change the plan will recur exactly as before.

And here is why it is one plan, not four. Work the cyber case, then imagine the 03:00 alarm was instead a chlorine-gas leak. Do the roles change? No - you still need an incident lead. Do you still classify by severity, still notify, still coordinate on-site and off-site? Yes. The only thing that changes is the technical procedure inside the containment box. The structure holds across operational, safety, security and environmental incidents - which is exactly what makes it an all-hazards plan.

One honest boundary. Your job as an engineer is not to be the hero commander improvising a response at three in the morning. It is to be the architect who designs the plan in daylight, so that when the alarm sounds the first move is always to make the physical process safe. You are not a forensics or security-operations specialist, and you are not writing the plan during the incident - you cannot.

Does it scale? You follow a pre-planned fire-escape route rather than designing one while the building burns; an incident response plan is the same promise, for the incidents you cannot see coming.

T4: Your Turn

A different 03:00 call. The plant's business network shows a ransomware note, and it appears to have reached the data historian that also feeds the control room's dashboards. The pumps are still running on their local controllers - for now.

  1. Classify. What severity tier is this, and why? What is the escalation trigger?
  2. Roles. Name three response-team roles and what each does first.
  3. Contain safely. Write the containment step. What must you protect first, and what would the wrong (IT-reflex) move be that could harm the process?
  4. Notify. Who must be told outside the organisation, and what starts their clock?

The test of a real answer: your step 3 must protect the running plant before it protects the data. If your first move isolates or shuts something that keeps the pumps controllable, you have written an office response for a plant - re-read the containment rule.

T5: The Guardrails

Incident response fails in a few predictable ways, and almost all of them are decided long before the incident - in whether the plan exists and what it says.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Writing the plan during the incident. The original sin. If the first time you think about roles and containment is at 03:00, you do not have a plan, you have a panic. The plan is a daylight document.

• IT-style containment in a plant. Isolating a controller or cutting power to "contain" a cyber threat can trip the physical process and create a safety event. In OT, stabilise the process first - go manual - then contain the threat.

• No severity tiers. Without them, every event is either shrugged off or treated as a full emergency. Tiers, with clear escalation triggers, are what let a plant respond proportionately.

• Undefined roles. If nobody is named as the one who may say stop, everyone assumes someone else is deciding, and the decision is not made. Name the roles before you need them.

• Forgetting the notification clock. Regulators have statutory reporting windows, and they start when the incident occurs, not when you finish handling it. A well-handled incident reported too late is still a breach.

• Skipping the post-incident review. The response ends not at recovery but at a revised plan. Skip it and you have kept the exact gap that let the incident happen.

• Recovering before it is safe. Returning to automatic control because the alarm cleared, rather than because a test confirmed the process is within limits, is how the second incident is born.

Onalenna's plant did not fail because a set-point drifted; it nearly failed because nobody knew who could say stop. Now you can make sure they always do. Classify by consequence, assign the roles in daylight, contain in a way that keeps the process safe before it catches the threat, restore only once it is proven safe, notify inside the legal clock, and let every incident rewrite the plan. That is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a bad night and a disaster. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take one real plausible incident for an asset you know and write its one-page plan before you ever need it.
