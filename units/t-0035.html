The bearing trend is rising, I get it - but is failure next week or next quarter? My supervisor needs a date to book the crane and warn the hospital, not just 'it is getting worse'. How do I turn this rising line into a defensible remaining-life estimate?

T1: The Ache

It is a Tuesday morning at a water treatment works - the plant that pushes clean water up the hill to two townships and a hospital. You are the reliability technician on shift. In your hand is a printed vibration trend sheet for Pump 3, one of the three big raw-water pumps that lift river water into the works. The sheet shows the bearing readings, month by month.

Your supervisor, Zenande, is at your desk with her arms folded. She is not angry, but she is under pressure: the plant manager has asked her for a date. "When does Pump 3 need to come out?" she says. "I have to book the crane, order the bearing, and warn the hospital we'll be on two pumps for a day. I can't book all that for 'soon'."

You look at the numbers. The bearing vibration was flat at 2.0 mm/s for most of last year. Then it began to climb: 2.8 in March, 3.6 in April, 4.5 in May, 5.4 now. You know the alarm limit on this pump is 7.1 mm/s - past that, the bearing can seize and wreck the shaft, a R180,000 repair instead of a R12,000 bearing swap. You can feel the warm hum of the running pumps through the floor.

Here is the trap pulling at you: just tell Zenande "it's fine for now" because today's reading is still under the limit. That is the easy answer. It is also the answer that gets a pump seized at 2 a.m. on a Sunday with the hospital on one pump.

Before you read on, decide. Pump 3’s bearing vibration sat flat at 2.0 mm/s through January and February. Then March read 2.8, April 3.6, May 4.5, and today, 1 June, it reads 5.4. The alarm limit is 7.1 mm/s — past it the bearing can seize and wreck the shaft, turning a R12,000 swap into a R180,000 repair. Zenande needs to book a crane and warn the hospital. Write down what you would tell her — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what “still under the limit” is worth

• “It is 5.4 against a limit of 7.1. It is fine for now.” True today and useless tomorrow, and it is the answer that ends with a seized pump at 2 a.m. on a Sunday with the hospital on one pump. A level says where you are; it says nothing about how fast you are getting there.

• “It is rising. Change it at the next planned stop.” Sensible if the next stop happens to fall inside the window, and nobody has worked out where the window ends — so this is a guess dressed as a plan.

• “Change it now and stop worrying.” Safe, and it throws away whatever life is left, books a crane in a hurry, and teaches nothing about the next bearing.

• “Watch it weekly and act when it hits the limit.” That is a plan to notice the failure as it arrives. A crane and a hospital both need warning, and neither can be arranged on the morning the alarm goes.

• “Take an average of the readings and see how it looks.” Averaging the numbers destroys the one thing that carries the answer, which is the slope.

• The answer is in the shape of the climb rather than in today’s value. Two posts are fixed: where the trouble first became visible — the flat 2.0 lifting in March — and where the job stops, at 7.1. Between those two lies the whole warning window, and today’s reading simply says how much of it is left. The steps since March are +0.8, +0.9, +0.9 — not accelerating, so a straight line at about 0.87 a month — and from 5.4 there is 1.7 to go: roughly two months. That is a date Zenande can book a crane against. And it is quoted with bounds, not as a single lucky number, because the slope is measured from four points and the next month could bend it — which is exactly why she keeps measuring after she has booked.

You do not need to guess a date. You need a reliable, repeatable way to turn this rising trend into an honest estimate of how long the bearing has left - with bounds you can defend, not a single lucky number. Here is that method, worked on Pump 3.

T2: The Tool in Action

You have a part slowly getting worse, and you can measure how worse it is. Estimating remaining useful life (RUL) reads the condition trend - the line your measurements draw over time - and projects it to the failure point. It rests on one idea from maintenance science, the P-F interval: most parts do not fail in an instant, they decline and give signals. P is the potential-failure point (when a problem first becomes detectable - the vibration leaving its flat baseline); F is the functional-failure point (when it can no longer do its job - the alarm limit); the P-F interval is the warning window between them. RUL on any day is just how much of that window is left. Watch it on Pump 3.

Step 1 - Set the fixed posts

Healthy baseline = 2.0 mm/s (the flat, stable bearing-vibration level in January and February). Failure threshold F = 7.1 mm/s (the alarm limit; past it the bearing can seize and wreck the shaft - a R180,000 repair instead of a R12,000 swap). F is a ceiling the value climbs towards. Two fixed posts.

Step 2 - Find P, and characterise the trend

The reading was flat at 2.0 through February, then jumped to 2.8 in March and kept climbing - so P = the start of March. The rises since P: Mar->Apr +0.8, Apr->May +0.9, May->Jun +0.9 - very close to equal, about +0.87 mm/s per month. The steps are not getting bigger, so it is a straight-line (linear) trend climbing ~0.87 each month.

Step 3 - Extrapolate to F for the central estimate

Today (1 June) the reading is 5.4. Distance to F = 7.1 - 5.4 = 1.7 mm/s. At the central rate of 0.87/month: 1.7 / 0.87 = 2.0 months. So the central remaining-life estimate is about 2 months - the alarm limit is reached around early August. (Sanity-check the whole P-F interval: P at early March to F at early August is ~5 months, a believable warning window for a slowly-failing pump bearing.)

Step 4 - Put bounds on it, and act before the pessimistic one

Project around the central 0.87: a slower 0.70/month gives 1.7/0.70 = 2.4 months (reaches F mid-August); a faster 1.05/month gives 1.7/1.05 = 1.6 months (mid-to-late July). The honest answer: 'about 2 months - most likely early August, but plan for as early as mid-to-late July.' Recommend the bearing swap in the second week of July: inside the safe window with margin, and time to book the crane, order the bearing and warn the hospital now. A defensible window, not 'it is fine' and not a single brittle date.

See what the trend gave you. Not "it's fine" - which would seize the pump - and not a single brittle date, which the noise would break. A defensible window: about two months, most likely early August, possibly as early as mid-to-late July, with a recommended swap in the second week of July. The honest output of a remaining-life estimate is never one number - it is a bounded window with a recommended action set before the worst case, so the people who must book a crane, a part and a hospital notice can actually plan. A rising line became a dated, bounded, actionable answer.

T3: The Blueprint

You turn a trend into a remaining-life window in four moves.

  • 1. Set the fixed posts. The healthy baseline (the flat, stable level) and the failure threshold F (the alarm / minimum-safe value). Note whether F is a ceiling the value climbs to, or a floor it falls to.
  • 2. Find P, and characterise the trend. P is the first reading that clearly left baseline and kept moving - your warning window, and your rate maths, start there. Then: are the step sizes roughly equal (a straight line) or getting bigger (a speeding-up curve, so life is shorter than a straight line says)? Fit the simplest honest line.
  • 3. Extrapolate to F for the central estimate. Distance to go = F minus today; central rate = the average change per period since P; central RUL = distance ÷ rate; the date = today + RUL.
  • 4. Put bounds on it, and act before the pessimistic one. Project a slower and a faster trend for an optimistic and pessimistic bound; report "between X and Y, most likely Z"; schedule the work before the pessimistic bound, with margin.

The technique fits a part that degrades gradually and that you can measure repeatedly - bearing vibration, pipe wall thickness, oil-particle counts, motor temperature, tyre tread - and you need at least three or four readings since P (one or two points cannot define a trend). It does not fit parts that fail suddenly with no warning (a fuse, a snapped bolt, electronics) - there is no slope to extrapolate; nor data too noisy or sparse to see a real trend, where the single rising reading is a prompt to inspect, not a basis for a date. And one honest warning: extrapolation assumes the future behaves like the recent past - it can be wrong if conditions change (the pump runs harder, the water gets dirtier), which is exactly why you report a range and keep measuring as you close in. This is the prognosis step in a chain: a diagnostic like vibration analysis detects the fault and marks the P; this technique projects how long to F; and the P-F interval it measures is what the maintenance-task interval in RCM is built on.

Two disciplines keep the estimate honest. First, start the rate maths at P, not from the old flat baseline - the months when the part was healthy tell you nothing about how fast it is dying now, and including them flatters the answer. Second, always quote the range and act before the pessimistic bound: a single date is a hostage to noise, and the cost of being a fortnight early (a planned swap) is trivial beside the cost of being a day late (a seizure at 2 a.m.). And keep measuring as you close in - the nearer you get, the more readings sharpen the window, and a curve that starts to steepen is the part telling you the date just moved closer.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your remaining-life estimate honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• "It's still under the limit" is not "it's fine". Today's reading being below the threshold says nothing about how soon it will cross. The trend, not the level, holds the answer - a number well under the alarm can be a fortnight from it.

• Never give a single date. A lone number pretends to a precision the data does not have. Always give a window - optimistic to pessimistic - and recommend the action before the pessimistic bound, with margin.

• Start the clock at P, not at birth. Measure the rate from where the trend left baseline. Averaging in the healthy flat months makes the part look slower-dying than it is - a dangerous flattery.

• A curve is shorter than a line. If the steps are getting bigger each period, the failure is accelerating - life is shorter than a straight-line projection, so be cautious and re-measure more often.

• Three or four points, or it's a prompt not a date. One or two rising readings cannot define a trend. With too few, or data too noisy, the honest call is "inspect and keep measuring", not a forecast.

Zenande did not need reassurance and she did not need a single magic date - she needed a window she could plan a crane, a bearing and a hospital notice around, with a clear recommendation before the worst case. That is what a rising line becomes once you read it through the P-F interval: not a worry, but a schedule. Turn a rising trend into a defensible window, and you can plan the fix before the failure - early enough to be cheap and safe, late enough not to waste good life. Read the trend not the level, start the clock at P, quote the range, and act before the pessimistic bound - and the pump comes out on a Tuesday you chose, not at 2 a.m. on a Sunday it chose for you.
