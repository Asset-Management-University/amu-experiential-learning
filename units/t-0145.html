Step 1 of 5

Five thousand rows and no answers

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and two decisions are waiting on you this week. Next year's maintenance money has to be split between the raw-water system and the treatment stream, and whichever one is under-funded will be the one breaking down in the winter. And the storeroom needs to know which spares to hold, because the part that is not on the shelf at two in the morning is the part that keeps the town's water off until a supplier opens.

So the board asks you two perfectly fair questions. What does it cost us to maintain the whole raw-water system? And which class of pump fails most often across the plant?

You have an asset register with thousands of rows. And you cannot answer either question.

You look at why, and it is the same reason both times. The register is a flat list - assets typed in over years by different people in different formats, with no hierarchy that groups a pump under the system it serves, no standard classes that let one pump be compared with another, and failures logged against "the pump" rather than the part that actually broke. There is no way to roll a cost up to "the raw-water system", because nothing in the list knows what belongs to it. There is no way to compare pumps, because no two were entered the same way. You have all the data and can extract none of the answers - and adding more rows would only make it worse. The thing that is missing is not data. It is shape.

My register has everything in it and can answer nothing, because it was built as a spreadsheet of names. How do I build it as a structure - so it can roll up a cost, compare a class of asset, and trace a failure to the part that failed?

Before you read on, decide. Three reasonable questions, in one meeting. What does the raw-water system cost us to maintain in a year? How do our pumps of one class compare with another for failures? Which component actually failed on that unit in March? Your register has five thousand rows, it is genuinely maintained, and it cannot answer a single one of them. The same pump appears under three different names, no two assets were entered the same way, and every failure is logged against a whole machine. Your instinct is to add more data, and you can feel that it would not help. Write down what your register actually needs — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what is actually missing

• “Add more fields — capture more about each asset.” More columns on a flat list gives you a wider flat list. None of your three questions is unanswerable for want of a field; they are unanswerable because nothing relates one row to another.

• “Buy a proper asset management system.” Software will host a structure and will not invent one. Import this register into an excellent system and you have the same five thousand rows, more expensively, with better reporting on nothing.

• “Clean it up — standardise the names.” Genuinely necessary and it answers only one of the three. Consistent naming lets you find the pump; it still cannot roll a system cost upward or tell you which bearing failed.

• “Get everyone to enter data properly from now on.” Asks for discipline without giving anybody a scheme to be disciplined about. People invent conventions when none is supplied, which is precisely how three names for one pump happened.

• “Do the analysis by hand this once.” Buys one answer at great cost and teaches the organisation that the register cannot be trusted, so the next question gets asked of a person rather than of the record.

• What is missing is not data, it is shape. Start where it feels wrong to start — before anything is entered, name what the register must be able to answer, because those questions are the specification, and structure is the requirement rather than decoration. Three things follow. Every asset needs a classification that places it in a hierarchy — where it is, which system it belongs to, and how it subdivides into the parts that can actually be worked on — which is what makes a cost roll upward and a class compare sideways. Every asset needs one unique identity, consistently formed, because a thing entered three ways is three assets to every question you ask. And here is the discipline most registers miss: record a failure against the item that actually failed — the bearing, the seal — not against the machine containing it. That single practice is what makes failure data comparable at all, here and against any other plant. Then agree what good data looks like, so quality is a stated standard instead of the average of everybody's habits.

There are two standards for exactly this, and together they turn a list into a register. Here it is, for a slice of the works.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the structure - the same assets, built to answer questions instead of just to hold names.

1. What must this register be able to answer? (structure is the requirement)

Before a single asset is entered, name what the register must DO: roll up the total maintenance cost of the whole raw-water SYSTEM; compare the failure rate of one CLASS of pump against another across the plant; and trace any failure to the exact COMPONENT that failed. A flat list of asset names cannot answer any of these, however many rows it has. So structure is not decoration - it is the requirement. Build the register to answer these questions, or it will answer none of them.

2. How do I classify and locate every asset? (the ISO 14224 taxonomy)

ISO 14224:2016 gives a nine-level taxonomy. The upper levels (1-5) are LOCATION and FUNCTION - industry, installation, plant, and the SYSTEM the asset belongs to (raw-water, treatment, distribution). The lower levels (6-9) are the SUBDIVISION of the asset - equipment unit (the pump), subunit, COMPONENT, part. The load-bearing rule: a failure is recorded against the MAINTAINABLE ITEM that actually failed - the bearing, the seal - not against 'the pump'. That single discipline is what lets a failure here be compared to a failure in another plant, another country: same taxonomy, same boundaries, comparable data.

3. How does each asset get a unique identity? (functional location + consistent naming)

Every asset gets ONE unique identifier that encodes its place in the hierarchy - its functional location - and one consistent name. This is the structural cure for the chaos where the same pump lived in the register as 'Pump 1', 'P-01' and 'PUMP #1' and split its own history three ways. With a unique functional-location identity, an asset can be entered, found, costed and analysed as one thing. Identity is not bureaucracy; it is the difference between a pump that has a history and a pump whose history is scattered under three aliases.

4. Is each record complete, correct and conforming? (ISO 8000-110)

ISO 8000-110 turns 'data quality' from 'looks tidy' into something you can check. Each record must meet the SYNTAX (the defined format - a tag in the agreed pattern, a date in the agreed form), be SEMANTICALLY correct (each field means what it should, against the data dictionary - 'material' is a material, not a colour), and CONFORM to the register's data SPECIFICATION (the required fields are present and valid). 'Looks fine to me' is not data quality; conformance to a written specification, checkable by machine, is. That is what makes the register trustworthy enough to decide on.

5. What does the structure now let me do? (the payoff)

Now the board's questions answer themselves. ROLL UP: sum the maintenance cost of every asset under the raw-water system node - one query, because the hierarchy groups them. COMPARE: rank failures by pump CLASS across the plant - possible because every pump shares a standard class and boundary. TRACE: follow any failure to the maintainable item that caused it - because failures were recorded there, not against 'the pump'. The same 20 assets that were useless as a list are now a register that answers cost, reliability and performance questions on demand. The structure was the value all along.

Notice that box 1 comes before any asset is entered: you decide what the register must be able to answer, and the structure follows from that. By box 5, the two questions that defeated Onalenna answer themselves - not because she added data, but because she gave the data a shape. Let us name the pieces of that shape.

T3: The Blueprint

An asset register is not a spreadsheet of names; it is a structured hierarchy, and the structure is the whole value. Build it in five moves, each a question:

  • What must this register be able to answer? Start from the questions - roll up a cost by system, compare a class of equipment, trace a failure to its component - because those requirements decide the structure. A register that cannot answer them is a list, however many rows it holds.
  • How do I classify and locate every asset? Use the ISO 14224 taxonomy - a nine-level hierarchy. The upper levels (1-5) are location and function: industry, installation, plant, and the system the asset belongs to. The lower levels (6-9) are the subdivision of the asset: equipment unit, subunit, component, part. And the rule that makes reliability data worth anything: record a failure against the maintainable item that actually failed - the bearing, the seal - not against "the pump". That discipline, plus standard equipment boundaries, is what lets a failure here be compared with a failure anywhere else.
  • How does each asset get a unique identity? Every asset takes one unique identifier that encodes its place in the hierarchy - its functional location - and one consistent name. This is the structural cure for the chaos where one pump lived as "Pump 1", "P-01" and "PUMP #1" and split its own history three ways. With a single identity, an asset is one thing that can be found, costed and analysed.
  • Is each record complete, correct and conforming? Hold the data to ISO 8000-110, which turns "quality" from "looks tidy" into something checkable: correct syntax (the agreed format), correct semantics (each field means what it should, against a data dictionary), and conformance to the register's data specification (the required fields present and valid). "Looks fine to me" is not data quality; conformance to a written spec, checkable by machine, is.
  • What does the structure now let me do? Everything box 1 asked for. Roll up the raw-water system's cost in one query, because the hierarchy groups its assets. Rank pump failures by class, because every pump shares a standard class and boundary. Trace any failure to the component that caused it, because that is where it was recorded. The structure was the value all along.

One honest boundary. You build the structure: the taxonomy, the unique identity, the quality master data. You are not deciding what information to collect - that is the information strategy, a different unit - nor cleaning an already-broken dataset, nor running the CMMS work cycle. You are giving the register the shape those all depend on. A register is an architecture, not a spreadsheet.

Does it scale? A library where every book is shelved by a classification system lets you find any title and see everything you hold on a subject; the very same books piled in a heap by title let you find nothing and compare nothing - the taxonomy is the classification system, and it is the difference between a collection and a pile.

T4: Your Turn

A single treatment stream contains: a feed pump, the motor that drives it, an isolation valve, and a sand filter.

  1. Hierarchy. Place these into a short ISO 14224-style hierarchy - the system at the top, then equipment units, then a component beneath one of them.
  2. Identity. Assign a unique functional-location identifier to one of the assets (invent a sensible coding scheme and apply it).
  3. Data quality. State one ISO 8000-110 requirement the pump's record must meet - a format, a meaning, or a required field - and how you would check it.
  4. The payoff. Name one question this structured register can now answer that the old flat list could not.

The test of a real answer: in step 1, decide where you would record a failed bearing on the pump. If you would log it against "the pump" rather than against the bearing as a component beneath it, re-read the taxonomy - recording failures at the maintainable item is exactly what lets you compare and analyse them later.

T5: The Guardrails

Registers fail as decision tools in a few consistent ways, and every one of them is a failure of structure rather than of data volume.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• A flat list with no hierarchy. Thousands of rows and no way to roll a cost up to a system. Rows are not structure; the hierarchy is what turns a list into a register.

• No unique identity. The same asset entered three ways splits its own history and can never be costed or analysed as one thing. One asset, one identifier.

• Recording failures against "the pump". Log the failure at the maintainable item that actually failed, or you destroy the comparability the whole taxonomy exists to create.

• No standard boundaries. If one "pump" includes its motor and another does not, the two cannot be compared. Standard equipment boundaries are what make like comparable with like.

• "Looks fine" instead of conformance. Data quality is meeting a written specification you can check by machine, not a tidy-looking screen. Define the spec, then hold every record to it.

• Confusing structure with strategy or cleaning. Deciding what to collect is the information strategy; fixing an existing mess is data cleaning; running the work cycle is the CMMS. This job is the shape those all rely on - keep them distinct.

• A hierarchy deeper than the decisions. Nine levels are available, not compulsory. Build the taxonomy as deep as your decisions actually need and no deeper, or the register becomes too heavy to maintain.

Onalenna had a register with everything in it and answers to nothing, because it was built as a list of names. Now she can build it as a structure: decide what it must answer, classify and locate every asset in a standard taxonomy, give each a single identity, hold every record to a checkable quality standard, and watch the questions that defeated her answer themselves. An asset register is not where you store asset names; it is the architecture that turns them into decisions.
