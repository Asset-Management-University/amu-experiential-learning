My laptop died and I need it for my course - do I sell my investments at a loss or take on debt? Why does one small problem feel like it could wreck everything?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Katleho. The screen went black - not the gentle power-saving black, but a dead, final black. Your laptop, the one with your whole course on it, the one you need every single day, is gone. The repair shop confirms it within minutes: a new part is needed. R4,000.

A cold knot tightens in your stomach. You do not have a spare R4,000. And your mind starts racing through a short list of bad options.

You have that small investment you have been quietly building - the one you are genuinely proud of, growing slowly month by month. You could sell some of it. But the market is down right now; you would be selling low, locking in a loss, undoing months of careful progress to fix a laptop. Or, there is the store's credit offer by the till. 'Easy terms', the sign promised. But you know exactly what that interest does - you watched the snowball roll the wrong way two lessons ago. It feels like walking into a trap with your eyes open.

And the thing that really unsettles you is the size of it. This is not some huge disaster. It is one broken laptop - a normal, boring piece of bad luck that happens to everyone. Yet somehow it feels like it could knock your entire year off course.

My laptop died and I need it for my course - do I sell the investments I worked so hard to grow, at a loss, or take on debt that could crush me? Why does one small, normal problem feel like it could wreck everything?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Cornered by the R4,000, three answers tempt you - and the first two quietly do real damage. "I'll just sell some of my investments to cover it." But you would be selling at a bad moment, locking in a loss and breaking the slow compounding you fought to build - undoing the very thing you were proud of. "I'll put it on the store card, or get a quick loan." But that is the bad-debt snowball from two lessons ago: a R4,000 problem balloons into a far bigger, longer one as the interest piles up. "I don't need a boring cash buffer - I'd rather invest every spare rand for growth." But that is exactly how you ended up here: with nothing to absorb the shock, the very first emergency forces you to undo your investing anyway, at the worst possible time. Each of these treats the symptom; none fixes why a small shock became a crisis.

So before you read on: a R4,000 repair is not really that big - so why is it threatening your whole plan, and what was missing that would have turned it back into a mere inconvenience?

Notice why this small, ordinary problem feels so huge: a R4,000 repair is not actually that big - so why is it threatening to derail your whole year? Because it has nowhere to land. The shock has no choice but to smash straight into either your investments or your future. What is missing between the shock and the things you are trying to protect?
Imagine a version of you who had R6,000 sitting in a separate, easy-to-reach account - not invested, just there for exactly this. The laptop dies. What happens? You pay the R4,000, you carry on, your investments never even know it happened. The emergency becomes an afternoon, not a crisis. So what is the one thing that turns a disaster back into a mere inconvenience?
It is an emergency fund - a buffer of easy-to-reach cash, kept separate from both your spending and your investments, there to absorb life's shocks. Aim for a few months of your living costs, but ANY buffer beats none. It will not grow much - and that is the whole point: its job is to be safe and THERE, so that one bad surprise can never force you to sell your investments at a loss or fall into the debt trap. You build the fire-escape before the fire.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The laptop was never the real problem. The missing buffer was. Five moves:

  • See the real problem - the missing shock absorber: A R4,000 repair is a small, normal piece of bad luck - it should be an inconvenience, not a catastrophe. It only feels like it could wreck everything because there is no buffer to catch it. With nothing in between, the shock is forced to smash into the things you have been carefully building: your investments, or your future, in the form of debt. The problem is not the laptop. It is the gap where a buffer should be.
  • The fix - build a dedicated buffer: The answer is a separate pot of easy-to-reach cash, set aside for exactly these moments. The key word is SEPARATE: kept apart from your day-to-day spending account (so you do not drift into using it) and, just as importantly, apart from your long-term investments (so a shock never forces you to raid them). It is a fund with one job: to be there when life happens.
  • How big - start small, aim for a fortress: The classic target is three to six months of your essential living costs - enough to ride out a real setback like losing a job. But do not let that big number stop you starting. Begin where you are: even one month's worth, even a few hundred rand, is a fortress compared to zero. Any buffer is better than no buffer, and you build it the same way you build everything here - the gap, paid to yourself first.
  • Embrace 'boring' and safe - that is its superpower: This money's job is NOT to grow fast. Its job is to be safe and reachable within a day, every day. So it does not go into investments - it sits in a simple, no-fee savings account where it will barely grow. That low growth is not a weakness; it is the whole point. The emergency fund is the insurance that PROTECTS your real investments from ever being raided at the worst possible time. Do not confuse the two: one is for growing, this one is for guarding.
  • Name it, and build it FIRST: This is your emergency fund - your financial fire-escape. And it comes FIRST, before you start serious, long-term investing. It feels backwards to put money somewhere it will barely grow before putting it somewhere it will grow a lot - but it is the opposite of backwards. The buffer is what lets you stay invested through every storm without being forced to sell. You build the fire-escape before the fire ever starts.

The whole thing lives in one line: a small shock only becomes a crisis when there is no buffer to absorb it - so you build the buffer first, and a broken laptop stays a broken laptop instead of a wrecked plan. It feels strange to put money somewhere it will barely grow before putting it where it will grow a lot - but that boring, safe little fund is exactly what lets your real investments survive every storm without being raided. Emergencies are not a sign you did something wrong; they are simply life, and life always comes. The buffer is how you meet it standing up, on-plan, free - paying the surprise from a pot built for exactly that, while everything you are growing carries calmly on. Build the fire-escape before the fire, and one bad day can never again threaten the whole house.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Before you take this into your own life, watch the very same emergency hit two people who prepared differently.

See Mandla and Kgothatso face the same R4,000 surprise

Mandla and Kgothatso each face the exact same shock: the car they rely on to get to their part-time job breaks down, and the repair is R4,000.

Mandla has no buffer. To pay, he is forced to sell part of the small investment portfolio he had been proudly building - and because the market happens to be down, he sells at a loss, undoing months of progress. His only other option was a high-interest loan that would have turned the R4,000 repair into about R5,500 of debt over time, plus months of stress hanging over him. One ordinary breakdown sets his whole financial plan back.

Kgothatso has a R10,000 buffer she built up over the past year, sitting in a separate, easy-to-reach savings account - kept apart from her investments on purpose. The car breaks; she calmly transfers R4,000 to pay for it. The emergency is over by the afternoon. Her investments are completely untouched and keep right on growing. Her only task now is to trim her spending a little for a few months to top her buffer back up to R10,000. No loss, no debt, no panic - and her long-term plan never missed a beat.

Same shock, same R4,000. For one it was a setback that took months to recover from; for the other it was an inconvenient afternoon. The only difference was the buffer built in advance.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Now build your own shock absorber - before you need it:

  1. Open a separate, reachable home for it. Look at your banking app and pick (or open) a simple, no-fee savings account that is apart from your daily spending - close enough to reach in a day, far enough that you will not dip into it for nothing. That is where your buffer lives.
  2. Start it now, however small. Move a first amount in this month - even R200. The size barely matters at the start; what matters is that the fund EXISTS and is growing. Feed it the same way you feed everything here: a slice paid to yourself first, before it can be spent.
  3. Set your first target, then keep it boring. Aim first for one month of your essential costs, then work toward three to six. And resist the urge to 'make it work harder' by investing it - its whole job is to sit safe and ready, so it can protect everything else.

Life's shocks are not a question of 'if', only 'when' - the laptop, the car, the bill you never saw coming will arrive, for everyone. None of it is your fault, and none of it has to derail you. The boring little buffer you build today, before the fire, is exactly what lets you meet that day calmly, pay the surprise, and carry on climbing - your investments untouched, your plan unbroken, your freedom intact. That is you, being the change: quietly making sure that one bad day can never again threaten everything you are building.
