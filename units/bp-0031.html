Every option's good - and I'm stuck choosing

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, 21. Something wonderful and terrible has happened since you learned to really look: now you see opportunities everywhere. For a week it was thrilling. Now it is paralysing. You have three real ideas, each a genuinely good one:

  • Gas delivery - bring full cylinders to people's doors, change them over, take the empties.
  • Errand runs - help elderly neighbours carry their heavy shopping home on pension days.
  • Fix and resell - you have always been handy; mend broken kettles, irons and radios and sell them on.

And you cannot choose. Weeks pass. Each morning a different one feels like the winner, and by evening you have talked yourself out of it again. You are starting to feel like a fraud - all these ideas, all this talk, and not one step taken.

Then Ibraaheem catches you staring into space behind the counter. Not unkindly, he asks: "Still thinking about the gas? When are you actually starting?" And it spills out of you - that there are three ideas now, and you cannot pick, and you are scared of choosing wrong and wasting the little you have saved.

Ibraaheem does not laugh. He just says: "You can't compare them in your head, man. It's like trying to weigh a cloud, a rock and a bird all at once." And the real question lands - the one you could not put into words:

They're all good - that's the whole problem. So how do I actually compare them, instead of going in circles forever?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Ibraaheem's words stick: you cannot weigh a cloud, a rock and a bird in your hands at once. So how would you weigh them fairly?

Before you read on, try to see what you have been doing wrong. Each time you compare the three ideas, something keeps changing - what is it? And what would you have to hold STILL to compare them honestly?

I keep weighing them in my head, but the scale keeps changing. This morning the gas idea won because the need is huge. By lunch the repair idea won because it's cheap to start. I'm not comparing them - I'm just feeling them, one at a time.
What if I picked the few things that actually matter to me - cost to start, how soon it pays, how real the need, whether I'd stick with it - and judged EVERY option against the SAME few things?
Then it's not a feeling that shifts every hour - it's the same measures, side by side. The fog clears. One option won't be perfect, but one will be clearly good enough to start NOW.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

Here is what was really happening: you were not comparing the three ideas at all. You were judging each one, alone, by whatever measure happened to be in your head that minute. Gas won on "the need is huge." Repairs won on "it's cheap to start." They never met on the same scale - so none could ever truly win.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple. You pick the few things that matter to you, and you judge every option against the same few things. On paper, side by side. That is the whole tool:

  • 1. List your real options: the genuine candidates - not twenty daydreams, the two or three you'd actually do.
  • 2. Choose the few things that matter: the SAME measures for all of them - e.g. how little to start, how soon it pays, how real the need, whether you'd stick with it.
  • 3. Score each option, simply: High / Medium / Low against each measure. Not 1-to-10 - that fakes a precision you do not have.
  • 4. Read the pattern, not one box: the winner is rarely perfect; it is the one with the best BALANCE on the measures that matter most right now.
  • 5. Choose good-enough, and commit: you are picking the best FIRST bet, not your only bet ever. The others wait; they are not thrown away.

Two things keep this honest. First, score High / Medium / Low, not one-to-ten - a "7 versus 6" is a false precision that just hides another guess. Second, put a "heart" measure in there - something like "would I still want to do this in a month?" A choice your head can defend but your heart will quit is not the right choice. This tool is not cold maths; it is your head and your heart, finally looking at the same page.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

Ibraaheem tears the side off a cardboard box and grabs a marker - no laptop, no fancy form, just scrap and a pen. Together you choose four measures that matter right now, and score all three ideas, High / Medium / Low.

What matters: how real the need · how little to start · how soon it pays · would I stick with it

Gas delivery - need: HIGH · cost to start: MEDIUM · pays soon: MEDIUM · I'd stick with it: HIGH

Errand runs - need: HIGH · cost to start: LOW · pays soon: LOW (only near pension days) · I'd stick with it: MEDIUM (hard, tiring)

Fix and resell - need: LOW (a nice-to-have) · cost to start: LOW · pays soon: LOW (find it, fix it, find a buyer) · I'd stick with it: HIGH

The pattern: none is perfect - gas costs a bit more to start than the others. But gas has the best BALANCE on what matters most right now: a real, urgent need AND something you would happily keep doing. The choice is no longer a feeling. It is one you can defend - to Ibraaheem, and to yourself.

And notice what the matrix did NOT do: it did not hand you a magic "correct" answer, and it did not call your other two ideas stupid. It cleared the fog so you could see the trade-offs - and the errand and repair ideas are not binned, just parked. You have your first bet, not your only one.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

You walked in a ditherer and walked out with a decision you can stand behind - not because one idea was magically best, but because you finally compared them like with like. That tool is yours now, for every fork in the road.

For your own choice:

  1. List your real options. The two or three you would actually do - not every daydream.
  2. Pick the few measures that matter to YOU. Cost to start, how soon it pays, how real the need - and at least one of the heart: would you still want to do it in a month?
  3. Score every option on every measure, High / Medium / Low, on a scrap of paper. Then read the pattern, choose the clearly-good-enough one, and commit. Done beats perfect.
R

When you have drawn your grid and chosen, say your choice out loud to someone you trust. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring it there too.)

A decision you can defend - to others and to yourself - is what finally turns a person full of ideas into a person who builds. You do not need the perfect choice. You need a clear one you can start on tomorrow.
