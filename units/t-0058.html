My master fitter retires in six weeks, and 31 years of 'I can hear when it is right' walks out with him - knowledge that keeps million-Rand machines from seizing. How do I capture what he knows before it is gone?

T1: The Ache

It is a cold Tuesday morning on the floor of a heavy-maintenance workshop - the kind that overhauls draglines and dump-truck gearboxes for nearby coal mines. You are the maintenance engineering manager, standing next to Bay 4, where a stripped-down haul-truck final drive is laid out on the bench in twenty-odd labelled trays. In your hand is a single sheet of paper: Oom Kobus's retirement letter. Kobus has rebuilt these final drives for 31 years. His last day is in six weeks.

There is a smell of gear oil and hot brake cleaner in the air. Kobus himself is leaning on the bench, arms folded, watching a younger fitter struggle with the bearing pre-load. "He is setting it by feel," Kobus says quietly. "I can hear when it is right. The book gives you a torque number, but the book is wrong for this one - the housing has been line-bored twice." He taps his temple. "It is all in here, boss. Nobody wrote it down."

Your stomach drops. You run the numbers in your head: this team rebuilds 48 final drives a year, each one worth R1.9 million to a mine if it fails underground. Last year a botched pre-load seized a drive after 400 hours instead of 12 000 - a R2.3 million warranty claim and a furious client. Kobus caught the next three before they shipped. In six weeks he is gone, and with him 31 years of "I can hear when it is right". Two other senior fitters are thinking of leaving. The apprentices learn by standing next to the old hands. And the knowledge lives in three or four heads, walking out the door one by one. Sticky notes, a shared drive nobody trusts, and "go ask Kobus" is not a system.

How do I systematically capture, share and retain the critical knowledge that lives only in the heads of my experts - before it walks out the door?

Before you read on, decide. Kobus leaves in six weeks with thirty-one years and “I can hear when it is right”. The book’s torque number is wrong for a housing that has been line-bored twice, and only he knows it. Forty-eight final drives a year; last year a botched pre-load cost R2.3 million. Two more senior fitters are thinking of leaving. Write down what you would do with those six weeks. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one can actually capture

• “Get him to write it all down before he goes.” The obvious answer and it fails on the most valuable part. A torque figure writes down easily; “I can hear when it is right” does not — ask him to write that and you will get a paragraph that is true and teaches nobody anything.

• “Offer him a contract to stay on, or call him when we’re stuck.” Buys time, and buys it at the price of never doing this. It also makes the same bet again with a shorter fuse — and two other senior fitters are already thinking about leaving.

• “Pair the apprentices with him for six weeks.” Genuinely powerful and, on its own, unrepeatable: whatever they absorb leaves the building the day they do. It is a method rather than a system, and it needs to be pointed at the right jobs — six weeks is not enough for everything he knows.

• “Buy a proper document system and put everything in it.” You already have a shared drive nobody trusts. The problem is not storage — the things people trust are already stored; the dangerous knowledge was never captured in any form to store.

• “Capture everything he knows, systematically, starting now.” The right ambition and the wrong scope: thirty-one years does not fit in six weeks, and trying will produce a broad, shallow record that misses the line-bored housing. Something has to decide what gets captured first, and it should be what it costs when it is lost.

The clue is already in your own numbers: the R2.3 million failure came from something that was never in the book, and Kobus caught the next three by ear.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to capture what these people know, share it, and keep it - before it leaves the building. Here is that tool, worked on the six weeks you have left with Kobus.

T2: The Tool in Action

Two kinds of knowledge live in your team. Explicit knowledge you can already write down - a torque figure, a drawing, a checklist; easy to store and copy. Tacit knowledge lives in a person's hands and judgement - Kobus "hearing" the right pre-load; hard to write down, and it usually leaves when the person leaves. Most teams store explicit fine and are terrible at capturing tacit - and that gap is where the R2.3 million failures come from. A knowledge-management system (its backbone set out in the standard ISO 30401) is a planned way to capture, share and retain it. Watch it on Kobus's pre-load.

Step 1 - Find the critical knowledge

Walk the floor and score the tasks: final-drive bearing pre-load on line-bored housings, harm 5 x scarcity 5 = 25 (one person, huge harm - the top); boom-pin crack inspection by feel/sound, 5 x 4 = 20; gearbox shimming for re-machined cases, 4 x 3 = 12; routine oil-change, 2 x 1 = 2 (already written and known - leave it). The pre-load, score 25, is where the six weeks go first.

Step 2 - Sort explicit or tacit, and fit the capture

The base torque from the manual is explicit (easy). The CORRECTION when a housing has been line-bored - adjusting pre-load by 0.05-0.10 mm of end-float by how the housing rings when tapped - is tacit, and it is the dangerous, valuable part. So capture it person-to-person: film him rebuilding the next three drives talking every decision out loud (~40 min edited); two 90-minute sessions turning the feel into a rule - 'measure the bore; if it is more than 0.04 mm over nominal, reduce pre-load by 0.05 mm per extra 0.02 mm, then confirm by the tap-test'; and mentor two apprentices on the bench while he watches and corrects.

Step 3 - Store it, and make it flow

Create one entry, KM-014 (Final-Drive Pre-Load, Line-Bored Housings), holding the corrected procedure, the rule and a link to the videos; make the senior fitter Sphiwe its named owner. Then build it into the work: the final-drive job card now carries a line - 'Line-bored? Apply KM-014 correction and tap-test. Sign here.' - so the fitter cannot close the job without meeting it, and the video is one tap away on the bay tablet. The knowledge now travels with the task.

Step 4 - Keep it alive

Give KM-014 a review date - every 6 months, or whenever a housing is re-machined again - owned by Sphiwe. Write the danger plainly in the record: 'if this housing is line-bored a third time, the correction changes - re-measure, do not trust the old number.' Six weeks later the master retires; the next line-bored drive is rebuilt by an apprentice using the video, the rule and the job-card prompt, and Sphiwe's tap-test confirms it. The R2.3 million failure mode now has a system in front of it, not a single memory.

See what the system did. A vague dread - "31 years walks out in six weeks" - became a ranked list with one task lit up: the line-bored pre-load, score 25. You did not try to capture everything; you captured the riskiest 20% first. And the decisive move was naming the knowledge as tacit, because that chose the method: not a write-up, but filming his decisions, turning his "feel" into a numbered rule, and putting apprentices on the bench beside him. The single most important judgement is explicit-or-tacit - because the type of knowledge decides how you must capture it, and the dangerous, valuable knowledge is almost always the tacit kind nobody has written down. Stored once, owned by a name, and built into the job card so the knowledge travels with the task - the failure mode now meets a system, not a single memory.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the system in four moves - the plain backbone of ISO 30401.

  • 1. Find the critical knowledge. List the tasks where losing the know-how would hurt most, and rank them by harm if lost x scarcity (how few people hold it). The top of that list is where your scarce time goes first.
  • 2. Sort explicit or tacit, and fit the capture. Ask of the top item: can this be written plainly (explicit → a write-down), or does it live in judgement (tacit → person-to-person: record them deciding out loud, turn the feel into a numbered rule, mentor on the bench)? The type chooses the method.
  • 3. Store it, and make it flow. One trusted, searchable home with a named owner - then build it into the daily work (the checklist in the job card, the mentor on the bench), because a folder nobody opens is a dead archive.
  • 4. Keep it alive. A named owner, a review date and a trigger event, and a plain danger note. Knowledge rots.

A way to remember the arc: Find · Sort · Capture · Store · Share · Keep. The technique fits when critical knowledge sits in a few heads (the "hit-by-a-bus" risk), when losing it is expensive or dangerous, and when people are retiring, leaving or being promoted away from the tools. It is not the right tool when the knowledge is already fully written and trusted (then you just need a tidy library), when the task changes so fast yesterday's knowledge is worthless tomorrow, or when the real problem is that people won't share rather than can't - that is a trust problem, and a filing system will not fix a trust problem. Be honest about the trade-off: capturing Kobus's knowledge costs real time he could spend rebuilding, and a video will never fully replace 31 years of feel - so you capture the highest-risk 20%, not everything. A system that tries to capture everything captures nothing in time.

The check that tells you the system stands: if a new person could do the top task tomorrow using only what is in your card - the rule, the home, the flow, the owner - the system is in front of the failure. T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your knowledge system honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Tacit knowledge is the prize - and the hardest to catch. The valuable, dangerous know-how is almost always the kind nobody wrote down. If your "capture" is only filing the documents that already exist, you have missed the very thing that walks out the door. Name it tacit, and capture it person-to-person.

• A folder is not flow. Knowledge stored where nobody looks is the same as knowledge lost. Build it into the work - the job-card line, the mentoring pairing - so people meet it at the moment of need, not "some day".

• A wrong-but-trusted number is worse than none. Knowledge rots: a torque figure for a housing that gets line-bored again is dangerous if nobody updates it. Every item needs an owner, a review date and a plain danger note.

• Capture the riskiest 20%, not everything. A system that tries to record every detail records nothing in time. Rank by harm x scarcity and start at the top - the scarce, high-harm knowledge first.

• Write the explicit parts with care. The checklists and rules a capture produces are only as good as their clarity - write them with the discipline of technical communication, so the next person reads them once and acts, with no room to misread.

Kobus's 31 years were never going to fit in a folder - but the one skill that could seize a million-Rand machine now lives in a rule, a video and a job-card line that an apprentice meets every time, with a name beside it who keeps it true. A team's memory must be a managed asset, not a recurring liability: a knowledge-management system is not a library for facts - it is the engine that turns one person's hard-won craft into the whole team's strength. Capture the riskiest knowledge before it walks out the door, build it into the work, and give it an owner - and the expertise that used to leave with a retirement letter stays, and grows, in the hands of the people who come next.
