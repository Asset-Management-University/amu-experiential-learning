She was keen - then went quiet. What's really stopping her?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and you've just given a brilliant demo to the owner of a busy new restaurant. She lit up: "This could really work for us - it would save my kitchen so much chaos." You walked out ten feet tall, certain you'd landed a big account. You sent the quote, as agreed. Her reply: "Thanks, Muhammad. Looks interesting. Let me think about it, I'll get back to you."

A week passes. Silence. The glow of victory has curdled into a knot of worry. You're stuck between two bad choices: chase her, and you feel like a desperate pest; do nothing, and you feel like a pushover watching a perfect customer slip away. Worst of all is the not-knowing - you can't tell if the deal is alive or dead, and you've no idea why.

She was so keen - so what is really stopping her? And how do I find out without becoming a pest or wrecking the relationship?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your instincts scream one of two things - and both are traps. The pushy follow-up: "Hi, just checking in!"... then two days later, "Any thoughts?" You're shooting in the dark - maybe nagging her about a price she thinks is too high, maybe just irritating a busy person. It feels like pressure because it is pressure, and it rarely works. The polite retreat: "I'll respect her space, the ball's in her court" - so you do nothing, the lead goes cold, and a month later you spot a competitor's delivery outside her door. You lost it to silence, never knowing why.

Both fail for the same reason: you're acting without knowing the real obstacle. So how do you find it out - without pushing, and without vanishing?

She was keen, then went quiet - 'let me think about it'. That's almost never about thinking; it's a polite door over a real reason I can't see. Chasing feels pushy; vanishing loses it. There must be a third way.
The real reason is usually one of four: cost, trust, timing, or need. What if I gently NAME those and make it safe for her to tell me which - instead of guessing blindly and pestering?
Don't push, don't vanish: make it safe to be honest - 'is it the price, the timing, or are you just not sure we're the right fit? An honest no is totally fine.' Then respond to the REAL reason. The aim is clarity, not a forced yes.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

"Let me think about it" is almost never about thinking - it's a polite, human way of closing a door over an unspoken reason. Pushing on the door is aggressive; walking away accepts defeat. The skill is the third move: gently asking for the key. Five steps:

  • Know the small set of real reasons: 'let me think about it' is almost never about thinking - it's a polite door closed over an unspoken reason, and that reason is nearly always one of four: COST (too dear, or too much effort to switch), TRUST (not sure about you or the thing yet), TIMING (not now - a current contract, too busy, no budget this month), or NEED (they don't really need it after all). Four doors; one is the real one.
  • Avoid both traps: don't push, don't vanish: pushing ('just checking in!' again and again) feels like pressure because it IS pressure - and you're guessing blindly at which reason. Vanishing ('I'll respect her space') loses the account to silence, and you never learn why. Both fail for the same reason: you're acting without knowing the real obstacle.
  • The third move: make it SAFE to tell the truth: instead of pushing or vanishing, create a moment where honesty is easy. NAME the likely reasons yourself and invite the real one - giving them both the words and the permission to be blunt. Crucially, make 'no' a welcome answer. People hide the real reason to be polite; remove the awkwardness, and the truth comes out.
  • Respond to what's actually real: now you can help instead of guess. COST -> offer a smaller start or simple terms. TRUST -> a no-risk one-week trial, or a reference. TIMING -> a dated, welcome follow-up ('shall I check back when your contract ends?'). NEED -> thank them warmly; an honest no frees you both. You answer the real problem, not the silence.
  • The aim is CLARITY, not a forced yes: you are not trying to trap anyone into agreeing - you're turning a foggy stall into an honest conversation. An honest 'no' frees your energy for a real customer; a 'not now' gives you a real date; a real objection gives you a real problem to solve. Every honest answer is a win - including the no.

Here's the third move in real words - a short, honest message or call:

"Hi - Muhammad here from Tank You. Totally understand you need to think it over. I've found that when people say that, it usually comes down to one of a few things - and I'd genuinely value your honesty. Is it mainly the price? Is the timing just not right? Or are you not yet sure you can trust a new supplier with your kitchen? Whatever it is, please be blunt - an honest 'no' is completely fine and only helps me. Thank you!"

That short message does three quiet, powerful things: it shows you're not pushing; it hands her the words to name her hesitation; and it makes "no" a safe, welcome answer - which removes all the pressure. Now you're in an honest conversation, responding to what's real instead of guessing at silence. The aim is clarity, not a forced yes - and every honest answer, the no included, is a win.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only for selling - it's for any moment you need someone's commitment.

See the same move with a manager who says "leave it with me"

You've pitched a new project idea to your manager. She listens and says, "Interesting - leave it with me, I'll give it some thought." A week passes. Silence.

• Don't push: "Just checking if you thought about my idea?" (the pest).

• Don't retreat: say nothing, and let the idea quietly die.

• Make the third move: catch her over coffee - "Following up on that project idea. To understand the landscape: is the main hurdle likely the budget, getting buy-in from the finance team, or is the timing tricky with other priorities right now?"

Now she can answer honestly: "It's not the budget - it's that the finance team would have to sign off, and they're swamped." Suddenly you have a real next step: "That's helpful - shall I put together a one-page summary they can look at when they're ready?" You've turned a vague stall into a concrete action.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Think of one person right now whose "yes" you need - a customer, a manager, a collaborator - who seemed keen and then went vague: "sounds great", "I'll get back to you", "maybe". You're probably stuck between pushing and retreating. Take the third move instead:

  1. Acknowledge gracefully. "Totally understand you need to think it over / that you're busy."
  2. Make it safe - name the likely reasons. Offer the small set so they can simply pick: is it the cost or effort? the trust or risk? the timing? or is the need not really there?
  3. Make "no" welcome, then respond to the truth. "Honestly, any answer is a gift - even a 'not now'." Then meet the real reason: a smaller start, a trial, a dated follow-up, or a warm goodbye.

Draft that message this week and send it. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Practise the wording together first.)

The goal was never to force a yes - it was to replace fog with truth. A stall keeps you stuck, hoping, guessing. An honest answer - whatever it is - sets you free: to solve a real problem, to wait for a real moment, or to walk on to the next real customer with your energy intact. Make it safe for people to tell you the truth, and you'll spend your life responding to what's real instead of drowning in polite silence.
