The plant manager wants proof he can show the board, not a hunch - and twelve weeks to find why one furnace keeps failing. How do I run an investigation that finds the REAL cause, instead of spending twelve weeks proving what I already suspect?

T1: The Ache

It is a Monday morning at a ferrochrome smelter. You are the reliability engineer for the furnace section. On the desk is a single sheet: the tap-block replacement log for Furnace 3, printed by the maintenance planner, Zamokuhle, who is standing over you with her arms folded.

The numbers are not good. The copper tap-block - the water-cooled part the molten metal pours through - has been replaced four times in eighteen months. Each replacement costs R2.1 million in parts and 52 hours of furnace downtime, and at full tilt this furnace earns the plant about R310,000 an hour. So each failure is R2.1m + (52 x R310,000) = R18.22 million gone. Furnaces 1 and 2, the same design, have each needed one replacement in the same period.

Zamokuhle says it plainly: "The plant manager wants to know why Furnace 3, and he wants it on paper he can show the board. Not a hunch. Proof. He's given you twelve weeks." Twelve weeks, a real budget, a board that will read what you write and act on it - or not. You have done plenty of quick fault-finding before: look, guess, swap a part, move on. But this is different - this is find the true cause, prove it, cost it, and convince people who can stop the line.

You feel the pull already - the temptation to skip straight to a theory ("it's the cooling water, it's always the cooling water") and spend twelve weeks proving the thing you already believe. But a hunch dressed up as a report is still a hunch.

The plant manager wants proof he can show the board, not a hunch. How do I run an investigation that finds the REAL cause - instead of spending twelve weeks proving what I already suspect?

Before you read on, decide. Furnace 3 has eaten four copper tap-blocks in eighteen months. Furnaces 1 and 2 have had one each in the same period. Each failure costs about R2.1m in parts plus fifty-two hours of lost production at R310,000 an hour. The plant manager is sure it is the cooling water. You have twelve weeks and access to the maintenance log, the SCADA trends, the four failed blocks and the installation records. Write down how you would run the investigation — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what all of them leave undecided

• “Start with the cooling water — it is the obvious suspect and the manager already thinks so.” The fastest route to a report nobody can rely on. If the water is the cause you will have proved what was already believed; if it is not, twelve weeks are gone and the board receives a hunch with graphs attached.

• “Gather everything on all three furnaces first, then look for patterns.” This feels like rigour and produces a thick document. Without a question you cannot tell when you are finished, which findings matter, or what would have changed your mind — so whatever you happen to notice becomes the conclusion.

• “Cut the failed blocks open. The answer is in the metal.” Necessary, and it will tell you a great deal about how a block died. It cannot tell you why Furnace 3 and not Furnace 1 — and a single method, however good, has nothing to check itself against.

• “Compare Furnace 3 against 1 and 2. The difference is the cause.” The right instinct, and the comparison the whole job turns on. But three furnaces differ in dozens of ways, and a comparison finds differences rather than the one that matters most.

• “Ask the operators. They already know.” Cheap, usually right about something, and almost always skipped. It produces hypotheses — which is not the same as evidence a board can spend against.

• Every one of those is a method, and the quality of the investigation is decided before any of them is chosen. Two things decide it. First, a question tight enough to be answerable and to be wrong: not “prove the water is too hot”, but which cause dominates on this unit, in this window, against these two sister units — and which single fix buys the most reduction per rand. Second, writing down in advance what result would prove you wrong, because an investigation that cannot fail cannot inform anybody. Then braid several kinds of evidence together, so no single method can mislead you. And the arithmetic you already have is what buys the twelve weeks: four failures at about R18.22m each is roughly R72.9m over eighteen months, which is why this is worth investigating properly rather than quickly.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to run a substantial engineering investigation from a blank page to a report you can stand behind and defend - not the luck of a clever guess. Here is that method, worked on Furnace 3.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the master plan for taking a messy, expensive problem and turning it into a clear, evidence-backed solution your manager can act on. It runs in six stages across the twelve weeks - but two ideas carry it: braid several kinds of evidence so no single method can mislead you, and write down in advance what would prove you wrong so you cannot simply confirm your own hunch. Watch it on Furnace 3.

Step 1 - Scope one defensible question

Tighten 'Furnace 3 keeps eating tap-blocks' into: 'What is the dominant cause of premature copper tap-block failure on Furnace 3 (4 failures in 18 months) compared with Furnaces 1 and 2 (1 each), and which single intervention gives the greatest reduction in failure rate per rand spent?' One unit, one symptom, an 18-month window, two named sister units, the dominant cause (not pre-judged), and the best-value fix. Not 'prove the water is too hot' - that has already decided the answer.

Step 2 - Write the proposal

One page: the question; why it matters (each failure ~R2.1m parts + 52 h x R310,000/h = R18.22m; 4 failures = R72.88m over 18 months); what to measure (block lives, cooling inlet/outlet temperature and flow, tap temperature, wall thickness at autopsy, install differences); methods (failure-data analysis, root-cause analysis, thermal measurement during a tap, sister-unit comparison); data sources (the CMMS log, SCADA trends, the four failed blocks, install records); timeline (Wk1 scope, 2-5 collect, 6-8 analyse, 9-10 write, 11 cost, 12 defend). And, written first: 'if the cooling water is within spec AND the install records are identical, my cooling suspicion is wrong and I must follow the data elsewhere.'

Step 3 - Braid multiple methods until they agree

Four methods cross-check. Failure data: the block lives SHORTEN - 2,100 / 1,950 / 1,480 / 990 run-hours - a worsening trend, not random scatter. Sister comparison: F1/F2 ran 9,500 and 8,800 h to one failure each, so F3's blocks last ~1/5 as long - same design, so it is NOT the block. Thermal test: F3 cooling outlet runs 58 C against a 45 C design max, inlet normal at 32 C, flow DOWN 22% - the water leaves too hot because there is too little of it (a flow problem, not a hot-inlet problem). 5-Whys: the flow is low because F3's cooling-pump impeller is the old smaller spec (F1/F2 got a 2023 retrofit; F3's work order was closed by mistake in a planner handover). Autopsy: the worst block's wall thinned 40 mm -> 11 mm - chronic overheating, not one-off shock. All four point one way: undersized pump -> low flow -> overheating -> short block life.

Step 4 - Write to IMRD

Introduction: the R72.88m problem, the precise question, the F1/F2 comparison. Method: exactly how you pulled the data, instrumented the tap and ran the 5-Whys - written so the next engineer could repeat it. Results (facts only): the shortening lives (2,100 -> 990 h), the ~1/5 life versus the sisters, outlet 58 C and flow -22%, the smaller impeller, the 40 -> 11 mm wall loss. Discussion: the dominant cause is the un-retrofitted cooling pump; the limit (only four failures, so the trend line is short); the recommendation - fit the upgraded impeller to Furnace 3 and add a pump-spec check to the block-replacement procedure.

Step 5 - Cost it and defend it

Business case: the fix (upgraded impeller + install + 8 h planned downtime) = R0.55m + 8 x R310,000 = R3.03m, one-off; the saving (cutting from ~2.7 blocks/year to the sister rate ~0.7/year avoids ~2 failures/year x R18.22m) = R36.44m/year; payback R3.03m / R36.44m = ~0.08 year, about one month. Then defend: the maintenance head asks 'how do you know it is not bad water chemistry?' - you show the water log (in spec all 18 months) and that F1/F2 share the SAME water and do not fail. Same water, different pump, different life. The board approves the R3.03m - the defence held because the evidence was braided, not balanced on one method.

See what the method did. It refused the easy theory and instead braided four independent threads - failure data, a sister comparison, a thermal test, a 5-Whys - until they all pointed the same way: an un-retrofitted cooling pump starving Furnace 3 of flow. No single method proved the cause; together they cornered it - and that is what let the recommendation survive the hardest question in the room. When the maintenance head asked "isn't it the water chemistry?", the answer was already in the evidence: the sisters share the same water and do not fail. A defended, costed finding - a R3.03m fix that pays back in a month - rests on braided evidence, not on one brittle assumption.

T3: The Blueprint

You run a defensible investigation in five moves (the six stages, with costing and defence joined).

  • 1. Scope one defensible question. Tighten a messy complaint until it names one asset, the symptom, a measurable window and a sister baseline, asks for the dominant cause (not a pre-decided one) and the best-value single fix per rand.
  • 2. Write the proposal. A one-page contract: the question, why it matters in money, what you will measure, your methods and data sources, the timeline - and, written before you collect data, what evidence would change your mind.
  • 3. Braid multiple methods until they agree. Weave at least three (failure data, root-cause analysis, a physical test, a sister comparison); trust the cause only when they all point the same way.
  • 4. Write to IMRD. Introduction (the problem and question), Method (what you did, repeatably), Results (just the facts), Discussion (what it means, the limits, the recommendation) - the shared shape any engineer can check.
  • 5. Cost it and defend it. A one-table business case (cost, saving, payback), then answer "how do you know it is not something else?" - which you can, because you wrote the disproof in advance.

The thread through all of it: every claim is backed by evidence, and the whole story is told so clearly that a stranger could follow and repeat it - that is what separates professional research from a strong opinion. The technique fits when a quick fix is not enough and people need a board-grade answer they can trust and act on - a costly, recurring unknown. It is not the move for a fix-it-by-Friday fault (use ordinary fault-finding) or a problem too small to justify twelve weeks.

Two disciplines protect you from a confident wrong answer. First, write what would change your mind before you collect a single number - the line that names the evidence that would disprove your favourite theory is the one that keeps the whole project honest; without it, twelve weeks becomes an expensive way to confirm a bias. Second, never rest a board-grade claim on one method: a single thread can mislead, but three or four independent threads pointing the same way corner the cause - and braided evidence is what survives the hardest question in the room. Scope tight, disprove yourself early, and braid the evidence.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your investigation honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not prove your hunch - test it. The deadliest trap is spending twelve weeks confirming what you walked in believing. Write down, before you start, exactly what evidence would prove you wrong - and go looking for it.

• One method is one thread, not a case. Any single line of evidence can mislead. Braid at least three independent methods; trust the cause only when they converge on the same answer.

• Scope it or drown. A question that names no asset, no window and no baseline is a textbook, not a project. Tighten until it points at one thing you can actually answer in the time you have.

• A finding is not an action until the money is visible. Translate the technical cause into cost, saving and payback. A board acts on a business case, not on a thermal trace.

• Write it so a stranger could repeat it. The IMRD shape is not decoration - it is what lets another engineer follow your method, check your results, and trust your conclusion. Hidden steps are unprovable claims.

You can end with a one-page recommendation the board approves in a single meeting - because the cause was cornered by four agreeing methods, the disproof was written in advance, and the money was on the table. A hunch dressed up as a report is still a hunch; a defended, costed recommendation rests on braided evidence, not a single brittle assumption. Scope the question, write down what would prove you wrong, braid the methods until they agree, and tell the story so plainly that a stranger could check it - and you turn an expensive mystery into a decision people can trust.
