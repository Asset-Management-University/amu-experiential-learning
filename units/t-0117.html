Nothing broke

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and by every measure you have ever been taught, you are winning. Every pump is balanced. Every joint is sealed to spec. Every motor's insulation is tested and trended. The plant has never been more reliable - breakdowns are at an all-time low, and you are proud of it.

And yet the plant is in serious trouble.

Twice in three years, a "once-in-a-century" flood has swamped the low-lying pump house - a building nobody ever designed to sit underwater - and each time the town lost its supply for days. And now a new national carbon price has landed on the diesel and grid electricity the works runs on, quietly pushing its running cost above what the town can afford to pay for the water. Nothing broke. Your maintenance system asked "will it break?" and answered it perfectly. It just never thought to ask the other question - the one that is now sinking you.

My asset is flawlessly maintained and more reliable than ever, and it is STILL becoming a liability - not because it broke, but because the world around it changed. How do I see that kind of risk coming, when everything I was trained to watch says the asset is fine?

Before you read on, decide. The works has never run better. Your reliability record is the best in its history, the maintenance system is doing exactly what it was built to do, and you are justifiably proud of it. And yet: the pump house floods in rainfall that never used to happen, the catchment is thinner every year, and a rising price on the power and fuel it takes has pushed the cost of running it past what the town can afford to pay for the water. Nothing broke. The asset has twenty years of planned life left. Write down how you would have seen this coming — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question your system was never built to ask

• “Tighten the maintenance regime further.” More of the thing that is already working perfectly. Every instrument you have says the asset is healthy — and it is, and that is precisely the problem.

• “Nobody could have predicted this.” Comfortable, and not true. None of these arrived overnight: the rainfall pattern, the catchment and the direction of energy prices were all visible for years to anybody looking at them, which nobody was, because looking at them was not anybody's job.

• “Deal with the flooding — raise the pump house.” Right, and it is one item of several, and it is the response to the one that already hurt. Fix it alone and the drought and the running cost still sink you.

• “Put the price up.” Moves the problem to the people who cannot pay it, which for a town's water is not a solution but a different failure.

• “Replace it with something modern.” With what money, and against what threat? Replacing an asset that works, without having named what is actually coming for it, is how you buy a new version of the same exposure.

• Notice what your system is built to ask: will it break? — and it answers that superbly. It has no way of asking the other question, which is whether the world will still let this asset run, and still want what it produces. Two different kinds of change are coming at you and they need separating. There is what a changing environment does to the asset physically, at two speeds — sudden events that are getting more frequent, like your rainfall, and slow shifts that erode the conditions it was designed for, like your catchment. And there is what the world's response to that change does to its economics — prices, rules, expectations, better alternatives — none of which touch the asset at all, and any of which can make it uneconomic while it runs perfectly. The end state has a name-shaped precision worth holding on to: an asset that is sound, functional, and worthless well before the end of its planned life. Twenty years is long enough for all of this and far too long to leave it unasked.

There is a framework built for exactly this blind spot. Let us show you one finished assessment - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the one-page register that would have seen all of it coming - the floods and the carbon price both - long before they arrived. It asks not "will it break?" but "what could the changing world do to this asset?"

1. The new question - 'will the world still let it run?'

Maintenance asks 'will it break?' - and answers it well. Climate risk asks a different, more dangerous question: 'will the world still LET this asset run, and still want what it makes?' A perfectly reliable asset can still become a liability if the answer turns to no.

2. Physical risk - what a changing climate does TO the asset

Physical risk - what a changing climate does TO the asset. ACUTE (a single extreme event): extreme rainfall floods the low-lying pump house - high impact, likelihood rising, short horizon - response: raise it on a plinth, add flood barriers. CHRONIC (a slow shift): prolonged drought shrinks the catchment's raw water - response: secure a second source.

3. Transition risk - what the move to low-carbon does to its economics

Transition risk - what the world's MOVE to low-carbon does to the asset's economics. POLICY: a rising carbon price on its diesel and grid power pushes running cost above revenue - response: a solar feasibility study. MARKET/TECH: pressure to switch to cleaner tech - response: build the transition into the long-term plan. The danger is a STRANDED asset: worthless before the end of its planned life.

4. Assess - how bad, how likely, how soon

Assess each risk the same way: how bad (impact), how likely (likelihood), and how soon (time horizon - short, medium or long term). A simple matrix, so the biggest, nearest, most likely risks rise to the top and get a response first.

5. Govern and disclose - via the TCFD four pillars

Govern and disclose, via the TCFD framework's four pillars: GOVERNANCE (who owns this risk - here, the Head of Engineering), STRATEGY (the risks and opportunities, tested against scenarios - how does the asset fare in a 1.5C world versus a 3C world?), RISK MANAGEMENT (how we find and handle them), and METRICS & TARGETS (what we track, e.g. energy cost per unit). A list of problems is not a plan; this makes it one.

Notice that not one line on this page is about a worn bearing or a leaking seal. Every risk here comes from outside the asset - the climate hitting it, and the world's response to the climate reshaping its economics. This is a completely different lens from maintenance, and Onalenna's flawless programme simply did not have it. Now let us name how it works.

T3: The Blueprint

The feeling Onalenna has - that a perfectly good asset is suddenly a liability for reasons outside her control - has a name: climate risk. And here is the cleanest way to hold it against what you learned last time: measuring your carbon footprint was about your impact ON the climate; climate risk is about the climate's impact ON you. It comes in two distinct forces, and each part answers a question:

  • What could the climate physically DO to my asset? That is physical risk. It is either acute - a single sudden event, a flood or a storm or a heatwave that damages or disables the asset - or chronic - a slow, grinding shift, like a drying catchment or rising average heat, that degrades the asset or its inputs over years.
  • What could the world's REACTION to climate change do to my asset? That is transition risk - the risk from the whole economy moving to low-carbon. New policy (a carbon price or regulation) can make a carbon-heavy asset too expensive to run; new technology can make it obsolete; the market can simply turn away. At its worst this leaves you with a stranded asset - one that loses its value or becomes uneconomic before the end of its planned life.
  • How bad, how likely, how soon? You assess each risk by its impact, its likelihood, and its time horizon (short, medium or long term) - so the biggest, nearest, most likely ones rise to the top and get acted on first.
  • How do we manage this professionally? With the TCFD framework (the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures) and its four pillars: Governance (who owns the risk?), Strategy (the risks and opportunities, tested with scenario analysis - how does the asset fare in a 1.5-degree world versus a 3-degree one?), Risk Management (how we find and handle them), and Metrics & Targets (what we measure). A list of problems is not a plan; the pillars turn it into one - and increasingly, the law requires you to disclose it.

Does it scale? Just as we assessed the flood and carbon-price risk to the Corathyl works, a bank assesses the climate risk hiding in its loan book, a power utility assesses whether policy will strand its coal plants, and an airline assesses its exposure to volatile fuel prices and new emissions rules - the same two forces, the same framework, at every scale.

T4: Your Turn

Now make it personal. Assess the climate risk on an asset close to home: a family's petrol-powered car, a few years old.

  1. One physical risk: what could a changing climate physically do to it? (For example: more frequent hailstorms denting the panels; extreme heat ageing the tyres and interior faster.)
  2. One transition risk: what could the world's move away from petrol do to it? (For example: a future ban on petrol cars in city centres; or its second-hand value collapsing as cheaper electric cars take over - and rising fuel taxes.)
  3. The stranding question: could it become unusable or near-worthless long before the end of its mechanical life? And what is your response - sell it within a few years before the value drops, budget for higher fuel, lean on public transport?

T5: The Guardrails

Climate-risk assessment is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you trust a register.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Confusing it with your footprint. Writing "our CO2 emissions" as a risk. Your emissions are your impact ON the climate (that is carbon accounting). The risk is the carbon tax or regulation that follows from them - a transition risk. Keep the two directions straight.

• The maintenance mindset. Listing only physical risks ("will it break / flood?") and forgetting transition risks ("will the world still want it?"). The transition force is the one that quietly strands assets, and it is the easiest to miss.

• The "freak event" excuse. Waving away an extreme event as a one-off that will not happen again. The whole point is that the odds are changing - a "1-in-100-year" flood that comes twice in three years is telling you the 1-in-100 was wrong.

• The empty register. A neat list of risks with no owner and no response. A list of problems is not a plan - every risk needs someone accountable (governance) and an action (risk management), or it is just worrying on paper.

Onalenna did everything her training asked and still nearly lost the plant - because the danger was never going to come from inside the asset. Now you carry the lens she was missing: alongside "will it break?", you will ask "will the world still let it run, and still want what it makes?" - you will see the flood and the carbon price coming, name them as physical and transition risk, and turn them into an owned, scenario-tested plan instead of a nasty surprise. That foresight is fast becoming one of the most valuable things an engineer can offer. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, bring an asset you care about and assess its climate risk together.
