If everything is urgent, how do I tell what actually matters - and stop the loudest voice winning over the person who needs me most?

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Ighsaan, and you run a small, busy workshop that fixes things for the whole organisation - from the servers in the basement to the directors' laptops. You're good at your job and you care about keeping things running. But you're drowning, because every single ticket that lands on your desk is marked URGENT. The finance team's printer is urgent. The marketing director's slow computer is urgent. The new starter who needs a mouse is urgent. Your inbox is a wall of red flags, and people stop you in the corridor to say their job is the most important.

You work flat out - but always on whatever was shouted at you last or loudest. At the end of the week, exhausted, you see it: a ticket from three days ago, from the quiet person in logistics. The main warehouse door's control panel had been failing, and now it's stuck shut - nothing can get in or out. A genuine crisis, sitting at the bottom of the pile while you fixed three problems that could easily have waited. You feel frantic and unfair all at once.

If everything is urgent, how do I tell what actually matters - and stop the loudest voice winning over the person who needs me most?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

First you try to be fair with first-come, first-served - but a wobbly desk leg from yesterday shouldn't beat the sales system failing today an hour before month-end; the order of arrival tells you nothing about what matters. So you switch to serving whoever complains loudest or is most senior - and now you're a servant to power and pressure, spending all morning on a director's phone while the person whose livelihood depends on a working machine waits quietly for help that never comes. It feels deeply unfair, against the whole reason you do this. You even try doing a bit of every job to keep everyone happy - and split yourself so thin that nothing finishes; you start ten things, complete none, and the truly critical job still sits there.

The problem isn't your effort - it's that the word 'urgent' is doing too much work. So before you read on: what TWO different things might it be trying to say at once, and how would separating them let you rank fairly?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

If everything is top priority, nothing is - a self-declared 'urgent' carries no information once everyone uses it. The fix is to make the order rest on the WORK, not on who's in your doorway - an act of fairness, so your limited help reaches whoever needs it most. Five moves:

  • See the trap - a dead signal ordered by social force: when anyone can stamp their own job 'urgent', the stamp stops meaning anything - it's a dead signal. The queue then quietly orders itself by social force (who is loudest, nearest, most senior, most persistent) instead of by need - so the genuinely critical job, waiting quietly, loses to three loud trivial ones. The problem isn't your effort or care; it's that the signal everyone relies on has been emptied of meaning.
  • One word, two questions - how bad, and how soon: 'urgent' is a trap because it mashes two different questions into one word. Pull them apart: how BAD is it if this isn't done (the consequence), and how SOON before that bad thing lands (the time). A server whose failure stops the whole company is very bad; a flickering storeroom light is not. A till that's halted all sales needs fixing now; a wobbly chair needs fixing soon, not now. You cannot rank fairly until you ask both.
  • Agree the levels in the calm, not the doorway: get together with the people who send you work, while nothing is on fire, and agree a simple shared language for each question - say three levels of 'how bad' (a critical service or safety risk / one person or team stopped / an annoyance) and three of 'how soon' (within hours / within days / within weeks). You're not imposing rules; you're building a shared understanding, set in calm so the words mean the same thing to everyone when the heat comes.
  • Require both, and confirm - don't just accept: change the request: instead of one 'urgent' tick-box, ask for both answers - 'how bad?' and 'how soon?'. Then CONFIRM them, especially at first, because people will mark everything worst-and-soonest to jump the queue. A gentle check does it: 'you've marked this printer jam as a critical service stop - can you help me understand what service has stopped?' Confirming, not blindly accepting, is what keeps the whole system honest.
  • Combine by a rule everyone agreed: turn the two answers into one order using a rule you all agreed beforehand - bad-and-soon goes to the very top, trivial-and-distant to the bottom, the rest in between (a future disaster that's bad-but-not-soon still ranks high; a trivial-but-immediate hassle ranks low). Now when someone asks why their job waits, you have a fair, defensible answer that rests on the work and shared rules, not on who has the sharpest elbows. The rule is a sensible default your judgement can still override.

See it free Ighsaan. He stops taking the red flags at face value and asks two questions of all thirty 'urgent' jobs - and only three turn out to be truly bad-and-soon; the warehouse door (a critical service stopped, right now) rises to the very top, while the loud-but-trivial settle where they belong. When a director asks why their laptop isn't first, he has a fair, calm answer that rests on rules they all agreed - not on who shouted. The quiet logistics crisis would never again sit for three days. Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a repair desk - any all-urgent queue hides the same trap. Watch a council's housing repairs office.

See the same split protect the most vulnerable

A housing repairs office gets hundreds of calls a week: a tenant with no heating in winter, a resident with a dripping tap, a broken fence, a flickering hall light. If they all land in one pile marked 'URGENT', the loudest or most persistent caller gets their dripping tap fixed before the elderly resident with no heat. Split 'how bad' from 'how soon' and the order becomes fair:

• No heating in winter: bad (a risk to health), and soon (it's happening now) - near the very top.
• Broken fence on a public path: moderately bad (a safety risk), needed this week - in the middle.
• Dripping tap: an annoyance, fixable in a few days - lower down.

The system protects the vulnerable by focusing on the real-world consequence of not doing the work, not on the volume of the complaint.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at a queue you manage - your work inbox, your team's to-do list, your family's requests - and find where the single word 'urgent' is causing chaos, rewarding the loudest and leaving real needs unmet. Drowning under an all-urgent queue isn't a personal failing; it's what happens when the signal is broken. You don't need to work harder - you need to order the work with shared wisdom:

  1. See the two questions hidden in 'urgent': how bad will it be if this isn't done, and how soon before that happens?
  2. Agree the levels in the calm with the people who make the requests - a shared language for what matters, set before the next fire.
  3. Ask for both answers, confirm them, and combine by your agreed rule - and let the work itself, not the loudest voice, decide the order.

Being overwhelmed by a wall of 'urgent' and serving the loudest was never weakness or poor discipline - it's what happens to any caring person when the one signal everyone trusts has been emptied of meaning; you were trying to help everyone at once. The fix isn't more effort, it's a small, shared system, agreed in the calm and gently kept honest. And it is, at heart, an act of fairness: when the order rests on real consequence rather than on who pushes hardest, your limited time and energy reach the people who need them most - the quiet ones, the ones not standing in your doorway, the ones a broken signal would have left waiting. Ranking honestly is how you keep faith with all of them.
