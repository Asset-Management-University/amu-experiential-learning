Step 1 of 5

The cheap pump set is R800,000 less to buy - but the board has promised the public a 20% carbon cut, and my folder only has purchase prices. How do I measure the whole-of-life footprint of each option before I sign?

T1: The Ache

It is a cold Tuesday morning at the Stellaford pump station, on the edge of the Masego Water Board's supply area. You are the design engineer, standing in the old pump hall with a thin folder in your hand. The two pumps in front of you have run for nineteen years. One is leaking at the seal, and the bearing whine is loud enough that you raise your voice over it.

Your manager, Lebogang, leans against the doorframe, arms folded, waiting. The board has approved money to refurbish this station, and you have two options. Option A is a cheaper cast-iron pump set: R1.8 million to buy and install, and it draws 220 kW running. Option B is a high-efficiency set with a better hydraulic design: R2.6 million, and it draws 180 kW.

This morning the choice felt simple: Option A is R800,000 cheaper, so take A. But last week the board signed a climate commitment. They promised the public a number - a 20% cut in the carbon footprint of water supply by 2030 - and promised to report it every year. Lebogang asks one question across the noise: "If we pick the cheap one, what does it do to our footprint number? And does the cheap one stay cheap once we count the next twenty years of electricity?"

You look down at your folder. It has the two purchase prices. It has nothing about energy over twenty years, nothing about the steel and copper that went into making each pump, nothing about scrapping, and nothing about a carbon number you can put your name to. "Cheapest to buy" and "best for the board's promise" might not be the same answer - and right now you cannot prove either way.

I need to measure the whole-of-life environmental impact of each option, from the raw materials all the way to disposal, so I can put a defensible carbon number in front of the board - and choose honestly.

Before you read on, decide. Option A is R800,000 cheaper. Last week the board promised the public a 20% cut in the carbon of their water by 2030, and promised to report it every year. Your folder has two purchase prices and nothing else — no twenty years of electricity, no steel and copper, no scrapping, no number you could sign your name to. Write down how you would answer Lebogang. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one can be published

• “Take A — it’s R800,000 cheaper.” This morning it felt simple, and it answers a question about today with twenty years still to pay for. A purchase price is the smallest part of what a pump costs, and the part that is easiest to see.

• “Work out the electricity over twenty years and add it to the price.” A large improvement and still half a sum. It settles the money and leaves the board’s promise unanswered — and it ignores the steel and copper that had to be made before either pump arrived.

• “Take the efficient one — it must be better for the footprint.” Probably right, and you cannot say so yet. The efficient pump is more of a machine, and more machine means more carbon to build; if you skip that stage you have chosen your preferred answer and then found reasons.

• “Give the board a rough figure — about eighteen per cent.” They promised to report annually and publish. A figure that is roughly right and cannot be shown is exactly what a public promise gets met with when nobody does the whole sum, and you will be asked to defend it every year.

• “Ask the manufacturers for their environmental figures.” Worth having as an input and it is their number for their product, prepared by people who want the sale — and neither of them knows how many hours your pump will run.

Notice what Lebogang actually asked, because it is two questions and both need the same sum: what does the cheap one do to our footprint number, and does the cheap one stay cheap once you count twenty years of electricity?

The method that does this is a Life Cycle Assessment. Here it is, worked on the very choice in front of you.

T2: The Tool in Action

A Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) adds up an asset's environmental impact across its whole life - not just while it runs, but while it is being made and after it is thrown away. People call it "cradle to grave": from the cradle (raw materials) to the grave (disposal). Look at only the purchase price and you ignore twenty years of electricity; look at only the running energy and you ignore the steel that had to be made first. An LCA forces all the life stages onto one page, so no stage can hide the cost of another. Watch it settle the two pumps.

Step 1 - Fix the fair comparison (functional unit + boundary)

The functional unit: supply Stellaford's water for 20 years, running 6,000 hours a year. Both options pump the identical water, so the comparison is fair - if Option B moved less water or ran fewer hours, comparing them straight would be cheating. The boundary: cradle to grave, all four life stages, because the board's question is about the whole life, not just the factory. The impact we measure: carbon footprint, in tonnes of CO2 over 20 years.

Step 2 - Count the making (embodied: materials + manufacture)

Materials (mass x carbon factor: cast iron 2.0 kg CO2/kg, copper 4.0 kg CO2/kg): Option A 4,000 kg iron = 8.0 t + 250 kg copper = 1.0 t = 9.0 t; Option B 4,800 kg iron = 9.6 t + 350 kg copper = 1.4 t = 11.0 t. Manufacture and transport (a flat supplier estimate): A 3.0 t, B 3.5 t. Option B's making is 2.5 t heavier - the efficient pump costs more carbon to build. Hold that thought.

Step 3 - Count the running (the use bucket - usually the biggest)

Energy = power (kW) x hours per year x years. The South African grid emits about 1.0 kg CO2 per kWh (high - much of it is coal). Option A: 220 kW x 6,000 h x 20 = 26,400,000 kWh x 1.0 = 26,400 t CO2. Option B: 180 kW x 6,000 h x 20 = 21,600,000 kWh x 1.0 = 21,600 t CO2. The use bucket dwarfs everything else - Option B saves 4,800 t here. This is the moment the picture turns: the making gap (B 2.5 t worse) is tiny beside the energy gap (B 4,800 t better).

Step 4 - Count the ending, then add up

End of life (both pumps are mostly recyclable metal, a small credit): Option A -1.0 t, Option B -1.5 t (more metal recovered, and more copper, which recycles to a larger credit per kilogram than steel). Add the four buckets - Option A: 9.0 + 3.0 + 26,400 - 1.0 = 26,411 t; Option B: 11.0 + 3.5 + 21,600 - 1.5 = 21,613 t. The whole-life totals are in.

Step 5 - Read and report (the defensible number)

Option B emits 4,798 t less CO2 over its life - about 18% lower than Option A - almost entirely because it uses less electricity. The extra R800,000 to buy buys nearly an 18% cut, landing the board most of the way to its 20% promise from this one station, and the higher price is recovered many times over in both carbon and energy cost. The sentence Lebogang signs: 'Over 20 years, Option A emits 26,411 tonnes of CO2 and Option B 21,613; B is 18% lower, almost entirely on electricity, and its higher price is repaid many times over.'

See what the LCA did. The purchase price said "take the cheap one"; the whole-life carbon number said the opposite, and said it with evidence. And see the mission in it, because this is honest accounting in the service of a promise made to the public. The board did not promise its shareholders a dividend - it promised the people it serves a 20% cut in the carbon of their water, and pledged to report it every year. A number like that can be met with spin or met with truth, and the difference is whether someone did the whole sum. The LCA is what lets the engineer hand the board a figure she can sign her name to and publish: not "about 18%, we think", but 26,411 tonnes against 21,613, almost all of it electricity. It also refuses the comfortable half-truth in both directions - it makes the engineer admit that the efficient pump costs MORE carbon to build (honesty against her own preferred answer), and then shows that this is dwarfed many times over by the energy it saves. And the stewardship is real: the R800,000 that looked like waste this morning is recovered many times over in both carbon and electricity cost, so the choice that is right for the planet turns out right for the rate-payer too. The money is the measure; the aim is the true footprint known, the promise kept with integrity, and scarce public money spent on what actually serves the people and the years to come.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the assessment in five steps.

  • 1. Fix the fair comparison. The functional unit (the same job) and the boundary (cradle to grave).
  • 2. Count the making. The embodied impact - materials, and manufacture and transport.
  • 3. Count the running. The use bucket - energy over the whole life, times the grid factor.
  • 4. Count the ending, then add up. The end-of-life credit, and the four buckets summed.
  • 5. Read and report. The defensible number and the sentence you can sign.

The ideas underneath. The functional unit is a fair, like-for-like basis of comparison - you compare "the job done" (the same water over the same years), not "a pump" against "a pump", or one option could cheat by doing less. The boundary is where you start and stop counting: cradle to grave (whole life) is the honest default; cradle to gate stops at the factory and ignores use and disposal. The life splits into four buckets: materials (the "embodied" impact built into the steel and copper before it switches on), manufacture and transport, use (the running energy - usually by far the biggest for a powered asset), and end of life (scrapping or recycling, often a small credit). The agreed rulebook is ISO 14040, so everyone counts the same way. It fits when you are choosing between options at the design stage while the choice is open, when the running impact is large and long, and when you must report a defensible number (for example an ESG report - Environmental, Social and Governance, the public scorecard organisations now publish); it does not fit a tiny or short-lived decision, or one where you only care about cost - LCA measures environmental impact, and its money-world cousin, whole-life costing, is best run beside it.

Two disciplines keep the assessment honest. First, pin the functional unit before you count anything - the most common way an LCA lies is an unfair comparison, where one option quietly does less work (fewer hours, less output, a shorter life); fix "the same job, proven" first, and most of the cheating is gone before it starts. Second, follow the impact to where it actually lives, and say so even when it spoils your preferred answer - for a long-running powered asset the use bucket usually dwarfs the rest, so a cheaper, dirtier-to-run option almost never wins on the whole life; but you must also count honestly the embodied carbon of the option you favour, name it out loud, and let the full sum - not your hope - decide.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your assessment true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Pin the functional unit first. Compare the job done, not the object. If two options do not do the identical job, fix that before you count a single tonne, or the whole comparison is a cheat.

• The price tag hides the use bucket. For a powered asset, twenty years of energy usually dwarfs the purchase price. Counting only what you pay on day one is the most expensive mistake in the book.

• Count the embodied carbon, even against yourself. The efficient option often costs more carbon to build. Say so - then let the whole sum, not your preference, decide.

• Name your boundary and your grid factor. Cradle to grave or cradle to gate; the local grid's kg CO2 per kWh. State them, because a different boundary or factor gives a different number, and a defensible figure shows its working.

• Measure carbon and cost side by side, not instead. LCA counts impact, whole-life costing counts rands. Run both - the board needs to see that the right choice for the planet is usually the right choice for the purse.

You came into the pump hall with two prices and a promise you could not yet keep. You can leave with a number you can sign: Option A emits 26,411 tonnes of CO2 over twenty years and Option B emits 21,613 - 18% lower, almost entirely on electricity - and the R800,000 extra is repaid many times over in carbon and energy cost. Count the whole life, not just the price tag, so one stage cannot hide the cost of another - and the number you report is the truth. That is how a promise to the public is kept with integrity rather than spin: not by the cheapest sticker or the greenest brochure, but by doing the whole sum, owning every stage, and letting the full life decide.
