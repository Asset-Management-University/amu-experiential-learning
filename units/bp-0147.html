Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Zanenkosi, an integrity engineer at a large fuel storage terminal - miles of pipelines and dozens of big tanks. The phone rings, and your gut goes cold before you answer. It is the shift supervisor from the North Zone: "we've got a weep on the old transfer line, PL-1138. Not a gusher, but steady."

You pull up the asset register. PL-1138 - a section of it is a "dead-leg", a capped-off spur from an old project, tucked behind a tangle of active pipes, still wrapped in its original, now-weathered insulation. No fuel has flowed through it in a decade.

On your other screen is the inspection dashboard: green ticks, hundreds of them. For two years your team has been diligent - walked the main pipe racks, visually checked the clean, accessible lines, taken thickness readings at the designated easy-to-reach points. Your compliance score is 98 percent; your desk holds a thick folder of completed checklists. Then the supervisor's voice cuts in: "the thing is, it's leaking from the bottom, right underneath. No one's looked under there in years. It was never on the list." A leak like that threatens your colleagues, the neighbours and the ground, and could cost millions in lost product, clean-up and fines.

We did thousands of inspections and followed the plan, yet the failure came from a spot nobody ever looked at - how do I stop inspecting the easy places and start finding the real degradation?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to react with more of the same. "Inspect more often" - you propose halving every interval, looking every three months instead of six. But that just doubles the cost of looking in the same easy, low-risk places: you are polishing the shiny parts of the car twice as often while the chassis rusts through underneath, unseen - more work, not more insight. "The old plan was wrong, I'll make a new one" - so you build a fresh master schedule, but on the same logic: access platforms, clear walkways, designated points. You are re-arranging the search under the same streetlight, trusting an inherited map that was itself built for convenience, not for finding corrosion. "Inspect everything, everywhere" - in frustration you suggest stripping all insulation and sensoring every inch; the manager gently shows you the estimate: twenty years and bankruptcy, and you would drown the one critical spot of wall-loss in a sea of data.

Doing more of the wrong thing is pointless, trusting the old way is what caused this, and doing everything is impossible. So before you read on: the real question is not how MUCH you inspect - it is how you decide WHERE to look at all. How?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The breakthrough comes when you stop thinking about your inspection schedule and start thinking about rust, fatigue and erosion. You must let the DEGRADATION drive the inspection, not the calendar or the walkway. You were searching under the streetlight because the light was better - but the keys were dropped in the dark, fifty metres away. Five moves:

  • Understand how it actually fails: the breakthrough is to stop thinking about your inspection schedule and start thinking about rust, fatigue and erosion - the actual ways this asset dies. Before you decide where to look, map the real degradation mechanisms at work and WHERE each one acts: corrosion under insulation on warm, wet pipes; pitting where water sits stagnant in a dead-leg; accelerated corrosion on a tank floor resting on a damp foundation. An inspection plan that does not start from how the asset fails is just a tour of the accessible - it can be 98 percent complete and still blind to the one spot that matters. Let the failure mechanism, not the walkway, tell you where the risk lives.
  • Inspect where it fails, not where it is easy: this is the move that breaks the streetlight habit: go to where the degradation IS, even when it is awkward, dirty and hard to reach, and stop spending your effort where the looking is merely convenient. That means roping up to cut inspection windows in the insulation on high lines, sending a crawler across the annular plate of a tank floor, getting into the dead-leg behind the pipe rack - the dark corners where water pools and corrosion thrives. The easy, clean, accessible points were chosen because they are easy, not because they are where the asset is dying. Coverage of the convenient is not coverage of the critical - point the torch where the darkness is.
  • Set the interval by risk, not one calendar: a single calendar interval for everything guarantees you look too often at the harmless and too rarely at the dangerous. Set each interval by the RATE of degradation times the CONSEQUENCE of failure: a low-pressure diesel line in a bunded, contained area might safely wait ten years; a high-pressure petrol line near the site boundary, where a failure threatens people and the neighbourhood, gets looked at every twenty-four months. This is risk-based inspection, and it is the same focus-on-the-vital-few discipline as bp-0158 - concentrate your scarce inspection effort where a failure would do the most harm and where degradation moves fastest, and stop spending it evenly on things that do not need it.
  • Use the right method for the mechanism: the tool must match the failure, or you will look straight at the problem and not see it. A visual walk-round cannot find wall-loss happening on the inside of a pipe; corrosion under insulation is invisible until the insulation comes off or the right technique sees through it; a crack inside a weld needs radiography or advanced ultrasonics, not an eyeball. So choose the method for the mechanism you mapped: ultrasonic thickness for internal wall-loss, radiography for weld cracks, the crawler for the tank floor. This is the partner of bp-0100 (trust the reading) and bp-0096 (get a signal at all) - here the discipline is making sure the signal you take is actually capable of revealing the degradation you are hunting.
  • Close the loop - make the plan learn: the plan must become a living, intelligent system, not a fixed list. Feed what every inspection finds - and what it does NOT find - straight back into where, when and how you look next: discover corrosion under insulation on one pipe elbow and you immediately schedule checks on all similar elbows across the terminal; find a tank floor cleaner than expected and you can safely extend its next interval and spend that effort elsewhere; and feed every finding into root-cause work (bp-0134) so you attack the mechanism, not just the spot. Confidence then rests not on a thick folder of completed checklists but on actually knowing the condition of your assets - especially in the places you never used to look.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

You already know how to do this - think about looking after your own house.

See degradation-led checking at home

A calendar-based approach walks through the living room on the first of every month and declares the house "inspected" - it is easy, and the room always looks fine. A degradation-led approach is completely different: you do not check the living-room wall, you check where trouble actually starts.

• the sealant around the shower, where water gets in and causes damp;
• the wooden fence posts right at ground level, where they rot;
• the attic after a heavy storm, to catch a new leak;
• the back of the kitchen cupboard where the plumbing runs, for a slow drip.

You look in the dark, awkward, hard-to-reach places, because that is where a house actually "fails". You are not inspecting for comfort - you are hunting for problems before they grow.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own inspection, audit or quality-check programmes. Where are you looking under the streetlight - diligently checking what is easy while the real degradation happens in the dark? Stop celebrating the thick folder of completed, low-value checks and start building real confidence.

  1. Map the failure. Ask your team: "if this asset fails, what are the three most likely ways, and where?" Forget the current plan - find where the rust, wear or fatigue actually occurs.
  2. Find the dark spot. Name one inspection you do purely because it is easy and on a schedule, and one high-risk area you do NOT inspect because it is difficult or hard to access - and swap the former for the latter.
  3. Challenge the calendar. Take one one-size-fits-all interval and ask: "does everything here really degrade at the same speed and carry the same risk?"
  4. Match the method. Ask: "are we using the right tool - or trying to find internal corrosion with a visual check, or check critical data by glancing at a summary?"

This was never about a lazy or careless team - yours did thousands of honest checks and followed the plan they were given; the plan itself pointed them at the light, not the danger. Moving from calendar-based to degradation-led inspection is a move from the false comfort of being busy to the real confidence of being effective - and from protecting a compliance score to protecting the people, the neighbours and the ground that a hidden failure would harm. It is how you finally start looking for your keys where you actually dropped them.
