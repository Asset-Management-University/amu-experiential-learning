Busiest month ever - and the bank is empty

Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Muhammad, and you've just had the best month Tank You has ever seen. Deliveries every single day. Customers phoning to thank you. You barely sat down - dawn to dark, full out, and proud of every bead of sweat. This, you thought, is what winning feels like.

Then Ibraaheem shows you the bank balance, and your stomach drops. There's almost nothing there. You stare at it, certain there's a mistake. Every single delivery was paid for, in full, in cash. You did MORE than ever before. So where on earth is the money?

It makes no sense - and that's the most frightening part. You set your price the obvious way: you looked at what the shop down the road charges and matched it - you can't really charge more, or customers just phone someone else. But you never once sat down and added up what one delivery actually costs YOU. And the cruel truth, when it dawns, is almost too much: the busier you got, the LESS you had. You weren't working hard toward wealth. You were working hard to lose money faster.

Every sale was paid, I've never been busier - so where is the money? How do I price so that being busy actually means being better off?

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Notice where your price came from: the shop down the road. You looked OUTWARD - at the competitor, at what felt fair - and never INWARD, at your own true costs. That's the whole trap. A price copied from someone else is a guess about THEIR business, not a fact about yours.

Before you read on: think about everything that goes into one single delivery - not just the gas. What did it really take from you to get one full cylinder to one customer's door? List the things that quietly cost you, even when no cash visibly leaves your hand.

I set my price by matching the shop down the road. But I never once added up what ONE delivery actually costs me. I looked outward at the competitor, never inward at my own costs.
The costs I forgot are the ones that don't leave as obvious cash - fuel, airtime, wear on the bakkie, and the big one: my own time. If I'm not paying myself, I'm a volunteer in my own business.
Shrink it to ONE delivery. Add up every cost, including my hours - that's the floor. Now compare it to the price I can actually get. If I set the price, it's floor + profit; if the market fixes it (like gas), I must get my cost below it. Profit comes from my process, not just my price.

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

The problem felt huge - "my whole month's money is a mess." The fix is to make it tiny: stop looking at the month, and look at ONE. One delivery. If one delivery makes money, the month makes money. Here's how to be sure it does:

  • Busy is NOT the same as profitable: you can be paid in full for every sale, work flat out, delight everyone - and still go broke. Worse: if each sale loses money, doing MORE digs the hole DEEPER. Volume of a loss is just a bigger loss.
  • The villain: the costs you can't see: you priced by copying the competition, and forgot the costs that don't obviously leave your hand: fuel and airtime, the slow wear on your bakkie and tools, and - the big one - your own time. Skip those and the price is a guess, not a number.
  • Pay yourself - your time is a real cost: if you don't price in your own labour, you've made yourself the busiest, worst-paid worker on your own staff. Decide what an hour of your time is worth, and count it. This isn't extra; it's a real cost of every sale.
  • Cost ONE unit, then compare to the price you can get: shrink the whole-month panic to ONE sale. Add EVERY cost (materials + the bits each sale uses + a slice of your bills + your time) = your true floor. Set it beside the price you can actually charge. If you set your own price, it's floor + a fair profit. If the market sets it (like gas at R260), the gap between your floor and that price IS your profit - or your loss.
  • Can't raise the price? Lower your cost: if your true cost is above what you can charge, the answer is rarely a higher price - the market won't pay it, and undercutting just loses faster. The real lever is your cost: buy better (in bulk), work smarter (more per trip), cut waste. Profit is built in your process, not your price. Never keep selling below your floor.

The heart of all of it is the third point, so let it land: your time is a real cost. When you don't price in your own hours, you haven't made your business cheaper - you've quietly turned yourself into its hardest-working, lowest-paid employee, working for free while believing you're building something. Paying yourself isn't greed or luxury. It's the difference between a business and a slow act of self-sacrifice.

And the profit you add on top isn't greed either. Profit is the fuel: it's what lets you survive a flat tyre, a sick week, a slow season - and what lets you grow enough to keep serving the very customers who love you. A business that doesn't profit can't last to help anyone. Price from your costs out, never from the competitor in.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

So Muhammad and Ibraaheem stop staring at the month and cost a single delivery, honestly, on the back of a page.

See the true cost of one delivery - and why "busy" was bleeding him

What he charged: R260 - the same as every other gas outlet. He can't charge more; the customers would simply phone someone else.

What ONE delivery actually cost him, back then:

• The gas he buys to refill (small amounts, no bulk deal): R205
• Fuel for the trip (sparse routes, only a few drops): R35
• His own time (about 20 minutes a drop): R40
• A share of his monthly bills (airtime, insurance, spread over deliveries): R10
• TRUE COST: R290

The gut-punch: he charges R260 and it costs him R290 - he LOSES R30 on every single delivery. In his busiest month he did 100 of them. That "best month ever" actually cost him R3,000 of his own money - and the more he delivered, the more he lost. The empty bank wasn't a mystery; it was maths.

The fix - and the surprise: he can't raise the price (the market is R260). So the answer isn't a bigger number on the invoice - it's a smaller true cost. He starts buying gas in bulk (R205 down to about R150) and packs his routes so each trip makes many drops instead of a few (fuel R35 down to about R15, and faster, so his time per drop falls too). His true cost drops from R290 to about R205 - and now the very same R260 leaves him roughly R55 profit on every delivery. Same price, same door-to-door service, a business that finally pays. (That hard-won discipline - cheaper gas, denser routes - is exactly what, years on, earned Tank You its own truck.)

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

The empty bank wasn't bad luck or a head for figures you don't have - it was a price built by looking the wrong way. Turn and look inward, and the fog clears. For anything you sell:

  1. Cost ONE unit, fully. Add up everything one sale truly takes: the obvious materials; the bits each sale uses up (fuel, airtime); a small slice of your "big bill later" costs (set aside a little each sale for the tyres, the phone, the repair that's coming, so it's never a crisis); and your own time. That total is your floor.
  2. Pay yourself, always. Your hours are a real cost. Decide what an hour of your work is worth and count it in - or you're a volunteer in your own business.
  3. Compare your true cost to the price you can actually get. If you set your own price, it's true cost + a fair profit. But if the market fixes your price - as it did for Muhammad's gas at R260 - you often can't charge more. Then the lever is to LOWER your true cost (buy in bulk, do more per trip, cut waste), never to keep selling below your floor.
