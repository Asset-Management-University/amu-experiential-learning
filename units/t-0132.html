I know exactly what is wrong with that scheme and 'it is in a bad way' is not a sentence anyone can act on - how do I turn what I saw into money and dates?

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and the regional water board has directed Corathyl to take over the neighbouring Stellaford Water Scheme, which has been failing quietly for years.

You spend two days on the site. You come back knowing, in your bones, that the place is a liability - three pumps you would not trust through a summer, a chlorination system with no working residual monitoring, cracks in a reservoir roof. The seller's asset register says those pumps were refurbished two years ago. You have stood next to those pumps.

Then you sit down in front of the investment committee and discover that none of what you know is usable. It is in a bad way is not a sentence anybody in that room can act on. It cannot move a price. It cannot be argued with, which sounds like a strength and is actually the problem: it cannot be checked either, so it carries no weight at all.

You are the only person in the building who has stood on that site, and you are the least useful person at the table.

I know exactly what is wrong with that scheme and I cannot say it in a form anyone can use. How do I turn what I saw into money and dates?

Before you read on, decide. Two days on a failing site, a seller's register you do not believe, and a committee that thinks in money. Write down what you would put in front of them - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does in that room

• "Write a detailed technical condition report." The natural answer and it is what you were trained to produce. It will be read by nobody in that meeting, because a committee cannot convert "advanced bearing deterioration" into anything it can vote on - and the more thorough it is, the less likely it is to be read.

• "Say plainly that the seller's register is wrong." It may well be, and you cannot prove it, and the moment it sounds like an accusation the discussion becomes about your judgement of people instead of about the pumps. There is a way to record the same fact that cannot be argued with.

• "Recommend against the acquisition." Possibly the right conclusion and it is not your recommendation to make - and a no with no numbers behind it is easily overruled by a yes with numbers, however weak those numbers are.

• "Give them a rough total - call it twenty million of work needed." Much closer, and a cost with no date can be absorbed into almost any business case: the committee simply spreads it over a comfortable number of years and the problem dissolves.

• "Argue that the asking price is too high." The instinct to fight on their ground, where you are a beginner and they are not - and where the discount rate and the market multiple will be produced against you by people who use them daily.

• What the first five have in common is that they either stay in the language you know or reach into a language you do not. There is a third thing, and it is the only ground on which nobody in that room can beat you: what must be spent on that scheme, and by when. A cost with a date is not a technical opinion and it is not a financial argument - it is a cash-flow obligation that travels with the asset, and a committee cannot absorb it, dispute it, or ignore it. It is the one thing you can produce and they cannot.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the paper she took into that committee. Five boxes, one page, and every number in it is checkable.

1. The due diligence - what I verified, beside what the seller claims

VERIFIED BY ME: vibration readings on all three primary pumps show advanced bearing deterioration; the chlorination dosing system has no functioning residual monitoring; the reservoir roof has visible structural cracking at four bays. SELLER'S CLAIM, NOT VERIFIED: the asset register records all three pumps as 'refurbished, 2024', and lists the roof as 'serviceable'. I could find no work orders, no parts invoices and no commissioning records for that refurbishment. I am not calling anyone dishonest. I am recording that the register is the seller's CLAIM and I have no evidence for it.

2. The mandatory capital schedule - what must be spent, and by when

Replace three primary pumps, beyond economic repair - R 12.0m, within 24 months. Chlorination compliance upgrade, required by the regulator - R 4.5m, within 12 months. Reservoir roof structural repair - R 3.5m, within 36 months. TOTAL MANDATORY: R 20.0m. Each line carries a date because the committee is not buying a list of problems, it is buying a cash-flow obligation. 'Needs work' is worth nothing to them. 'R 4.5m inside twelve months or we lose the operating authorisation' is worth everything.

3. The valuation adjustment - so what is it actually worth?

Asking price R 60.0m, less R 20.0m of mandatory capital expenditure, gives a recommended offer of R 40.0m. That is the whole calculation, and it is deliberately one the seller can verify line by line. I am not arguing about the discount rate or the market multiple - that is not my table. I am saying that whoever owns this scheme must spend R 20.0m on it, on a fixed timetable, before it is compliant and reliable, and that obligation transfers with the asset.

4. Expect every line to be challenged - and know which ones survive

The seller's response comes back in a week and disputes two lines. The chlorination R4.5m: not disputable, because it is the regulator's requirement with a stated date, and Onalenna simply attaches the compliance notice. The pumps at R12.0m: challenged as 'they run fine' - and she has vibration readings, taken by her, dated, on all three, against the manufacturer's own limits. The roof at R3.5m within 36 months: this one rests on her judgement of how fast the cracking will progress, and she says so. She offers to re-date it to 48 months subject to a structural survey, and is explicit that this moves the DATE and not the money - R3.5m is still required, and what a later date is worth in the price is the committee's arithmetic rather than hers. Two lines held on evidence, one moved on evidence rather than on pressure, and nothing conceded that she had measured.

5. Say plainly what is not yours - and hand it over

The last paragraph of Onalenna's paper lists what it does not decide: whether Corathyl should acquire Stellaford at all, what the scheme is worth as an income stream, how the acquisition should be financed, and what discount rate applies to any of it. 'Those are the committee's questions and I have not attempted them. What I have brought is what must be spent on that scheme, by when, and how confident I am in each line.' The chair reads it out loud - and from then on, whenever an engineering figure is questioned in that room, somebody asks whether Onalenna has looked at it.

Watch where the boundary falls, because it is the whole method. She supplied the physical facts, costed and dated - which nobody else in that building could have produced - and then stopped. Box 3 is one subtraction that the seller can verify line by line and cannot dismiss as negotiating theatre. Box 4 shows what happens when the schedule is attacked: two lines held on evidence she had gathered herself, one conceded on evidence rather than on pressure, and the concession moved a date rather than a number she had measured. And box 5 is the part that feels like giving something away and is what makes the rest of the paper trustworthy - she names the questions she has not answered, including whether Corathyl should buy the scheme at all. She brought ground-truth to the table. She did not try to build the table. That is why, from then on, an engineering figure questioned in that room gets the same response: has Onalenna looked at it?

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the second one is where the value is created.

  • 1. Two columns: what you verified, and what the seller claims. Never let them share a column.
  • 2. Convert condition into a costed, dated schedule - every line an amount against a deadline.
  • 3. Adjust the price by simple subtraction, so the other side can check every line.
  • 4. Mark what each line rests on before it is challenged: a regulator, a measurement, a quotation, or your judgement.
  • 5. Name what you are not answering, and hand it to whoever should.

What you have produced is technical due diligence, and its discipline is that column rule. This is not cynicism and you are not accusing anyone of dishonesty: an asset register is a document written by the party selling the asset, so it is a claim, and a claim with no work orders, invoices or commissioning records behind it is exactly that. Write both columns and let the gap between them speak for itself.

The move that changes the meeting is the second one, and it is worth being precise about why. Everything the previous owner did not do is a deferred maintenance backlog, and left as a backlog it is a technical grumble. Put a date against each line and it becomes something else entirely: a cost with no date is an opinion; a cost with a date is a cash-flow obligation, and an obligation is something a committee can price. "The chlorination system needs attention" is worth nothing to them. "R4.5m inside twelve months or the operating authorisation lapses" reorganises the whole meeting - and it does so without you having argued about money at all.

One honest boundary, and it is the point of move 5. You are becoming an investment-literate engineer: somebody who can hold their own in an investment committee because they bring the one thing nobody else there can produce. Your work is to quantify physical reality; the committee's is to price risk. The costed, dated schedule is the indispensable input to their valuation models, their discount rates and their credit structures - which is why staying on your own side of that line is what makes your contribution unbeatable rather than what limits it.

Two limits. This produces a floor, not a valuation: what must be spent says nothing about what the asset earns, and a scheme can be worth buying at a price well above your adjusted figure. And it is only as good as the evidence behind each line, which is exactly why move 4 exists - a schedule where every line rests on your judgement alone will not survive its first serious challenge.

One neighbouring question has a unit of its own. When the financing offered prices itself against sustainability targets that will be yours - measured and independently verified every year for the life of the loan - which of those you can honestly sign for is t-0294.

T4: Your Turn

Corathyl is now offered a small private industrial water recycling plant, asking price R18.0m. Your site inspection finds: the membrane filtration skid is at end of life and must be replaced within 18 months (R5.0m); the effluent discharge monitoring required by the regulator has never been installed and must be in place within 6 months (R1.2m); and the standby generator failed its load test and must be replaced within 24 months (R0.8m). The seller's register lists the membrane skid as "overhauled last year" and you can find no supporting records.

Task 1 - Split the columns and build the schedule. Write what you verified and, separately, what the seller claims that you could not verify. Then give the schedule with every line costed and dated, and the total.

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

VERIFIED: membrane skid at end of life; no discharge monitoring installed; generator failed its load test. SELLER'S CLAIM, UNVERIFIED: the skid was overhauled last year - no work orders, no invoices, no commissioning records. Schedule: discharge monitoring R1.2m within 6 months; membrane skid R5.0m within 18 months; generator R0.8m within 24 months. Total mandatory: R7.0m. Good answers keep the unverified overhaul claim out of the findings entirely and record it as the seller's claim - and date every line, because a R7.0m total with no dates can be spread over a decade by anyone who wants to.

Task 2 - Adjust, and defend. State the recommended offer with the one sentence of logic behind it. Then mark what each of your three lines rests on, and say which one you would expect to concede.

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

R18.0m less R7.0m = R11.0m recommended, and the sentence: whoever owns this plant must spend R7.0m on it to a fixed timetable before it is compliant and reliable, and that obligation transfers with the asset. What each line rests on: the discharge monitoring is the regulator's requirement - attach the rule and it cannot be argued; the generator rests on a load test, a document with a result; the membrane skid rests on your judgement of remaining life, which is the one to expect a fight over and the one to state most carefully. Good answers say they would move on the skid's date if given a survey, and not on the other two - and notice that conceding the soft line is what makes the hard ones immovable.

Task 3 - Name what is not yours. Write the closing paragraph: the questions this paper does not answer, and who they belong to.

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

Whether Corathyl should acquire this plant at all; what it is worth as an income stream; how any acquisition should be financed; what discount rate applies. All the committee's, none of yours. Good answers say this in the document rather than in the meeting, and understand why it strengthens rather than weakens the paper: a report that stays inside what its author verified is worth more than one that reaches into pricing, and the moment you argue a discount rate you have left the only ground on which nobody can beat you.

T5: The Guardrails

Engineers go wrong in an investment committee in a small number of very consistent ways. Most of them are failures of nerve rather than failures of knowledge.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Letting the seller's register into your findings. The moment "refurbished 2024" appears in your verified column, the report is no longer yours. Keep the columns apart and let the reader see which is which.

• A number with no date. "R12m of pump work" can be absorbed by almost any business case. "R12m within 24 months" cannot be. The deadline is not a detail attached to the cost - it is half the finding.

• A schedule that has never been stress-tested. Mark what each line rests on before the meeting, not during it. A line resting on a regulator's requirement or a dated measurement survives anything; a line resting on your judgement needs to be stated as such, and moved on evidence if evidence arrives.

• Conceding under pressure rather than under evidence. The two look identical from the outside and they are opposites. "I will re-date that subject to a survey" is professional; "I suppose it could be less" costs you every other line on the page.

• Calling the adjusted figure a valuation. It is a floor built from obligations, not an estimate of worth - the scheme may still be worth buying above it. Say which you have produced.

• Turning the column rule into an accusation. "The register is the seller's claim and I have no evidence for it" is unanswerable. "They are misrepresenting the asset" invites a defence, and the meeting becomes about people.

• Crossing the line. The instant you argue the discount rate or the market multiple, you have left the only ground on which you were unbeatable and joined a discipline where you are a beginner. Say "that is not my table" and mean it.

Onalenna knew exactly what was wrong with Stellaford and could not make it count for anything. Now you can. Separate what you verified from what you were told, convert condition into costs with deadlines attached, let one subtraction adjust the price in a way the other side can check line by line, know before the meeting what each line rests on - and end by naming the questions that are not yours. A cost with no date is an opinion; a cost with a date is an obligation that travels with the asset. That is not finance. It is engineering, presented in the only language that room understands - and it is the reason an asset manager belongs in it.
