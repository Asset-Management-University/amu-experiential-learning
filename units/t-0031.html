Step 1 of 5

I have a flat list of 41 shutdown jobs and a manager demanding to know this morning - not a guess - whether the furnace will be back on in nine days. How do I turn the pile into one honest finish date, and find which jobs must never slip?

T1: The Ache

It is 06:40 on the morning of the Furnace 3 shutdown at Mkhulu Ferrochrome, an inland smelter. You are Neo, the shutdown planner. Once a year each furnace is cooled, opened, repaired and restarted - a shutdown, or turnaround - and nothing is produced while it is cold. The air on the casting floor is still warm from yesterday's last tap, and smells of burnt slag and diesel.

In your hand is a single sheet: the job list the maintenance teams gave you. Forty-one jobs, top to bottom, in no order - replace tap-block, reline the hearth, swap the cooling pump, re-bolt the electrode column - each with a name and a rough number of hours. That is all.

Mr Mosegi, the plant manager, walks over and does not smile. "Neo. The board wants Furnace 3 back on in nine days. Are we going to make it or not? I need an answer this morning, not a guess." You have six teams on site - mechanical, refractory (the brick-lining crew), electrical, crane, cleaning, and the inspectors - and they all want to start now. But the cleaning crew must cool and empty the furnace before anyone climbs in; the pump cannot be wired until it is bolted down. Some jobs must wait; some can run together. Adding up all the hours gives a number far bigger than nine days - but the teams work side by side, so total hours is not the answer.

The board wants Furnace 3 back on in nine days. Are we going to make it or not - an answer this morning, not a guess? How do I turn a flat list of 41 linked jobs into one honest finish date, and find which jobs must never slip?

Before you read on, decide. Furnace 3 must be back on in nine days. There are 41 linked jobs and six teams who can work side by side. The eight that drive the date: cool down (1 day, first); open and remove the tap-block (1, after cool-down); clean and inspect the hearth (1.5, after opening); reline the hearth (4, after cleaning); replace the cooling pump (1, needs only the cool-down — it is outside the furnace); wire that pump (1, after replacing it); re-bolt the electrode column (2, after opening); final inspection and start-up (1.5, needs the reline, the wiring and the electrode column all done). Write down whether you will make it, and which jobs must never slip — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why adding up the hours lies

• “Add the hours and see. Thirteen days of work in nine — we will not make it.” The sum is right and the conclusion is wrong, because six teams work at the same time. Total hours would only be the answer if one person did everything.

• “Hand the list out and let each team start where it likes.” This is last year, and last year ran four days late at a cost of R3.6 million. Everyone stays busy and the jobs that decide the date wait their turn behind jobs that did not matter.

• “Put the most people on the longest job — the four-day reline.” The right job, for a reason not yet established. And it is only right if the reline is on the chain that sets the date, which nobody has checked.

• “Work more hours. Nightshift everything.” Spends money and goodwill uniformly across jobs, most of which have room to spare. It buys hours where hours are not the constraint.

• “Ask the six supervisors for their dates and take the latest.” Each will answer for their own package, honestly, and nobody in the room owns the joins between them — which is where the date actually lives.

• Follow the dependencies rather than the hours and the answer falls out. Cool-down finishes on day 1; opening on day 2; cleaning on day 3.5; the reline on day 7.5. Meanwhile the pump — which needed only the cool-down — is replaced by day 2 and wired by day 3, and the electrode column is done by day 4. Start-up cannot begin until the last of its three feeders is finished, and that is the reline on day 7.5, so the furnace lights on day 9.0. You make it, exactly, with nothing to spare. And the same picture tells you the second thing: cool-down, opening, cleaning, relining and start-up are the chain that decides the date — an hour lost on any of them is an hour lost on the whole shutdown, while the pump and the electrode column could each slip for days and change nothing. That is where the supervision goes, and it is not where the biggest crowd would naturally stand.

You feel the pull to just hand the list out and let every team start where they like, sorting clashes as they happen. That is how last year went - and last year ran four days late: R3.6 million you do not want to explain again. A flat list cannot tell you the finish date, or which jobs decide it. Here is the method that can.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is the Critical Path Method (CPM). When jobs depend on each other, the finish date is not the sum of the hours - it is set by the longest chain of jobs that must happen one after another. CPM draws all the jobs as a network of "this must finish before that can start", finds that longest chain - the critical path - and so gives you the shortest possible finish time and the jobs that decide it. Watch it run on Furnace 3.

Step 1 - List the jobs, durations and dependencies

Neo groups the 41 jobs into 8 packages that drive the date. A: cool down (1.0 day, first, nothing before it). B: open and remove tap-block (1.0, needs A). C: clean and inspect hearth (1.5, needs B). D: reline the hearth (4.0, needs C). E: replace cooling pump (1.0, needs A only - it is outside the furnace, so it runs in parallel). F: wire the pump (1.0, needs E). G: re-bolt the electrode column (2.0, needs B). H: final inspection and start-up (1.5, needs D and F and G).

Step 2 - Draw the logic network (what runs in parallel)

[A] -> [B] -> [C] -> [D] -> [H]; branching from [A] also [E] -> [F] -> [H]; branching from [B] also [G] -> [H]. Drawing it shows that D, E and G can all run at the same time once their feeders are done. That is exactly why adding up the hours lied - the six teams work side by side, not one after another.

Step 3 - Forward and backward pass (the honest duration)

Forward pass: A finishes day 1.0, B day 2.0, C day 3.5, D day 7.5; H waits for the latest of its feeders (D at 7.5, F at 3.0, G at 4.0) = 7.5, and finishes at 9.0. PROJECT DURATION = 9.0 days - the honest answer Mr Mosegi asked for at 06:40. The backward pass then walks back from day 9.0 to give each job its latest start, ready for the float sum.

Step 4 - Find the critical path (the date-deciders)

A, B, C, D and H all have zero float: the critical path is A -> B -> C -> D -> H = 9.0 days. The pump jobs E and F each have 4.5 days of float; the electrode column G has 3.5. So the 4-day hearth reline (D) is the single longest critical job - the one that truly decides whether Furnace 3 makes nine days, and the one Neo will guard.

Step 5 - Check resources, then protect the path

The crane is wanted by both B and E on day 1 to 2 - a clash. But E has 4.5 days of float, so Neo moves E's crane slot; the finish does not move, because she spent float, not critical time. Then she protects D: stage all the brick before the shutdown, book the best refractory crew, watch it like a hawk. Crashing E, F or G would be pointless. Her one-line answer to the board: 'Nine days, and the hearth reline is the job that decides it.'

See what the five steps did. They turned a flat list of 41 jobs into one honest number - nine days - and named the single job that decides it: the four-day hearth reline. And see why that matters beyond the money: the honest answer replaces the hopeful guess that ran four days late, so a manager can tell the board the truth instead of a wish. The one network coordinates six crews so they work side by side instead of colliding in the dark - it respects their time and skill. And it focuses scarce effort on the few jobs that actually decide the outcome, instead of everyone rushing everything. The R900,000 a day is the measure of what chaos costs - a cold furnace serves no one - but the gift of the tool is the calm, coordinated, truthful plan, made before the first crew picks up a tool.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the schedule in five moves.

  • 1. List jobs and dependencies. Every activity, its duration, and - the heart of it - what must finish before it can start.
  • 2. Draw the logic network. Boxes for jobs, arrows for "must come before"; independent chains run in parallel.
  • 3. Forward and backward pass. Left to right gives the honest duration; right to left gives each job's latest start.
  • 4. Find the critical path. Float = latest start minus earliest start; the zero-float jobs are the date-deciders.
  • 5. Check resources and protect the path. Resolve clashes by spending float; guard the critical jobs.

The ideas underneath: an activity is one job with a duration; a dependency is a "must come before" rule; the logic network (or precedence diagram) is all your jobs drawn as boxes joined by dependency arrows. The critical path is the longest chain of dependent jobs from start to finish - its length is the shortest the whole job can take - and a job on it is a critical activity with zero float: slip it a day, slip the whole shutdown a day. Float (or slack) is the spare time a job has before it starts to delay the finish. Integrated scheduling means planning all the disciplines in ONE network, not six separate lists that collide on the day. CPM fits when you have many jobs that depend on each other, a finish date that matters, and durations you can estimate even roughly. It is not for a handful of separate jobs with no real dependencies (a list is enough), nor for work so uncertain it is pure guesswork (plan ranges and contingency instead), nor for routine identical work (a fixed standard plan beats redrawing a network each time).

Two disciplines keep the schedule honest. First, be truthful about the dependencies, not hopeful - the single most common way a plan lies is a "must come before" someone wished away to make the date look better; if you cannot actually start a job until another truly finishes, draw it that way, because the network is only as honest as its weakest arrow. Second, spend your effort only on the critical path - shortening a job that has float feels productive and changes nothing; the date moves only when a critical job moves, so that is where the extra shift, the split, the best crew and your whole attention belong.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your scheduling true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Total hours is not the finish date. When teams work in parallel, the sum of all the hours is meaningless. The longest dependent chain sets the date - nothing else.

• Be honest about dependencies. A wished-away "must come before" makes the plan look good and the shutdown run late. Draw the real order, even when it hurts the number.

• Only critical jobs move the date. Shortening a job with float wastes effort. Find the zero-float chain and put your whole attention there.

• Spend float to fix clashes. When two jobs want the same crane, move the one with slack, not the critical one. You have spare time on the non-critical jobs - use it.

• Plan all disciplines in one network. Six separate lists collide on the day. One integrated network is what stops the crews fighting for the same furnace at the same hour.

You came onto the casting floor with a flat list of 41 jobs and a manager who wanted the truth by nine o'clock. You can give him something a list never could: one honest finish date, the chain of jobs that decides it, the slack you can borrow, and the one job to guard above all others. The honest answer is nine days - and the hearth reline is the job that decides it. Not a hopeful guess handed out at the gate, but a calm, coordinated plan that respects six crews and tells the board the truth - so the furnace comes back when you said it would, for everyone whose work depends on it running.
