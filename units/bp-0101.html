Movement 1 - The Felt Problem

You are Aaliyah, and you run a small bakery the neighbourhood relies on. You've always coped with little hiccups - a late flour delivery, a short power dip - by improvising on the day, and it's always been fine. Then a power cut hits the whole area on a Thursday afternoon, projected to last 72 hours, right before your busiest weekend - which includes a R5,000 order for the local market.

Suddenly there's no winging it. You freeze, then spend hours phoning around for a generator to hire - every one already taken. You call your baker; she can't help. You lose the entire R5,000 order, and worse, you have to turn away the dozens of regulars who come to you for their daily bread. In 72 hours, years of trust wobble. And the sting that won't leave you: it wasn't even unforeseeable - power cuts happen here - you simply never once decided, calmly and ahead of time, what you'd actually do.

Movement 2 - Your Attempt

Your first instinct is to keep improvising - "we'll figure it out when it happens", because it's always worked. But improvising in the moment is exactly what failed: the moment you need a plan is the worst possible moment to invent one. So you try to plan for every disaster - fire, flood, theft, illness, power, the supplier, and on and on - until you drown in the endless list of catastrophes and give up; you can't predict them all. Or you buy more insurance and feel covered - but money helps you rebuild after; it doesn't tell anyone what to do in the first hour, or keep the doors open for the people who need you today.

You can't list every disaster. So before you read on: what do all of them have in common - and what would you prepare for instead?

Movement 3 - The Worked Solution

You cannot prepare for every disaster - but every disaster, whatever its cause, takes away something you depend on. So you prepare once, for the loss of each dependency, in writing, and you rehearse it. Five moves:

  • See the trap - winging it lulled you: handling every small interruption on the fly slowly trained you to believe you don't need a plan - improvising scales beautifully right up until the loss is too big to wing, and then the cost is real and human (the order gone, the regulars turned away, the trust shaken). The skill that served you on the small stuff is exactly what fails on the big stuff, and the worst moment to write a plan is the moment you need it.
  • Don't plan per disaster - plan per dependency: you can't predict every catastrophe and you don't need to: fire, theft, illness, a power cut, a supplier collapse all reduce to the same thing - 'we lost X'. So stop trying to list disasters; instead list what you DEPEND ON (people, power, supplies, money, tools, access) and prepare, once, for the loss of each. A handful of dependencies covers a thousand disasters.
  • For each loss, decide three things in advance, in writing: for the loss of each dependency, write down before you ever need it: WHO is allowed to decide and act (so nobody freezes waiting for permission); what MINIMUM must keep running; and HOW you recover or route around it (the standby, the backup supplier, the partner, the call list). In writing, because a plan in your head alone vanishes the moment panic arrives. (Identifying which dependencies matter most is its own work.)
  • Define the minimum by who needs you: the minimum that must keep running is never 'business as usual' - it's the smallest thing that still serves the people who'd be hurt if you simply stopped. For a bakery in a blackout that's the regulars' daily bread, not the wedding cakes; naming it that way keeps you from both over-promising and abandoning the people who depend on you. The minimum is defined by them, not by your turnover.
  • Rehearse it - a plan you haven't tested is a wish: walk the plan through before you need it - a quiet dry run, a drill - because rehearsal finds the broken phone number, the key nobody has, the standby generator that won't start, while the gaps are still cheap to fix. An untested plan only feels like safety; a rehearsed one is safety. (Testing one specific backup properly is part of this step.)

See how it would have changed Aaliyah's 72 hours. Her "loss of power" plan already exists: she is the named decider; for any outage over three hours she activates a standing partnership with a bakery in an unaffected neighbourhood; the minimum is "the regulars' daily bread - sacrifice the pastries and the big one-off orders"; the recovery route is the partner's ovens in their off-hours. She calls the market organiser calmly and keeps the relationship for next time, puts a sign on the door - "Power cut: your daily bread is at [partner bakery] from 8am" - and keeps her promise to her community even in a crisis. The same power cut; a completely different outcome, because the thinking was done in the calm. You can't prepare for every disaster, but every disaster takes away something you depend on - so prepare once per dependency, not per disaster: for each, decide in advance and in writing who decides, the minimum that must keep running for the people who need you, and how you recover - then rehearse it, because the worst moment to write the plan is the moment you need it, and a plan you haven't tested is only a wish.

Movement 3.5 - The Guided Step

This isn't only about a bakery - anyone who depends on something can be floored by its loss. Watch a freelancer whose only laptop dies.

See the same plan for a freelancer's one laptop

A freelancer's whole livelihood runs on one laptop. Improvised, the day it dies is panic: days of lost work, missed deadlines, angry clients. With a plan written in advance for that one dependency:

• Decider: the freelancer (no one to wait for - just act on the plan).
• Minimum: immediately email key clients from their phone - "a technical issue, a 24-hour pause" - so no one is left guessing.
• Recovery: go to a pre-identified library or co-working space, log into cloud storage that's always synced, and carry on from a borrowed machine.
• Rehearsed: tested once a year, so they know the sync really works before they need it.

One dependency, one page, one dry run - and a dead laptop becomes a half-day wobble instead of a week-long disaster.

Movement 4 - In Your Own World

Look at your own work, your household, or something you lead. You don't need to predict the future - only to prepare for the loss of what you already know you rely on:

  1. List the 5-7 things you truly depend on. People, power, supplies, money, tools, access - the handful whose loss would floor you.
  2. For each, on a single page, decide three things in advance. Who decides and acts? What minimum must keep running for the people who need you? What's the recovery route?
  3. Then, one quiet afternoon, walk it through. A dry run finds the broken number, the missing key, the standby that won't start - while it's still cheap to fix.

Coping by improvising was never laziness or denial - it's the natural result of small problems always yielding to quick thinking, which quietly teaches you that you don't need a plan at all. The gap isn't a flaw in you; it's just a habit you haven't learned yet, and learning it doesn't mean living in fear or planning for every imaginable horror - only writing a page for the handful of things you already know you depend on, and walking it through once. Do that, and when the hard day comes you won't freeze: you'll act, calmly, on a decision you already made - and the people who count on you won't be abandoned, because you cared enough to think it through before the storm. That is what it means to be ready: not to predict the disaster, but to have already decided how you'll keep faith with the people who need you.
