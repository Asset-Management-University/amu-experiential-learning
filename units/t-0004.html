Step 1 of 5

What does it really cost - and what should I charge?

T1: The Ache

You worked harder this month than ever. Up early, finishing late, barely a day off. The orders came; you filled them. And then you check the money - and there's almost nothing there. Maybe even less than last month.

It makes no sense. You're busy. People are buying. So where does it go? And underneath that, a quieter, scarier question: am I even making money on each sale - or am I quietly losing it, one order at a time?

And here's the trap that makes it so hard: you can't just charge more. Your customers pay the going rate - the same as everyone else selling what you sell. Put your price up and they simply buy elsewhere. So if the money's vanishing, raising the price isn't the escape. You need to see the truth first: what does one sale really cost you?

I'm working flat out and there's no money left - and I can't just charge more. Am I making a profit on each sale, or losing on every one without knowing it?

Before you read on, decide. Muhammad delivers 9kg gas cylinders. A refill costs him R205; the bakkie uses about R35 of fuel per delivery; each drop takes him about twenty minutes; his monthly bills — airtime, a bit of maintenance, the rest — come to R1,000, and he does about 100 deliveries a month. Every other outlet sells a 9kg refill for R260, so that is what he charges. He has just had his busiest month ever and his account is nearly empty. Write down whether he is making or losing on each delivery, and what you would tell him to do — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the question the sheet is actually for

• “He is making R55 a delivery — R260 in, R205 for the gas.” The sum almost everybody does, and the reason so many hard-working businesses quietly fail. It counts the cost that arrives with an invoice and nothing else, because the invoice is the part that feels like the cost.

• “Put the price up until it works.” The instinctive escape, and it is already shut: every other outlet is at R260, so a cylinder at R290 is a cylinder nobody buys. He does not get to set this price. The market set it before he arrived.

• “Do more deliveries — volume will sort it out.” This is the belief that made the busiest month the emptiest one. If a sale loses money, volume is not the cure; volume is the machinery of the loss.

• “Stop counting his own time — he would be sitting at home anyway, so it is free.” The most tempting answer here, because it is the one that makes the sheet balance. It balances by borrowing from him. A business that only works while its owner goes unpaid is not a business that works; it is a job that pays nothing, and it will feel exactly like this one.

• “Count everything — the gas, the fuel, his time, a share of the bills.” The right move, and the hard one to arrive at unprompted. But notice it produces a number and then stops: R205 and R35 and about R40 of his time and R10 of bills is R290 against R260, so he loses R30 every time he delivers, and R3,000 across a hundred-delivery month. It tells him he is bleeding. It does not tell him what to do.

• And that is the thing worth taking from this. The sheet's job is not to arrive at a price — he cannot set the price. Its job is to make one sale's true cost visible, so that the only place profit can come from becomes obvious: the cost side. Buy gas in bulk and R205 becomes R150; pack more drops into one trip and R35 of fuel becomes R15; get quicker and R40 of his time becomes R30. Same R260 to the customer, true cost now R205 — and +R55 a delivery instead of −R30. When you cannot move the price, the profit is on the cost side — and you cannot work on a cost you have never counted.

Muhammad hit this exact wall with Tank You. Here is the simple sheet he used to finally see the truth - look at it first, then we'll build yours.

T2: The Tool in Action

Muhammad's busiest month ever left his bank account nearly empty. He couldn't understand it - until he sat down and counted what one delivery truly cost him, then set it next to what he could actually charge. Here is his finished cost-sheet:

1. The one thing you sell

One full 9kg cylinder, delivered and swapped.

2. Direct costs (what each sale uses up)

Gas refill R205 + bakkie fuel R35 = R240.

3. Your own time (pay yourself!)

About R40 of my time per delivery (sparse routes, ~20 min each).

4. Share of your monthly bills

Monthly bills R1,000 divided by 100 deliveries = R10 each.

5. = True cost of one (add boxes 2 + 3 + 4)

R240 + R40 + R10 = R290 true cost.

6. What you can actually charge

R260 - the same price as every other gas outlet.

7. = Profit or loss on each (box 6 minus box 5)

R260 - R290 = -R30. He was LOSING R30 on every single delivery.

8. Losing or too thin? Your cost levers

Buy gas in bulk (R205 to R150), pack more drops per trip (fuel R35 to R15), get faster (time R40 to R30) = true cost R205 = +R55 profit, at the same R260.

There it is, in black and white. Other outlets sell a 9kg refill for R260, so that's all he can charge too - but his true cost, once he counted the gas, the fuel, his own time and a share of his bills, was R290. He was losing R30 on every single delivery - and across a busy month of 100 deliveries, that's R3,000 of his own money gone, while feeling busy and successful. The harder he worked, the deeper the hole.

And notice what the sheet did not tell him to do: charge R290. He can't - the market is R260. So where does profit come from? Look at box 8. Let's walk through how the sheet is built, and where the real answer lies.

T3: The Blueprint

Almost everyone who prices "by feel" misses the same hidden costs. The sheet just makes you count all of it, in three honest layers.

  • Layer 1 - Direct costs. What each sale physically uses up: materials, supplies, fuel. (Muhammad, early on: gas R205 + fuel R35 = R240.)
  • Layer 2 - Your own time. The most-skipped cost of all. You are the most important worker in your business, and you are not free. Put a fair value on the time one sale takes. (Muhammad: R40.) Skip this, and you've hidden your biggest cost from yourself.
  • Layer 3 - A share of your monthly bills. The costs that come whether you sell or not - rent, data, insurance, licences. Add them up for the month, then divide by a realistic number of sales. (Muhammad: R1,000 of bills ÷ 100 deliveries = R10 each.)

Add the three and you have your true cost (Muhammad: R240 + R40 + R10 = R290). Now set it against what you can actually charge - which, for most small traders, the market decides, not you (R260). The gap between them is your profit, or your loss: R260 - R290 = minus R30. Every delivery was costing him money.

Here is the deep truth most people never learn: when you can't raise your price, profit is built in your PROCESS, not your price. Undercutting the market is a race to the bottom that kills you faster. The real lever is your true cost - and you can move it.

That's box 8, and it's how Muhammad turned the loss into a living, at the same R260:

  • Buy cheaper. As he grew, he bought gas in bulk - dropping it from R205 to about R150.
  • Do more per trip. He packed his routes so each trip made many drops, not a few - cutting fuel per delivery from R35 to about R15. (This "drops per trip" is the one number that makes or breaks a delivery business.)
  • Get faster, value your time. Tighter routes meant less time per drop (R40 to about R30).

His true cost fell from R290 to about R205 - so the same R260 now leaves a R55 profit on every delivery. Same price, same service - a real business, built by attacking his costs. (That journey is exactly what earned Tank You its truck.)

One honest note: your true cost is the floor - the line you must clear to survive. The market sets the ceiling. And your process - how cheaply and efficiently you work - decides how much room sits between them. That room is your profit.

T5: The Guardrails

A few traps that catch almost everyone - glance through, then go cost something for real.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Forgetting to pay yourself. The number one reason hard workers stay broke. Your time is a real cost - always put it in.

• Thinking the only fix is a higher price. Often you can't raise it - the market sets it. The bigger lever is your true cost: buy better, work denser, cut waste.

• Undercutting to win customers. Dropping below the market price to pull people in is a race to the bottom - you sell more and lose more. Win on service and efficiency, not a price that sinks you.

• Lumping the bills in with the direct costs. Keep them apart: costs that change with each sale, and bills that come anyway. You need both to see the true picture.

• Thinking profit is greedy. Profit is not greed - it's the fuel that lets your business survive, grow, and keep serving people. A business that loses money helps no one, least of all your family.

• Confusing busy with profitable. More sales at a loss is a bigger loss. Know your true cost first; then every extra sale truly builds you.

That sick feeling of being busy but broke was never a sign you're bad at business or bad at maths. It only ever meant the true costs were hidden from you - your own time most of all. Count all of it, and you turn guessing into knowing: you see whether each sale builds you or bleeds you, and - when you can't touch the price - you find your profit in your process instead.
