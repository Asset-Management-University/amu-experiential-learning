The board wants the top risk as a single colour, but the amber box was shaded in years ago with no working, the data is thin, and I suspect the colour is wrong. How do I judge whether this risk matrix can be trusted?

T1: The Ache

It is a wet Tuesday morning at a small hydro power station tucked into a river gorge. You are the reliability engineer. On the table is a single sheet of laminated A3: the station risk matrix - five rows (Rare to Almost Certain) up the side, five columns (Negligible to Catastrophic) across the top, a grid of red, amber and green squares. Your manager, Nkosenathi, taps the sheet with one finger. "The board wants the top risk for Unit 2's main bearing. Just give me the colour."

You look at the bearing risk. Someone before you placed it in the amber box: "Likely" by "Major". Unit 2's bearing has failed only once in eleven years. You have an oil sample showing a small rise in iron particles, a vibration reading that is up but not alarming, and a technician's note that the cooling water "ran warm last month". Three weak, scattered clues and one rare event. The amber box says "Likely" - but likely compared to what? You did not place it there and you cannot see the working. Nudge it to red and the board approves a R4.2-million overhaul; leave it amber and the overhaul waits and you carry the risk.

The honest truth is that the matrix gives you a colour but hides the reasoning - and with this little data, you are not even sure the colour is right. Nkosenathi needs an answer she can defend in front of the board, not a square someone shaded in years ago.

How can I defend a R4.2-million decision with a single colour from a grid when the reasoning is lost, the data is thin, and I suspect the colour is wrong?

Before you read on, decide. The bearing sits in the amber square — “Likely” by “Major” — placed there by a contractor in 2019 on professional judgement, with no notes. It has failed once in eleven years. A failure costs about R4.2 million; if it seizes and scores the shaft, closer to R12 million. On the same grid, in the same amber, sits the station transformer: about a 1-in-50-year event costing around R30 million. Nkosenathi must defend a R4.2m decision to the board. Write down what you would tell her — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what two identical ambers are hiding

• “It is amber. Amber means act. Approve the R4.2 million.” Defensible right up to the first question from the board, which will be why — and the honest answer is that somebody shaded a square seven years ago and left no working.

• “Re-score it properly with the team and see where it lands.” Better, and it still produces a colour by the same method that produced the last one. A fresh guess in the same uniform.

• “Get more data before deciding.” Reasonable, and one failure in eleven years is the data — there will not be much more without waiting for failures.

• “Use a finer grid — five by five instead of three by three.” More boxes, same weakness. Precision in the picture is not precision in the reasoning.

• “Trust the contractor. They were the expert.” They may well have been. The board is not asking who decided; it is asking on what basis, and that was never written down.

• Test the square instead of arguing with it. Ask for the working — none exists, which is the first finding, because a colour with nothing behind it is a guess wearing a uniform. Test the scales: one failure in eleven years is about 9% a year, while this grid’s “Likely” means something nearer 30–60%, so the bearing is sitting too high; and “Major” is a band that its worst case escapes entirely. Then check the ranking, which is where the grid gives itself away: the bearing runs at roughly 0.09 × R4.2m ≈ R378,000 a year, the transformer at 0.02 × R30m = R600,000. The transformer is the larger risk and the grid has painted them the same colour. Two amber boxes, opposite priorities — and a decision staked on the colour would have spent the money in the wrong place while feeling entirely rigorous.

You need a reliable way to judge whether this matrix can be trusted - to look behind the coloured square and test it against the known traps before you stake a decision on it. Here is that tool, worked on the bearing.

T2: The Tool in Action

A risk matrix is the coloured grid you know: likelihood up one side, consequence along the other, a colour in each cell. It is popular because it is quick to read - a fine communication tool. But it is a weak decision tool when the stakes are high or the data is messy, because the colour can quietly mislead. Critiquing it means testing the placement against four known traps and recommending a fix. Watch it on the bearing.

Step 1 - Demand the working

The bearing sits in 'Likely x Major' (amber). Ask who placed it and on what basis: a contractor in 2019, 'professional judgement', no notes. Trap found - hidden, arbitrary scoring. A colour with no working behind it is a guess wearing a uniform.

Step 2 - Test the scales

Likelihood: 'Likely' is a word, not a number. The bearing failed once in 11 years - a base rate of about 1 in 11 per year, ~9%. On this grid 'Likely' implies ~30-60%, so the placement is too high (centring bias). Consequence: 'Major' covers R1m-R10m, but a seized bearing that wrecks the shaft could reach R12m - the real spread crosses two boxes (range compression).

Step 3 - Check the ranking

Expected loss = probability x cost. The bearing: ~0.09 x R4.2m ~ R378,000/year. The station transformer, also amber: ~0.02 x R30m = R600,000/year. The transformer is the bigger risk, yet the grid paints them the same colour - ranking reversal. Two amber boxes, opposite priorities.

Step 4 - Recommend the fix

Keep the matrix for board summaries, but: replace word-scales with numeric bands (% per year, rand); split 'Major' into two narrower bands; require the working behind every placement; and for a high-stakes, sparse-data risk like this bearing, back the colour with a quantitative model - escalate to a Bayesian belief network, which combines the three weak clues into one defensible number (here, ~79%, far above the vague 'Likely').

See what the critique exposed. The amber square looked like an answer; tested, it came apart - placed on no working, sitting too high for a 9% base rate, compressing a R1m-to-R12m spread into one box, and painting a smaller risk the same colour as a bigger one. A risk matrix is a fine picture for a conversation, but a weak basis for a high-stakes decision - because a single coloured box can hide an undefined scale, a compressed range, and a reversed ranking all at once. The critique does not bin the matrix; it shows you exactly where the colour cannot be trusted, and what to demand instead - so the decision rests on reasoning you can defend, not a square shaded in years ago.

T3: The Blueprint

You critique any matrix placement in four moves.

  • 1. Demand the working. Ask who placed the risk in its box, when, and on what basis. No notes, just "judgement"? That is the first trap - hidden, arbitrary scoring: a colour with no working is a guess wearing a uniform.
  • 2. Test the scales. Likelihood: what real % per year does the word mean? Compute the base rate (failures ÷ years) and check the placement against it. Consequence: what rand range does the box cover - does one box compress a 100x spread, and does your real worst case even fit (range compression)?
  • 3. Check the ranking. Compute expected loss = probability x cost for this risk and for another sharing its colour. Does the arithmetic reorder them (ranking reversal)? Two boxes the same shade can demand opposite responses.
  • 4. Recommend the fix. Numeric bands, split compressed boxes, demand the working; keep the matrix for communication - and for high-stakes, sparse-data risks, escalate to a quantitative model.

The traps are not folklore: seminal research by risk analysts Tony Cox ("What's Wrong with Risk Matrices?", 2008) and Douglas Hubbard (The Failure of Risk Management, 2009) showed how the grid misleads - range compression (one box covers wildly different losses), centring bias (people avoid the corners, so everything drifts to amber), ranking reversal (the colours put risks in the wrong order), hidden scoring (the jump from one row to the next is undefined), and same colour, different meaning (two amber risks needing opposite action). The matrix fits a quick shared conversation; it fails a high-stakes call on thin data. When it fails you, the correct next step is to escalate to a quantitative model that shows its working - one that combines several weak evidence sources into a single defensible probability. In the bearing's case, combining the three clues that way turned a 9% base rate into a defensible ~79% - far more than the vague "Likely". That modelling is a technique in its own right, and it earns its own unit rather than a mention here.

The discipline that protects you is simple: never pass on a colour you cannot defend. A base rate is one division (failures ÷ years); an expected loss is one multiplication (probability x cost) - two pieces of arithmetic that turn "it feels amber" into "here is the number, and here is why the box is wrong". And keep the two jobs of a matrix straight: it is a fine way to show a room a risk at a glance, but a poor way to decide a high-stakes question on thin data. When the decision is big and the data is thin, demand the working - or build it yourself.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your critique honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• No working, no trust. A colour placed on "judgement" with no notes cannot be defended to a board or a regulator. Demand who, when and on what basis - and if there is no answer, that absence is your headline finding.

• A word is not a number. "Likely" and "Major" hide undefined scales. Always convert: likelihood to a % per year from the base rate, consequence to a rand range. Half the traps vanish the moment the words become numbers.

• Beware the amber drift. Centring bias parks everything in the comfortable middle. If almost every risk is amber, the matrix has stopped telling things apart - and is no longer doing its one job.

• Same colour is not same priority. Run the expected-loss arithmetic before you trust the ranking; the grid can quietly put a bigger risk below a smaller one. Two amber boxes can need opposite actions.

• Critique, do not bin. The matrix is still a good picture for a conversation. Keep it for communication, fix its scales for decisions - and for a high-stakes risk on thin data, escalate to a quantitative model.

The amber box looked like an answer until you asked it four questions, and it could not survive any of them: no working, a likelihood too high, a consequence too wide, a ranking the wrong way round. That is not a failure of nerve - it is the difference between a square someone shaded years ago and a decision you can stand behind in a boardroom. Trade the false certainty of a coloured box for the defensible clarity of a well-tested tool. Look behind the colour, demand the reasoning, and when the stakes are high and the data is thin, do the arithmetic yourself - because the people downstream of that decision deserve a number that is true, not a colour that is comfortable.
