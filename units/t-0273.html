Step 1 of 5

The board wants R2 million for a third generator - how do I work out the real chance we lose theatre power, and show them where the money actually belongs?

T1: The Ache

The plant room at Ntabeni Regional Hospital smells of warm diesel. Two standby generators sit under their covers, serviced, load-tested, logged. Above you, through the ceiling, is Theatre 2.

Dr Reddy, who runs the theatres, is telling you about the fourteenth of March. "We were forty minutes into a caesarean. The lights went. Eleven minutes on head torches and a bag." She is not angry. That is what makes it hard to hear. "The generators started. I could hear them running. The power just never arrived up here."

Xolani Motaung, who manages clinical engineering and has to write the board paper, puts two pages in front of you. The first is a proposal: R2 million for a third generator. The second is a letter from the hospital's insurer, and it asks one question. What is the probability that theatre power is lost for more than fifteen minutes, per year?

You can name every cause in that plant room. You cannot add them up. And the third generator is about to be bought on the strength of a feeling - because a third one must be safer, mustn't it? - when Dr Reddy has just told you the generators were running the whole time.

How do I turn a room full of causes into one honest number for the outcome that matters - and prove which spend actually moves it?

Before you read on, answer Xolani. He has to send the insurer a number by lunchtime, and he is asking you. Write yours down - a probability, and one line on how you got it. Not a shortlist to choose from; your own number, in your own handwriting, with your name effectively on it. The rest of this unit is worth far more once you have committed to an answer and felt where it runs out.

Here is everything you have. Two generators, each of which has refused to start on 11 of the last 214 monthly tests. One automatic changeover switch that carries everything the generators make up to the theatre. About 40 grid outages a year. One event in March, and none in the two years before it.

Now compare - the three answers this question usually gets, and where each one runs out

• "Once in three years, so about a third per year." This counts history, and it is honest. But it can only describe the hospital you already had - ask it whether the R2 million helps and it has nothing to say, because a third generator has no history.

• "Each generator is 95%, so two of them make us 99.75% covered." You did real arithmetic, and the arithmetic is right. But look at what it counted: the generators, and only the generators. It never asked what everything the generators produce has to pass through on its way to Theatre 2.

• "It is very unlikely." True, probably. It is also the sentence that lets R2 million be spent on the wrong part, because nothing in it can be checked or argued with.

• And the answer worth respecting: "I cannot say - I have the causes but no way to combine them." That is exactly right, and it names the missing move. Every answer above starts at the parts and hopes to arrive at the outcome. The move you are missing goes the other way: start at the outcome Dr Reddy cares about, and work down to the parts.

T2: The Tool in Action

Start at the outcome and work downwards, asking at every branch whether the thing above needs all of the causes below it or just any one of them - until you reach causes you can put a measured number on. Then combine back up. Watch it run on Ntabeni's plant room.

Step 1 - Name the one outcome, precisely, with a clock on it

You write it on the board with Dr Reddy standing there: 'Theatre power lost for more than 15 minutes during a procedure.' Not 'a power problem'. Not 'a generator failure' - that is a cause, not the outcome. Fifteen minutes is Dr Reddy's line, because that is how long a theatre can hold a patient safely on battery light and hand ventilation. Everything below this line now has one job: to explain how the hospital arrives at exactly that.

Step 2 - Work down: for each branch, does it need ALL of them, or ANY of them?

The demand comes from the grid going down - Ntabeni sees about 40 grid outages a year. Given an outage, the theatre goes dark if ANY of two things happens. Either both generators fail to start (that branch needs ALL of them - generator 1 fails AND generator 2 fails, because one alone carries the theatre). Or the automatic changeover switch fails to transfer - and then it does not matter what the generators do, because everything they produce passes through that one switch. So the top branch is an ANY, sitting over the switch and over the both-generators ALL. Ivan Coetzer, who has maintained that switch for nine years, looks at the picture and says quietly: 'That is what happened in March.'

Step 3 - Put a number on each cause at the bottom, from evidence you can show

Each generator fails to start on demand about 5 times in 100 - 0.05 - from the hospital's own monthly start-test log, 11 refusals in 214 tests over three years. The changeover switch fails to transfer about 2 times in 100 - 0.02 - the manufacturer's figure for a switch on this maintenance regime, and Ivan notes it has never been exercised under load. Three numbers, each with a source you can name in the board paper. Nothing else in the picture gets a number of its own.

Step 4 - Combine upwards through the branches to the top number

Both generators failing is an ALL branch, so 0.05 x 0.05 = 0.0025 - one in 400 outages. The switch stands alone at 0.02 - one in 50. The top is an ANY branch, so roughly 0.02 + 0.0025 = 0.0225. Just over 2 in every 100 grid outages. Across 40 outages a year that is 0.9 - Ntabeni should expect to lose theatre power in the middle of a procedure about once a year. Dr Reddy does not need this explained; she has been in that theatre.

Step 5 - Find the smallest groups that cause it, and price the options against them

Two groups are enough on their own: {the switch} by itself, and {generator 1 and generator 2} together. The switch group carries 0.02 of the 0.0225 - about 89% of the entire risk - and it needs nothing else to go wrong with it. Now test the board's R2 million third generator: three generators failing together is 0.05 x 0.05 x 0.05 = 0.000125, so the top drops from 0.0225 to 0.0201 - a 10% improvement, and still about one dark theatre a year. Now test a R180,000 job on the switch - a second transfer path and a load-tested exercise regime, taking it from 0.02 to 0.004: the top drops to 0.0065, from about one dark theatre a year to about one every four years. A 71% cut in the risk for less than a tenth of the money. That is the board paper: not an opinion about generators, but a picture that shows the money was pointed at the wrong part.

See what the picture did that no list could. It found that everything both generators produce passes through one switch, so the switch alone carries about 89% of the risk - and the R2 million third generator moves the number by about a tenth, while R180,000 spent on the switch cuts it by more than two thirds. Nobody in that plant room was being careless. The generators were genuinely well maintained; that is precisely why the eye kept landing on them. The picture is what made the shared part visible - and it is the difference between a hospital that buys a feeling and a hospital that buys four quiet years in Theatre 2.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, downwards then back up.

  • 1. Name the outcome precisely, with a time bound, in the words of the person who suffers it.
  • 2. Work down - at every branch, does it take all of the causes below, or any one? Stop at causes you can measure.
  • 3. Number the bottom only, each from evidence you can show. Everything above is calculated.
  • 4. Combine upwards - all branches multiply, any branches roughly add - to the top number.
  • 5. Find the smallest sufficient groups, see which one carries most of the risk, and test every proposed spend against it.

This is Fault Tree Analysis (IEC 61025), and now that you have run it, its words are just names for what you did. The outcome you named at the start is the top event. The measurable causes at the bottom are basic events. A branch that needs all of its causes is an AND gate; one that needs any of them is an OR gate. Each smallest group of basic events that is enough on its own to cause the top event is a minimal cut set, and the one carrying the largest share of the total is the dominant cut set. A cut set with only one member - like Ntabeni's switch - is a single-event cut set, and it is the most dangerous thing a tree can show you: one part, no accomplice needed, and every redundancy you own downstream of it counts for nothing.

The method fits when you must put a defensible probability on one named outcome and compare what different spends would do to it. It does not fit when your question is "which of my hundred failures should I work on first" - that is a ranking job, and it belongs to a criticality analysis, which is a different unit and a different day.

T4: Your Turn

Take one outcome you would hate to explain afterwards - in your plant, your building, your business - and build its tree. Name it with a clock on it. Work down through the branches. Number only the bottom, from evidence. Combine up. Then find the smallest sufficient groups and test your own next spend against them.

Two disciplines keep the number honest. First, hunt the shared part before you draw anything else - ask of every pair of things you believe are independent: what do they both pass through, both sit on, both draw from, both depend on one person for? That question is the whole reason the tree earns its keep, because a shared part turns a proud redundancy into a single-event cut set, and no amount of buying more halves will help. Second, make every bottom number show its source, and say out loud where you estimated - a tree carries real authority in a board room, and that authority is borrowed entirely from the evidence underneath it. A number whose source you cannot name is not a measurement; it is your feeling wearing a decimal point.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your tree true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A vague top event gives a vague answer. "Power problems" cannot be counted. "Theatre power lost for more than 15 minutes during a procedure" can. If you cannot say when it has happened and when it has not, you cannot put a number on it.

• Never put a number on a branch. Only the bottom causes get measured; everything above is worked out. A number written on a middle branch is a guess that has smuggled itself past the arithmetic.

• Check what your "independent" halves have in common. Two generators, one switch. Two pumps, one panel. Two servers, one power strip. Two people who both know the system, one car pool. Independence is a claim, and the tree is where you test it.

• Do not spend before you read the cut sets. The instinct is to buy another of whatever you already have. The tree exists so you can see, before the money leaves, whether that would move the number at all.

• Say what you estimated. A tree with one honest "this figure is the manufacturer's, we have never tested it under load" is worth more than a tidy one that hides it - and if that estimate turns out to be the dominant cut set, testing it is now the cheapest work you can possibly do.

You walked into that plant room with a room full of causes, an insurer's question you could not answer, and R2 million about to go to the wrong part. You can hand Xolani one page instead: the named outcome, the picture, the three sourced numbers, the 2.25% per outage, the roughly one dark theatre a year - and the two cut sets that show a R180,000 job on the switch buying more than ten times what the generators would. This is how a room full of causes becomes one number a board cannot wave away, and how the money finds the part that is actually holding the risk. For Dr Reddy, it is simply this: the next fourteenth of March, the lights stay on. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one outcome you would hate to explain, and draw its tree together.)
