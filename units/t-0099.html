Step 1 of 5

Steer by the whole, not one number

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. Head office wants costs down. So you do the obvious, disciplined thing: you watch the maintenance cost report like a hawk, and you defer a handful of "non-urgent" pump overhauls to hit the number.

And it works. For three months your cost-per-megalitre is the best in the region. Your manager praises your financial discipline in the regional meeting. You feel sharp, in control, rewarded.

Then a primary pump - one of the overhauls you deferred - fails. Not a small fail: a catastrophic one. The town is on emergency supply for three days. The repair, done in a panic, costs ten times what you "saved". The water-quality margin gets frighteningly thin during the crisis - a near-miss you will not forget. You hit your target perfectly. And the target drove you straight into a wall, because it was a single number, blind to the reliability quietly collapsing underneath it.

I hit the one number they asked for - and it caused a disaster, because chasing it wrecked things no one was measuring. How do I steer performance so that winning on one measure doesn't quietly destroy everything else?

Before you read on, decide. You were given one number: bring maintenance spend down. You did it — exactly, honestly, with no corner cut that anybody could name. Planned work got deferred when it could defensibly wait; overtime was refused; a couple of overhauls slid into next year. The number was hit. Then reliability collapsed, the crisis cost more than the saving, and for a frightening few days the water-quality margin was thinner than anybody wants to remember. Write down what you would do so that hitting a target cannot do this again — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what a single number actually does

• “Set a more sensible cost target next year.” A better number is still one number, and whatever it protects, everything it does not name is still declared free. Next year the cheapest route to it will run somewhere else, and you will not know where until it fails.

• “Refuse targets like that in future.” Rarely available, and it also gives up something worth having. Cost genuinely matters; it simply cannot be the only thing that does.

• “Add a reliability target alongside it.” Much better, and it is halfway. Two numbers are more robust than one and will still be met by consuming whatever the second one does not name — and both of them report what has already happened.

• “Combine everything into one overall score.” This feels like the sophisticated version and reproduces the original problem exactly: a composite lets a gain in one dimension pay for a loss in another, quietly, which is precisely what just happened.

• “Watch things more closely.” With what? The measures you had all reported success right up to the crisis, because the crisis was assembled out of things none of them measured.

• A single number does not measure performance — it selects what may be sacrificed. You did not fail the target; you paid for it, out of things nobody had named. So the fix has two halves and needs both. Measure across the several dimensions that each, alone, could stop this operation — safety, the licence to operate, the service itself, the assets underneath it, and cost as one of them rather than above them — and hold them in tension rather than adding them up. Then, on each, carry not only a measure of what has already happened but one that moves before it does. Look back at this year: the overtime was climbing for months. That was not a cost figure. It was reliability failing, months early, in plain sight, and nothing in the reporting was pointed at it.

There is a tool that puts your hands on the whole wheel, not one spoke of it. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is what would have caught it: Corathyl's performance on one page - not one number, but a balanced set across every dimension that matters. Read across it, and notice that Cost is only the last of five.

1. Safety

LEADING (predicts): % of planned safety checks done on time - target 100%. LAGGING (outcome): recordable safety incidents - target 0. Protects: the lives of staff and the public. Non-negotiable.

2. Compliance (licence to operate)

LEADING: % of required water-quality tests done on schedule - target 100%. LAGGING: number of water-quality or discharge breaches - target 0. Protects: our legal right to keep operating at all.

3. Service Quality (the mission)

LEADING: reservoir kept above the safe minimum level - target always. LAGGING: hours of supply interruption to the town - target under 4 hours a year. Protects: the community's water, the reason we exist.

4. Asset Reliability

LEADING: proactive (planned) work as a share of all work - target over 80%. LAGGING: unplanned downtime hours - target falling each quarter. Protects: the assets that quietly hold up safety, compliance and service.

5. Cost

LEADING: overtime hours (rising overtime is the early smell of reactive firefighting) - target steady. LAGGING: maintenance spend vs budget - target on budget. Held in TENSION with the four above: cost is one corner, never the whole.

See it now? Cost is one corner. Beside it sit Safety, Compliance, Service, and Reliability - and each carries both a leading measure (that warns you early) and a lagging one (that records what happened). On this page, deferring those overhauls would have turned "proactive work" red months before the pump blew. The disaster would have announced itself. Now let us walk through how it is built.

T3: The Blueprint

This is a Balanced Performance Scorecard - and building one is required by ISO 55001, clause 9.1 (monitoring, measurement, analysis and evaluation). Its whole purpose is to defeat one stubborn truth of human nature: what gets measured gets managed. Measure one thing, and people will optimise that one thing - and quietly wreck everything you forgot to measure.

Picture a waterbed. Push down hard in one corner - Cost - and the problem does not vanish; it bulges up somewhere else you are not looking - Reliability, Safety. A balanced scorecard puts a sensor on every corner, so you can see the whole bed move. Each part, as the question it answers:

  • What are the vital dimensions? For any asset-heavy operation, usually five: Safety, Compliance, Service Quality, Asset Reliability, and Cost. Miss one, and that is exactly where the bulge will appear. (Compliance stands alone from quality - it is your legal licence to operate at all.)
  • How do I see the future, not just the past? Use both kinds of measure. A lagging indicator records an outcome that already happened (unplanned downtime, a cost overrun) - the crash in the rear-view mirror. A leading indicator is something you do now that predicts that outcome (the share of planned vs reactive work, safety checks done on time). Leading measures are how you steer before the wall, not after.
  • How do we all mean the same thing by a number? Use standard definitions - the SMRP best-practice metrics, or the ISO 22400 dictionary of KPIs. (Note: that dictionary tells you how to define a measure cleanly so everyone agrees and you can benchmark - it does not tell you which to use or what target to set. That judgement is yours.)
  • Where is it actually used? The scorecard is the heart of the Management Review (ISO 55001 cl 9.3) - the regular meeting where leaders look at the whole picture and decide. And when a measure goes red, that is not a failure of the works - it is a win for the scorecard: an early warning that triggers a fix (the improvement process, cl 10). A red is a gift.

Does it scale? Onalenna's one page balances Maintenance Cost against Asset Reliability and Water-Quality Compliance for a town's water. An airline's scorecard balances Cost per Seat-Kilometre against Maintenance Schedule Compliance and On-Time Departures - so that cutting cost can never quietly cut safety. Same discipline; different corners of the same waterbed.

T4: Your Turn

Now you build a scorecard you cannot game. Pick something you run - your studies, a sports team, a society, a part-time job. Then:

  1. Choose three or four dimensions that pull against each other (for your studies: Results, Health, Money, Relationships). The tension is the point - if they all push the same way, you have not found the real ones.
  2. Give each a leading and a lagging measure. (Health - leading: gym sessions this week; lagging: hours slept a night.) The leading one is what you can act on today.
  3. Set an honest target for each - and notice how a target on one stops you wrecking another. (Chase "study hours" alone and Health goes red; the balance protects you from yourself.)

Start with the dimensions. Naming the corners you must not crush is most of the work.

T5: The Guardrails

A scorecard is powerful, but a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these, then strengthen any measure that needs it.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• The single-number tyranny. Steering by one measure, however good. It was Onalenna's whole disaster. Every important number needs a counterweight.

• The rear-view mirror. Only lagging measures. You will see every crash - perfectly, and too late. Always pair each outcome with a leading measure you can act on now.

• The vanity measure. Counting what is easy and flattering ("meetings held", "reports written") instead of what actually drives the outcome. Ask: if this number is great but the real result is poor, is it worth measuring?

• The dashboard jungle. Fifty measures no one can steer by. If everything is important, nothing is. A whole works runs on perhaps ten to fifteen measures, total - the vital few.

• Gaming the number. Hitting the measure while missing the point - closing work orders without finishing the work to lift the "closure rate". A measure you can game is worse than no measure; it lies to you with a straight face.

Onalenna hit her one number and it nearly drowned a town. What she lacked was not effort or honesty - it was a way to see the whole board while she played one piece. Now you hold it: a balanced few measures, leading and lagging, across every corner that matters, so no single number can ever again steer you off a cliff. Measure the whole, watch the leading edges, and treat every red as the gift it is. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, bring your scorecard to your next session.
