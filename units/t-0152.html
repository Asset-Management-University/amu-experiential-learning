Sign it off, or test it forever

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and a new pump set has arrived, ready to be accepted into service. The acceptance decision is yours, and the room offers you two options, both of which feel wrong.

Your manager wants you to sign it off: it is from a good supplier, it looks well made, get it into service. But a pump can look perfect and deliver the wrong duty, or be rated for the wrong pressure, and no amount of looking will ever tell you that. The supplier, when you push, offers the other extreme: a full six-week test programme to prove everything - which is impossibly expensive and which nobody will sanction.

So you are trapped between unacceptable risk and unaffordable rigour. You have a list of what the pump is supposed to do, and no sensible plan for how to prove it does. And underneath even that sits a quieter worry: suppose it passes every check against the specification - did anyone confirm the specification itself was right for what the works actually needs now?

I have to accept a new asset without either signing on faith or testing forever - and I have a list of requirements but no plan to prove them. How do I prove each requirement is met, proportionately, and check the requirements themselves were right?

Before you read on, decide. The pump set is on the slab and the contractor wants it signed today. You have a requirements list — a duty point it must deliver, a coating that must be present, a nameplate rating, a demonstration that one operator can start it alone, an efficiency figure — and no plan at all for proving any of it. Signing on the strength of how well made it looks would take ten minutes and prove nothing about the duty. Testing everything properly is weeks and a fortune nobody will sanction. And the specification was written three years ago, before the works' demand changed. Write down how you would accept it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why the requirements are not alike

• “Sign it — it is clearly well built and the supplier is reputable.” Proves what the eye can reach, which is craftsmanship. A beautifully made pump can deliver entirely the wrong duty, and the eye has no access to that at all.

• “Test everything before accepting anything.” Unaffordable, and it will not happen — so in practice you will run out of time and money and sign on appearance anyway, having spent both.

• “Accept it and hold retention until it proves itself in service.” A commercial lever rather than a proof. It also means finding out in service, on the town's water, which is the thing acceptance exists to avoid.

• “Ask the supplier for their factory test certificates.” Genuinely useful evidence for some requirements, and it is a proof of what happened on their bed rather than what you are receiving here. It also covers whichever requirements they chose to test.

• “Get a specialist to inspect it.” Buys a better pair of eyes and stays inside the same method. Inspection is one kind of proof, and it is the wrong kind for a duty point or an efficiency figure.

• The way out is to notice that these are not the only two options, because the requirements are not alike. Each asks a different question, so the proof should follow from what is being claimed rather than from a general policy about rigour. Some things can only be settled by operating under controlled conditions and measuring — the duty point, the efficiency — which is the most rigorous and the dearest, so spend it there. Some are better settled by calculation, where a physical test would be impractical or absurd. Some are settled by looking at a specific feature against the specification, which costs almost nothing: the coating is there, the nameplate says what it must. And some are settled by operating it to show the capability exists — one operator starts it — which is qualitative and entirely sufficient for a qualitative claim. Match a proportionate method to each and acceptance becomes both affordable and honest. Then the quieter question, which this whole plan can pass while missing: everything above checks the pump against the specification, and nobody has asked whether the specification is still right for what the works needs now. A thing can meet every stated requirement and be the wrong thing.

There is a plan for exactly this, and it fits on one page. Here it is, for the pump set.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the V&V plan - proof for every requirement, matched to what it actually needs.

1. How will I prove each requirement? (not look-and-sign, not test-everything)

Accepting the new pump set is a choice between two wrong answers unless you plan it. 'It looks well made, sign it off' proves only what the eye can see - and a pump can look perfect and still deliver the wrong duty. 'Test everything before we accept' is weeks of work and a fortune nobody will sanction. The right answer is neither: every requirement needs a PROOF, but not the same proof. Some are proven by a test, some by a glance, some by a calculation, some by a live demonstration. The job is to match a proportionate method to each requirement - proof, not faith, and not overkill.

2. What are the four methods, and what is each best for? (test, inspection, analysis, demonstration)

Four standard ways to prove a requirement, ranging in rigour and cost. TEST: operate under controlled conditions and MEASURE - detailed quantitative data, the most rigorous and usually the dearest. ANALYSIS: prove it by calculation or modelling, using engineering principles when a physical test is not practical. INSPECTION: examine a feature or marking against a specification - often the cheapest (a coating is present, a nameplate says the right rating). DEMONSTRATION: operate the system to SHOW a capability is present - a qualitative proof (it starts, one operator can run it), without a test's detailed numbers. The art is knowing which one each requirement actually needs.

3. Which method proves each requirement? (the V&V matrix)

Match a method to each requirement of the pump set - the cheapest that adequately proves it. 'Delivers the duty flow at the specified head' -> TEST on the test stand (you need the measured curve, a glance will not do). 'Correct pressure rating and coating' -> INSPECTION of the nameplate and the certificate. 'Adequate NPSH margin for our suction conditions' -> ANALYSIS (a calculation, not a test). 'Can be isolated and started by one operator' -> DEMONSTRATION (watch one person do it). Four requirements, four different methods - because testing the coating would be silly and inspecting the pump curve is impossible.

4. Did I build it right - and is it the right thing? (verification vs validation)

Two different questions, and you need both. VERIFICATION asks: does the pump meet its written SPECIFICATION - is it built RIGHT? That is what the matrix above proves. VALIDATION asks: does it meet the real NEED - is it the RIGHT thing? And here is the trap: the pump could pass every verification and still be the wrong pump, because the DUTY in the specification was wrong - specified for last year's demand, not the growing town's. So alongside verifying against the spec, validate against the actual demand. Passing the wrong requirements perfectly is still failing.

5. The plan I accept it against (the V&V plan)

The V&V plan, on one page: a row per requirement - the requirement, the METHOD (test/inspection/analysis/demonstration), the ACCEPTANCE CRITERION (the exact pass/fail: 'flow >= 120 l/s at 45 m head, within 3%'), and WHEN it is checked - plus the validation check that the specified duty still matches the real demand. Now acceptance is not a signature of faith or an endless test programme; it is a defined, proportionate set of proofs, complete and affordable, that says exactly what 'accepted' means. Vague criteria make 'pass' an opinion; a criterion with a number makes it a fact.

Notice box 3 does the thing the two bad options could not: it gives each requirement a different proof, so nothing is signed on faith and nothing is tested that a glance or a calculation would settle. And box 4 catches the deeper trap - that you can prove a pump meets its spec perfectly and still have the wrong pump. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

Accepting an asset is proving it meets its requirements - proportionately. Five questions build the plan:

  • How will I prove each requirement? Not by looking and signing, not by testing everything - every requirement needs a proof, but not the same proof. Some are proven by a test, some by a glance, some by a calculation, some by a live demonstration. The job is to match a proportionate method to each.
  • What are the four methods? Four standard ways to prove a requirement, ranging in rigour and cost:
    • Test: operate under controlled conditions and measure - detailed quantitative data, most rigorous, usually dearest.
    • Analysis: prove it by calculation or modelling, using engineering principles when a physical test is not practical.
    • Inspection: examine a physical feature or marking against a specification - often the cheapest.
    • Demonstration: operate the system to show a capability is present - a qualitative proof, without a test's detailed numbers.
  • Which method proves each requirement? Build the V&V matrix: assign each requirement the cheapest method that adequately proves it. The duty flow needs a test; the pressure rating and coating need an inspection; the NPSH margin needs an analysis; single-operator start-up needs a demonstration. Testing the coating would be silly; inspecting the pump curve is impossible - the method follows the requirement.
  • Did I build it right - and is it the right thing? Two different questions. Verification asks whether it meets the written specification - "built right" - which the matrix proves. Validation asks whether it meets the real need - "the right thing". And a system can pass verification and fail validation: the pump meets a spec that was written for last year's demand, so it is a perfect answer to the wrong question. Verify against the spec; validate against the need.
  • What is the plan I accept it against? Assemble the V&V plan: each requirement, its method, its acceptance criterion (an exact pass/fail with a number), and when it is checked - plus the validation check. Now "accepted" means something defined, proportionate and complete.

One honest boundary. You plan the proof - choosing the proportionate method for each requirement and validating against the real need as well as verifying against the spec. You are not writing the requirements themselves, and you are not doing the detailed uncertainty analysis of a model - those are separate crafts. You are deciding how each requirement is proven, sensibly. And keep the two words apart: verification is "built right", validation is "the right thing", and you need both.

Does it scale? It is the difference between proving a house was built to the plans - inspect the wiring, test the taps, measure the rooms - and proving the plans were for a house you actually want to live in; the first is verification, the second is validation, and a beautifully built house to a bad design fails the one that matters most.

T4: Your Turn

A new standby generator is being accepted. Its requirements include: starts within 10 seconds of a mains failure; rated for the full site load; weatherproof outdoor enclosure; can be operated by the duty operator.

  1. Assign a method to each of the four requirements - test, inspection, analysis or demonstration.
  2. Justify one. Take one requirement and say why that method, and not a cheaper or dearer one, is the right proof.
  3. Write an acceptance criterion for the start-up-time requirement - an exact pass/fail.
  4. Validate. Write one validation question that checks whether these requirements themselves meet the real need (for example - is "full site load" the load the site will actually have next year?).

The test of a real answer: in step 1, you should not have used "test" for all four - the weatherproof enclosure is an inspection, the start time is a test, the load rating is an analysis, single-operator use is a demonstration. If every method came out "test", you have reached for rigour instead of matching it, which is the expensive half of the same mistake as signing on faith.

T5: The Guardrails

Acceptance goes wrong at the two extremes and in the gap between the two words.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• "It looks fine, sign it." Inspection alone, which proves only what is visible and misses the duty, the rating, the margin - everything that matters and cannot be seen. A glance is one method, not the whole plan.

• "Test everything." Unaffordable and unnecessary when an inspection or a calculation would give the same confidence. Rigour where it is not needed is just cost, and it is the reason the important tests never get funded.

• Method-to-requirement mismatch. Using a dear method where a cheap one proves it, or a cheap one where only a test gives the confidence needed. The method follows the requirement, in both directions.

• Verifying but never validating. Proving the asset meets a specification that was itself wrong - passing the wrong requirements perfectly. Always ask whether the spec still matches the real need.

• A requirement with no method. If a requirement has no proof assigned, it is never actually checked - and unchecked requirements are exactly the ones that fail in service.

• Vague acceptance criteria. "Adequate flow" makes "pass" a matter of opinion and argument. A criterion with a number makes it a fact everyone can agree on.

• V&V as an afterthought. Deciding how to prove things only at the end is too late; the plan is built from the requirements onward, so the proofs are designed in, not scrambled for.

Onalenna was trapped between signing on faith and testing forever, with a list of requirements and no way to prove them. Now she can build the plan: give each requirement the proportionate method that proves it, write acceptance criteria with numbers, and check not only that the asset was built to the spec but that the spec was right for the need. Acceptance stops being a leap of faith or an unaffordable ordeal and becomes a defined, honest set of proofs.
