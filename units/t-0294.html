Step 1 of 5

The bank will lower our rate if we hit sustainability targets - and an outside auditor checks them every year for five years. Which ones do I dare sign for?

T1: The Ache

You are Vusi Ndlovu, and you run a road freight business with fifty-one trucks out of a yard near Harrismith.

Eighteen of those trucks are past their economic life and the finance to replace them is agreed in principle - R30 million - on one condition that is not really a condition at all: the bank will link the interest rate to sustainability targets. Hit them and the rate steps down. Miss them and it steps up.

The relationship manager is pleasant about it and has already drafted three suggestions. Two of them you could probably live with. The third is "improve the driver wellbeing score by 20 per cent", and it is the one that has kept you awake.

Because you want it. You lost two drivers to exhaustion and one marriage to the long-haul roster in the last four years, and if anything on that page deserves to be a target it is that one. You would fund it tomorrow.

And there is no score. There has never been a score. You could invent one - a survey, a rating, something - but you know exactly how that goes: a good number in year one when everyone is enthusiastic, a different person running it in year three, and an outside auditor asking you to demonstrate that the year-four figure was produced the same way as the year-one figure, in front of a lender, with your name on the report.

So you are sitting with three proposals and no way to sort them, and the feeling that saying no to the one you care about most would be the cowardly answer rather than the professional one.

The bank will lower my rate for hitting sustainability targets, and an outside auditor checks them every year for five years. Which of these can I actually sign for - and how do I refuse the one I want most?

Before you read on, decide. Three proposed targets, five years of external verification, R30 million, and the one you most believe in is the one you cannot measure. Write down which you would sign and what you would say about the third - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one does with the third target

• "Sign all three - it shows commitment, and we will work out the wellbeing measure later." The generous answer, and "later" is a person who does not work here yet being asked to reproduce a method nobody wrote down. It also puts the target you care about most on the list most likely to be quietly dropped.

• "Sign the two safe ones and say nothing about the third." Comfortable in the meeting and it leaves the lender thinking you were not interested in driver welfare, which is the opposite of true - and it wastes the one moment when your reason would have carried weight.

• "Push back on the whole thing - we should not price a loan on promises." Understandable, and it declines a rate reduction for work you are largely doing anyway, and it treats every target as if it were the unmeasurable one.

• "Ask the bank to define the targets - they are the experts." They are experts in loans. They cannot know which of your numbers you can still produce in year four, and a target set by somebody who will not have to measure it is the most dangerous kind.

• "Pick whichever targets are easiest to hit." The rational-sounding one, and it buys a rate reduction for improvements that were coming anyway - which is decoration, and the person exposed when somebody notices is the one whose signature is under the number.

• What the first five are missing is that these three proposals are not the same kind of object and cannot be sorted by how much you want them. A target you sign here becomes an operational promise that an outsider will examine every year for five years - so the question is not which ones matter most, but which ones you can still produce, the same way, in year four, when everyone who agreed them has moved on. There are three tests for that, they take an afternoon, and one of them is what lets you refuse the target you care about most and be respected for it.

T2: The Tool in Action

Five moves on the three proposed targets, before anything is signed.

1. Understand what you are actually signing - and what happens if you miss

Vusi Ndlovu reads the term sheet twice. The facility is R30 million to replace eighteen trucks; the money is not ring-fenced to anything green. The margin steps 25 basis points either way on the targets - on R30 million that is R75,000 a year, up or down. Missing a target is expressly not an event of default; nobody calls in the loan. So R75,000 is the whole financial stake, against five years of an outside auditor examining figures with his name on them - and that ratio is the first thing that tells him how to choose. He is not managing a threat. He is making a promise.

2. Put every proposed target through three tests, in writing

The relationship manager proposes three. ROAD SAFETY, as accidents per million kilometres: matters obviously to a freight operator; measurable from the telematics system, which has recorded every incident for three years; comparable, because the industry association publishes a national figure. Passes all three. FUEL USE, as litres per hundred tonne-kilometres: matters; measurable from fuel cards against weighbridge tickets, both already audited for tax; comparable to their own three-year record. Passes. DRIVER WELLBEING, 'improve the driver wellbeing score by 20 per cent': matters enormously - Vusi has lost two drivers to burnout and would fund it tomorrow. But there is no score. There is no method to make one that a verifier would accept the same way for five years. It fails the second test.

3. Evidence the baseline before you argue about the target

Safety baseline: 4.1 recordable accidents per million kilometres, the mean of the last three full years from telematics, not the best of them - last year alone was 3.6 and using it would have quietly made every future target a third harder. Fuel baseline: 3.8 litres per hundred tonne-kilometres, from fuel-card litres against weighbridge tonnages, both already examined by the auditors for other reasons. Vusi writes the method for each in four lines - source, period, formula, who can reproduce it - and sends it to the verifier BEFORE the targets are agreed, so that the one thing nobody can dispute later is where the numbers started.

4. Set targets you would set anyway, and check they are neither free nor fantasy

Safety: 4.1 down to 2.9 by year 5 - a 29 per cent reduction, and Vusi can name what buys it, because the new trucks carry lane-departure and fatigue monitoring as standard and the fleet renewal is what the loan is for. Fuel: 3.8 down to 3.3, about 13 per cent - modest-looking, and honest, because most of the easy gain is the same new fleet and he will not pretend the driving will improve by itself. Both are things he would be doing anyway, which is exactly the point: the loan is not buying the improvement, it is pricing his willingness to be measured on it.

5. Refuse what you cannot produce - in writing, with the reason

Vusi writes it into the proposal rather than saying it on a call: 'We decline driver wellbeing as a KPI. It is material and we take it seriously, and we cannot measure it on a consistent basis that would survive five annual verifications. We are instead committing, outside the loan terms, to a quarterly driver forum and a funded fatigue-management programme, and we will report on both in our annual statement without them affecting the margin.' The relationship manager accepts it in a day. The programme goes ahead. And the two KPIs that were signed are ones Vusi can still produce in year four, when the person who negotiated the loan has moved on and only the method he wrote down remains.

Look at what the three tests did, and what they did not do. They did not rank the targets by importance - driver wellbeing is arguably the most important thing on that page, and it is the one that got refused. They sorted the proposals by whether Vusi can still produce the number in year four, which is a completely different question and the only one the next five years will actually ask. Two things make the refusal work rather than look like avoidance: it is in writing with the reason, and it comes with what he will do instead - a funded fatigue programme reported outside the loan terms, so the commitment survives without being priced. And notice the ratio he worked out in the very first box, because it governs everything after it: R75,000 a year of margin against five years of an outside auditor examining figures with his name on them. When the financial stake is that small next to the reputational one, the right instinct is not to maximise the rate reduction - it is to sign only what you can defend, and say so plainly.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the third is the one people skip because it is not the exciting part.

  • 1. Get the mechanics straight - what the rate step is worth a year, and what missing actually means.
  • 2. Put each proposal through three tests: does it matter, can you measure it identically every year, can it be compared to something outside you?
  • 3. Evidence the baseline before arguing about the target, and write down its method.
  • 4. Calibrate: neither a number you would reach anyway nor one that needs everything to go right.
  • 5. Refuse what fails a test, in writing, with the reason and with what you will do instead.

The instrument is a sustainability-linked loan. What it changes is what the money costs: the rate is tied to your measured performance against targets you agreed, and the money itself is yours to spend as the business requires. That is what distinguishes it from a green loan, which restricts the use of the funds instead - a distinction worth holding precisely, because the two are routinely confused. The targets are measured by key performance indicators, and the published principles for these loans are explicit that a KPI must be material to the business, measurable on a consistent methodological basis, and benchmarkable against an external reference - which is exactly the three tests, in the market's own words. The rate step is called a margin ratchet, and the fact that reframes the whole thing for an operator: in these instruments missing a target is normally a price adjustment, not a default. Nobody calls in the loan. Which is precisely why the choice belongs to whoever will have to produce the number rather than to a lawyer - it is not a legal risk to be managed once, it is an operational promise, externally verified, every year.

Where it fits: any commitment somebody outside will check repeatedly - a lender's targets, a customer's supply conditions, a licence condition, a grant's reporting, a public pledge. Three limits worth being straight about. The margin is not the prize: R75,000 against a R30 million fleet renewal is real and small, smaller than the fuel the new trucks save, and if the only argument for a target is the rate step then it is the wrong target. Passing the three tests does not make a target ambitious - measurable and trivial is a combination lenders see often, and it is why the third test exists. And the tests judge what you can promise, never what matters: the thing you refuse may well be the most important thing on the page, and refusing it as a KPI is not the same as declining to do it.

T4: Your Turn

Do each task before opening its reveal.

Task 1 - Sort three proposals. A commercial laundry is offered the same kind of loan. The lender proposes: (a) reduce water use per tonne of laundry by 20 per cent; (b) increase the proportion of staff who feel valued by 25 per cent; (c) reduce electricity per tonne by 15 per cent. The laundry has five years of metered water and electricity readings against weighed loads, and has never surveyed its staff. Put each through the three tests and say which you would sign.

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

(a) and (c) pass all three: both are material to a laundry, both are measurable identically every year from meters against weighed loads that already exist, and both can be compared to the laundry's own five-year record and to published industry figures. (b) fails the second test - there is no survey, so there is no consistent method, and inventing one now means the year-one figure and the year-four figure are produced by different people with different questions. Good answers say clearly that (b) may well be the most important of the three and that this is not the test being applied.

Task 2 - The baseline that costs you. The laundry's water use per tonne over five years was 24, 23, 21, 22 and 18 litres, the 18 being last year, when a major leak was finally repaired in March. The lender's draft uses last year as the baseline. What do you say, and what do you propose instead?

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

You object, and not because 18 is wrong - it is because 18 is a part-year benefit of a repair that only applied from March, so it is neither a normal year nor a full one. A 20 per cent reduction from 18 is 14.4; from a defensible three-year mean of about 20.3 it is 16.2, and the difference is the whole target. Propose: use the three most recent full years, or use last year and re-state it as though the repair had applied all twelve months, whichever you can evidence - and write the method down before the target is agreed. Good answers note that the leak repair is a real improvement that should be reflected somewhere, and that the argument is about which year is representative, not about claiming credit twice.

Task 3 - The one you cannot sign. Name a target you would genuinely want to commit to in your own work and could not measure consistently for five years. Write the two-sentence refusal: why it fails, and what you will do instead outside the terms.

Try it yourself, then check what good looks like

A real answer is uncomfortable, because it names something you care about. The refusal has a shape: state that it is material and that you take it seriously; state precisely which test it fails and why - usually that no method exists which an outsider could apply identically in year four; then name the commitment you are making anyway, outside the priced terms, and how you will report it. If your answer was something you never wanted in the first place, the exercise has been dodged - the discipline is refusing a good-sounding number you cannot produce, and the reason it is hard is that you wanted it.

Now take a commitment somebody is asking you to make - a lender, a customer, a funder, a regulator, a board - and run the five moves. What is the mechanism and what does missing mean? Which proposals pass all three tests? Where does each baseline come from, over what period, and who could reproduce it? Would you be pursuing this improvement anyway, and can you name what buys it? And which one are you going to refuse, in writing, with the reason and the alternative?

T5: The Guardrails

These commitments go wrong in a few very consistent ways, and almost all of them are settled before signing.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Promising something you cannot measure twice the same way. If you cannot write the measurement method in four lines before signing - source, period, formula, who can reproduce it - you do not have a target, you have a hope, and a verifier will ask for that method every year.

• An unevidenced baseline. Every target is a change from a starting point. If the starting figure cannot be evidenced, the target built on it collapses at the first review - and it collapses as your failure.

• A baseline taken from a good year. The quietest way to make five years hard. Use a defensible mean, or a re-stated full year, and say which you used and why.

• An easy target on something that does not matter. Measurable and trivial is a combination lenders see often. It converts the instrument into decoration, and the person exposed is whoever signed the number.

• A target set by whoever will not have to measure it. The lender knows loans; the regulator knows rules. Only you know which figure you can still produce in year four when the people who agreed it have moved on.

• Chasing the margin. The rate step is usually small beside the thing being financed. It is a discipline and a signal, not the prize - if the only argument for a target is the margin, it is the wrong target.

• Confusing the two instruments. A green loan restricts what the money may be spent on; this kind restricts nothing and changes what the money costs. Mixing them up in a meeting is the fastest way to lose the room.

• Refusing by silence. Declining a target without saying why reads as indifference to the thing itself. Put the refusal, the reason and the alternative commitment in the document - it is the line that earns you the most credit in the whole proposal.

You sat with three proposed targets, the one you cared about most impossible to measure, and the feeling that refusing it would be cowardice. It is the opposite. Two targets signed with their methods written down and their baselines evidenced before the numbers were argued; one refused in writing, with the reason and with a funded programme committed anyway outside the priced terms - and a relationship manager who now knows exactly how much your signature is worth. Sign what you can still produce in year four, not what you most want to be true. Refusing a good-sounding promise you cannot keep is the most professional line in any document.
