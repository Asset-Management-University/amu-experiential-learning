Ten minutes to be heard

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works, and you have ten minutes with the management team to get a failing pump replaced. You have earned this slot. The work behind it is the best you have ever done - months of readings, a clean analysis, a conclusion you would stake your name on.

So you present it the way you did it. The background. How you gathered the data. The method. The readings. You are still working through the analysis, building carefully towards your recommendation, when the chair glances at the clock and says, kindly enough: "We are out of time. Can you send it round?"

And that is that. The recommendation - the entire point, the one thing you needed them to hear - never gets said out loud. You look at their faces on the way out and they are not hostile; they are just lost. Weeks later nothing has been decided, the pump has failed again, and the best technical work of your life is sitting unopened in six inboxes. The engineering was not the problem. The engineering was excellent.

My analysis was right, and it still changed nothing - I ran out of time before I ever reached the point. How do I put technical work in front of busy, mixed decision-makers so that they actually decide?

Before you read on, decide. You had ten minutes and three people: finance, operations, safety. You began where the work began — the monitoring history, the method, the vibration analysis — and at minute eight the finance lead asked what it would cost, which was on a slide you never reached. Nothing was decided. Weeks later the pump has failed again and your report sits unopened in six inboxes. The engineering was excellent; nobody disputed a word of it. Write down how you would use those ten minutes differently — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the instrument you were actually holding

• “Ask for more time — thirty minutes instead of ten.” You would lose them at minute eight of thirty instead. The constraint that beat you was not the clock; it was that the thing they had to decide was at the end.

• “Send the full report in advance so they arrive briefed.” They did not open it after the meeting, when they knew it mattered. Sending it earlier moves the same unread document to a different day.

• “Cut the technical detail — simplify it.” Half right and the wrong half. Simplifying a chronological story still leaves the conclusion at the end; you would arrive at minute six instead of minute twelve, and still be explaining rather than asking.

• “Add better slides — charts of the vibration data.” This makes the evidence prettier for a room that never disputed the evidence. Not one of those three people was going to decide on a spectrum plot.

• “Follow up individually afterwards.” Sometimes necessary, and here it is a repair for a meeting that should have worked. It also multiplies your effort by three and still leaves the decision homeless.

• The trouble is that you brought a report to a room that needed a different instrument entirely, and it runs in the opposite direction. Put the conclusion in the first sentence — what should happen, what it costs, by when — so that if they hear nothing else they have heard the thing they must decide, and everything after exists only to hold it up. State the problem in the language they decide in: not that the vibration baseline shifted, but that this pump fails roughly every ninety days and each failure costs about R1.2 million in lost production. Bring three facts, not thirty — the ones that carry the decision, with the rest left where they belong, in the report. And end with a specific ask: a named decision, from named people, by a date, because a room that has merely been informed will not decide. One thing more, and it explains the questions you could not answer: that was not one audience. Three people were asking three different questions, and a briefing has to answer each of them.

There is a one-page tool that fixes this, and it works by turning everything you know about writing a report upside down. Let us show you one - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the page Onalenna should have walked in with - the whole ten minutes planned, ending in a decision instead of an apology.

1. The room - who decides, and what does each of them need?

The room: the finance lead (needs the cost and what it saves, in rand); the operations lead (needs to know what it does to production); the safety lead (needs the risk of doing nothing). Three people, three different questions - and one briefing that answers all three.

2. The bottom line - one sentence, said first

One sentence, said first: 'We should replace the P-101 pump with the upgraded model, at R850,000, before the end of this quarter - to stop a failure that is costing us far more than the fix.' Everything after this exists only to support that sentence.

3. The problem - in THEIR language, not yours

Not 'the vibration baseline has shifted beyond the control band' but: 'This pump is failing roughly every 90 days, and each failure costs about R1.2 million in lost production and puts our supply commitment at risk.' Same fact, said in the language the room decides in.

4. The vital few facts - three, not thirty

Three facts that carry it: (1) three failures in nine months, all with the same signature; (2) the upgraded model has had no such failures across fifteen other sites in two years; (3) the whole fix costs R850,000 - less than a single one of those failures. Thirty more facts exist; they belong in the report.

5. The ask and the next step - specific, and decidable

The ask: 'I need approval for the R850,000 by Friday.' The next step: 'If it is approved, I place the order on Monday and the pump is in before quarter-end.' A vague ending gets a vague answer; a specific ask can actually be said yes to.

Notice what has happened to the order. The conclusion, which she was building towards at the end, is now the first thing said. The method - which took her months and which she is rightly proud of - does not appear at all. It has not been thrown away; it is sitting in the written report behind her, ready for anyone who wants it. What is in front of the room is only what the room needs in order to decide. Now let us walk through why that works.

T3: The Blueprint

What you just saw is a decision briefing, and the single most important thing about it is this: a briefing is not a report read aloud. They are two different tools with two different jobs. Put them side by side and you can see it instantly:

  • A written report runs: background, method, data, analysis, and finally the conclusion. It is built for the record - so that anyone, later, can follow your reasoning and check it.
  • A briefing runs the other way round: the conclusion and the ask first, then why it matters in their terms, then only the few facts that carry it. It is built for a decision - here, now, from people with ten minutes.

The briefing is your report inverted and filtered. Each part answers a question:

  • Who decides, and what does each of them need? The finance lead, the operations lead and the safety lead are asking three different questions. A briefing that answers only your own question answers none of theirs.
  • If they remember one sentence, what is it? That is the bottom line, and it goes first - the recommendation, not the suspense. This is sometimes called putting the bottom line up front, and it is the standard everywhere decisions are made under time pressure.
  • Why should they care - in their terms? Translate. "The vibration baseline has shifted" becomes "this pump fails every ninety days and each failure costs about R1.2 million". Note carefully: the fact has not changed and nothing has been softened. Only the language changed. This is translation, never simplification - and the technical statement is still there, traceable, in the report.
  • Which few facts carry it? Three, not thirty. Your job in a briefing is to filter, not to broadcast everything you found. The rest is not lost; it is in the leave-behind.
  • What exactly am I asking for? A specific decision, from a named person, by a date - and what you will do the moment you have it. "So that is the situation" gets you nothing. "I need approval for R850,000 by Friday" can be said yes to.

Does it scale? The ten minutes Onalenna needs to get a pump replaced use the very same structure as a chief executive putting a multi-billion-rand acquisition to a board, or your own five-minute value pitch on a final-year project - the decision first, the language of the room, the vital few facts, a clear ask.

T4: Your Turn

Now plan a briefing of your own. Take something real that you actually want decided - a final-year project proposal, funding for a society event, a change you want at work or at home.

  1. Name the room. Who must say yes? Beside each person, write the question they are really asking.
  2. Write the bottom line - one sentence, the thing you want decided. Say it out loud. If it takes more than one breath, cut it.
  3. Translate the problem into their language - money, time, risk, or what it costs them to do nothing.
  4. Choose the vital few facts. Aim for three; more than four risks a data flood. Which facts truly carry the decision?
  5. Write the ask - what, from whom, by when - and the next step you will take the moment you get it.

T5: The Guardrails

Briefing well is a craft, and a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you walk into the room.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Telling it chronologically. Walking the room through your journey and saving the recommendation for the end - exactly what cost Onalenna her ten minutes. Invert it: the answer first, always.

• The jargon wall. Technical terms that quietly exclude the very person who controls the money. If they cannot follow you, they cannot approve you. Translate - without changing a single fact.

• The data flood. Presenting all thirty findings because you worked hard for them. It buries the three that matter. Filtering is not hiding; it is doing your job.

• The vague ending. Finishing with "so that is the situation" instead of a specific ask. No ask, no decision - and you will be back next quarter with the same problem.

• Reading the report aloud. The report is a different artefact with a different job. Brief it; do not recite it.

• No leave-behind. If the detail has nowhere to live, a short briefing starts to feel thin and indefensible. The written report behind you is what makes brevity confident rather than shallow.

Onalenna's engineering was never the problem - her work was excellent and it still changed nothing, because it never reached the moment of decision. Now you hold the tool that carries it there: know the room, lead with the answer, speak in their language, bring only the facts that carry it, and ask for something specific. Do that, and your good work stops dying in inboxes and starts turning into pumps replaced, budgets approved, and things that actually get fixed.
