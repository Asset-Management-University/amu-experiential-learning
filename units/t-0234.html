One real customer

T1: The Ache

You try to speak to everyone, so no one hears you. Your posts, your pitch, your pricing - all aimed at "anyone who might want this" - come out so general that they land on no one in particular. And underneath the vague marketing is a vaguer problem: you cannot actually picture the person you are selling to.

So every decision feels like a guess. Where should you advertise? You do not know where they are. What should you say? You do not know what they want. What should you charge? You do not know what they will pay. Every choice is a shrug, because the person you are serving is a ghost - and you cannot aim at a ghost.

I am trying to sell to everyone, so my marketing reaches no one and every decision is a guess - because I cannot even picture who my customer really is. How do I get clear on who I serve?

Before you read on, decide. Three decisions are sitting in front of you and every one is a shrug. Where should you advertise? You do not know where they are. What should you say? You do not know what they want. What should you charge? You do not know what they will pay. You are working hard and the product is good. And in your phone are three customers who have come back four or five times each — one of whom told you exactly why she buys from you rather than the shop up the road, and you have never thought about that remark since. Write down how you would get clear on who you serve — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and why 'everyone' is the comfortable trap

• “My product suits everyone — I should not turn people away.” It feels safe and it is exactly why nothing works. A message built for everyone is heard by no one, and a business pointed everywhere cannot aim at all.

• “Advertise more widely to find them.” Spreads thin effort across people who were never going to buy, and with no budget it is the most expensive possible way to learn nothing.

• “Copy what a similar business does.” Adopts their customer along with their tactics. If their buyer is not yours, you will be executing somebody else's plan competently and getting nothing.

• “Do a survey.” Better than guessing and it skips what you already have. Three loyal customers and a remark you have never examined are better evidence than a survey of strangers, and they cost nothing.

• “Imagine my ideal customer and write it down.” Half right and the wrong half — a portrait invented at a desk describes your assumptions, and will confidently point you in the direction you were already facing.

• So: pick one real person and understand them well enough to recognise them in a queue. Narrowing feels risky and is actually the unlock — it is clarifying rather than limiting, you can always widen later, and you can only aim if you are pointed somewhere. What you need to know divides into what they are like and what they want: roughly who and where they are, so you can find them at all; what they are trying to achieve; and what is currently frustrating them, because a problem somebody actually feels is what makes them act. Then the practical half — where they already spend their attention, what would make them trust somebody they have not bought from before, and what they would object to before buying. Each of those turns one of your shrugs into a decision. Two disciplines keep it honest. Build it from evidence rather than imagination — those three customers, that remark. And hold what it is for: it is a tool for empathy, to understand somebody's world well enough to serve them, not to find the lever that makes them buy.

You draw one real customer, in detail, from evidence. Let us build the portrait.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you build a sharp, evidence-based portrait of the one customer you serve - across the six things you must know to reach and sell to them - and then use it to make every decision clear.

1. Why one portrait, not everyone

The first thing to un-learn is the comforting fallacy that your customer is 'EVERYONE'. It feels safe - why turn anyone away? - but it is the reason nothing works, because a message built for everyone is heard by NO ONE, and a business pointed everywhere cannot aim. The opposite move feels risky and is actually the unlock: pick ONE real customer and get to know them so well you could recognise them in a queue. YOUR DECISIONS BECOME CLEAR THE MOMENT YOU STOP TRYING TO SERVE 'EVERYONE' AND START SERVING ONE REAL PERSON YOU HAVE TAKEN THE TIME TO UNDERSTAND. And narrow is NOT limiting - it is CLARIFYING; you can always widen later, but you can only aim if you are pointed somewhere. And hold onto what the portrait is for: it is a tool for EMPATHY, built to understand a person's world so you can serve them well - not to exploit them, but to see them clearly enough to help them solve a problem that matters to them.

2. Six dimensions (1): situation, goals, pains

The portrait covers SIX things, and the first three tell you who the person is and what they are after. DEMOGRAPHICS AND SITUATION - roughly who and where: age, income level, location, life stage - so you can FIND them, because you cannot reach a customer whose whereabouts you have not pictured. GOALS AND ASPIRATIONS - what they are genuinely trying to achieve - so you can SPEAK to it, in their words, about their hope rather than your features. PAINS AND FRUSTRATIONS - what blocks them from that goal - so you can SOLVE it, because the pain is the reason they will ever pay you. One honesty here, and it matters: WHEN YOU DESCRIBE A PERSON'S SITUATION OR WHAT THEY CAN AFFORD, YOU ARE DOING SO WITH RESPECT. THIS IS ABOUT MEETING SOMEONE WHERE THEY ARE, NOT REDUCING THEM TO A STEREOTYPE. A PORTRAIT IS DRAWN FROM EVIDENCE ABOUT REAL INDIVIDUALS, TO HELP YOU PRICE YOUR WORK FAIRLY AND FIND THE PEOPLE YOU CAN BEST SERVE. IT IS NEVER AN EXCUSE FOR CARICATURE.

3. Six dimensions (2): current solution, trigger, price

The next three dimensions turn understanding into a business. CURRENT SOLUTIONS - what they are ALREADY doing about the problem - so you can BEAT it, and this dimension carries a hidden lesson: YOUR REAL COMPETITION IS OFTEN NOT ANOTHER BUSINESS AT ALL BUT A HABIT OR A WORKAROUND - 'I skip it', 'I make do', 'I do it myself' - and you win by being clearly better than THAT. PURCHASE TRIGGERS - the event, the timing, or the emotion that actually makes them act - so you can TIME your offer to the moment they are ready, instead of shouting at people who are not. PRICE SENSITIVITY - the range they accept and why - so you can PRICE it where the value meets what they will genuinely pay. Six dimensions, one person, and each is a lever you will pull later - find them, speak to them, solve their pain, beat their current solution, time your offer, price it right.

4. Build it from evidence, not imagination

A portrait is only worth something if it is TRUE, and here is the fatal trap: IT IS FAR EASIER TO INVENT A CUSTOMER THAN TO FIND ONE. The invented customer is a fantasy who happens to want exactly what you already make, at the price you want to charge, for the reasons you find flattering - and building on them feels wonderful, because THEY CONFIRM YOUR PLAN INSTEAD OF TESTING IT. THAT COMFORT IS THE DANGER. A real portrait is drawn from EVIDENCE: the actual people you have talked to and watched (the customer-discovery conversations), so it reflects how customers really think and decide, not how you wish they would. IF YOUR PORTRAIT TELLS YOU ONLY GOOD NEWS, YOU DID NOT BUILD IT FROM EVIDENCE; YOU BUILT IT FROM HOPE. Go and find the real person before you draw them - a portrait that never delivers an inconvenient truth was never real.

5. Use it - or you never built it

The portrait is a tool you USE, not a document you file, and the test of a real one is simple: DOES IT CHANGE WHAT YOU DO? Each dimension points straight at a decision. The DEMOGRAPHICS tell you WHERE TO MARKET. The GOALS AND PAINS tell you WHAT TO SAY. The CURRENT SOLUTION tells you WHO YOU ARE REALLY COMPETING WITH. The TRIGGER tells you WHEN TO APPEAR. The PRICE SENSITIVITY tells you WHAT TO CHARGE. Run every marketing, product and pricing choice through the portrait and ask 'WHAT WOULD THIS PERSON DO?' IF YOUR PORTRAIT SITS IN A DRAWER AND NONE OF YOUR DECISIONS WOULD CHANGE WITHOUT IT, IT WAS NEVER A PORTRAIT - IT WAS A PARAGRAPH. And keep it alive: real customers surprise you, so update the portrait as you learn - it is a living picture, not a monument.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. Someone is starting a mobile car-wash service - they come to you and wash your car where it is parked. They currently describe their customer as "anyone with a car." Build them a real portrait.

  1. Draw the six-dimension portrait of one specific, real customer: demographics/situation, goals, pains, current solution, purchase trigger, price sensitivity.
  2. Name the real competition. What is this customer's actual current solution - the habit or workaround you are really up against?
  3. Turn it into decisions. Take two dimensions of your portrait and turn each into one concrete business decision.
  4. Evidence vs invention. Explain the difference between this evidence-based portrait and one the founder might have invented to suit their own plans.
  5. With respect. Check that your description of the customer is drawn with respect and evidence, and avoids caricature.

The test of a real answer: five things.

First, a sharp portrait, for example: demographics - car-owning professionals, busy, who value their weekend time, in a particular set of suburbs or an office precinct; goals - a clean car without spending a Saturday morning on it; pains - no time, and the effort of driving to and queuing at a wash; current solution - washing it themselves occasionally, or letting it stay dirty; trigger - a special occasion, a visitor, or simply the grime crossing a threshold; price sensitivity - will pay a premium over a fixed-location wash for the come-to-you convenience, within reason.

Second, the real competition: usually "I will get around to doing it myself" or "I just leave it dirty" - a habit, not another car wash. You win by beating the habit (make it effortless), not only by beating other washes on price.

Third, two decisions: from the trigger, offer a quick booking exactly when the car crosses that grime threshold (a simple reminder); from the price sensitivity and demographics, set a convenience premium and target the suburbs where time is worth more than the extra rand. The mark of a real answer is that a portrait dimension drives each choice.

Fourth, evidence vs invention: an invented portrait would be "a customer who loves my exact package at my exact price" - flattering and useless; an evidence-based one comes from actually asking car owners what they do now and what would make them switch, and it may tell you inconvenient truths (they will not pay as much as you hoped, or the real barrier is trust about scratches).

Fifth, with respect: the portrait describes people's real situations and budgets to serve them fairly and find who is genuinely a good fit - it meets them where they are, and never tips into caricature or looking down on anyone.

T5: The Guardrails

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Aiming at "everyone". A business for everyone speaks to no one. Pick one real customer; narrow is clarifying, and you can widen later.

• A portrait that fits anyone. "Busy people who want quality" is not a portrait - it guides nothing. Make it specific enough to recognise the person.

• Inventing a customer who agrees with you. The convenient fantasy confirms your plan instead of testing it. Draw the portrait from real people you have actually talked to.

• Skipping the evidence. If the portrait tells you only good news, it came from hope, not data. Go and ask before you draw.

• Forgetting the current-solution dimension. Your real competition is often a habit or workaround, not another business. Know what the customer does now, and beat that.

• A portrait you never use. If no decision would change without it, it is a paragraph in a drawer. Run every choice through it.

• Caricature or stereotype. Describe a person's situation and budget with respect, from evidence - to serve them, never to look down on them.

• Treating it as fixed. Real customers surprise you. Update the portrait as you learn; it is a living picture, not a monument.
