The board set aside R12 million in 2009 to close this 34-year-old plant - but there is a 40 m stack, a tailings dam, oil-soaked soil, and 210 people to pay. How do I estimate honestly what it truly costs to leave the land safe and legal, and prove the money is there?

T1: The Ache

You are standing on the gantry above the old sinter line at the Stellaford Manganese Sinter Plant, an inland manganese works that has run for 34 years. Last month the board signed the paper: it closes in 18 months. Now it is your job to say what closing it will actually cost.

In your hand is a single sheet from the Finance Director, Reneilwe. On it, in her neat handwriting, is one number: R12 million. Under it she has written: "This is what we set aside for closure back in 2009. Is it enough? Board needs your answer in three weeks."

You walk the plant. The sharp, dusty bite of old manganese ore has settled into everything for three decades. You see the things her number never counted: a 40 m stack that has to come down, two electric furnaces, kilometres of conveyor, a tailings dam holding 600,000 tonnes of fines, an old transformer bay that everyone says has oil-soaked soil under it, and a workforce of 210 people who will need their last pay and their certificates. Reneilwe's number is 16 years old - a guess, made before the tailings dam grew, before the new environmental rules, before the rand fell.

You have a strong urge to just take her R12 million, add a bit "to be safe", and hand it back. That would be quick. It would also be a number built on nothing - and if it is wrong by half, the company carries a hole it never planned for, or worse, the site is left half-cleaned and the company is found liable years later.

A 16-year-old guess of R12 million is all we set aside to close a 34-year-old plant. How do I estimate honestly what it truly costs to take it down and leave the land safe and legal - and prove to the board the money is actually there?

Before you read on, decide. A thirty-four-year-old plant: a stack to bring down, two furnaces, kilometres of conveyor, a tailings dam holding 600,000 tonnes, a transformer bay everyone says has oil-soaked soil under it, and 210 people who will need their last pay and their certificates. Set against all of it: R12 million, guessed sixteen years ago. Write down how you would produce an honest number. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one leaves the community with

• “Take Reneilwe’s R12 million and add a bit to be safe.” Quick, and it is a number built on nothing. Her figure predates the tailings dam growing, the new environmental rules and the currency — and “a bit” is not an estimate, it is a hope with a decimal point.

• “Get a demolition contractor to quote it.” Useful for the part they do, and it is one bucket of four. A demolition price does not cover disposal of what comes down, the cleanup of what is in the ground, or the 210 people’s final pay and certificates — and a quote for a quarter of the job reads like a total.

• “Estimate the soil contamination from what people say is under the transformer bay.” Everybody says it, and nobody has looked. Contamination is the one cost that cannot be guessed at any price, because the difference between a small patch and a plume is the difference between a line item and the whole estimate.

• “Deal with it closer to the time — the plant is still running.” The most comfortable answer and the most expensive. Money set aside late is money that was spent elsewhere, and a closure discovered to be unaffordable is a site left half-cleaned with the company found liable years afterwards.

• “Give the board a range so we’re not held to a figure.” Honest-sounding, and a range with nothing underneath it is the old guess in a wider suit. The board is not only being asked what it costs — they are being asked whether the money is actually there, and no range answers that.

Hold on to what “finished” means here, because it decides what you count: a plant is not finished when production stops. It is finished when the land is safe, the water is clean, the workers are paid in full, and the law is satisfied.

You need a reliable, defensible way to estimate what it truly costs to take this plant down and leave the land safe and legal - and to show the board the money is really there to do it. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

The method is a decommissioning cost estimate and site remediation plan: work out what it costs to safely shut down, take apart and dispose of the asset, and what it costs to clean up and hand back the land - then prove the money is funded and the plan is legal. The costs split into honest buckets (decommissioning, dismantling, disposal, remediation), and the cleanup is never guessed: you open the ground and price what is really there. Watch all the steps run on the Stellaford closure.

Step 1 - Set the boundary, then break it down (WBS)

For the Stellaford closure: IN - the sinter line, two furnaces, the 40 m stack, conveyors, the transformer bay, the tailings dam, all buildings and the land; OUT - the rail siding (handed to the rail operator) and the staff housing (sold separately). The WBS splits into decommission (make-safe), dismantle and demolish, disposal, remediation, and workforce - each broken down until priceable: disposal, for instance, into scrap-steel sale, general waste, and hazardous waste (asbestos, contaminated soil).

Step 2 - Assess the ground (do not guess the dirt)

A R450,000 contaminated-land assessment finds the truth under the plant: transformer-oil soil to 1.8 m depth over 900 m2 - about 1,600 tonnes to dig out and treat - and a tailings dam that is stable but must be capped and re-vegetated, not removed (removal would cost far more and is not required). The rest of the site is within limits. Now you can price the remediation honestly, not by guesswork.

Step 3 - Price each line (with confidence), net off salvage

Key priced lines: make-safe R1,800,000 (high); dismantle furnaces and line R6,500,000 (med); demolish the 40 m stack R2,200,000 (med); asbestos roof R2,400,000 (med); contaminated soil R3,200,000 (med); tailings dam cap and re-vegetate R10,900,000 (low - the big uncertain line); workforce final pay, retrenchment and certificates for 210 people R9,800,000 (high); plus conveyor, general waste, closure management and the R450,000 assessment - subtotal R41,550,000. Salvage: 3,400 tonnes of scrap steel at R2,800 = R9,520,000 income. Net before contingency: R41,550,000 - R9,520,000 = R32,030,000.

Step 4 - Add contingency, then compare to the provision

The tailings line is low-confidence and large, so you apply a blended +35% contingency on the net: R32,030,000 x 0.35 = R11,210,500, for a total funded estimate of R43,240,500 - presented as a range of about R38 million to R48 million, honest about the uncertainty. Against the R12 million set aside in 2009, the funding gap is about R31 million: the old provision is short by more than three times. That is the headline.

Step 5 - Build the funded, compliant plan

Across the 18 months: make-safe first, then dismantle, then the soil and tailings remediation, then environmental sign-off. Funding route: top up the closure provision from operating surplus so the financial-provision requirement is met before final closure. Compliance: submit the closure plan; the soil and tailings work satisfies the remediation conditions. Hand-back end-state: 'land safe for light industrial use, groundwater within limits'. The board gets one page - estimate R38-48m, best R43.2m, gap R31m, the funding plan and the legal route - a defensible answer, not a guess.

See what the estimate did. It turned a 16-year-old guess of R12 million into an honest, defensible R43 million - and named a R31 million gap the board had to be told about. And see the mission in it, because this technique is a test of integrity more than arithmetic. A closed plant is not finished when production stops; it is finished when the land is safe, the water is clean, the workers are paid in full, and the law is satisfied - and all of that costs real money that someone must actually fund. The deepest wrong in asset management is to take the profit for thirty-four years and then leave the community a poisoned, abandoned site and an unpayable bill. This method refuses that. It counts the contaminated soil, it counts the tailings, it counts the 210 people's final pay and certificates, and it forces the company to PROVE the money is set aside - financial provision, not a promise. Money here is the measure of an honest closure, never a number to be quietly minimised; the aim is land handed back safe and people sent off whole.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the estimate and plan in five moves.

  • 1. Set the boundary, then break it down. What is in and out; a Work Breakdown Structure of priceable pieces.
  • 2. Assess the ground. A contaminated-land assessment - do not guess the dirt.
  • 3. Price each line, net off salvage. Every piece with a confidence; the scrap income listed separately.
  • 4. Add contingency, compare to the provision. Money for the unseen; the honest range; the gap.
  • 5. Build the funded, compliant plan. Schedule, funding route, closure plan, hand-back end-state.

The ideas underneath. Decommissioning is making the asset safely dead (isolate power, drain, purge, lock out energy); dismantling is taking it apart; disposal covers scrap steel (which sells, paying you back), general waste, and hazardous waste (asbestos, oily soil - far costlier); remediation is making good - cleaning land and water so the site is safe and legal. A Work Breakdown Structure breaks the big job into pieces small enough to price - if it is not on the list, it is not costed, and that is how estimates go wrong. Contingency is money for what you cannot see yet, scaled to your confidence (an early estimate carries a wide band, often +/-30% to +/-50%, tightening as you investigate). A contaminated-land assessment samples soil and water so you can price the cleanup honestly. And the law's key idea is financial provision: a closure plan under environmental law (in South Africa the National Environmental Management Act, and for mines the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act) requires you to prove the money for closure exists, not just promise it - your estimate is that proof, set against the closure provision already put aside. This fits any asset being retired where dismantling, disposal and land impact are real - plants, mines, tank farms, substations; it is overkill for a clean swap-out of a small asset with known scrap value and no land impact.

Two disciplines keep the estimate honest. First, never price remediation you have not investigated - the contaminated soil under that transformer bay could be a R500,000 job or a R5 million one, and the only way to know is to open the ground; an estimate that guesses the dirt is the one that leaves the company with a hole and the land half-cleaned. Second, show the gap, do not soften it - the temptation is to shrink the number so the board is not alarmed, but a closure under-provisioned is a liability deferred onto the future and the community; the honest R31 million gap, stated plainly today, is a kindness compared with the abandoned, unfunded site it prevents.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your closure estimate true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• An old provision is a guess, not a budget. A number set aside years ago, before the rules and the rand changed, is a starting question - not the answer. Re-estimate from the real site, today.

• If it is not on the WBS, it is not costed. The stack, the tailings, the soil, the people - break the whole job into priceable pieces, or the missing line becomes the surprise that sinks the budget.

• Do not guess the ground. Remediation cost lives in the soil and water you have not sampled. Pay for the assessment; it is the cheapest line that saves the dearest mistake.

• List salvage separately, scale contingency to confidence. Never bury the scrap income inside a cost; never hide the uncertainty. Show the gross, the recovery, and an honest band.

• Prove the money, then state the gap. Financial provision means funded, not promised. If the estimate exceeds what is set aside, say so plainly - a deferred liability lands on the community.

You came onto the gantry with a 16-year-old number and three weeks to answer for it. You can hand the board something far better than a padded guess: a bounded job, a sampled site, every line priced with its confidence, the salvage netted off, an honest +35% on the uncertainty, and a R31 million gap named out loud - with a funding plan and a legal route to close it. A defensible, funded, compliant closure plan that leaves the land safe and legal, the workers paid their full due, and proves the money is really there. That is what it means to finish well: not to walk away when the profit stops, but to hand back the ground clean and the people whole. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
