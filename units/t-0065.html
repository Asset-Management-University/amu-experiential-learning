My pump failed five times this year, three last year, two the year before - the gaps are clearly shrinking, but is it really getting worse, or just bad luck? I could quote an average by lunch, and it would hide the very thing I am being asked.

T1: The Ache

It is a wet Tuesday morning at the Tswelopele Water Treatment Works, a medium-sized plant that pushes clean water to about 40,000 homes. You are the reliability engineer. On your desk is a single sheet: the repair log for Raw-Water Pump P-2 over the last three years.

The plant manager, Onkarabile, is in your doorway with her coat still wet. She is not angry, but she is tired, and she wants a straight answer. "That pump went down again on Sunday. That makes five times this year. Last year it was three. The year before, two. Tell me - is this pump just having a run of bad luck, or is it actually getting worse?"

You look at the dates. The repairs are bunching up - the gaps used to be eight, nine months; now they are weeks apart. The budget meeting is on Friday, and Onkarabile will ask whether to keep nursing P-2 or to spend R380,000 on a replacement.

There is a voice in your head: just average the failures and quote a mean time between failures. Quick. A number by lunch. But you know an average hides the very thing she is asking - whether the gaps are shrinking. An average treats "getting worse" and "steady" as the same thing. They are not the same, and Friday's decision turns entirely on which one is true.

Eight months, then nine, then weeks. Every instinct says this pump is dying - but how do I tell a real trend from a run of bad luck, well enough to answer "how sure are you?" on Friday?

Before you read on, answer Onkarabile. Seven repair dates on one sheet, R380,000 on Friday's agenda, and a plant manager who wants a straight answer. Write down what you would tell her and what you would rest it on. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four answers, and what each one can and cannot survive

• "Quote the mean time between failures." Quick, correct arithmetic, and it answers a different question. An average is a single number describing the whole period, so it treats "steady" and "getting rapidly worse" as identical - which is the one distinction Friday's decision turns on.

• "Compare this year with last year - five against three, so it is getting worse." Much closer, and it is where most of us would stop. It also cannot survive the follow-up question: with numbers this small, how often would five-then-three happen to a pump that was not deteriorating at all? Nobody in the room can answer that, so the finding collapses the moment somebody pushes.

• "Plot the dates and look at them." Genuinely useful, and the gaps really are bunching. Looking at a chart is judgement, not evidence, and it gives you no way to say how confident you are - which is precisely what was asked.

• "Replace it - the pattern is obvious and it is only R380,000." Might well be right, and it commits real money on the strength of a pattern that has never been tested. If it turns out the gaps were coincidence, that money was needed elsewhere in the plant.

• The gap under all four is the same. Each one either ignores how sure it is, or cannot say. What is needed is a test that compares this pump's actual failure dates against what pure bad luck would have produced - and returns not just a direction, but a strength of evidence you can defend out loud on Friday.

T2: The Tool in Action

First settle what kind of thing you are looking at, because it decides which maths is even allowed. Then turn the actual failure dates - in order - into a single number that says which way they are drifting and how strongly. Then read that number against a line you chose in advance, and say plainly what it does and does not support. Watch it run on P-2.

Step 1 - Is it repairable, or renewal?

Raw-Water Pump P-2 is repaired and put back into service each time - the same pump throughout. So it is a repairable system, and you use a trend test, not a Weibull lifetime plot. An average would treat 'getting worse' and 'steady' as the same thing, hiding the very question Onkarabile is asking.

Step 2 - Test for a trend (the Laplace test)

P-2's 7 failure times (days since you started watching): 254, 513, 657, 794, 903, 987, 1,036; T = 1,096 days. The sum is 5,144, so the mean failure time is 5,144/7 = 734.86 days - later than the no-trend middle of 1,096/2 = 548 (failures clustered toward the end, a hint of wear-out). U = (734.86 - 548) / (1,096 x sqrt(1/84)) = 186.86 / 119.59 = +1.563.

Step 3 - Read the verdict honestly, recommend

U = +1.563 is positive (deteriorating) but BELOW +1.64 (the 90% line). The honest reading: 'P-2 is more likely getting worse than not, but I can't yet swear to it at the 90% level - it is right on the edge, and the gaps are clearly shrinking; one or two more failures will push it over.' The MTBF here would be 1,036/7 = about 148 days, a flat number that hides the drift. The recommendation: budget the R380,000 replacement now and schedule it before the next dry season, with P-2 on monthly vibration checks so the next failure is caught planned, not at 2 a.m.

See what keeping the failures in order bought. The average would have said "148 days between failures" and closed the conversation, because an average throws away the sequence - the one thing Onkarabile actually asked about. The test keeps the order, and returns +1.563: positive, so deteriorating, but below the +1.64 line that ninety-per-cent confidence needs. And then it does the thing that is rarer than any calculation. It does not round that up to sound decisive. The honest sentence is "more likely getting worse than not, but I cannot yet swear to it at ninety per cent - it is right on the edge, and one more failure will settle it", and the recommendation that follows is to budget the replacement now and watch closely. That is a harder thing to say in a budget meeting than either "it is fine" or "it is failing", and it is the only one of the three the evidence carries. For 40,000 homes it means a pump replaced on evidence rather than on a flat number or a hunch.

T3: The Blueprint

Three moves, and the first one decides whether the other two are legitimate at all.

  • 1. Is it repairable, or renewal? The split that decides the whole method.
  • 2. Test for a trend. The Laplace test turns the dates into one number, U.
  • 3. Read the verdict honestly, recommend. State U against the line; name the trade-off.

The ideas underneath. An item that is thrown away and replaced starts each life with the clock at zero, so its lives can be pooled and its typical lifetime plotted - which is a different world with a different instrument, and t-0040 is where that one is learned. A repairable system is fixed and keeps running, so you watch the whole string of its failures and never restart the clock; using renewal tools on it gives a confident, wrong answer (the "just average it" trap). The Laplace trend test turns the failure times into a test statistic, U: near 0 is stable, clearly positive is deteriorating, clearly negative is improving; the cut-offs (+/- 1.64 for 90% sure, +/- 1.96 for 95%) are lines you pick before you look. It fits one repairable system whose failure dates you have in order and whose direction you need to defend. It is the wrong tool for an item that is replaced rather than repaired - there the clock restarts each time and a lifetime plot is the right instrument - and it is the wrong tool for comparing two machines with each other. Its honest limit is sample size: with seven failures the test can tell you which way the evidence leans and cannot give you certainty, and saying so is part of using it properly.

Two disciplines keep the answer honest, and the second is the harder of the two. First, never average a repairable system. It is the most common mistake in this work precisely because the arithmetic is correct: an average discards the order of the failures, and the order is the entire question. Second, choose your confidence line before you look at the answer, and then refuse to move it. A number that lands just short of the line you set is not a disappointment to be talked around - it is the measurement telling you exactly how much weight it can bear, and reporting it as certainty is how an engineer spends a reputation. "On the edge, budget now, one more failure settles it" is the sentence that survives being right and being wrong.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your analysis true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Repairable is not renewal. Do not put a repaired-and-returned system through a Weibull lifetime plot. Watch the whole string of failures; never restart the clock at each repair.

• An average hides the trend. Mean time between failures treats "getting worse" and "steady" as identical. The order of the failures is the question - a trend test keeps it.

• Pick the confidence line before you look. Decide on +/- 1.64 (90%) or +/- 1.96 (95%) in advance, and when U lands just short, say so - do not round a near-miss up to a verdict.

• A trend is about one system's own history, not a comparison. This test asks whether these failures are speeding up, not whether this pump is worse than that one. Those are different questions with different maths.

• Seven failures is seven, not a certainty. With a short record the test tells you which way the evidence leans and no more. Report the lean and the limit together; the next failure is genuinely informative, and saying so is not hedging.

You came to the desk with a bunching repair log, a tempting average, and a plant manager who wanted a straight answer by Friday. You can give her something better than a straight answer - a true one: P-2's failures are drifting toward wear-out at +1.563, real but just short of the ninety-per-cent line, so budget the replacement now and watch for the failure that will settle it either way. Do not average a repairable system. Keep the failures in order, test them, and say out loud how much the answer can carry. For 40,000 homes, that is the difference between a pump replaced on a Tuesday in daylight and one that goes at two in the morning. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
