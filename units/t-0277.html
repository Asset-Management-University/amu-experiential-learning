Step 1 of 5

Our criticality register ranks assets by what they cost - and the thing that stopped the whole plant for nine hours is number 31 on it. How do I rank by what actually holds the place up?

T1: The Ache

Bhengu Bottling fills 24,000 bottles of spring water an hour, six days a week, for a supermarket chain that fines you for short deliveries. Sixty-one people work here, most of them on the line, and a stopped plant is a stopped shift for every one of them. You are the asset manager. On the desk in front of you is the criticality register - ninety-six assets, ranked, colour-coded, reviewed every year, and the thing every maintenance budget in this plant has been argued from for a decade.

Behind you on the wall is the July board. Nine hours down. R840,000 in lost production, R110,000 of preforms that warmed and had to be scrapped, and a R70,000 fine - R1.02 million, and sixty-one people sent home at nine in the morning.

It was the compressor. A little skid-mounted unit in a shed behind the workshop, R180,000 new, in nobody's photographs of the plant. Its motor bearing seized at 06:20 and by 06:21 the blow-moulder could not form a bottle, the filler valves would not open, the capper would not turn and the palletiser would not lift. Not slower. Nothing.

You find it on the register. Number thirty-one. Below two labellers, below a shrink-wrapper, below a forklift.

And here is the part that keeps you at the desk after everyone has gone: the register is not wrong about anything. Every figure in it is accurate. The filler really does cost R4.1 million and the compressor really does cost R180,000. It has been ranking assets faithfully by exactly what it was designed to rank them by - and it put the thing that can stop the entire plant thirty-first, and nobody who read it for ten years ever saw the problem.

My criticality list ranks each asset by what it is worth on its own. What stops the plant is not what an asset is worth - it is what sits behind it. How do I rank by that instead?

Before you read on, re-rank them. Ninety-six assets, one register, and you now know it put the compressor thirty-first. Write down what you would rank by instead, and how you would work that figure out for all ninety-six. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four things people re-rank by, and where each one leaves you

• "Rank by the cost of an hour down." A real improvement, and much closer to the right idea. But you have to know that cost per asset before you can rank, and the only assets you know it for are the ones that have already stopped you. The compressor's number was R840,000 - as of last month. Before July it was zero.

• "Rank by failure history." Sound engineering, and it ranks by how often something breaks rather than by what happens when it does. A reliable asset that stops everything and a temperamental one with a spare on the shelf come out in exactly the wrong order.

• "Have the engineers score everything one to five for consequence." This is how most registers are built - experts scoring consequence from one to five - and what it can find is bounded by what the room collectively pays attention to. In this plant, as in most, everyone had walked past the compressor shed for ten years.

• "Rank by whether it has a spare." Now you are onto it - and notice what that question is really about. It is not a property of the asset at all. It is about what sits behind it if it stops.

• Every register in this building ranks assets by what each one is - its price, its age, its failure rate, its size. What actually stopped the plant in July is where the compressor sits: everything the line does passes through it, and nothing stands behind it. That is not a property you can read off an asset. It is a property of the arrows between them, and no list has arrows.

T2: The Tool in Action

Draw every asset as a dot and every dependency as an arrow - including the services that appear on no drawing. Count what each one touches. Then trace every route from where things start to where they must arrive, and ask what share of those routes each asset lies on. Watch it run on the bottling line.

Step 1 - Draw what feeds what, including the things that are not on the line

Sindi Ndlela draws the bottling line first, because it is the part everyone pictures: preform intake to blow-moulder to filler to capper, then to either labeller - there are two - to the palletiser, cold store and dispatch. Then she asks the question nobody asks: what feeds these? Compressed air goes to the blow-moulder, the filler valves, the capper and the palletiser. Clean-in-place feeds the filler. Chilled glycol feeds the cold store. She adds the compressor, the CIP skid and the chiller as dots with arrows. The compressor is in a shed behind the workshop and appears on no drawing in the building.

Step 2 - Count how many things each one touches - and notice that this is not the answer

The filler touches four - CIP, capper, blow-moulder and its own supply. The palletiser touches three. The compressor touches four as well. Sindi writes the counts up and the room is unimpressed: the filler is busiest, which everyone already knew, and it is the most expensive machine in the building, which everyone also knew. Counting connections has told them what they came in believing. She leaves it on the board anyway, because in a minute it will be the thing that makes the real answer visible.

Step 3 - Trace every path from where things start to where they must arrive, and see what lies on all of them

Every bottle must go intake, blow-moulder, filler, capper, a labeller, palletiser, cold store, dispatch. The two labellers each carry about half the routes, because either can take the work. But the blow-moulder, filler, capper and palletiser sit on every single route, and so - through its arrows into four of them - does the compressor. Sindi says it plainly: without air, the blow-moulder does not form, the filler valves do not open, the capper does not turn and the palletiser does not lift. Not slower. Nothing. And it is not on the line, so nobody has ever counted it as part of the line.

Step 4 - Put the position ranking next to the money ranking, and look at what disagrees

The register, ranked by replacement value: filler first at R4.1 million, blow-moulder second, palletiser fourth, labeller A ninth, compressor thirty-first at R180,000. Ranked by position: blow-moulder, filler, capper, palletiser and compressor all carry every route; labeller A carries half. Two rows disagree, and both matter. The compressor moves from thirty-first to joint first - it is the nine hours in July, and it is still the cheapest thing in the top ten. And labeller A comes down, because labeller B exists: nobody had ever noticed that the ninth-most-protected asset in the plant already has a stand-in.

Step 5 - Spend on position, and check the gap is not somewhere you cannot see

A second compressor, plumbed in parallel with an automatic changeover, costs R210,000 - a fifth of what July cost once the lost production, the scrapped preforms and the fine are added up, and a twentieth of the filler it protects. It is approved in one meeting because the argument is now a picture rather than a plea. And the drawing keeps giving: Sindi's last arrow is one she nearly left off - only one person on site is certified to reset the CIP skid after an alarm, which puts a human being on every route with no alternative behind them. That is the next conversation, and it is not an engineering one.

See which of the two counts did the work. Counting connections told the room what it already believed - the filler is busiest and most expensive. It was tracing the routes that moved the compressor from thirty-first to joint first, and brought labeller A down because labeller B was quietly standing behind it the whole time. Notice that neither of those findings is about an asset's own properties. Nothing about the compressor changed; what changed is that the arrows were drawn, and once they were on paper the argument for a second one took a single meeting instead of a decade. And the drawing kept going after the machines ran out - one certified person on every route, with nobody behind them, which is the same shape of risk in a form no asset register could ever hold.

T3: The Blueprint

Five moves, and the third is the one that changes the ranking.

  • 1. Draw dots and arrows - every asset, every dependency, and the services and people that are on no asset list.
  • 2. Count each one's connections. Useful, and rarely surprising.
  • 3. Trace every route from start to must-arrive, and ask what share of them each asset lies on.
  • 4. Put that ranking beside your existing one and read only the disagreements.
  • 5. Spend on position - and go back for the arrows you nearly left off.

What you have just done is network analysis applied to an asset base, and its vocabulary is only names for the two counts you made. The picture of dots and arrows is a network; each asset is a node and each dependency an edge. The count of arrows touching a node is its degree - the measure that told you what you already knew. The share of start-to-finish routes that must pass through a node is its betweenness, and it is the one that reordered the register, because it is not asking how connected something is but how much depends on it having no alternative. A node that every route must cross is a single point of failure, and one with a partner beside it is redundant - which is why labeller A, ninth on the money list, needs less protection than the shed outside.

It fits anything where something has to get from one place to another through a chain of dependencies - a plant, a water scheme, a power reticulation, a supply chain, a software system, an approval process, a hospital patient flow. What it cannot tell you is how likely anything is to fail, or what a stoppage would cost: it ranks by exposure, not by risk, and those are different questions that belong beside each other.

T4: Your Turn

Take the operation you are responsible for - a plant, a service, a business, a department. Draw the dots and the arrows on one page, and force yourself to add the services and the people that are on no list anywhere. Then trace every route from where things start to where they must arrive, and mark what lies on all of them. Put that beside whatever ranking you use today, and read only the rows that disagree.

Two disciplines decide whether the drawing finds anything. First, draw the things that are not on the asset register - compressed air, water, steam, power, the network, the one person certified to do a thing, the supplier with no second source, the permit that has to be renewed. Anything already on a register is being looked after by somebody; the whole reason a plain junction or a shed outside can stop everything for nine hours is precisely that it was never on the list to begin with, and your drawing will inherit that blind spot unless you go looking for it deliberately. Second, read the disagreements, not the agreements. Where position and price agree, you are already protecting the right things and the exercise has taught you nothing. The two or three rows where the rankings pull apart are the entire value of the work - and one of them is usually cheap enough to fix in the same week you find it.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your ranking true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A list cannot hold what matters here. Position is a property of the arrows, not of the assets, so no register - however carefully scored - can show it. That is why a faithful, accurate register put the compressor thirty-first.

• Draw dependency, not layout. Two machines standing beside each other may need nothing from one another, and a shed a hundred metres away may feed all of them. Follow what supplies what, not what is near what.

• Connection count is not criticality. It flatters the busy, expensive things you already worry about. It is the routes that must pass through something that tell you what would stop.

• Include people, permits and suppliers as nodes. The single point of failure with no spare on the shelf is very often a person on leave, a licence lapsing, or one firm that makes the part.

• Exposure is not risk. This ranks what would stop you, never how likely it is to happen or what it would cost. Put it beside those questions; do not let it answer them.

• Redundancy on the drawing is not redundancy in fact. Two paths that pass through one shared thing further along are one path. Follow each route all the way to the end before you count them as alternatives.

• This ranks for production, not for protection. It finds what stops the operation - so the fire pump, the emergency stop, the bund, the effluent plant and the spill kit will all score near zero, because none of them is on the route to the dispatch bay. Rank safety, environmental and legal duties on their own terms, on their own page, beside this drawing and never underneath it. A method that answers one question well will answer a different question confidently and wrongly.

You sat at that desk with an accurate register that had faithfully ranked the thing that stopped your plant thirty-first, and no way to say what was wrong with it. You can put a different page in front of the board now: one drawing, the routes traced, the compressor and four line machines on every route, labeller A quietly stood down because labeller B exists - and a second compressor at R210,000 against a nine-hour stoppage whose lost production alone was four times that. This is how a criticality ranking stops measuring what things are worth and starts measuring what holds the place up. And it is how you find the one certified operator standing alone on every route, before the week they are on leave. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one page of your own dots and arrows and trace the routes together.)
