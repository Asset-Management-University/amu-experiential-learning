What will be different in four years

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, and you have everything you asked for. A maturity assessment, honestly scored, showing exactly where Corathyl's asset management capability sits and where it needs to be. A mandate to improve it. And a budget - four years, thirty-two million rand.

Then the finance director stops you in the corridor and asks a reasonable question, kindly.

So what will be different in four years, and how will we know?

And you find you can talk for five minutes without answering it. You can describe activities beautifully - we will implement, we will develop, we will embed, we will roll out. Every one of those is something you will be doing. Not one of them is a condition anybody could later check.

You have also watched this before. Two improvement programmes at other utilities, neither of them cancelled in a meeting. They were just quietly starved, eighteen months in, after the sponsor who believed in them moved on - and nobody left in the building could say precisely what they had been for, or what they had already bought.

You are about to spend four years and thirty-two million rand on something you cannot yet describe to a person who was not in the room.

I have the diagnosis, the mandate and the money, and I cannot state what will be different in four years or how anyone would know. How do I write a charter that authorises this properly - and is still defensible in year three, to people who did not commission it?

Before you read on, decide. Four years. R32 million. Approved, funded, nothing blocking you. Then the finance director asked what will be different when it is finished, and how anybody would know — and you did not have an answer. Your draft says you will implement a standard, develop capability and embed a culture. There is a precedent in this building: a programme that ran for three years, spent real money, and ended when the two people who believed in it moved on — and nobody left can now say what it was for or what it had already bought. Write down what your authorising document should contain — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the grammar test that catches everybody

• “Write a detailed plan of the work — workstreams, phases, deliverables.” A very good account of what you will be doing, and nothing anybody can check. The dead programme in your building had an excellent plan too.

• “Get the sponsor to sign it, so it has authority.” Authority that leaves when the sponsor does. That is the precise failure you already have a precedent for, and a signature makes the document more personal rather than less.

• “Set milestones and report progress monthly.” Reports progress against activities. Three years in, you would be able to show that a great deal has been done and still be unable to say whether anything is different.

• “Define the benefits in the business case.” Right, and note what is about to be lost: the moment work starts, the comparison is gone forever. A benefit claimed later without a baseline taken now cannot be proved, and what cannot be proved will eventually be disbelieved, however real it was.

• “Keep it flexible — we will learn as we go.” Sensible about method and fatal about purpose. A programme that is flexible about what it is for is one that can be redefined by whoever arrives in year three.

• Write it as an institutional document rather than a personal one — something that still makes sense in year three, to people who did not commission it. The test is a matter of grammar and it catches almost everybody: implement, develop, embed are all things you will be doing, and none of them is a condition anybody can check — which is exactly why programmes described that way run for years without anyone being able to say whether they are working. State instead what will be TRUE that is not true today, so a stranger could verify it: maintenance planned from evidence rather than habit, unplanned downtime down from 6.5 to 4.0 per cent, every critical asset carrying a plan its own engineers can explain. Then baseline every benefit now, before anything changes. And say where this stops and who decides — scope and authority left implicit get re-litigated continuously by people who arrive later with different assumptions. Written that way, the programme survives the people who believed in it.

You write it as an institutional document, not a personal one. Here it is.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is the programme charter - what will be true, what it is worth, where it stops, who decides, and who it affects.

1. The vision as a CHANGED STATE, not an activity

Write what will be TRUE in four years that is not true today - a changed STATE, not a list of activities. The test is a verb test. 'We will IMPLEMENT ISO 55001', 'we will DEVELOP capability', 'we will EMBED a culture' are all things you will be DOING; none of them is a condition anybody can check, which is why programmes described this way can run for years without anyone being able to say whether they are working. Instead: 'Maintenance is planned from evidence rather than habit, unplanned downtime has fallen from 6.5 to 4.0 per cent, and every critical asset has a defensible maintenance plan its own engineers can explain.' Anchor the vision to the MATURITY ASSESSMENT's target state so it is evidenced rather than aspirational - the assessment tells you where you are and where you intend to be, and the vision is that target written in plain language for people who will never read a maturity score.

2. Benefits - baselined now, and ramped honestly

Every benefit gets a MEASURE and a BASELINE TAKEN NOW, before anything changes - because the moment the programme starts the counterfactual is gone, and an unbaselined benefit can never be proved and will therefore be disbelieved. Then ramp it honestly. Baseline unplanned downtime 6.5 per cent of 8,760 hours = 569.4 h/yr; target 4.0 per cent = 350.4 h/yr; SAVED 219.0 HOURS A YEAR, worth R8.76m a year at R40,000 an hour of lost production - AT STEADY STATE. But benefits do not start on day one: year 1 realises 0 per cent (you are building capability, not harvesting it), year 2 40 per cent = R3.50m, year 3 80 per cent = R7.01m, year 4 100 per cent = R8.76m, CUMULATIVE R19.27m against a programme cost of R32m. SO AT THE END OF THE PROGRAMME IT IS STILL R12.73m UNDERWATER, and cumulative breakeven arrives part-way through YEAR 6 - two years AFTER the programme ends. PUT THAT IN THE CHARTER. A charter that hides it is arranging its own cancellation in year 3, when somebody sees R24m spent and asks what has been bought.

3. Scope - and especially what is OUT

State what is in scope, and then do the part almost every charter skips: STATE WHAT IS OUT, explicitly and by name. A multi-year improvement programme with a budget is the most attractive destination in the organisation for every good idea that has never been funded, and without a written boundary it will accumulate them - until it is responsible for everything, accountable for nothing, and impossible to judge. Name the exclusions: this programme does not replace the CMMS, does not cover the capital renewal backlog, does not take over operations training. Then name the CHANGE-CONTROL PROCESS by which something could be added - who may propose it, who decides, and the rule that anything added brings its own time and money or displaces something already in scope. An exclusion without a route to revisit it is brittle; a scope without exclusions is not a scope.

4. Governance that can actually decide (and survive a change of sponsor)

Name DECISION RIGHTS, not meetings. A schedule of committees tells you when people will gather; it does not tell you who may approve a R2m variation, who may change a target, who may stop the programme, or what happens when they disagree. So write the thresholds: what the programme manager decides alone, what the steering group decides, what goes to the board. Then the item that a MULTI-YEAR charter must contain and a one-year charter can ignore: SPONSOR SUCCESSION. Over four years the sponsor WILL change - that is not a risk, it is a certainty - and the programme must be handed over as a governed thing rather than as somebody's personal project. This is why the charter is not a political document for the current sponsor but an INSTITUTIONAL ASSET: a contract between the programme team and the organisation's FUTURE leadership, written to be defensible to people who were not in the room when it started.

5. The stakeholder management plan - including who can stop this

Name every group affected, what each one needs from the programme and what the programme needs from them - and then the question charters avoid: WHO CAN STOP THIS? Not who is important, but who holds a veto in practice - the regulator, the union, the finance director, the operations manager who controls access to the plant. Engage those relationships deliberately and early, because discovering a veto in year 2 is discovering it too late. And this above all: COMMITMENT TO A JUST TRANSITION. This programme will change how people work. The charter formally acknowledges that this creates uncertainty and requires careful, respectful management. We commit to a process that is transparent and fair. The stakeholder management plan will not treat employees as a passive audience to be managed, but as active participants in the change. Their concerns will be actively sought, and the programme is mandated to invest time and resources in retraining, redeployment support, and clear communication, in partnership with HR and line management.

Box 2 contains the sentence most charters leave out, and it is the one that saves them: this programme is still twelve million rand underwater on the day it finishes. Say it at the start and it is a plan. Discover it in year three and it is a scandal. Let us name the ideas.

T3: The Blueprint

A charter for a multi-year programme is not a document for the person who commissioned it. It is an institutional asset - a contract between the programme team and the organisation's future leadership, written to be defensible to people who were not in the room when it started. Five questions:

  • What will be true? The vision as a changed state, not an activity. Apply the verb test: implement, develop, embed and roll out all describe what you will be doing. Anchor it to the maturity assessment's target so it is evidenced, not aspirational.
  • What is it worth, and when? Every benefit gets a measure and a baseline taken now - once the programme starts, the counterfactual is gone. Then the realisation ramp, and the payback year, named in the charter.
  • Where does it stop? Scope, and above all explicit exclusions, plus the change-control route by which something could be added - bringing its own time and money, or displacing something already in.
  • Who decides what? Decision rights and thresholds, not a schedule of meetings. And sponsor succession, which over four years is a certainty rather than a risk.
  • Who is affected, and who can stop it? The stakeholder management plan - what each group needs, and who holds a practical veto, engaged early rather than discovered in year two.

The honest arithmetic, and why it belongs in the charter. A four-year transformation costing R32m, saving 219 hours of unplanned downtime a year worth R8.76m at steady state, realises nothing in year one and only R19.27m across the whole programme. It finishes R12.73m underwater and breaks even part-way through year six. That is not a bad programme - it is a normal one, because capability takes time to build before it pays. The failure is never the ramp; it is a charter that promised as though there were no ramp, and then met somebody in year three holding a spreadsheet.

And a commitment the charter must carry - a just transition. This programme will change how people work. The charter formally acknowledges that this creates uncertainty and requires careful, respectful management. We commit to a process that is transparent and fair. The stakeholder management plan will not treat employees as a passive audience to be "managed", but as active participants in the change. Their concerns will be actively sought, and the programme is mandated to invest time and resources in retraining, redeployment support, and clear communication, in partnership with HR and line management.

One honest boundary. You are writing the charter that authorises and frames the programme. You are not running it, not performing the maturity assessment that diagnoses the need, and not delivering the improvement projects inside it. The assessment tells you what is wrong; the charter says what you will do about it, what that is worth, and who may decide.

Does it scale? A charter is a will, not a wish list. Its entire purpose is to be read and acted on by people who cannot ask you what you meant - which is why the ones that fail are never the ones with poor intentions, but the ones written in language only their author could interpret.

T4: Your Turn

A regional transport authority has completed a maturity assessment of its asset management. It scores 1.8 out of 5 against a target of 3.5. The board has approved a three-year programme at R18m. The headline claim in the approval paper reads: "Implement a modern asset management system and embed a culture of reliability."

The one measurable opportunity identified is bus fleet availability, currently 82 per cent, with a credible target of 90 per cent. The fleet is 120 buses. Each additional available bus-day is worth R2,400 in avoided hired-in replacement capacity.

  1. Rewrite the vision. The approval paper's sentence fails the verb test. Rewrite it as a changed state that an outsider could check.
  2. Baseline and value. Compute the additional available bus-days per year at the target, and their annual value at steady state.
  3. Ramp it. Assume realisation of 0, 50 and 100 per cent across the three years. Give the cumulative benefit, the net position at the end of the programme, and the year in which it breaks even.
  4. Bound it. Write one exclusion you would put in the charter, and the rule for adding anything to scope.
  5. Govern and engage. Name one decision right with a threshold, and identify who could stop this programme in practice.

The test of a real answer: three ways to go wrong here.

First, in step 1, if your rewrite still contains implement, develop, embed, establish or roll out, you have rewritten the same sentence. Ask what a passenger or an auditor would be able to observe.

Second, in step 3, do the arithmetic before you form a view. The answer is uncomfortable and it is supposed to be: this programme also does not pay back inside its own window. A learner who quietly adjusts the ramp to make it work has done the single most common dishonest thing in programme finance.

Third, in step 5, "who can stop this" is not the same question as "who is most senior". Think about who controls access to the depots, who represents the drivers whose work will change, and who signs the regulatory return.

T5: The Guardrails

Transformation charters fail slowly, and almost always for reasons visible on page one.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• A vision made of activities. Implement, develop, embed. Nobody can check any of them, so nobody can ever say whether the programme worked.

• Benefits with no baseline. Taken after the programme starts, the counterfactual is gone. The number becomes unprovable, and unprovable numbers are eventually treated as invented.

• A ramp that starts in year one. Capability is built before it is harvested. A charter promising full benefits immediately is writing a deficit it will be blamed for later.

• Hiding that payback falls outside the programme. The most common cause of quiet cancellation. Said at the start it is a plan; discovered in year three it looks like concealment.

• No exclusions. A funded multi-year programme attracts every unfunded good idea in the organisation until it is responsible for everything and judgeable on nothing.

• Governance that meets but cannot decide. A calendar of committees is not decision rights. Write the thresholds and who holds them.

• No sponsor succession. Over four years the sponsor changes. A programme that is somebody's personal project dies with their promotion.

• Stakeholders informed rather than engaged. Especially the ones who hold a practical veto and are discovered in year two.

• A charter written to win approval. It will win approval and lose year three. Write it to be defended, not to be signed.

• Treating the maturity assessment as the programme. The assessment is the diagnosis. The charter is what you intend to do about it.

• Treating the people as an implementation risk. The transformation is delivered by people whose jobs it changes. A charter that plans to manage their resistance has already decided not to hear them.

Onalenna had the diagnosis, the mandate and the money, and could not say what would be different. Write the vision as a changed state an outsider could check; baseline every benefit now and ramp it honestly, naming the payback year even when it falls outside the programme; bound the scope with explicit exclusions and a route to change them; write decision rights and thresholds rather than a schedule of meetings, and plan for the sponsor to change because over four years they will; and treat the people whose work this alters as participants rather than as an audience or a risk. A charter is not a document for the person who signs it. It is written for whoever is holding it in year three - and it must answer them without you. If you are working with an AMU Facilitator, take an improvement initiative already running in your organisation and ask the corridor question about it: what will be different, and how will we know?
