The penalty and the plan

T1: The Ache

Your critical service provider is failing, and you feel it every day in the plant's performance - the breakdowns that sit too long, the response that never comes when you need it. You know it in your bones. But every conversation about it is a fog of opinions and excuses: you say they are slow, they say you are unfair, you both remember different months, and nothing is ever settled or fixed.

Here is why the argument never ends: you are fighting it with impressions instead of evidence. You have no measured assessment of what they actually delivered against what they actually promised, no consistent way to apply the consequence you both agreed to, and no real plan that would make it right rather than just make you both feel worse.

My contracted service provider keeps missing the mark and I feel it in the plant, but every conversation is a he-said-she-said about impressions. How do I assess their performance fairly and actually fix it?

Before you read on, decide. Third review meeting this year. You say their response times have slipped badly; he says they have been within target apart from one difficult month; you both mean it. Neither of you has a figure. The agreement on the shelf specifies response and resolution times and a service credit for missing them, and nobody has ever drawn the actual numbers out of the logs — which are sitting in the system and would settle it in an hour. Meanwhile a pump waited nine days for a part and the plant felt it. Write down how you would assess their performance fairly and actually fix it — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what a snapshot cannot show

• “Escalate to their management.” Sends the same impressions up a level, where somebody more senior will hear a version of it and ask for evidence you do not have. It also spends goodwill you will need for the plan.

• “Put them on notice — threaten to terminate.” A threat you cannot presently justify, on a contract you may not be able to replace quickly. If they call it, you are worse off and your credibility is spent.

• “Charge the service credit and see if it focuses them.” Applying a consequence you have not evidenced invites a dispute you will lose — and even applied correctly it is worth being clear that money moving does not restore the service.

• “Log every failure from now on.” Right in direction and it starts the clock from today, when you already hold months of history in the system that would establish the pattern now.

• “Rebuild the relationship — the meetings are too adversarial.” Understandable, and a warmer conversation about impressions is still a conversation about impressions. It will feel better and change nothing.

• Replace impressions with evidence, and two disciplines make the measure honest. Measure what the agreement actually specifies — the stated response and resolution times, drawn from the logs rather than from anybody's memory of a bad week — because “they seem slow” cannot be argued with or acted on. And measure over several periods rather than one snapshot. This is the discipline most often skipped and it matters most, because a trend tells a truth a snapshot hides: one poor month is noise, and anyone can have a hard month, but four poor months out of six is a pattern — and the entire difference between a rough patch and consistently failing only appears when the periods are lined up. Then two things that get confused. Apply the consequence the contract already provides, calculated from the evidence and applied consistently rather than invented in frustration — and understand that its job is to be a signal, not a remedy. What restores the service is a specific plan with named actions and dates, agreed with them, aimed at the causes the measurement exposed.

You measure, you apply the agreed consequence, and you build a plan. Let us walk the discipline.

T2: The Tool in Action

This is how you conduct a supplier performance assessment - measuring against the promise over time, applying the consequence the contract already set, and turning the shortfall into a specific plan that actually restores the service.

1. Measure the promise against the delivery - over time

The assessment begins by replacing IMPRESSIONS with EVIDENCE, and two disciplines make the measure honest. FIRST, MEASURE WHAT THE SLA ACTUALLY SPECIFIES, from records: the agreed key performance indicators - availability, response time, resolution time, quality - NOT a vague sense that 'they seem slow', and drawn from the logs, not from anyone's memory of a bad week. SECOND, MEASURE OVER MULTIPLE PERIODS, NOT A SINGLE SNAPSHOT. This is the discipline people most often skip and it matters most, because A TREND TELLS A TRUTH A SNAPSHOT HIDES: one bad month is noise - anyone can have a hard month - but four bad months out of six is a PATTERN, and the whole difference between 'a supplier had a rough patch' and 'a supplier is consistently failing' only appears when you line the periods up. Assess the RUN, not the moment.

2. Apply the agreed consequence - fairly

When the evidence shows a genuine, measured shortfall, you apply the consequence the contract ALREADY provides. The SLA usually defines a SERVICE CREDIT or penalty for missing a target - commonly a SET PERCENTAGE OF THE PERIOD FEE for each breach - and your job is to CALCULATE IT PER THE CONTRACT and apply it by the rules. This is the part that turns a grievance into a legitimate act, so hold the principle firmly: A FAIR ASSESSMENT MEASURES THE PROMISE AGAINST THE EVIDENCE, NOT A PERSON AGAINST AN OPINION. THE RULES WERE AGREED IN ADVANCE. APPLYING THEM CONSISTENTLY IS AN ACT OF RESPECT. The penalty is not something you invent in anger, and it is not a bigger stick for the supplier you happen to dislike - it is the consequence both parties agreed to, applied the same way to everyone, from the same evidence. THAT CONSISTENCY IS PRECISELY WHAT MAKES IT FAIR RATHER THAN A WEAPON.

3. The penalty is a signal, not a solution

Here is the trap that catches people who have just learned to calculate a service credit: THEY THINK THE CREDIT IS THE RESPONSE. IT IS NOT. A penalty RECORDS the miss and creates pressure to improve - but it REPAIRS NOTHING on its own; the plant is still down too long, the service is still failing, and you have simply taken some money off an invoice. APPLYING THE PENALTY IS THE END OF THE ACCOUNTING, NOT THE END OF THE WORK. A penalty records a failure; a specific remediation plan builds the success. So do not mistake the deduction for the fix - the money coming off the invoice changes the ledger, not the service, and the service is the only thing you actually needed. The work is what comes next.

4. The real work: a remediation plan that is specific and measurable

A remediation plan is only real if it is SPECIFIC AND MEASURABLE - named corrective actions, measurable milestones, dates, and a review point. 'THE SUPPLIER WILL TRY HARDER' IS NOT A PLAN; it is a hope with no way to tell whether it came true. A real remediation item reads like this: 'RESTORE FIRST-RESPONSE TIME TO THE AGREED TARGET OF FOUR HOURS BY THE END OF NEXT MONTH, VERIFIED BY THE RESPONSE LOG, REVIEWED AT 30 AND 60 DAYS.' You can CHECK that; you cannot check 'try harder'. And the pressure is LADDERED, each rung a measurable chance to recover before the next: a first miss is a WARNING; a repeated miss triggers a formal CORRECTIVE ACTION PLAN; continued failure a BUSINESS REVIEW; and only persistent failure the EXIT or termination the contract allows. You climb the ladder deliberately, giving recovery a real chance at each step, rather than leaping from a bad month to a threat.

5. Diagnose the cause, aim at recovery

One judgement decides whether any of this will work, and it is where the wise assessor differs from the merely strict one: DIAGNOSE WHY THE TARGET WAS MISSED BEFORE YOU CHOOSE THE RESPONSE, because the same shortfall has three very different causes and cures. If it is a shortfall of EFFORT or attention - they can do it, they just are not - you ENFORCE the credit and hold them to the remediation plan. If it is a shortfall of CAPABILITY - they genuinely cannot do it yet - a penalty alone will never fix it, because YOU CANNOT FINE A CAPABILITY INTO EXISTENCE; the answer is to DEVELOP the supplier. And if the cause is YOUR OWN SIDE: BEFORE YOU ASSESS YOUR PARTNER'S PERFORMANCE, ASSESS YOUR OWN. IF THE FAILURE WAS ON YOUR SIDE - INFORMATION YOU SENT LATE, ACCESS YOU DID NOT PROVIDE, A TARGET THAT WAS NEVER REALISTIC - THE HONEST FIX STARTS WITH YOU. And underneath it all: A PENALTY RECORDS THE MISS. IT DOES NOT FIX THE SERVICE. THE REAL WORK IS THE REMEDIATION PLAN THAT HELPS YOUR PARTNER SUCCEED, BECAUSE THEIR SUCCESS IS THE SERVICE YOU NEED. You are not trying to win the argument; you are trying to get the plant running.

T4: Your Turn

Here is your situation. Corathyl contracts a maintenance-service provider at a monthly fee of R60,000, with an SLA to respond to a plant breakdown within 4 hours. The contract sets a service credit of 3 percent of the monthly fee for each month the response target is missed. Onalenna pulls six months of response-log data: the target was met in months 1 and 2, and missed in months 3, 4, 5 and 6.

  1. Assess it. Compare actual against target across the six months. Is this a blip or a pattern - and how do you know?
  2. Calculate the credit. Work out the total service credit due, per the contract's formula. Show your working.
  3. Author a remediation item. Write one remediation requirement that is specific, measurable, and time-bound.
  4. Diagnose and choose. Name which of the three causes you suspect (effort, capability, or your own side), and which response you would therefore choose: enforce the penalty and remediate, initiate supplier development, or fix an internal process.

The test of a real answer: four things.

First, the assessment: it is a pattern, not a blip - four consecutive misses after two good months is a clear, worsening trend, exactly the thing a multi-period view reveals and a single reading would hide. The consistency and the direction (getting worse) are the evidence.

Second, the calculation: 3 percent of R60,000 = R1,800 per missed month; four missed months (months 3, 4, 5 and 6) = R1,800 x 4 = R7,200 total service credit due. Calculated per the contract, not invented.

Third, the remediation item: something like "restore first-response time to within the 4-hour target by the end of month 7, verified by the response log, with a review at 30 and 60 days" - checkable, dated, measurable. Not "improve response times".

Fourth, diagnose and choose: any of the three can be right, and the mark of a real answer is that the response fits the cause - if they have the crews but are not prioritising Corathyl (effort), enforce the credit and hold the remediation; if they cannot cover after-hours because they lack the staff or skill (capability), develop them rather than only fining them; and if Corathyl recently changed the site-access rules so the crew physically cannot reach the plant in 4 hours (your own side), the honest fix is to correct that internally before penalising anyone.

T5: The Guardrails

A penalty records a failure; a specific remediation plan builds the success. Measure the promise against the evidence, apply the agreed consequence fairly, and fix the gap with a plan that fits the cause.

The common traps - and how to dodge them

• Judging on impressions. "They seem slow" settles nothing and starts a feud. Measure the agreed KPI from records, and let the evidence speak.

• A single snapshot instead of a trend. One bad month is noise; a pattern is a problem. Assess over multiple periods so you can tell the difference.

• Inventing the penalty. The consequence is the one the contract set, not one you reach for in anger. Calculate it by the rules.

• Applying the rules inconsistently. A penalty applied to one supplier and waived for another is a weapon, not an instrument. Apply the same rules, the same way, to everyone.

• A vague remediation. "Try harder" cannot be checked. Name the action, the milestone, the date, and the review - or it is a hope, not a plan.

• A penalty with no remediation. Taking money off the invoice does not fix the service. The credit is the signal; the plan is the fix.

• Fining a capability gap. If they genuinely cannot do it, a penalty will never conjure the ability. Develop the supplier instead.

• Penalising your own failure. Late information, poor access, an impossible target - assess your own side first. The honest fix may start with you.

• Using penalties as a profit centre. Squeezing a partner for credits burns the relationship you depend on. The aim is recovery, not revenue - or victory.

The endless argument with your failing provider was never going to be settled by stronger opinions, because it was built on impressions. So replace them: measure what they actually delivered against what they actually promised, across enough periods to tell a bad month from a bad pattern; apply the service credit the contract already set, by the rules, consistently; and then put your real effort where the service actually gets fixed - a specific, measurable remediation plan, escalated fairly, with the response matched to the cause and your own side honestly examined first.
