Two years of service and zero failures, and the honest way to find these bearings' life takes eight years - how do I get a real answer in months?

T1: The Ache

It is a cold, bright Tuesday at Windkrans Wind Farm, up on the escarpment outside Bethlehem in the Free State. You are the reliability engineer. You are standing in the workshop container at the foot of Turbine 7, and in your gloved hands you are holding a gearbox bearing the size of a dinner plate - a high-speed shaft bearing, pulled at the last service. You can feel a faint roughness as you turn the inner race with your thumb. It is not failed. But it is not new.

Across the bench, the asset manager, Lerato, is leaning on the doorframe with her arms folded. She has a board meeting on Friday. "I need a number," she says. "These bearings cost R180,000 each, fitted. We have 40 turbines. If I replace them all on a fixed three-year schedule I waste good life. If I wait for them to fail, a seized bearing wrecks the gearbox and that is R2.1 million and a crane hire. So which is it? When do they actually wear out?"

You smell the warm oil and grease off the open gearbox behind you. You know the honest answer is: you do not know yet. The bearings have been in service two years and not one has failed. The only instrument that would answer Lerato properly is time - watch them until they die, count the ages, and you have your number. That takes about eight years, by which time the turbines will have been re-bladed, the design will have changed and the question will be stale. Friday is in three days.

Two years of service and zero failures, and the honest way to find a bearing's life takes eight years. How do I get a real answer in months instead?

Before you read on, tell Lerato how you would get her a number. Forty turbines, R180,000 a bearing, R2.1 million if one seizes, two years of service, no failures, and a board meeting on Friday. Write down what you would actually do - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five answers, and what each one can honestly deliver

• "Wait for the first few failures." The only method that needs no assumptions at all, and it is correct, and it costs eight years and the first failures are gearboxes at R2.1 million each. Correctness is not the only thing an answer has to be.

• "Use the manufacturer's rated life." Fast and free, and it is a figure from a test rig under clean, steady load. These bearings live on an escarpment with gusting torque and it is exactly the mismatch that makes rated life a poor guide - which is why Lerato is asking you and not the catalogue.

• "Use industry failure data for wind gearboxes." Genuinely better, and it describes other people's turbines in other people's wind. It also gives you no way to answer the next question, which is what a design change or a different lubricant would do.

• "Monitor them and act on the readings." Right, and worth doing, and it tells you which bearing is deteriorating rather than what the population's life is. Lerato is asking a fleet question about forty turbines and a replacement policy.

• "Give her a best estimate now and refine it later." Honest about its own uncertainty, and it is a guess with a decimal point that will be quoted for five years as if it were measured.

• What every one of them accepts is that you must either wait for real time to pass or borrow somebody else's. The move none of them makes is to ask whether real time is actually necessary - whether a bearing can be made to live its whole life in front of you, faster, in a way that still tells you the truth about the ones on the escarpment.

You need two tools that work together. Here is what they produce when you run them on Lerato's bearings.

T2: The Tool in Action

Find the one way these bearings actually fail. Raise the stresses that drive that failure and nothing else, so the same failure arrives sooner. Measure how much sooner, and divide back. Then - and this is where most tests go wrong - check the answer against what the field already shows you, and be ready to distrust your own harshest test level. Here is what it produced on the Windkrans bearings.

The field facts: normal operating load on the high-speed bearing 10 kN; normal operating temperature 70°C; bearings in service 2 years with zero failures so far; a seized bearing destroys the gearbox at R2.1m, a planned swap costs R180,000.

The test plan. The true failure mode, found by sectioning two retired bearings, is sub-surface fatigue spalling - driven by load, sped up by temperature. Both are stresses you can raise without inventing a new failure, so ALT fits. You test at:

LevelTest loadTest temperature
Normal (field)10 kN70°C
ALT level A20 kN90°C
ALT level B30 kN90°C

Six bearings per level, run day and night. Level A (20 kN, 90°C) fails at a median 600 hours; Level B (30 kN, 90°C) fails at a median 200 hours; a separate trial at field temperature (20 kN, 70°C) fails at a median 900 hours.

The result. Fitting the inverse power law to the load step gives an exponent n ≈ 2.7. Combining the load acceleration (22.7 ≈ 6.5x) with the temperature acceleration measured between the two trials (1.5x for the 70-to-90°C rise) gives a total acceleration factor AF ≈ 10x at Level A - Level A ages a bearing about 10 times faster than the field.

Translate test life into field life - and watch the first answer go wrong. Level A bearings lasted 600 test hours. At AF = 10: 600 test hours x AF 10 = 6,000 field-equivalent hours. Convert to years - the turbine runs the high-speed shaft loaded roughly 3,000 hours a year (it is not windy all the time): 6,000 / 3,000 = 2 years? That is alarmingly short - and it does not match the field, where two-year bearings are only rough, not failed. This mismatch is the point of the walkthrough: it tells you Level A was too harsh a combination, and you must lean on the lower-stress trial (20 kN, 70°C, 900 hours), whose AF is only the load part, 6.5x: 900 test hours x 6.5 = 5,850 field-equivalent hours - still check against reality. You reconcile by trusting the gentler, field-temperature trial and the load exponent together, and you land on a median field life of about 7 years for the high-speed bearing under normal duty. The harsh combined level over-accelerated; you keep it only to confirm the exponent, not to set the headline number. Always reconcile the model against what the field already shows.

See what the test actually delivered, and what it nearly delivered instead. The harshest level - 30 kN at 90 degrees - gave a clean number that was wrong: translated back it implied a two-year median life, when the bearings on the escarpment are two years old and merely rough. That mismatch is the finding. Piling on stress does not simply speed up a failure; past a point it starts producing a different one, and the arithmetic afterwards is faultless and about the wrong thing. Leaning on the gentler field-temperature trial and using the harsh level only to confirm the load exponent gives a median field life of about seven years for the high-speed bearing - a number from four months of testing that Lerato can take to Friday, and that can be re-tested when the design changes rather than waited out again. And notice what made it trustworthy: not the fitting, but the moment somebody checked it against a bearing they were holding in their hand.

T3: The Blueprint

Four moves get you from no data to a measured life, and the fourth is the one that keeps you honest.

  • 1. Name the true failure mode, then gate the ALT. Section or inspect a failed (or retired) part. Ask: what stress drives this real failure, and can I raise that stress without causing a different one? On the bearing: sub-surface fatigue spalling, driven by load and sped up by temperature - both raisable without inventing a new failure. That is the gate. If raising the stress would melt a seal or snap a part instead, ALT does not fit; you have learned about the seal, not the bearing.
  • 2. Choose stresses and levels. Pick the stress (or stresses) that drive the true failure - for the bearing, load via the inverse power law (life falls as a fixed power of load - a "life is proportional to (1 ÷ load)n" rule, steep for fatigue) and temperature via the Arrhenius model (life falls by a known amount per extra degree, driven by the chemistry of the grease and oil film). Set test levels above normal but below the point something else would break - the bearing tests stayed under 100°C so the seal was never at risk.
  • 3. Run the test and fit the model. Run several parts per level, record median time to failure, and compare two same-temperature levels to solve for the load exponent: load ratio raised to the power n equals the life ratio. For the bearing, load rose 1.5x (20 to 30 kN) and life fell 3x (600 to 200 hours), so 1.5n = 3 and n ≈ 2.7 - a steep exponent, exactly what fatigue does.
  • 4. Build the acceleration factor and translate to field life. The acceleration factor (AF) is the single number that says how much faster the test ages the part than normal use - multiply test life by AF to get field life. Build it from each stress's contribution (load part 22.7 ≈ 6.5x, temperature part 1.5x, total AF ≈ 10x) and always reconcile against what the field already shows: the harshest combined level over-accelerated and, taken alone, implied an alarmingly short 2-year life that did not match the field, where two-year bearings are only rough, not failed. Trusting the gentler, field-temperature trial and the load exponent together gives the honest number: about 7 years median field life. Keep the harsh level only to confirm the exponent, never to set the headline number.

When ALT fits: real-life failures are too rare or too slow to wait for, and you can name a stress that genuinely speeds the true failure mode. When it does not: raising the stress changes what breaks (a new failure mode appears), or you have no validated stress model to trust - then your fast answer is just a fast wrong answer.

And be clear about what a measured life is not. It is a figure for the population under a stated duty - a median, with a spread around it - and it says nothing about which of the forty bearings is closest to going. Turning a life into a replacement policy means describing the in-between conditions a part passes through and how fast it moves between them, which is a separate piece of work with its own arithmetic - and a measured life from a test like this one is exactly what that work needs as an input. A life figure used directly as a replacement age will replace good bearings and miss bad ones, because the median is not a schedule.

Task 1 (warm-up) - gate it or reject it. A plastic water meter housing cracks in service after about eight years. You want an answer in three months, so you propose testing it at 95 degrees to speed the cracking up. The housing's plastic softens at 80 degrees. Is this a valid accelerated test? Say what you would do instead.

What good looks like

No. Above 80 degrees you are no longer ageing the failure you care about - you are melting the housing, which is a different failure and one that never happens in the field. The test would produce a clean number about nothing. What to do instead: stay well below the softening point and accelerate a stress that genuinely drives the real cracking - pressure cycling, or ultraviolet exposure if the meters sit in sunlight - and confirm by sectioning a test housing that the cracks look like the field cracks. That last check is the gate: if the failure surface does not match, stop, whatever the arithmetic says.

Task 2 - find the acceleration factor. A relay is tested at 125°C and fails at 400 hours. Its Arrhenius temperature model says running it at the field temperature of 55°C gives an acceleration factor of AF = 25 (test ages it 25x faster than field). What is the predicted field life in hours, and roughly in years if the relay is energised 8,760 hours a year?

What good looks like

Field life = 400 x 25 = 10,000 hours. In years: 10,000 ÷ 8,760 ≈ 1.1 years. Good answers note this is a median (middle) life, not a guarantee - half will fail sooner.

Task 3 - choose the stress, defend it. You must run an ALT on a rubber conveyor belt whose real failure is cracking from sunlight (UV) and ozone over years. Name the stress you would raise and the one you must NOT raise, and say why.

What good looks like

Raise UV/ozone exposure (a lamp chamber) - that accelerates the true failure mode. Do not simply raise mechanical load or speed to extreme - that would snap or stretch the belt (a new failure mode) and teach you nothing about cracking. The principle: accelerate the real mode, never invent a new one.

Task 4 (harder) - fit a load exponent, then use it, then distrust it. A chain is tested at two loads at the same temperature: at 4 kN it lasts 800 hours; at 8 kN it lasts 200 hours. (a) Find the inverse-power-law exponent n. (b) The chain's normal working load is 2 kN. Using your exponent, predict its life at 2 kN. (c) The chains in service have been running for six years at about 2,000 loaded hours a year and none has broken. Does your prediction survive that, and what would you do about it?

What good looks like

(a) Load doubled, life fell by a factor of 4, so 2n = 4 and n = 2. (b) Halving the load from 4 kN to 2 kN multiplies life by 22 = 4, so 800 x 4 = 3,200 hours. (c) Six years at 2,000 hours is 12,000 hours in service with no failures, and the prediction says 3,200 - so the prediction is wrong by a factor of about four, and the field is the thing that is right. A complete answer says what that means: extrapolating an exponent fitted between 4 and 8 kN all the way down to 2 kN is a long reach outside the tested range, and the real failure at low load may not even be the same one. What to do: test a level at or near 2 kN rather than extrapolating to it, and treat the field's 12,000 clean hours as the strongest single piece of evidence you hold. A test that disagrees with a machine somebody is standing next to loses.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep both tools true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• The stress you raised produced a different failure. Section a test part and compare the failure surface with a real one from the field. If they do not match, the test has told you about the new failure and nothing about the one you care about - and the arithmetic afterwards will be faultless.

• Your harshest level set the headline number. The most accelerated point is the most likely to have crossed into a different mechanism. Use it to confirm the exponent; take the headline from a gentler level, and say which one you took it from.

• You extrapolated far outside the range you tested. An exponent fitted between two high stresses is a poor guide to a much lower one. Test near the duty you actually care about rather than reaching down to it.

• The field disagrees with your answer and you defended the answer. Real machines that have run for years without failing are the strongest evidence in the room. When the test and the field disagree, the test is wrong until proven otherwise.

You stood in that container holding a bearing that was rough but not failed, with two years of data, zero failures, and Friday in three days. You can give Lerato a measured number instead of a guess: a median field life of about seven years for the high-speed bearing under normal duty, from four months of testing - and the honest account of how you got it, including the harsh level whose clean answer you threw away because a bearing in your hand disagreed with it. This is how you find out what something's life actually is, without waiting the life to find out. And it is re-testable: when the design changes next year, you run it again in four months rather than starting another eight-year wait. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one part whose life you do not know and gate its test together.)
