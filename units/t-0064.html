I cannot promise this vessel will never crack - so can I promise that if it does, it leaks where somebody notices rather than tearing open?

T1: The Ache

You are at the design desk at a pressure-vessel shop in Mpumalanga, the inspection file open in front of you. Through the window you can see the vessel itself on its cradle in the bay - a long carbon-steel cylinder, 25 mm thick, that will run at 19 bar of hot process fluid a few metres from where people work. The bay door is open; you can hear the grinder running on the next job.

The file says the workshop found and repaired a root crack last week. It also says the inspection method has a known reliable floor: it can be trusted to catch root flaws 1.2 mm tall and larger - but flaws smaller than that can slip through. So a small flaw could be sitting in a weld right now, below what the scan can promise to see.

The client's engineer phones while you are reading. "Just tell me it's safe to sign. The install crew is on site Monday." Your name goes on the safety case. It would be easy to write "all found flaws repaired - fit for service" and send it. That sentence is true and useless: it says nothing about the flaw the method might have missed.

And here is the thing you cannot write your way around. You will never get the chance of a flaw being in there to zero. Inspection has a floor, welding has variation, and hot process fluid at 19 bar does not care how careful the shop was. So the question that actually decides whether anybody is hurt is not whether this vessel might crack. It is what happens in the moment it does.

Because there are two completely different answers to that, and they land a few metres from where people work. A crack that goes through the wall and weeps can be smelled, seen, alarmed, and the plant shut down. A crack that goes through the wall and keeps running tears the vessel open. Same steel, same pressure, same crack - and the difference between an incident report and a funeral.

I cannot promise this vessel will never crack. So can I promise that if it does, it leaks where somebody notices, rather than tearing open?

Before you read on, decide what you would put in the safety case. A 25 mm wall at 19 bar, hot fluid, people a few metres away, an inspection floor of 1.2 mm, and an install crew arriving Monday. Write down what you would actually sign - your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the five things safety cases say here, and what each one promises

• "All found flaws repaired - fit for service." True, signable, and the one most likely to be written. It is a statement about what was found, and the whole difficulty is the flaw below the detection floor that was not.

• "Thicker wall, bigger margin, more conservative design." Real engineering and it makes cracking less likely without saying anything about what happens if one occurs. A thicker wall can also tear.

• "Inspect it more often." Genuinely valuable and it is looking for the crack. If the crack appears between inspections and runs, the inspection interval is not what saved anybody.

• "Fit leak detection." Now you are close - and on its own it is a hope rather than an argument. A leak detector is only useful if the failure actually presents as a leak big enough to detect, and nothing so far establishes that it would.

• "Reduce the pressure until it is unarguable." Safe, expensive, and it postpones the question rather than answering it. At some pressure the plant does not work.

• Notice what all five are about: making failure less likely. All of them are worth doing and not one of them tells you which of the two failures you would get. That is a separate thing you can actually calculate - and if it comes out the right way, it is the strongest sentence a safety case can contain: not "this will not fail" but "if it fails, it fails in the way that gives people time".

T2: The Tool in Action

Watch every step. At Bergview Pressure Fabrication, a 30-person shop in Mpumalanga, a carbon-steel pressure vessel, 25 mm wall thickness, sits on the cradle at 19 bar of hot process fluid. The workshop's inspection (a qualified PAUT root procedure) has a proven reliability floor of a90/95 = 1.2 mm - it cannot promise to catch root flaws smaller than that. The lead engineer, Thabo, must produce the safety case.

Thabo has to win a chain twice, and the inputs he needs are the ones the shop already has: a 25 mm wall, 19 bar, the leak monitoring fitted to this plant, and the weld toughness measured from the procedure coupons - mean 95 MPa-root-m after the post-weld heat treatment the shop applies as standard.

Step 1 - Find the through-wall crack length that just forms (the leaking crack). He takes the largest flaw the inspection could have missed - 1.2 mm, the floor of the procedure - and grows it forward until it penetrates the full 25 mm wall. At the moment it goes through, the through-wall crack has a length of 2c = 38 mm. This is the crack that would start leaking.

Step 2 - Test leg one: is the leak detectable, with margin? He calculates the leak rate from a 38 mm through-wall crack at 19 bar: about 0.9 litres per minute of hot fluid. The plant's leak monitoring can reliably detect 0.1 litres per minute. The detectable leak is roughly 9 times smaller than what this crack would produce - comfortable margin. Leg one passes.

Step 3 - Test leg two: is that same crack stable, or does it run? He now asks: at 2c = 38 mm through-wall, is the wall still strong enough that the crack does not keep tearing? He computes the critical crack length - the length at which the crack would run unstably - using the measured weld toughness: 2ccritical = 71 mm. The leaking crack (38 mm) is well below the critical length (71 mm). So when the crack first goes through-wall and starts to leak, it is stable - it does not run. Leg two passes.

Step 4 - State the LBB margin and the verdict. The leaking length is 38 mm; the critical length is 71 mm - a margin of about 1.9x. Both legs pass: a through-wall crack would leak detectably and stay stable long enough to be seen and the plant shut down. Leak-Before-Break is demonstrated. Thabo records the verdict: "If a through-wall crack ever forms, it leaks at ~0.9 L/min (9x detection threshold) and remains stable to 71 mm (1.9x margin). Failure mode is detectable leak, not rupture. Fit for service, with leak monitoring as a credited safeguard."

Step 5 - Record the honest residual. He notes plainly what the case depends on: the toughness data, the PWHT actually being applied and verified, the leak monitor being maintained, and material temperature staying in the ductile range (LBB is void if the steel can go brittle). The safety case is only as good as those conditions holding.

See what the chain actually bought. Nothing here made the vessel less likely to crack - the wall, the pressure and the inspection floor are all exactly as they were. What changed is that the failure mode is now established rather than hoped for: a crack that goes through this wall leaks at nine times the detection threshold, and stays stable to nearly twice its leaking length before it would run. That is why the leak monitor stops being a comfort and becomes a credited safeguard - it is protecting against a failure the analysis has shown would actually present as a leak. And the residual is written down in the same breath, because the argument holds only while its conditions do: the heat treatment applied and verified, the monitor maintained, and the steel kept in its ductile range. Leak-before-break in cold steel is not a weaker argument. It is not an argument at all.

T3: The Blueprint

Start with the plain question behind all of this: how do we know a cracked part is still safe to use?

You will not answer it by making failure less likely. Every control that does that - a thicker wall, a better weld, more inspection - lowers the chance of a crack and says nothing about its character. The chance itself is a separate question with its own arithmetic, and t-0285 is where that one is answered; the detection floor this case rests on comes from a study of its own, in t-0279. This unit answers the question those two leave open, and it is the one that decides whether anybody has time to walk away.

Now the second plain question: if a crack ever does grow through the wall, what happens at that moment?

There are two ways a pressurised wall can fail once a crack reaches through it. A leak is a small through-wall crack letting fluid weep out - it can be seen, smelled or detected, and the system shut down. Annoying, not catastrophic. A break is a crack long enough that the moment it goes through-wall, it keeps running and the wall tears open. Sudden, dangerous release.

Leak-Before-Break (LBB) is the analysis that proves which one you get. The logic is a chain you must win twice: first, detectable leak - the through-wall crack that forms must leak at a rate your monitoring can actually detect, with margin (Step 2); second, stable, not running - at that same crack length, the wall must still be strong enough that the crack does not keep tearing, so it stays put long enough for the leak to be noticed and the plant shut down (Step 3). If both hold, the failure mode is a manageable leak, not a rupture - a powerful safety argument, but only when earned, not assumed.

When this fits: a pressurised or contained system where a through-wall crack is credible, the consequence of a rupture is serious, and there is monitoring that could act on a leak. It is the argument that lets a plant run with a residual risk it cannot eliminate.

When it does not, and this is the important one: the whole argument is void where the material can fail in a brittle, fast way - low toughness, low temperature, the wrong steel. There a crack can run before it leaks, and every number above becomes a decoration on a false conclusion. Check the temperature range and the toughness data first, and if the steel can go brittle in service, say that leak-before-break cannot be claimed rather than claiming it with caveats. It is also not a substitute for the controls that reduce the chance of cracking; it is what you argue in addition to them, never instead.

The reference rule-books you work to: BS 7910 - the flaw-assessment rules behind the crack-stability calculation; API 579-1 / ASME FFS-1 - Fitness-For-Service, the wider framework for judging in-service equipment with flaws; R6 - a long-standing procedure for the failure assessment diagram used inside the calculation.

Attempt each of these four before you read "what good looks like".

Task 1 (warm-up) - which failure would you get? A colleague says: "We have leak detection on the line, so we are covered." Attempt: give two things that must be true before leak detection can be credited as a safeguard, and one condition that would make the whole argument void.

What good looks like

Two things that must be true: (1) the through-wall crack that would form must leak at a rate the monitoring can actually detect, with margin - a leak at the detection threshold is not detected reliably; and (2) at that same crack length the wall must still be strong enough that the crack stays put rather than running, so there is time for the leak to be noticed and the plant shut down. The condition that voids it: material that can fail in a brittle, fast way - low toughness, or service temperature low enough for the steel to go brittle. There a crack can run before it leaks, and the detector protects nobody. Marks lost for treating the detector as the argument; the detector is what you credit after the analysis shows the failure would present as a leak.

Task 2 - win or lose the chain (numbers). A vessel's assessment gives a through-wall crack that would leak at 0.25 litres per minute; the plant's monitoring reliably detects 0.2 litres per minute. The leaking crack length is 52 mm and the critical length at which it would run unstably is 60 mm. Does leak-before-break hold? Say what you would do.

What good looks like

No - both legs are too close to call it. Leg one: the leak is only 1.25 times the detection threshold, which is not margin, it is a coincidence - detection thresholds are themselves uncertain and a slightly fouled sensor puts the leak below it. Leg two: 52 mm against a critical 60 mm is a margin of 1.15, and every input behind those two lengths carries its own spread. What to do: do not claim leak-before-break on these numbers. Either widen the margins - lower the detection threshold with better monitoring, or raise the critical length with a toughness improvement - or state plainly that the failure mode cannot be shown to be a leak and let the decision be made on that. A complete answer states the honest sentence out loud: a margin of 1.15 on a safety argument is a number that will not survive the first person who checks the spread on its inputs.
