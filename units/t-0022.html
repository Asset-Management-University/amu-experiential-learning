The insurer wants to know how we chose the materials, and maintenance asks which lines to inspect and where - but all I have is a metallurgist's materials list, not a plan. How do I name every way this unit can corrode before the steel is even cut?

T1: The Ache

It is a cold Tuesday morning at the Baywater Refinery, a coastal refinery in a small harbour town. You are the integrity engineer on the Unit 4 revamp - a new crude-distillation unit that will run hotter, on a heavier and more sour crude, than the old one it replaces. In your hands is the piping diagram for the bottom of the crude column: a hot run carrying 343 C residue at 2.1% sulphur, and an overhead line up top running wet, salty vapour at 118 C.

The project manager, Ms Diop, leans over your shoulder. The unit must start up in fourteen months, and she asks the question you have been dreading: "The metallurgist gave us a materials list. Carbon steel here, some 9-chrome there. But the insurer wants to know how we decided. And the maintenance team is asking which lines they will need to inspect, where, and how often. Where is that written down?"

It is not written down. You have a materials list, but a materials list is not a plan. Somewhere in this unit, hot sour oil will quietly thin a carbon-steel elbow from the inside; somewhere up in that cold overhead, salt and water and a trace of acid will pit a line you did not think to watch. You know that if a single line is wrong, it does not show up at the design review - it shows up three years from now, as a leak, a fire, or a shutdown that costs millions of rand a day, and that puts people and the coastline in harm's way.

The metallurgist gave us a materials list, but the insurer wants to know HOW we decided, and maintenance asks which lines to inspect, where, and how often - where is that written down? How do I name every way this unit can corrode before the steel is even cut?

Before you read on, decide. Unit 4’s crude column has three very different worlds inside and around it. The bottoms run at 343 °C with 2.1% sulphur and a total acid number of 1.8. The overhead vapour line and condenser sit at 118 °C, wet and salty. The outside is carbon steel in coastal air. You have a metallurgist’s materials list. The insurer wants to know how you decided; maintenance wants to know which lines to inspect, where, and how often. Write down how you would produce that — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and the thing a materials list cannot tell anybody

• “Use the metallurgist’s list. They are the expert.” It is a list of answers with no record of the questions. It cannot tell maintenance where to look, and it cannot show an insurer how anything was decided — which is exactly what both of them asked for.

• “Specify better steel everywhere and stop worrying.” Expensive, and it still tells nobody where to inspect. Some mechanisms are not stopped by a better alloy at all, so the money buys confidence rather than protection.

• “Copy the inspection plan from a similar unit.” The steel may be the same; the conditions are not. The same metal in a different regime faces different threats, and a borrowed plan looks most convincing exactly where it is least applicable.

• “Commission it, then measure what is actually corroding.” That learns the answer after the steel is cut, when the one decision you could still have changed — the material — has already been made.

• “List everything that could possibly corrode it, to be safe.” A list with no discrimination cannot be acted on. Maintenance cannot inspect everything, and an inventory that names implausible threats loses its authority for the real ones.

• The move is to stop treating the unit as one object. Split it into zones that each see one regime — hot bottoms, wet overhead, coastal exterior — and ask of each zone which mechanisms are credible under its actual conditions, checked against stated thresholds rather than felt: 343 °C sits inside the sulphidation range and the sulphur is high, so that is credible; an acid number of 1.8 is well above the threshold and the temperature is in the band, so that is credible too. And write down what is NOT credible, and why — that is the half that turns a list into a decision an insurer can audit. Then say of each threat what it does, where it does it, and how fast, and the inspection plan falls out of the rates instead of out of habit.

You need a reliable, repeatable way to name every credible degradation threat before the steel is cut, and to set out, line by line, how the project will hold the corrosion back. Guessing is not good enough; nor is starting from a blank page. Here is that method.

T2: The Tool in Action

What you need is two linked deliverables, produced together: a damage-mechanism inventory - every credible way each part of the asset can degrade, named using API RP 571 (the industry catalogue of damage mechanisms) - and a corrosion-control programme, the plan that says what you will do about each threat through material selection, coatings and cathodic protection. Watch them built for Unit 4.

Step 1 - Break the asset into corrosion loops

Unit 4's crude column splits into three loops: CL-01, the hot bottoms (343 C, 2.1% sulphur, TAN 1.8); CL-02, the overhead vapour line and condenser (118 C, wet and salty - HCl + H2S + water); CL-03, the external carbon steel exposed to coastal air. Each loop sees one regime, so each faces its own threats.

Step 2 - Identify the credible damage mechanisms (API RP 571)

CL-01 at 343 C with 2.1% sulphur and TAN 1.8: sulphidation is credible (343 C sits inside the 260-540 C range, and the sulphur is high) and naphthenic-acid corrosion is credible (TAN 1.8 is above the ~0.5 threshold, 343 C is in the 230-400 C band). High-temperature H2/H2S corrosion, chloride cracking and creep are NOT credible here. So CL-01 carries two real internal threats.

Step 3 - Classify each (what, where, how fast)

Sulphidation: general wall thinning, worst at high-velocity bends, about 0.9 mm a year on carbon steel. Naphthenic-acid corrosion: localised grooving at turbulent spots (elbows, pump discharge), up to 2.5 mm a year. A carbon-steel line with a 6 mm corrosion allowance would be eaten through in 6 / 2.5 = under three years at those spots - far too fast to accept.

Step 4 - Choose the control (material, coating, cathodic protection)

CL-01: upgrade to 9Cr-1Mo (chromium drops sulphidation from 0.9 to ~0.1 mm/yr) and to Type 317L stainless at the naphthenic hot spots (its molybdenum holds that rate below 0.05 mm/yr). CL-02 (overhead): a water wash plus chemical injection to kill the acid dew point, with C276 at the worst condenser inlet. CL-03 (external): thermal-spray aluminium under the insulation (it survives the 60-175 C corrosion-under-insulation band) plus epoxy on bare steel, and impressed-current cathodic protection at -0.85 V for the buried line and tank floors.

Step 5 - Write it down (the inventory + control-programme document)

The maintenance team reads it and knows exactly which lines to inspect, for which mechanism, at which spots. The insurer reads it and sees a defensible basis, not a hope. And every materials choice traces back to a named threat and a real rate - written down before the steel was cut, governing the asset for the thirty years it will run.

See what the five steps did. They turned "we think this will be fine" into a written, defensible record: every loop, every credible threat, its rate, and the chosen defence - finished before a single pipe was ordered. This is the quiet heart of asset integrity: corrosion is a slow, invisible killer, and the only way to beat it is to name it in a design office years before it would otherwise surface as a leak on the coast. You replace hope with engineering. And one honest document serves everyone at once - the maintenance team know exactly what to inspect and where, the insurer sees a defensible basis, and the people who will work on that unit are protected by choices made before the steel was cut.

T3: The Blueprint

You build the inventory and programme in five moves.

  • 1. Break into corrosion loops. Divide the asset into runs of equipment that see the same fluid and conditions; split wherever temperature, fluid or velocity changes.
  • 2. Identify the credible mechanisms. Run API RP 571 against each loop's real conditions; name only the threats the temperature, fluid and metal actually allow.
  • 3. Classify each. What it attacks, where to look, and how fast - the corrosion rate that lets you design against it.
  • 4. Choose the control. Material selection, coatings, or cathodic protection - the right defence in the right place.
  • 5. Write the document. One controlled record, a row per loop-per-mechanism - the asset's integrity backbone for life.

The ideas underneath: a corrosion loop (or circuit) is a stretch of equipment in one fluid-and-condition regime, so a single set of threats applies to all of it; a damage mechanism is a named way metal degrades (sulphidation, naphthenic-acid corrosion, corrosion-under-insulation), and RP 571 tells you for each what it attacks, what triggers it, and where to look; and the three classic controls map to where corrosion strikes - material selection for internal process attack, coatings for external and atmospheric attack, and cathodic protection for buried or immersed steel. This fits any new project or major revamp of fixed equipment (vessels, piping, tanks, exchangers), and it belongs at design, so the answers shape the materials before steel is bought. It is not for simple low-hazard items (a handrail, an office duct), and it does not cover rotating-machinery or electrical failure modes (use FMEA / vibration there) - RP 571 is about fixed-equipment material degradation.

Two disciplines keep the inventory honest. First, name only what is credible, but name all of it - a mechanism you leave off the list is a mechanism no one will ever inspect for, and that is exactly how the unwatched line fails; walk the whole RP 571 catalogue against the real conditions, every time. Second, justify every control by its named mechanism - an alloy upgrade, a coating or a cathodic-protection system that is not tied to a specific threat and rate is either a waste of money or a false comfort; the document must let anyone trace each choice back to the degradation it defeats.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your corrosion-control programme true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• A materials list is not a plan. A column of alloys with no reasoning behind it cannot tell maintenance what to inspect or satisfy an insurer. The reasoning - threat, rate, defence - is the deliverable.

• Do not skip a credible mechanism. The line that fails is almost always the one no one named. Run the whole catalogue against each loop's real conditions; an unnamed threat is an uninspected one.

• Put the right control where corrosion strikes. Material selection beats internal process attack; coatings beat external and atmospheric attack; cathodic protection beats buried and immersed corrosion. The wrong control in the wrong place is false comfort.

• Tie every choice to a rate. "Upgrade to stainless" means nothing without the mechanism and the rate it defeats. A control with no named threat behind it is either waste or wishful thinking.

• Do this at design, not after. Once the steel is cut, your cheapest and best defences - the right alloy in the right place - are gone. The inventory is worth most before a single pipe is ordered.

You came in with a materials list and a project manager asking where the reasoning was written down. You can give her something far stronger: one controlled document that names every credible way this unit can corrode, the rate of each, and the defence chosen against it - so the maintenance team knows what to watch, the insurer sees engineering instead of hope, and the people who will run that plant are kept safe by decisions made months before commissioning. Forge the asset's integrity backbone in the design office - name every threat and its defence before the steel is cut - and you prevent, quietly and in advance, the leak, fire or shutdown that would otherwise arrive three years on. That is what it means to give an asset a long and safe life. (Working with an AMU facilitator? Bring one real unit and build its first corrosion loop together.)
