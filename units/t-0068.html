I have 47 pages of notes and memos for the pump station that keeps a town's taps running, and no way to prove, when it is built, that every promise in them was kept - how do I turn the pile into promises I can test?

T1: The Ache

It is a wet Tuesday morning at the Stillwater pump station, on the edge of a town of about 40,000 people. You are the design lead for Stillwater Municipal Water, the small utility that keeps the taps running here. The old station - two ageing pumps and a control panel held together with hope - is being replaced, and that is your job.

On the desk in front of you is a thick lever-arch file: 47 loose pages. An email from the operations manager ("the new station must never let the reservoir run dry"), a scribbled councillor's note ("residents want no more 3-day outages"), a page torn from a supplier brochure, a health-and-safety memo, a hand-drawn sketch of the pipework. The pages smell faintly of damp and old toner.

Across the desk, Mrs Sasekani, the operations manager, taps the file with one finger. She is not angry, but she is firm. "The last upgrade, we built a station that pumped beautifully - and then found it could not be switched off safely for maintenance. Nobody had written that down. We spent R180,000 fixing it after the fact. I cannot have that again."

You feel the pull to just start drawing. You know pumps; you could sketch the layout this afternoon, send it to the contractor by Friday, and look decisive. But you have 47 pages and no single place that says what the station must actually do, who asked for each thing, or how you will prove - when it is built - that every promise was kept. Start drawing now and you are designing on top of contradictions you have not read; and when something is forgotten, like the safe-shutdown last time, it costs real money and real water.

Somewhere in these 47 pages is a promise that will be forgotten, and I will not find out which one until it has cost R180,000. How do I turn a pile of wishes into promises I can prove were kept?

Before you read on, try it on the one that already went wrong. Last time, the forgotten promise was that the station must be safe to shut down for maintenance. Write down how you would have caught that one before the build - not with hindsight about pumps, but with something you could have done to those 47 pages. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare - the four answers, and what each one would have caught

• "Read the file more carefully." Honest, and the safety memo was in the file last time too - somebody did read it. Reading finds what you are looking for on the day you read, and nothing in a pile of pages tells you how many promises it contains.

• "Use a checklist of things a station must do." Genuinely useful, and it would have caught this one, because safe isolation is on every such checklist. It would not have caught the promise specific to this town that is on no checklist anywhere - and there is always one.

• "Have the operations manager sign off the design." Right, and it rests everything on one person spotting an absence. Absences are the hardest thing to see: nothing on a drawing says "the missing thing goes here."

• "Test it properly once it is built." Essential, and by then the concrete is poured. It also tests only what somebody thought to write a test for, which is the same problem arriving later and more expensively.

• Look at what all four have in common. Every one of them needs a person to notice an absence - and nothing in a file, a drawing or a test plan makes an absence visible. What is missing is a form in which a forgotten promise shows up as a blank you cannot help seeing.

T2: The Tool in Action

Read the pile once and pull out every distinct thing somebody needs. Turn each one into a single sentence a person could pass or fail, carrying a number and the name of whoever asked for it. Then put them in a table with a column for what delivers each promise and a column for the test that proves it - and read the blanks before you read anything else. Watch it work the Stillwater file.

Step 1 - Extract the needs from the pile (what + why, not how)

You read all 47 pages once and mark every 'must'. Six distinct needs surface: never let the reservoir run dry; no more 3-day outages; isolate each pump safely for maintenance (the one forgotten last time); keep running if one pump fails; running cost no higher than the old bill; status visible without climbing into the pit. Six raw needs - still vague, and that is fine; Step 2 sharpens them.

Step 2 - Write each need as a testable 'shall' requirement

The reservoir holds 2,000 m3 and the town uses about 1,800 m3/day, so the thresholds are real. REQ-01: the station shall keep reservoir level above 30% (600 m3) at all times [source: ops]. REQ-02: shall restore full supply within 4 hours of a single pump fault. REQ-03: each pump shall be isolatable for maintenance without stopping supply [the R180,000 lesson]. REQ-04: shall meet 1,800 m3/day with one pump out of service. REQ-05: monthly electricity shall not exceed R42,000. REQ-06: status shall be visible on a ground-level panel.

Step 5 - Trace every promise with an RTM

Each requirement gets a row. REQ-03 traces back to the safety memo and forward to the isolation valves and a 'shut one pump, confirm supply continues' test. An empty design cell is a promise nobody is keeping; an empty source cell is something nobody asked for. The empty cells are exactly the gaps that cost R180,000 last time - and now you see them before a single length of pipe is ordered.

See what happened to the absence. Forty-seven loose pages became six sentences that can each be passed or failed - and then a table whose empty cells say, without anyone having to notice anything, exactly which promise has nothing delivering it and which delivered thing nobody ever asked for. That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth being clear about: it does not help you look harder. It changes a missing promise from something a person has to spot into a blank square in a row. The R180,000 last time was not lost to a bad pump. It was lost to a promise nobody wrote down, in a form where not writing it down left no trace at all.

T3: The Blueprint

Three moves, and the third is the one that makes an absence visible.

  • 1. Extract the needs. Read the whole pile; pull every raw "must" - what and why, not how.
  • 2. Write testable requirements. Each need becomes one "shall" statement with an ID, a number or clear test, and a named source.
  • 3. Trace every one. A row per requirement, back to whoever asked and forward to what delivers it and the test that proves it - then read the blanks.

This is requirements engineering, and now that you have done it its words are just names for the moves. Each sentence you wrote is a requirement, and the word shall is not formality - it is what stops a promise being softened later by whoever finds it inconvenient. The test you attached to each one is its verification, and the rule you were applying without naming it is that a requirement nobody can pass or fail is not finished. The table is a Requirements Traceability Matrix, and traceability is the property you used twice in opposite directions: backwards to whoever asked, so nothing is being built that nobody wanted, and forwards to what delivers it and the test that proves it, so nothing anybody asked for quietly disappears.

It fits wherever more than a handful of promises are being made about something that will be built, and somebody will one day have to show they were kept. It is overkill for the small and throwaway - replacing one valve needs no matrix. And it has one limit worth saying out loud: it will faithfully trace a set of requirements that is missing something nobody ever thought of. It protects promises; it does not discover them.

Two disciplines keep the work honest. First, if a requirement is not testable, it is not finished - "fast", "reliable", "user-friendly" are wishes, not requirements; push each one until it carries a number or a clear yes/no test someone could actually run, because an untestable promise can never be proven kept. Second, hunt the empty cells - they are the whole point - an empty design cell is a promise nobody is keeping, an empty source cell is something nobody asked for; the matrix earns its keep not when it is full but when it shows you, early and cheaply, exactly where it is not.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your requirements work true.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Do not start drawing. The pull to sketch the design before you have read the needs is the exact trap that cost R180,000. Capture what it must do before you decide how.

• If you cannot test it, it is not a requirement. "Fast" and "reliable" prove nothing. Push every statement until it has a number or a clear yes/no test - "fills the reservoir within 4 hours", not "fills quickly".

• Every requirement needs a named source. If you cannot say who asked for it, you may be building something nobody needs. Trace it back, or strike it out.

• Read the blanks before you read the rows. A full-looking matrix is comfortable and tells you nothing you did not already know. The empty cells are the entire output of the work, and they are the first thing to look at, not the last.

• It protects promises; it does not discover them. A matrix will trace a complete-looking set that is missing something nobody thought of, and look just as tidy. Go and ask the people who will live with the thing what is not in your list.

• Match the formality to the stakes. A pump station for 40,000 people earns the full method; replacing a single valve does not. The skill is doing the amount the risk demands, not all of it always.

You came in with a lever-arch file of 47 pages, a memory of R180,000, and no way to tell which promise in that pile would be the one to go missing. You can leave with six sentences that can each be passed or failed, every one carrying the name of whoever asked for it - and a table whose blank cells point straight at the promise nothing is delivering, without you having to notice anything at all. This is how a forgotten promise stops depending on somebody being sharp enough to spot an absence. For 40,000 people whose taps run off this station, that is the difference between a thing that pumps beautifully and a thing that also switches off safely. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
