T1: The Ache

You are at a desk in the reliability office of a platinum concentrator plant; the low hum of the mill comes through the wall. In your hand is a printed maintenance history for the eight slurry pumps that feed the flotation circuit - run hours, and against each one a word: failed or still running.

The plant manager, Mthandeni, stops at your desk, tense, holding a quote for spare bearing assemblies. "Finance wants one number from me by Friday," he says. "How many run hours before these pumps start failing? Do I order spares now, or in six months?" You feel the pull to just average the failure hours and hand him a single figure - two minutes' work.

But the record will not let you. Four pumps failed at 1,450, 3,300, 5,200 and 7,100 hours. The other four are still running - they have not failed yet, at 5,000, 7,500, 9,000 and 10,000 hours. Average only the four that failed and you throw away the four that are quietly proving the pumps can last - and an average tells Mthandeni nothing about how the risk grows: whether these pumps wear out slowly, fail at random, or are dying young from a fault. A single mean would be a confident answer to the wrong question.

Before you read on, decide. Four pumps failed at 1,450, 3,300, 5,200 and 7,100 hours. Four more are still running, at 5,000, 7,500, 9,000 and 10,000. Mthandeni needs one number by Friday and a decision about ordering spares. Write down what you would give him. Your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and what each one throws away

• “Average the four that failed — about 4,300 hours.” Two minutes’ work, and it is a confident answer to the wrong question. It discards the four pumps quietly proving these things can last — one of them has run to 10,000 hours, more than twice the figure you would be handing him — and he will order spares against a number the evidence already contradicts.

• “Average all eight, using their current hours.” It stops throwing the runners away and it tells a lie about them: it records a pump that has survived 10,000 hours as though it failed at 10,000. Every one of those four rows is understated, so the answer is pulled down by exactly the evidence that should push it up.

• “Wait until more have failed and we’ll know properly.” Honest, and it is a decision to let the plant teach you by failing. The information you want is already in the file; and by Friday you would be telling Finance you have nothing, when in fact you have eight pumps’ worth of evidence.

• “Just order the spares — it is cheaper than a stoppage.” Sometimes true, and it answers whether rather than when, which is the whole of his question. Spares bought two years early are cash on a shelf; spares bought a month late are the stoppage you were avoiding.

• “Replace them all on a fixed schedule to be safe.” A real policy, and one that is only correct if these pumps wear out. If they fail at random, calendar replacement buys nothing at all; if they die young from a fault, it makes things actively worse by putting fresh pumps into the dangerous part of their life more often.

Notice what every answer above is missing. Not a number — a shape. Whether the risk rises with age, stays flat, or falls, decides which of these policies is even allowed.

You need a way to turn this mixed record - some failed, some still running - into an honest picture of pump life: when failures start, how fast the risk climbs, and how sure you can be. Here is that tool, worked on these eight pumps.

T2: The Tool in Action

Life-data analysis takes a set of "ages at event" - some where an item failed, some where it is still running - and fits a curve to the whole pattern. The curve is usually the Weibull, described by two numbers: beta (the shape - how the failure risk changes with age) and eta (the scale, or characteristic life). The trick the average misses: a still-running pump is a suspension - real information, not missing data. Watch the four steps turn the messy record into an honest answer.

Step 1 - Gather the honest dataset

Eight slurry pumps, same model, all failing (when they fail) on the drive-end bearing - one mechanism, so one honest fit. Four FAILED at 1,450 / 3,300 / 5,200 / 7,100 run hours; four are STILL RUNNING (suspensions, event = 0) at 5,000 / 7,500 / 9,000 / 10,000 hours. Averaging only the four failures (4,263 h) would throw away the four pumps already outliving it.

Step 2 - Fit the Weibull, read the SHAPE first

Fit the whole record (MLE handles the censored points) and read the shape first: beta is about 1.8. Greater than 1 means WEAR-OUT - the bearings get more likely to fail as they age, so an age-based replacement makes sense. (A beta near 1 would be random failure; below 1, infant mortality.)

Step 3 - Read the life numbers and the bounds

eta is about 8,000 run hours - the age about 63% have failed by. R at one running year (8,760 h) is only about 31% - most are gone by a year. B10 is about 2,300 hours - by then 1 in 10 has failed, the age to plan spares around. And the honesty: with only four failures the 90% confidence band is WIDE (reliability at 8,000 h could plausibly be anywhere from 0.20 to 0.55) - say so, do not hide it.

Step 4 - Decide from the shape

beta is above 1 here (wear-out), so a planned replacement age is valid: hold spares from before B10 (about 2,000-2,300 h) and plan bearing renewal before the ~8,000 h characteristic life - not run-to-failure. Had beta been about 1, calendar replacement would have been useless; below 1, replacing on age would have made it worse. The shape chose the strategy.

See what the average would have hidden. It would have said "about 4,300 hours" and told Mthandeni nothing useful. The Weibull fit says something far more powerful: the shape is wear-out (beta about 1.8), so a planned replacement age is the right policy, and the spares should be on the shelf by about 2,300 hours, not after. The single most important output is not a number of hours - it is the shape, because the shape decides whether age-based maintenance helps at all. A beta near 1 would have meant calendar replacement was a waste; below 1, that replacing on age would have made things worse. And the engineer stays honest: with only four failures the confidence band is wide, and the report says so.

T3: The Blueprint

You run the analysis in four steps. The order matters: shape before numbers.

  • 1. Gather the honest dataset. Ages at event for like items that fail by the same mechanism - failures and still-running ones. A suspension (still running) is entered at its current age with an event flag of 0; it is real information, and dropping it biases your answer toward "fails sooner than it really does". One failure mode per fit - mixing two hides both.
  • 2. Fit the Weibull and read the shape (beta) first. Fit the whole record with a method that handles censoring (maximum likelihood, MLE). Then read beta before anything else: below 1 = infant mortality, about 1 = random, above 1 = wear-out.
  • 3. Read the life numbers and the bounds. eta = the age about 63% have failed by; R(t) = the chance still running at age t (R(t) = exp(-(t/eta)^beta)); B10 = the age 10% have failed, for spares. Then the confidence bounds - wide bounds are the data telling you to be humble.
  • 4. Decide from the shape. The policy follows from beta, not from the average.

A few terms, each once: censored / suspension = reached an age without failing (its failure is still in the future); characteristic life (eta) = the 63%-failed age; B10 = a conservative service life; confidence bounds = the honest band around a fit from a sample. The tools are just calculators of the same maths - a commercial package like Weibull++, or the free Python library lifelines (a failure is event 1, a suspension event 0). And the honest limits: too few failures (one or two) means any fit is a guess with huge bounds - say so; mixed failure modes must be split first; and changed duty or ore may mean old data no longer describes the future. Life data describes what happens to a population over time - it does not tell you the condition of one specific machine today.

Two disciplines save you from a confident wrong answer. First, never delete the survivors: the pumps still running are not "not yet data" - they are the strongest evidence the equipment can last, and leaving them out always makes the picture look worse than it is. Second, read beta before you quote a single hour, because the shape decides the whole strategy - and if you have only a handful of failures, quote the wide bounds out loud rather than a false precision. A humble honest curve beats a confident wrong mean every time.

T5: The Guardrails

A few lines keep your life-data analysis honest.

The traps - and how to stay true

• Never average away the survivors. A still-running item is information, not a blank. Drop the suspensions and you bias every answer toward "fails sooner than it really does" - the exact opposite of the honesty the analysis exists to give.

• Read the shape before the number. The age means little until you know beta: above 1, age-based replacement works; about 1, the calendar is useless; below 1, replacing on age makes failures more common. Quoting a life without the shape is quoting the timing of the wrong strategy.

• One failure mode per fit. Mix bearing failures with seal failures and one curve hides both - usually as a tell-tale "dog-leg" in the plot. Split the data by mechanism first; this is the most-skipped and most-important step.

• Few failures, wide bounds - say so. With only a handful of failures the confidence band is wide, and that is the truth, not a weakness. State the band out loud; every new failure or suspension will sharpen it.

• It is a population, not a machine. Life data tells you how a fleet behaves over time; it cannot tell you the health of one specific unit today. For that, reach for condition monitoring - and let the failure shape, not a calendar, set the policy it serves.

Mthandeni asked for one number, and the kindest, truest thing you can give him is not the number he asked for but the one he needs: not "about 4,300 hours", but "these bearings wear out, hold spares by 2,300 hours, renew before 8,000 - and my confidence is wide until more fail". Do not ask for one number; ask for the shape. The shape of the failure tells you the strategy; the numbers and their bounds tell you the timing and the risk. Get that right and a messy record of failures and survivors becomes an honest, defensible plan. (Working with an AMU facilitator?
