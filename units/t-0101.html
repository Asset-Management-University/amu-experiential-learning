Ask the data yourself

T1: The Ache

You are Onalenna, asset manager at Corathyl Water Treatment Works. You have done the hard part: you know exactly where the answer lives. The record of every pump failure this year sits in one table - work_orders - in the maintenance system. You just need to know which pumps failed most, and how much downtime each one cost. Then you can decide which to overhaul first.

But you cannot get in. The system is the reporting team's. So you send an email: "Please send me a report of which pumps failed most this year." You wait. Three days. A week. Finally a spreadsheet arrives - and it lists every maintenance job, not just failures, with no totals. It answers a slightly different question to the one you asked. So you email again, more carefully this time. And you wait again.

Two weeks gone. The overhaul decision has slipped, the risk carried all the while - and the whole time, the answer was sitting right there in that table, just behind a pane of glass you were not allowed to reach through.

I know exactly which table holds my answer - but I have to beg someone else to fetch it, and wait weeks for a report that asks the wrong question. How do I just ask the data directly, myself?

Before you read on, decide. The table is called work_orders. Each row is one job: which asset, what type (failure or service), how many hours of downtime, and the date. There are hundreds of rows. What you need, to make the overhaul call, is simple to say out loud: for this year's failures only, show me each pump with how many times it failed and its total downtime, worst first. You know the table. You know the fields. You have waited two weeks for somebody else to answer a slightly different question. Write down how you would get the answer — your own answer, not a shortlist to choose from.

Now compare — the five answers, and how close your sentence already is

• “Email again, more precisely this time.” You have done that. The specification travels through somebody who does not carry the decision, so the translation loses whatever you did not think to say — and you find out what that was in another fortnight.

• “Ask for the whole table exported and do it in a spreadsheet.” Workable for hundreds of rows and it breaks quietly as they become thousands. It also puts a stale copy of the truth on your laptop, which will be out of date by the time anybody asks again.

• “Get a report built properly, once, so it is there next time.” Sensible, and it answers this question forever. The one that matters is the second question — the one that only becomes obvious after you have seen the first answer — and that will need another report.

• “Ask the analyst to sit with me so we get it right.” Genuinely the best of the four, and it costs their morning every time you are curious. Curiosity that has to be booked is curiosity that stops.

• “Learn to program.” Not necessary, and the belief that it is, is exactly what has kept this decision waiting a fortnight.

• Read your own sentence again, and notice its shape: show me each pump, with how many times it failed and its total downtime; from the work-orders table; where the job was a failure and the date is this year; grouped by pump; worst first. That is not a description of a question — it very nearly is the question, in the form the table would accept. Naming what you want, saying which table, saying which rows qualify, saying how to group them and how to order them is the whole shape of asking, and there is remarkably little else to it. The glass is thinner than it looks. And the thing you gain is not mainly the two weeks: it is the second question, and the third, which currently cost a fortnight each and therefore never get asked at all.

There is a way - and it is far more within your reach than you think. Let us show you what it looks like - finished - first.

T2: The Tool in Action

Here is the whole thing - the key that reaches through the glass. First, a peek at the raw table you are asking; then the few lines that ask it; then the clean answer they hand back, in seconds.

The raw table - work_orders (a few of its hundreds of rows):

asset = Pump_103, type = failure, downtime_hours = 9, date = 2026-02-11
asset = Pump_103, type = service, downtime_hours = 2, date = 2026-03-04
asset = Pump_088, type = failure, downtime_hours = 6, date = 2026-04-19
asset = Pump_103, type = failure, downtime_hours = 11, date = 2026-05-02
... hundreds more ...

The few lines that ask the question:

SELECT asset, COUNT(*) AS failures, SUM(downtime_hours) AS total_downtime
FROM work_orders
WHERE type = 'failure' AND date >= '2026-01-01'
GROUP BY asset
ORDER BY failures DESC;

The answer it hands back, instantly:

Pump_103 - 7 failures - 56 total downtime hours
Pump_088 - 4 failures - 23 total downtime hours
Pump_041 - 2 failures - 9 total downtime hours

Read those five lines aloud and notice something: they are almost plain English. Show me each pump and its failure count and total downtime; from the work-orders table; where the job was a failure this year; grouped by pump; worst first. The two-week wait just became two seconds. Now let us walk through it, line by line.

T3: The Blueprint

That structured way of asking a database a question has a name: the Structured Query Language, or SQL (many say "sequel"). It is the global standard for asking questions of data - and here is the freeing truth: you do not need to be a programmer. SQL is a tool for getting answers, as much a core engineering skill as reading a P&ID or using a spreadsheet. It is built from just a handful of clauses, and each one answers a plain question. Here is the query, broken into its five parts:

1. SELECT - what do you want to SEE in the answer?

SELECT asset, COUNT(*) AS failures, SUM(downtime_hours) AS total_downtime -- show me each pump, how many times it failed, and its total downtime hours.

2. FROM - which table holds the data?

FROM work_orders -- the maintenance table from your systems map (t-0100), where every job is recorded.

3. WHERE - which rows do you care about?

WHERE type = 'failure' AND date >= '2026-01-01' -- only the failures, and only this year.

4. GROUP BY - how to gather rows for counting and totalling?

GROUP BY asset -- gather the rows pump by pump, so COUNT and SUM are worked out separately for each one.

5. ORDER BY - how to sort the answer?

ORDER BY failures DESC -- worst first (DESC means descending, biggest at the top), so the pump that failed most is the first row.

That is the whole grammar of asking. Notice it builds the way you think: what do I want, from where, for which rows, gathered how, sorted how. (One rule the database is strict about: the clauses must come in exactly that order - SELECT, FROM, WHERE, GROUP BY, ORDER BY.)

Does it scale? Onalenna asks a few hundred work-orders which pump failed most. An aerospace engineer at a major airline asks millions of flight records which component fails most across the whole fleet - using the very same five clauses. The data gets bigger; the way you ask it does not change at all.

T4: Your Turn

Now you ask a question yourself. Here is a friendly little table called tuck_shop_sales, with columns: item, price, quantity, sale_date. Your question: which three items earned the most money this month?

Build your query one clause at a time, the same five boxes:

  • SELECT the item, and the total money it made (the price times the quantity, summed).
  • FROM the tuck_shop_sales table.
  • WHERE the sale happened this month.
  • GROUP BY item, so each item's money is totalled separately.
  • ORDER BY that total, biggest first - then the top three rows of your result are your three best sellers.

T5: The Guardrails

Asking the data yourself is a real power, and a few traps catch almost everyone the first time. Glance through these before you run anything for real.

The common traps - and the one bright red line

• THE RED LINE: only ever ask, never change. As an engineer reading data, you use SELECT - which only looks. You do NOT use UPDATE or DELETE on a live system; those change or destroy real records, and a single careless line can wipe a year of history. Read-only. Always. This is non-negotiable.

• The "give me everything" trap. Asking for every column (SELECT *) instead of the few you need. It is slow, messy, and hides the answer in noise. Name the columns you actually want.

• The forgotten WHERE. Leave out the filter and you analyse the entire table - every job ever, not just this year's failures - and your answer is quietly wrong.

• The right query, the wrong question. A query can run perfectly and still answer something you did not mean to ask. Always check the result against the real question in your head.

• Trusting the number blindly. SQL faithfully totals whatever is in the table - even if the data is poor ("fixed pump" logged as the failure type). A clean answer from dirty data is still wrong. (That is why your information strategy and systems map come first.)

Onalenna lost two weeks to a question she could have answered in two seconds - not because she lacked the answer, but because she lacked the key. Five plain clauses are that key. Learn to ask - what, from where, which rows, grouped how, sorted how - and the data behind the glass will simply answer you, in any system, for the rest of your career.
